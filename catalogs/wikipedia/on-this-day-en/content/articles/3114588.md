An **off-year election** in the [United States](3434750.md) is typically a [general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elections_in_the_United_States) held in an odd-numbered year when neither a [presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_presidential_election) nor a [midterm election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_midterm_election) takes place.[^cite_note-Time1927-1][^cite_note-CBS-2] At times, the term "off-year" may also be used to refer to midterm election years,[^cite_note-3] while the term "off-cycle" can also refer to any election held on another date than [Election Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_Day_\(United_States\)) of an even-numbered year.[^cite_note-4]

Off-year elections during odd-numbered years rarely feature any election to a federal office, few state legislative elections, and very few gubernatorial elections. Instead, the vast majority of these elections are held at the county and municipal level. On the ballot are many [mayors](101146.md), a wide variety of [citizen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_initiative) and [legislatively referred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislative_referral) incentives and referendums in various states, and many more local public offices. They may also feature a number of [special elections](155434.md) to fill vacancies in various federal, state, and local offices. Jurisdictions that hold off-year elections require more frequent voting than jurisdictions that consolidate elections in even-numbered years.

## Background and rationale

Off-year elections often feature far fewer races than either presidential or midterm elections and generate far lower [voter turnout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voter_turnout) than even-numbered election years.[^cite_note-5]

While the [fixed-term elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed-term_election) for U.S. president have always been held on even-numbered years, this was not always the case for congressional elections. Before Congress began to standardize elections for the [House of Representatives](19468510.md) in 1872, individual states could schedule theirs into the first months of an odd-numbered year.[^cite_note-npr-6] [Senate](24909346.md) elections were more problematic prior to the ratification of the [Seventeenth Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seventeenth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution) in 1913. Under the original rules of [Article 1, Section 3 of the U.S. Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_One_of_the_United_States_Constitution#Section_3:_Senate), senators were chosen by [state legislatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_legislature_\(United_States\)) instead of [direct elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_elections). This meant that they were affected by [legislative deadlock](3786577.md), and seats would remain vacant for months or years until their state legislatures could agree on whom to send to the Senate.[^cite_note-7]

The political calculations of incumbent politicians appears to be the most common thread over the years guiding decisions around election timing for the few states with off-year elections.[^cite_note-npr-6] The lower turnout also benefits well-organized special interest groups that often make up local political machines,[^cite_note-8] making it easier for their favored candidates to [capture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulatory_capture) more of a government.[^cite_note-Anzia-9] Even though large majorities from both major political parties want to shift to on-cycle elections,[^cite_note-Anzia-9] these interest groups have used their political power to slow down some but not all of the reform efforts, with California, Arizona and Nevada seeing significant success in shifting local elections on-cycle.[^cite_note-Zoltan-10]

## Federal elections

[Regularly scheduled elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed-term_election) for the [Senate](24909346.md) and the [House of Representatives](19468510.md) are always held in even-numbered years. Elections for these offices are only held during odd-numbered years if accommodating a special election—usually either due to incumbents resigning or dying while in office.

Special elections are never held for the U.S. president. If the president dies, resigns, or is (via impeachment conviction) removed from office, the successor is determined by the [presidential line of succession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_presidential_line_of_succession), as specified by the [United States Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Constitution) and the [Presidential Succession Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidential_Succession_Act), and serves the rest of the presidential term.

## State elections

Five states elect their respective governors to four-year terms during off-year elections: [Kentucky](253074.md), [Louisiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_governors_of_Louisiana), [Mississippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_governors_of_Mississippi), [New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governor_of_New_Jersey), and [Virginia](253093.md).[^cite_note-11] Kentucky, Louisiana, and Mississippi hold their gubernatorial elections during the off-year before the presidential election; e.g. the [2023 elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_United_States_gubernatorial_elections). New Jersey and Virginia then hold theirs in the off-year after the presidential election; e.g. the [2025 elections](69180119.md).

Louisiana, Mississippi, New Jersey, and Virginia also hold off-year state legislative elections.

Off-years may also feature a wide variety of [citizen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_initiative) and [Legislative referred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislative_referral) incentives and referendums in various states, as well as a number of special elections to fill various state offices. States may also allow [recall elections](129203.md), such as the [2021 California gubernatorial recall election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_California_gubernatorial_recall_election).

## Local elections

Many races held during off-year, odd-numbered election years are for offices at the municipal and local level. Other municipalities and local governments instead consolidate their elections in even-numbered years to save costs, increase voter turnout, and have a far more representative group of voters.[^cite_note-Zoltan-10]

[**<sup>2</sup>**](#ref_HouseDelegates2) As well as all six [non-voting delegates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-voting_members_of_the_United_States_House_of_Representatives) of the U.S. House.

[**<sup>3</sup>**](#ref_HousePR3) As well as five [non-voting delegates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-voting_members_of_the_United_States_House_of_Representatives) of the U.S. House. The [resident commissioner of Puerto Rico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resident_commissioner_of_Puerto_Rico) instead serves a four-year term that coincides with the presidential term.

[**<sup>4</sup>**](#ref_NEandVTGovs4) The governors of New Hampshire and Vermont are each elected to two-year terms. The other 48 state governors and all five territorial governors serve four-year terms.

[**<sup>5</sup>**](#ref_LtGov5) In 26 states and 3 territories the lieutenant governor is elected on the same ticket as the governor: AK, CO, CT, FL, HI, IL, IN, IA, KS, KY, MD, MA, MI, MN, MT, NE, NJ, NM, NY, ND, OH, PA, SC, SD, UT, WI, GU, MP, VI.

[**<sup>6</sup>**](#ref_VTofficials6) Like the governor, Vermont's other officials are each elected to two-year terms. All other state officers for all other states listed serve four-year terms.

[**<sup>7</sup>**](#ref_Comptroller7) In some states, the comptroller or controller has the duties equivalent to a treasurer. There are some states with both positions, so both have been included separately.

[**<sup>8</sup>**](#ref_StateLeg8) This list does not differentiate chambers of each legislature. Forty-nine state legislatures are bicameral; Nebraska is unicameral. Additionally, Washington, DC, Guam, and the US Virgin Islands are unicameral; the other territories are bicameral. All legislatures have varying terms for their members. Many have two-year terms for the lower house and four-year terms for the upper house. Some have all two-year terms and some all four-year terms. Arkansas has a combination of both two- and four-year terms in the same chamber.

[**<sup>9</sup>**](#ref_SBE9) Most states not listed here have a board appointed by the governor and legislature. All boards listed here have members that serve four-year staggered terms, except Colorado, which has six-year terms, and Guam, which has two-year terms. Most are elected statewide, some are elected from districts. Louisiana, Ohio, Guam, and the Northern Mariana Islands have additional members who are appointed.
