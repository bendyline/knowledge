**Dublin**[^cite_note-14] is the [capital](181337.md) and largest city of [Ireland](14560.md).[^cite_note-15][^cite_note-16] Situated on [Dublin Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Bay) at the mouth of the [River Liffey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Liffey), it is in the [province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provinces_of_Ireland) of [Leinster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leinster), and is bordered on the south by the Dublin Mountains, part of the [Wicklow Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wicklow_Mountains) range. Dublin is the largest city by population on the island of [Ireland](147575.md); at the [2022 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_census_of_Ireland), the city council area had a population of 592,713, while the city, including suburbs, had a population of 1,263,219, and [County Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Dublin) had a population of 1,501,500.[^cite_note-2022mapdcc-7][^cite_note-2022mapdcands-17][^cite_note-18] Various definitions of a metropolitan [Greater Dublin Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Dublin_Area) exist.

A settlement was established in the area by the [Gaels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaels) during or before the 7th century,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDickson2014x-19] followed by the [Vikings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viking). As the [Kingdom of Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Dublin) grew, it became Ireland's principal settlement by the 12th century, following the [Anglo-Norman invasion of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Norman_invasion_of_Ireland).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDickson2014x-19] Dublin expanded rapidly from the 17th century and was briefly the second largest city in the [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire) and sixth largest in [Western Europe](33800.md) after the [Acts of Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_of_Union_1800) in 1800.[^cite_note-20] Following independence in 1922, Dublin became the capital of the [Irish Free State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Free_State), [renamed Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_the_Irish_State) in 1937.

## Etymology

See also: [Other names of Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_European_cities_in_different_languages:_C%E2%80%93D#D)

The name *Dublin* comes from the [Middle Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Irish) *Du(i)blind* (literally "Blackpool"),[^cite_note-\:2-21] from *dubh* [\[d̪ˠuβˠ\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA) "black, dark" and *linn* [\[l̠ʲin̠ʲ(dʲ)\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA) "pool". This evolved into the [Early Modern Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Modern_Irish) form *Du(i)bhlinn*,[^cite_note-\:2-21] which was pronounced "Duílinn" [\[ˈd̪ˠiːlʲin̠ʲ\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Irish) in the [local dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_language#Leinster). The name refers to a dark tidal pool on the site of the castle gardens at the rear of [Dublin Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Castle), where the [River Poddle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Poddle) entered the [Liffey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Liffey).

The Middle Irish pronunciation is preserved in the names for the city in other languages such as [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English) *Difelin*, [Old Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse) *Dyflin*, modern [Icelandic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icelandic_language) *Dyflinn* and modern [Manx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manx_language) *Divlyn* as well as [Welsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_language) *Dulyn* and [Breton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breton_language) *Dulenn*. Other localities in Ireland also bear the name *Duibhlinn*, variously anglicised as Devlin,[^cite_note-22] Divlin[^cite_note-23] and Difflin.[^cite_note-24] Variations on the name are also found in traditionally Gaelic-speaking areas of [Scotland](26994.md), such as *An Linne Dhubh* ("the black pool"), which is part of [Loch Linnhe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loch_Linnhe).

The Viking settlement was preceded by a Christian ecclesiastical settlement known as *Duibhlinn*, from which *Dyflin* took its name.[^cite_note-Clarke44-25] Beginning in the 9th and 10th centuries, there were two settlements where the modern city stands. The Viking settlement of about 841, *Dyflin*, and a Gaelic settlement, Áth Cliath ("ford of hurdles")[^cite_note-26] further up the river, at the present-day [Father Mathew Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Father_Mathew_Bridge), also known as Dublin Bridge, at the bottom of [Church Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_Street,_Dublin).

*Baile Átha Cliath*, meaning "town of the hurdled [ford](30432825.md)", is the common name for the city in Modern Irish, which is often contracted to *Bleá Cliath* or *Blea Cliath* when spoken.[^cite_note-27] *Áth Cliath* is a place name referring to a fording point of the River Liffey near Father Mathew Bridge. *_Baile Átha Cliath_* was an early Christian monastery, believed to have been in the area of Aungier Street, currently occupied by [Whitefriar Street Carmelite Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitefriar_Street_Carmelite_Church).

## History

Main article: [History of Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Dublin)

For a chronological guide, see [Timeline of Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_Dublin).

Humans have inhabited the area of [Dublin Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Bay) since prehistoric times. Fish traps discovered from excavations during the construction of the [Convention Centre Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convention_Centre_Dublin) indicate human habitation as far back as 6,000 years ago. Further traps were discovered closer to the old settlement of the city of Dublin on the [south quays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_quays) near [St. James's Gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._James's_Gate), which indicate [mesolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesolithic) human activity.[^cite_note-28][^cite_note-29]

[Ptolemy's map of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy's_map_of_Ireland), of about 140 AD, provides possibly the earliest reference to a settlement near Dublin. [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy), the Greco-Roman astronomer and [cartographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartographer), called it *[Eblana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eblana) polis* ([Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language): Ἔβλανα πόλις).[^cite_note-Holder-30]



A statue of a [Viking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vikings) on a [longship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longship) in Dublin

The Viking settlement of about 841 was preceded by a Christian ecclesiastical settlement known as *Duibhlinn*, from which *Dyflin* took its name.[^cite_note-Clarke44-25] Evidence indicating that Anglo-Saxons occupied Dublin before the Vikings arrived in 841 has been found in an archaeological dig in [Temple Bar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_Bar,_Dublin).[^cite_note-31]

The Irish government recognised 988 as the year in which the city was settled and that this first settlement would later become the city of Dublin; Dublin celebrated its 'official' millennium in 1988.[^cite_note-it1986-32]

Beginning in the 9th and 10th centuries, two settlements later became modern Dublin. The subsequent Scandinavian settlement centred on the [River Poddle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Poddle), a tributary of the Liffey in an area now known as [Wood Quay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood_Quay). The Dubhlinn was a pool on the lowest stretch of the Poddle, where ships used to moor. This pool was finally fully infilled during the early 18th century, as the city grew. The Dubhlinn lay where the Castle Garden is now located, opposite the [Chester Beatty Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chester_Beatty_Library) within [Dublin Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Castle). *[Táin Bó Cuailgne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C3%A1in_B%C3%B3_Cuailgne)* ("The Cattle Raid of Cooley") refers to *Dublind rissa ratter Áth Cliath*, meaning "Dublin, which is called Ath Cliath".

### Middle Ages

In 841, the [Vikings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vikings) established a fortified base in Dublin.[^cite_note-33] The town grew into a substantial commercial centre under [Olaf Guthfrithson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olaf_Guthfrithson) in the mid-to-late 10th century[^cite_note-34] and, despite some attacks by the native Irish, it remained largely under Viking control until the [Norman invasion of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_invasion_of_Ireland) was launched from Wales in 1169.[^cite_note-Davies-35][^cite_note-36] The [hinterland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinterland) of Dublin in the Norse period was named in [Old Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse_language): *Dyflinnar skíði*, [lit.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literal_translation) 'Dublinshire'.[^cite_note-37] It was upon the death of [Muirchertach Mac Lochlainn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muirchertach_Mac_Lochlainn) in early 1166 that [Ruaidrí Ua Conchobair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruaidr%C3%AD_Ua_Conchobair), [King of Connacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_of_Connacht), proceeded to Dublin and was inaugurated *King of Ireland* without opposition.

According to some historians, part of the city's early economic growth was driven by the slave trade.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDickson201410-38] [Slavery in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_Ireland) and Dublin reached its pinnacle in the 9th and 10th centuries.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHolm1989x-39] Prisoners from slave raids and kidnappings, which captured men, women and children, brought revenue to the Gaelic Irish Sea raiders, and to the Vikings who had initiated the practice.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHolm1989p._335:_"the_very_idea_of_the_taking_of_prisoners_of_war_spread_to_the_Irish_\[from_the_Vikings\]_in_the_tenth_century"-40] The victims came from [Wales](69894.md), England, Normandy and beyond.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDickson201410-38]

The King of Leinster, [Diarmait Mac Murchada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diarmait_Mac_Murchada), after his exile by Ruaidhrí, enlisted the help of [Strongbow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_de_Clare,_2nd_Earl_of_Pembroke), the Earl of Pembroke, to conquer Dublin. Following Mac Murchada's death, Strongbow declared himself King of Leinster after gaining control of the city. In response to Strongbow's successful invasion, [Henry II of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_II_of_England) affirmed his ultimate sovereignty by mounting a larger invasion in 1171 and pronounced himself [Lord of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_of_Ireland).[^cite_note-Dublin.info-41] Around this time, the *county of the City of Dublin* was established along with certain liberties adjacent to the city proper. This continued down to 1840 when the [barony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barony_\(Ireland\)) of Dublin City was separated from the [barony of Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_\(barony\)). Since 2001, both baronies have been redesignated as the *City of Dublin*.



[Dublin Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Castle), with its 13th-century tower, was the fortified seat of [British rule in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_rule_in_Ireland) until 1922.

[Dublin Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Castle), which became the centre of Anglo-Norman power in Ireland, was founded in 1204 as a major defensive work on the orders of King [John of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_of_England).[^cite_note-42] Following the appointment of the first [Lord Mayor of Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Mayor_of_Dublin) in 1229, the city expanded and had a population of 8,000 by the end of the 13th century. Dublin prospered as a trade centre, despite an attempt by King [Robert the Bruce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_the_Bruce) of Scotland to capture the city in 1317.[^cite_note-Dublin.info-41] It remained a relatively small walled medieval town during the 14th century and was under constant threat from the surrounding native clans. In 1348, the [Black Death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death), a lethal plague which had ravaged Europe, took hold in Dublin and killed thousands over the following decade.[^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44]



[Father Mathew Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Father_Mathew_Bridge), also known as Dublin Bridge



Dubline, 1610; a contemporary map by John Speed (1896 reprint)

Dublin was the heart of the area known as [the Pale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pale_\(Ireland\)), a narrow strip of English settlement along the eastern coast, under the control of the [English Crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Crown). The [Tudor conquest of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tudor_conquest_of_Ireland) in the 16th century marked a new era for Dublin, with the city enjoying a renewed prominence as the centre of administrative rule in Ireland, where English control and settlement had become much more extensive. Determined to make Dublin a Protestant city, Queen [Elizabeth I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_I) established [Trinity College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College_Dublin) in 1592 as a solely Protestant university and ordered that the Catholic [St. Patrick's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Patrick's_Cathedral,_Dublin) and [Christ Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ_Church_Cathedral,_Dublin) cathedrals be converted to the Protestant church. The earliest map of the city of Dublin is [John Speed's Map of Dublin (1610)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Speed's_Map_of_Dublin_\(1610\)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMaxwell199756–57-45]

The city had a population of 21,000 in 1640 before a plague from 1649 to 1651 wiped out almost half of the inhabitants. However, the city prospered again soon after, thanks to the wool and linen trade with England, and reached a population of over 50,000 by 1700.[^cite_note-46] By 1698 the manufacture of wool employed 12,000 people.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMaxwell1997252-47]

### Early modern



[Henrietta Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrietta_Street,_Dublin), developed in the 1720s, is the earliest [Georgian street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_Dublin) in Dublin.

As the city continued to prosper during the 18th century, [Georgian Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_Dublin) became, for a short period, the second-largest city of the [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire) and the fifth-largest city in Europe, with a population exceeding 130,000. In comparison, some medieval streets and layouts (including the areas around [Temple Bar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_Bar,_Dublin), Aungier Street, Capel Street, and Thomas Street) were less affected by the wave of Georgian reconstruction, and much of Dublin's architecture and layout dates from this period.[^cite_note-48][^cite_note-49][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMaxwell1997220-50]

Dublin grew even more dramatically during the 18th century, with the construction of many new districts and buildings, such as [Merrion Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merrion_Square), [Parliament House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Houses_of_Parliament), and the [Royal Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_Hall,_Dublin). The [Wide Streets Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wide_Streets_Commission) was established in 1757 at the request of [Dublin Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Corporation) to govern architectural standards on the layout of streets, bridges, and buildings.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMaxwell199758–59-51] In 1759, the [Guinness brewery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinness_brewery) was founded, and would eventually grow to become the largest brewery in the world and the largest employer in Dublin.[^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53] During the 1700s, linen was not subject to the same trade restrictions with England as wool, and became the most important Irish export. Over 1.5 million yards of linen were exported from Ireland in 1710, rising to almost 19 million yards by 1779.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMaxwell1997256-54]

### Late modern and contemporary



The island of Ireland, showing location of Dublin city



[The GPO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Post_Office_\(Dublin\)) on [O'Connell Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O'Connell_Street) was at the centre of the 1916 [Easter Rising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Rising).



Damage in Dublin city centre following the 1916 [Easter Rising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Rising) with the ruins of the GPO to the left

Dublin suffered a period of political and economic decline during the 19th century following the [Acts of Union 1800](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_of_Union_1800), under which the seat of government was transferred to the [Westminster Parliament](13964.md) in London. The city played no major role in the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution), but remained the centre of administration and a transport hub for most of the island. Ireland had no significant coal reserves, the fuel of the time, and Dublin was not a centre of shipbuilding, the other main driver of industrial development in Britain and Ireland.[^cite_note-Davies-35] [Belfast](5046.md) developed faster than Dublin during this period on a mixture of international trade, factory-based linen cloth production and shipbuilding.[^cite_note-Lyons-55] By 1814, the population of Dublin was 175,319 as counted under the Population Act, making the population of Dublin higher than any town in England except London.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMaxwell1997139-56] The population had reached 233,159 inhabitants by the time of the [1841 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1841_census_of_Ireland).[^cite_note-57]

The [Easter Rising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Rising) of 1916, the [Irish War of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_War_of_Independence), and the subsequent [Irish Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Civil_War) resulted in significant physical destruction in central Dublin. The [Government of the Irish Free State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_the_Irish_Free_State) rebuilt the city centre and located the new parliament, the [Oireachtas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oireachtas), in [Leinster House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leinster_House). Since the beginning of [Norman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normans) rule in the 12th century, the city has functioned as the capital in varying geopolitical entities: [Lordship of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lordship_of_Ireland) (1171–1541), [Kingdom of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Ireland) (1541–1800), as part of the [United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_of_Great_Britain_and_Ireland) (1801–1922), and the [Irish Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Republic) (1919–1922). Following the [partition of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_of_Ireland) in 1922, it became the capital of the [Irish Free State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Free_State) (1922–1937) and is now the capital of Ireland. One of the memorials to commemorate that time is the [Garden of Remembrance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_of_Remembrance_\(Dublin\)).

Dublin was also a victim of the Northern Irish [Troubles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Troubles), although during this 30-year conflict, violence mainly occurred within Northern Ireland. A Loyalist paramilitary group, the [Ulster Volunteer Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_Volunteer_Force), bombed the city during this time – notably in an atrocity known as the [Dublin and Monaghan bombings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_and_Monaghan_bombings) in which 34 people died, mainly in central Dublin.

Large parts of Georgian Dublin were demolished or substantially redeveloped in the mid-20th century during a boom in office buildings. After this boom, the recessions of the 1970s and 1980s slowed construction. Cumulatively, this led to a significant decline in the number of people living in the centre of the city, and by 1985 the town had approximately 150 acres of derelict land which had been earmarked for development and 10 million square feet (900 thousand square metres) of office space.[^cite_note-58]

Since 1997, Dublin's landscape has changed. The city was at the forefront of Ireland's economic expansion during the [Celtic Tiger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_Tiger) period, with private-sector and state-led development of housing, transport, and business. Following an economic decline during the Great Recession, Dublin has rebounded and as of 2017 has close to full employment,[^cite_note-\:0-59] but has a significant problem with housing supply in both the city and surrounds.[^cite_note-60]

Civic Offices of [Dublin City Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_City_Council)

[Dublin City Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_City_Council) is a [unicameral](204304.md) assembly of 63 members elected every five years from [local electoral areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_electoral_area).[^cite_note-61] It is presided over by the [Lord Mayor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Mayor_of_Dublin), who is elected for a yearly term and resides in Dublin's [Mansion House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mansion_House,_Dublin). Council meetings occur at [Dublin City Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_City_Hall), while most of its administrative activities are based in the Civic Offices on [Wood Quay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood_Quay). The party or coalition of parties with the majority of seats assigns committee members, introduces policies, and proposes the Lord Mayor. The Council passes an annual budget for spending on areas such as housing, traffic management, refuse, drainage, and planning. The Dublin City Manager is responsible for implementing City Council decisions, but also has considerable executive power. Neighbouring local authorities in County Dublin are [South Dublin County Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Dublin_County_Council), [Fingal County Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fingal_County_Council), and [Dun Laoghaire-Rathdown County Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%BAn_Laoghaire%E2%80%93Rathdown_County_Council), and parts of the wider city and its suburbs lie in the functional areas of all three.[^cite_note-62]

### National



[Leinster House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leinster_House) on Kildare Street houses the [Oireachtas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oireachtas).

As the capital city, Dublin is the seat of the Irish parliament, the [Oireachtas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oireachtas). It is composed of the [President of Ireland](24676.md), [Dáil Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A1il_%C3%89ireann) as the house of representatives, and [Seanad Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seanad_%C3%89ireann) as the upper house. The President resides in [Áras an Uachtaráin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%81ras_an_Uachtar%C3%A1in) in [Phoenix Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenix_Park), while both houses of the Oireachtas meet in [Leinster House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leinster_House), a former ducal residence on [Kildare Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kildare_Street). It has been the home of the Irish parliament since the foundation of the [Irish Free State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Free_State) in 1922. The old [Irish Houses of Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Houses_of_Parliament) of the [Kingdom of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Ireland), which dissolved in 1801, are located in [College Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_Green,_Dublin).

[Government Buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Buildings) house the [Department of the Taoiseach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_the_Taoiseach), the Council Chamber, the Department of Finance, and the [Office of the Attorney General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attorney_General_of_Ireland). It consists of a main building (completed 1911) with two wings (completed 1921). It was designed by Thomas Manley Dean and Sir [Aston Webb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aston_Webb) as the [Royal College of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_College_of_Science_\(Ireland\)). The [First Dáil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_D%C3%A1il) originally met in the [Mansion House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mansion_House,_Dublin) in 1919. The Irish Free State government took over the two wings of the building to serve as a temporary home for some ministries. In contrast, the central building became the College of Technology until 1989.[^cite_note-63] Although both it and Leinster House were intended to be temporary locations, they became the permanent homes of parliament from then on.

For elections to [Dáil Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A1il_%C3%89ireann), five constituencies are wholly or predominantly in the Dublin City area: [Dublin Central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Central) (4 seats), [Dublin Bay North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Bay_North) (5 seats), [Dublin North-West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_North-West) (3 seats), [Dublin South-Central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_South-Central) (4 seats) and [Dublin Bay South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Bay_South) (4 seats). Twenty TDs are elected in total.[^cite_note-64] The constituency of [Dublin West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_West) (4 seats) is partially in Dublin City, but predominantly in [Fingal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fingal).

At the [2024 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Irish_general_election), the Dublin city area elected 5 [Sinn Féin](28175.md), 4 [Fianna Fáil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fianna_F%C3%A1il), 4 [Social Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_Democrats_\(Ireland\)), 3 [Fine Gael](11252.md), 2 [Labour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_Party_\(Ireland\)) and 2 Independents.[^cite_note-65]

## Geography

### Landscape



Satellite image showing the [River Liffey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Liffey) entering the [Irish Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Sea) as it divides Dublin into the [Northside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northside_\(Dublin\)) and the [Southside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southside_\(Dublin\))

Dublin is situated at the mouth of the [River Liffey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Liffey) and its urban area encompasses approximately 345 square kilometres (133 sq mi) in east-central Ireland. It is bordered by the Dublin Mountains, a low mountain range and sub-range of the [Wicklow Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wicklow_Mountains), to the south, and surrounded by flat farmland to the north and west.[^cite_note-Facts_about_Dublin-66]

#### Watercourses

See also: [List of rivers of County Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_rivers_of_County_Dublin)

The River Liffey divides the city into two, between the Northside and the Southside. The Liffey bends at [Leixlip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leixlip) from a northeasterly route to a predominantly eastward direction. This point also marks the transition from more agricultural land to urban development.[^cite_note-erdb-67] The city itself was founded where the [River Poddle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Poddle) met the Liffey. The early Viking settlement was also facilitated by the small [Steine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steine_of_Dublin) or Steyne River, the larger Camac[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEO'Brennan194024–25-68] and the Bradogue,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEO'Brennan194023–24-69] in particular.

Two secondary rivers further divide the city: the [River Tolka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Tolka), running southeast into Dublin Bay, and the [River Dodder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Dodder), running northeast to near the mouth of the Liffey. These, along with the Liffey, have multiple tributaries. Many lesser rivers and streams also flow to the sea within the suburban parts of the city.[^cite_note-TenDoz_rivers_sml_sea-70]

Two canals – the [Grand Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canal_of_Ireland) on the southside and the [Royal Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Canal_of_Ireland) on the northside – ring the inner city on their way from the west, both connecting with the [River Shannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Shannon).

### Climate

Similar to much of the rest of northwestern Europe, Dublin has a [maritime climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maritime_climate) ([*Cfb*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification)) with mild-warm summers, cool winters, and little temperature extremes. At [Merrion Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merrion_Square), the coldest month is February, with an average minimum temperature of 4.1 °C (39.4 °F). The warmest month is July, with an average maximum temperature of 20.1 °C (68.2 °F). Due to the [urban heat island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island) effect, Dublin city has the warmest summertime nights in Ireland. The average minimum temperature at Merrion Square in July is 13.5 °C (56.3 °F). The lowest July temperature ever recorded at the station was 7.8 °C (46.0 °F) on 3 July 1974.[^cite_note-auto-71]



Winter in Dublin



The city during summer

The highest temperature officially recorded in Dublin is 33.1 °C (91.6 °F) on 18 July 2022, at the [Phoenix Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenix_Park). A non-official record of 33.5 °C (92.3 °F) was recorded at Phoenix Park in July 1876.[^cite_note-72]

Dublin's sheltered location on the east coast makes it the driest place in Ireland, receiving only about half the rainfall of the west coast. [Ringsend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ringsend) in the south of the city records the lowest rainfall in the country, with an average annual precipitation of 683 mm (27 in).[^cite_note-73] The average annual precipitation in the city centre is 726 mm (29 in). At Merrion Square, the wettest year and driest year on record occurred within 5 years of each other. 1953 receiving just 463.1 mm (18.23 in) of rainfall, while 1958 recorded 1,022.5 mm (40.26 in).[^cite_note-auto-71] The main precipitation in winter is rain. Snow showers occur between November and March. Hail is more common than snow. Strong Atlantic winds are most common in autumn. These winds can affect Dublin, but because of its easterly location, it is the least affected part of the country. In winter, easterly winds render the city colder and more prone to snow showers.

Dublin has long summer days and short winter days. Based on satellite observations, [Met Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Met_%C3%89ireann) estimates that Dublin's coastal areas typically receive over 1,600 hours of sunshine per year,[^cite_note-74] with the climate getting progressively duller inland. Dublin airport, located north of the city and about 10 kilometres (6.2 mi) from the coast, records an average of 1,485 hours of sunshine per year. The station at Dublin airport has been maintaining climate records since November 1941. The sunniest year on record was 1,740 hours in 1959. The dullest year was 1987 with 1,240 hours of sunshine. The lowest monthly total of sunshine on record was 16.4 hours in January 1996. The highest was 305.9 hours in July 1955.[^cite_note-75]

In the 20th century, smog and air-pollution were an issue in the city, precipitating a ban on bituminous fuels across Dublin.[^cite_note-coalbangov-76][^cite_note-coalbantimes-77] The ban was implemented in 1990 to address black smoke concentrations, that had been linked to cardiovascular and respiratory deaths in residents. Since the ban, non-trauma death rates, respiratory death rates, and cardiovascular death rates have declined by an estimated 350 deaths annually.[^cite_note-78][^cite_note-coalbantimes-77]

## Cityscape

### Areas

#### City centre

Victorian Buildings on South Great George's Street, Dublin

Victorian-era buildings, such as the [George's Street Arcade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George's_Street_Arcade), are typical in the south inner city.

The historic city centre of Dublin is encircled by the [Royal Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Canal) and [Grand Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canal_\(Ireland\)), bounded to the west by [Heuston railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuston_railway_station) and [Phoenix Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenix_Park), and to the east by the [IFSC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Financial_Services_Centre,_Dublin) and the [Docklands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Docklands). [O'Connell Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O'Connell_Street) is the main thoroughfare of the inner city, and many Dublin Bus routes, as well as the Green line of the [Luas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luas), have a stop at O'Connell Street. The main shopping streets of the inner city include [Henry Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Street,_Dublin) on the Northside, and [Grafton Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grafton_Street) on the Southside.[^cite_note-87][^cite_note-88]

In some tourism and real-estate marketing contexts, inner Dublin is sometimes divided into a number of quarters.[^cite_note-quartersvisit-89][^cite_note-90] These include the Medieval Quarter (in the area of [Dublin Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Castle), [Christ Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ_Church,_Dublin) and [St Patrick's Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Patrick's_Cathedral,_Dublin) and the old city walls),[^cite_note-91] the Georgian Quarter (including the area around St Stephen's Green, Trinity College, and [Merrion Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merrion_Square)), the Docklands Quarter (around the [Dublin Docklands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Docklands) and [Silicon Docks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_Docks)), the Cultural Quarter (around [Temple Bar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_Bar,_Dublin)), and Creative Quarter (between South William Street and George's Street).[^cite_note-92]

#### Suburbs

Apartment blocks in Sandyford, Dublin

High density apartments in [Sandyford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandyford,_Dublin)

Dublin has dozens of suburbs; northside suburbs include [Blanchardstown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blanchardstown), [Finglas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finglas), [Ballymun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballymun), [Clontarf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clontarf,_Dublin), [Raheny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raheny), [Malahide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malahide) and [Howth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howth), while southside suburbs include [Tallaght](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tallaght), [Sandyford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandyford,_Dublin), [Templeogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Templeogue), [Drimnagh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drimnagh), [Rathmines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rathmines), [Dún Laoghaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%BAn_Laoghaire) and [Dalkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalkey).

Starting in the late 2010s, there was a significant amount of high density residential developments in the suburbs of Dublin,[^cite_note-93] with mid to high-rise apartments being built in Sandyford,[^cite_note-aidanmurphy-94] [Ashtown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashtown,_Dublin),[^cite_note-ronaldquinlan-95] and Tallaght.[^cite_note-96]

### Cultural divide

A north–south division once, to some extent, traditionally existed, with the River Liffey as the divider.[^cite_note-northsouthmyth2-97] The southside was, in recent times, generally seen as being more affluent and genteel than the northside.[^cite_note-northsouthmyth2-97] There have also been some social divisions evident between the coastal suburbs in the east of the city, and the newer developments further to the west.[^cite_note-98][^cite_note-99]

### Landmarks

Further information: [List of public art in Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_public_art_in_Dublin)



The [Spire of Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spire_of_Dublin) rises behind the statue of [Jim Larkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Larkin).

Dublin has many landmarks and monuments dating back hundreds of years. One of the oldest is [Dublin Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Castle), which was first founded as a major defensive work on the orders of England's [King John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John,_King_of_England) in 1204, shortly after the [Norman invasion of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_invasion_of_Ireland) in 1169, when it was commanded that a castle be built with strong walls and good ditches for the defence of the city, the administration of justice, and the protection of the King's treasure.[^cite_note-history-100] Largely complete by 1230, the castle was of typical Norman courtyard design, with a central square without a [keep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keep), bounded on all sides by tall defensive walls and protected at each corner by a circular tower. Sited to the south-east of Norman Dublin, the castle formed one corner of the outer perimeter of the city, using the [River Poddle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Poddle) as a natural means of defence.

One of Dublin's most prominent landmarks is the [Spire of Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spire_of_Dublin), officially entitled the "Monument of Light".[^cite_note-101] It is a 121.2-metre (398 ft) conical spire made of stainless steel, completed in 2003 and located on [O'Connell Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O'Connell_Street), where it meets Henry Street and North Earl Street. It replaced [Nelson's Pillar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nelson's_Pillar) and is intended to mark Dublin's place in the 21st century. The spire was designed by [Ian Ritchie Architects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_Ritchie_Architects),[^cite_note-102] who sought an "Elegant and dynamic simplicity bridging art and technology". The base of the monument is lit, and its top is illuminated to serve as a beacon in the night sky across the city.

The Old Library of [Trinity College Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College_Dublin), holding the [Book of Kells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Kells), is one of the city's most visited sites.[^cite_note-103] The Book of Kells is an illustrated manuscript created by Irish monks circa 800 AD. The [Ha'penny Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ha'penny_Bridge), an iron footbridge over the River Liffey, is one of the most photographed sights in Dublin and is considered to be one of Dublin's most iconic landmarks.[^cite_note-104]

Other landmarks and monuments include [Christ Church Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ_Church_Cathedral,_Dublin) and [St Patrick's Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Patrick's_Cathedral,_Dublin), the [Mansion House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mansion_House,_Dublin), the [Molly Malone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molly_Malone) statue, the complex of buildings around Leinster House, including part of the [National Museum of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Ireland) and the [National Library of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Library_of_Ireland), [The Custom House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Custom_House) and [Áras an Uachtaráin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%81ras_an_Uachtar%C3%A1in). Other sights include the [Anna Livia monument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Livia_\(monument\)). The [Poolbeg Towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poolbeg_Generating_Station) are also landmark features of Dublin, and visible from various spots around the city.

### Parks



An aerial view of [St Stephen's Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Stephen's_Green)

There are 66 [green spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_green_space), including 34 parks, within Dublin as of 2022,[^cite_note-105] with Dublin City Council managing over 1,500 hectares (3,700 acres) of parks.[^cite_note-106] Public parks include the [Phoenix Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenix_Park), [Herbert Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Park), [St Stephen's Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Stephen's_Green), [Saint Anne's Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Anne's_Park) and [Bull Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bull_Island). The Phoenix Park is about 3 km (2 miles) west of the city centre, north of the [River Liffey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Liffey). Its 16-kilometre (10 mi) perimeter wall encloses 707 hectares (1,750 acres), making it one of the largest walled city parks in Europe.[^cite_note-107] It includes large areas of grassland and tree-lined avenues, and since the 17th century has been home to a herd of wild [fallow deer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallow_deer). The residence of the [President of Ireland](24676.md) (Áras an Uachtaráin), which was built in 1751,[^cite_note-108] is located in the park. The park is also home to [Dublin Zoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Zoo), [Ashtown Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashtown_Castle), and the official residence of the [United States Ambassador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Ambassador_to_Ireland). Music concerts are also sometimes held in the park.

St Stephen's Green is adjacent to one of Dublin's main shopping streets, [Grafton Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grafton_Street,_Dublin), and to [a shopping centre named after it](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen's_Green_Shopping_Centre), while on its surrounding streets are the offices of several public bodies.

[Saint Anne's Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Anne's_Park) is a public park and recreational facility, shared between [Raheny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raheny) and [Clontarf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clontarf,_Dublin), both suburbs on the Northside. The park, the second largest municipal park in Dublin, is part of a former 2-square-kilometre (0.8 sq mi; 500-acre) estate assembled by members of the [Guinness family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinness_family), beginning with [Benjamin Lee Guinness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Lee_Guinness) in 1835. The largest municipal park is adjacent (North) [Bull Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bull_Island), also shared between Clontarf and Raheny, featuring a 5 km beach, Dollymount Strand.

## Economy

See also: [Economy of Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Dublin) and [Economy of County Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Dublin#Economy)



[Ulster Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_Bank) on [George's Quay Plaza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George's_Quay_Plaza)



[Google](1092923.md) headquarters in Dublin



View of [Boland's Mills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boland's_Mills)

The Dublin region is the economic centre of Ireland, and was at the forefront of the country's economic expansion during the [Celtic Tiger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_Tiger) period. In 2009, Dublin was listed as the fourth richest city in the world by [purchasing power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purchasing_power) and 10th richest by personal income.[^cite_note-117][^cite_note-118] According to *Mercer's 2011 Worldwide Cost of Living Survey*, Dublin was the 13th most expensive city in the [European Union](9317.md) (down from 10th in 2010) and the 58th most expensive place to live in the world (down from 42nd in 2010).[^cite_note-119] As of 2017, approximately 874,400 people were employed in the [Greater Dublin Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Dublin_Area). Around 60% of people employed in Ireland's financial, ICT, and professional sectors are based in this area.[^cite_note-120]

Several Dublin's traditional industries, such as food processing, textile manufacturing, brewing, and distilling, have gradually declined, although [Guinness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinness) has been brewed at the [St. James's Gate Brewery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._James's_Gate_Brewery) since 1759. Economic improvements in the 1990s attracted some global pharmaceutical and information and communications technology companies to the city and the Greater Dublin Area. Companies such as [Microsoft](19001.md), [Google](1092923.md), [Amazon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon.com), [eBay](130495.md), [PayPal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PayPal), [Yahoo!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yahoo!), [Facebook](7529378.md), [X](9988187.md), [Accenture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accenture), [TikTok](56822861.md), and [Pfizer](62304.md) now have European headquarters or operational bases in the city, with several located in enterprise clusters like the [Digital Hub](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Digital_Hub) and [Silicon Docks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_Docks). The presence of these companies has driven economic expansion in the city and has led Dublin to be referred to as the "Tech Capital of Europe".[^cite_note-\:0-59]

Financial services have also become important to the city since the establishment of Dublin's [International Financial Services Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Financial_Services_Centre,_Dublin) in 1987. More than 500 operations are approved to trade under the IFSC programme. The centre is host to half of the world's top 50 banks and to half of the top 20 insurance companies.[^cite_note-121] Many international firms have established major headquarters in the city, such as [Citibank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citibank). The [Irish Stock Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Stock_Exchange) (ISEQ), [Internet Neutral Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Neutral_Exchange) (INEX), and Irish Enterprise Exchange (IEX) are also located in Dublin. Dublin has been positioned as one of the main cities vying to host Financial Services companies hoping to retain access to the Eurozone after [Brexit](41688778.md). The Celtic Tiger also led to a temporary construction boom, with large redevelopment projects in the [Dublin Docklands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Docklands) and [Spencer Dock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spencer_Dock). Completed projects include the [Convention Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convention_Centre_Dublin), the [3Arena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3Arena_\(Dublin\)), and the [Bord Gáis Energy Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bord_G%C3%A1is_Energy_Theatre).

In the second quarter of 2018, Dublin touched its lowest unemployment rate in a decade, when it fell down to 5.7% as reported by the Dublin Economic Monitor.[^cite_note-122][^cite_note-123] In November 2022, Dublin was ranked as one of the worst cities in the world for travel, health and cost of living.[^cite_note-124] On 24 September 2022, thousands took to the streets in protest against the cost of living crisis.[^cite_note-125]

As of 2024, Dublin's [gross domestic product](12594.md) is €253.6 billion, making it one of the largest city economies in the [European Union](9317.md).

## Transport

Main article: [Transport in Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_in_Dublin)

### Road



The [M50 motorway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M50_motorway_\(Ireland\)) surrounding Dublin

The road network in Ireland is primarily focused on Dublin. The [M50 motorway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M50_motorway_\(Ireland\)), a semi-[ring road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_road) which runs around the south, west, and north of the city, connects important national primary routes to the rest of the country. In 2008, the [West-Link](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West-Link) toll bridge was replaced by the [eFlow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EFlow) barrier-free tolling system, with a three-tiered charge system based on electronic tags and car pre-registration.[^cite_note-126]

The first phase of a proposed eastern bypass for the city is the [Dublin Port Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Port_Tunnel), which officially opened in 2006 to cater mainly for heavy vehicles. The tunnel connects [Dublin Port](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Port) and the [M1 motorway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M1_motorway_\(Republic_of_Ireland\)) close to Dublin Airport. An inner and outer orbital route also surrounds the city. The inner orbital route runs approximately around the heart of the Georgian city. The outer orbital route runs primarily along the natural circle formed by Dublin's two canals, the [Grand Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canal_of_Ireland) and the [Royal Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Canal_of_Ireland), as well as the North and South Circular Roads.

The 2016 [TomTom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TomTom) Traffic Index ranked Dublin the 15th most congested city in the world and the 7th most congested in Europe.[^cite_note-127][^cite_note-128]

#### Bus



[Dublin Bus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Bus)

Dublin is served by a network of nearly 200 bus routes that cover the city and its suburbs. The majority of these are provided by [Dublin Bus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Bus), with a modest number having been transferred to Go Ahead Ireland since 2018. Several smaller companies also operate. Fares are generally calculated on a stage system based on distance travelled. Several fare levels apply to most services. A "Real Time Passenger Information" system was introduced at Dublin Bus bus stops in 2012, which displays the projected arrival time of the next bus based on its GPS position. The [National Transport Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Transport_Authority_\(Ireland\)) is responsible for integrating bus and rail services in Dublin. It has been involved in introducing a pre-paid smart card, called a [TFI Leap Card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TFI_Leap_Card), which can be used on all of Dublin's public transport services.[^cite_note-129]

The [BusConnects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BusConnects) programme includes several proposed improvements to Dublin's bus network, including new spine and orbital routes. The spine routes are intended to increase the frequency of buses along major corridors, and the orbital routes aim to "provide connections between suburbs and town centres, without having to travel into the City Centre".[^cite_note-130] In 2022, Dublin Bus began the process of electrifying its fleet with new [battery-powered buses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battery_electric_bus), with plans for 85% of Dublin buses to be zero-emission by 2032.[^cite_note-131]

#### Cycling



[Dublinbikes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublinbikes) terminal in the [Docklands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Docklands)

The [2011 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_census_of_Ireland) indicated that 5.9% of commuters in Dublin cycled. A 2013 report by Dublin City Council on traffic flows crossing the canals in and out of the city found that just under 10% of all traffic was made up of cyclists, representing an increase of 14.1% over 2012 and an 87.2% increase over 2006 levels. The increase was attributed to measures such as the Dublinbikes bike rental scheme, the provision of cycle lanes, public awareness campaigns promoting cycling, and the introduction of the 30 km/h city centre speed limit.[^cite_note-132]

Dublin City Council began installing cycle lanes and tracks throughout the city in the 1990s, and as of 2012 the city had over 200 kilometres (120 miles) of specific on- and off-road tracks for cyclists.[^cite_note-133] In 2011, the city was ranked 9th of major world cities on the *Copenhagenize Index of Bicycle-Friendly Cities*.[^cite_note-134] The same index showed a fall to 15th in 2015,[^cite_note-135] and Dublin was outside the top 20 in 2017.[^cite_note-136]

[Dublinbikes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublinbikes) is a [self-service bicycle rental scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicycle_sharing_system) which has been in operation in Dublin since 2009. Sponsored by [JCDecaux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JCDecaux) and [Just Eat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Just_Eat), the scheme comprises hundreds of unisex bicycles stationed at 44 terminals across the city centre. Users must either subscribe to an annual Long Term Hire Card or purchase a three-day ticket.[^cite_note-137] As of 2018, Dublinbikes had over 66,000 long-term subscribers making over 2 million journeys per year.[^cite_note-138][^cite_note-139]

### Rail



[Dublin Connolly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Connolly)

[Heuston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Heuston_railway_station) and [Connolly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Connolly_railway_station) stations are the two main railway termini in Dublin. The [Dublin Suburban Rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Suburban_Rail) network consists of five railway lines serving the [Greater Dublin Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Dublin_Area) and outlying commuter towns.[^cite_note-140] [Iarnród Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iarnr%C3%B3d_%C3%89ireann) provides [Commuter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commuter_\(Irish_Rail\)) services from Connolly station northwards to [Drogheda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drogheda) and [Dundalk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dundalk); westwards to [Maynooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maynooth), [M3 Parkway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M3_Parkway_railway_station) and [Longford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longford); and from Heuston station southwestwards to [Portlaoise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portlaoise). Commuter services are operated using [diesel multiple units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diesel_multiple_unit). The [Dublin Area Rapid Transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Area_Rapid_Transit) (DART) network, an [electrified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railway_electrification) commuter railway network which runs primarily along the coast of Dublin, was launched in 1984. The DART consists of 32 stations as of 2025, running from [Malahide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malahide) and [Howth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howth) southwards as far as [Bray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bray,_County_Wicklow) and [Greystones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greystones).[^cite_note-141][^cite_note-142][^cite_note-143] The network is being significantly expanded in the 2020s as part of the [DART+](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Area_Rapid_Transit#DART+) project, with future services to run to Drogheda, Maynooth, M3 Parkway and [Hazelhatch and Celbridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazelhatch_and_Celbridge_railway_station).[^cite_note-144] In 2025, passengers for Dublin suburban services and the DART were 14.3 million and 22.6 million, respectively.[^cite_note-145]

Iarnród Éireann also provides [InterCity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/InterCity_\(Iarnr%C3%B3d_%C3%89ireann\)) services from Heuston station to [Cork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cork_\(city\)), [Galway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galway), [Waterford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterford), [Tralee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tralee), [Westport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westport,_County_Mayo) and [Limerick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limerick); and from Connolly station to [Sligo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sligo) and [Rosslare Europort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosslare_Europort).[^cite_note-146] *[Enterprise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enterprise_\(train_service\))*, a cross-border inter-city rail service between Dublin and Belfast, was launched in 1947 and is jointly operated by Iarnród Éireann and [NI Railways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NI_Railways).[^cite_note-147]



[Luas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luas) trams at the [Tallaght](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tallaght) terminus

&#x20;Dublin once had an extensive system of [trams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_tramways), but this was largely phased out by 1949. A new [light rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_rail) system, often described as a tram system, the [Luas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luas) was launched in 2004.[^cite_note-148] The Luas is operated by the [KeolisAmey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KeolisAmey), having been operated from commencement to August 2026 by [Transdev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transdev),[^cite_note-149] and carried 55 million passengers in 2025.[^cite_note-150] The network consists of two interconnecting lines; the [Red Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Line_\(Luas\)) links the [Docklands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Docklands) and city centre with the south-western suburbs of [Tallaght](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tallaght) and [Saggart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saggart), while the [Green Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Line_\(Luas\)) connects northern inner city suburbs and the main city centre with suburbs to the south of the city including [Sandyford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandyford) and [Brides Glen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brides_Glen_Luas_stop), mostly along the former route of the [Harcourt Street railway line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harcourt_Street_railway_line). Together, these lines comprise a total of 67 stops and 44.5 kilometres (27.7 mi) of track.[^cite_note-Luas_-_Frequently_Asked_Questions-151] Construction of a 6 km extension to the Green Line, bringing it into the north of the city, commenced in June 2013 and was opened for passenger travel on 9 December 2017.[^cite_note-152]

A [metro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapid_transit) service is proposed under the name of [Metrolink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MetroLink_\(Dublin\)), and planned to run from Dublin's [northside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northside,_Dublin) to [Charlemont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlemont_Luas_stop) via [Dublin Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Airport) and [St. Stephen's Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Stephen's_Green).[^cite_note-153]

### Rail and ferry

[Dublin Connolly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Connolly) is connected by bus to [Dublin Port](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Port) and ferries run by [Irish Ferries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Ferries) and [Stena Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stena_Line) to [Holyhead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holyhead_railway_station) for connecting trains on the [North Wales Coast Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Wales_Coast_Line) to [Chester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chester_railway_station), [Crewe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crewe_railway_station) and [London Euston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euston_railway_station). Dublin Connolly to Dublin Port can be reached via [Amiens Street, Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amiens_Street,_Dublin) into [Store Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Store_Street) or by Luas via [Busáras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bus%C3%A1ras) where [Dublin Bus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Bus) operates route 53 to the Irish Ferries Terminal.[^cite_note-154]

### Air

#### Dublin Airport



[Dublin Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Airport)

[Dublin Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Airport) (owned and operated by [DAA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DAA_\(Irish_company\))) is located north of Dublin city, near [Swords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swords,_County_Dublin) in the administrative county of [Fingal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fingal). The headquarters of Ireland's flag carrier [Aer Lingus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aer_Lingus) and regional airline [CityJet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CityJet) are located there, and those of low-cost carrier [Ryanair](262878.md) are nearby. The airport offers a short and medium-haul network, domestic services to regional airports in Ireland, and long-haul services to the United States, Canada, and the Middle East. Dublin Airport is the 11th busiest in the European Union,[^cite_note-155] and by far the busiest airport on the island of Ireland.

In 2015 and 2016, transatlantic traffic grew, with 158 summer flights a week to North America, making it the sixth-largest European hub for that route over the year.[^cite_note-156] Transatlantic traffic was also the fastest-growing segment of the market for the airport in 2016, in which a 16% increase from 2015 brought the yearly number of passengers travelling between Dublin and North America to 2.9 million.[^cite_note-dub2016record-157]

From 2010 to 2016, Dublin Airport saw an increase of nearly 9.5 million passengers in its annual traffic,[^cite_note-dub2016record-157] as the number of commercial aircraft movements also grew from 163,703 in 2013 to 191,233 in 2015.[^cite_note-158] Passenger traffic has continued to increase during the 2020s. In 2025, 36.4 million passengers passed through the airport, making it the airport's busiest year on record.[^cite_note-159]

#### Other air transport

Dublin is also served by [Weston Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weston_Airport) and other small facilities, by a range of helicopter operators, and the military and some State services use [Casement Aerodrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casement_Aerodrome) nearby.

## Education

Dublin is the largest centre of education in Ireland, and is home to four universities and several other higher education institutions. It was the European Capital of Science in 2012.[^cite_note-160][^cite_note-161]



The Old Library at [Trinity College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College_Dublin)

The [University of Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Dublin) is the oldest university in Ireland, dating from the 16th century, and is located in the city centre. Its sole constituent college, [Trinity College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College_Dublin) (TCD), was established by [Royal Charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Charter) in 1592 under [Elizabeth I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_I_of_England). It was closed to Roman Catholics until 1793, and the [Catholic hierarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_hierarchy) then banned Roman Catholics from attending until 1970.[^cite_note-162] It is situated in the city centre, on College Green, and has over 18,000 students.[^cite_note-163]

The [National University of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_University_of_Ireland) (NUI) has its seat in Dublin, which is also home to the associated *constituent university* of [University College Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_Dublin) (UCD), which has over 30,000 students. Founded in 1854, it is now Ireland's largest university.[^cite_note-164] UCD's main campus is at Belfield, about 5 km (3 mi) from the city centre, in the southeastern suburbs.



[Michael Smurfit Graduate Business School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Smurfit_Graduate_Business_School)

As of 2019, Dublin's principal, and Ireland's largest, institution for technological education and research, [Dublin Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Institute_of_Technology) (DIT), with origins in 1887, has merged with two major suburban third level institutions, [Institute of Technology, Tallaght](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_Technology,_Tallaght) and [Institute of Technology, Blanchardstown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_Technology,_Blanchardstown), to form [Technological University Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_University_Dublin), Ireland's second largest university by student population. The new university offers a wide range of courses across engineering, architecture, the sciences, health, journalism, digital media, hospitality, business, art and design, music, and humanities. It has three long-term campuses, at [Grangegorman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grangegorman), Tallaght, and Blanchardstown.

[Dublin City University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_City_University) (DCU), formerly the [National Institute for Higher Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_for_Higher_Education) (NIHE) Dublin, offers courses in business, engineering, science, communication, languages, and primary education. It has around 16,000 students, and its main campus is located about 7 km (4 mi) from the city centre, in the northern suburbs. Aside from the main Glasnevin Campus, the Drumcondra campuses include the former [St. Patrick's College of Education, Drumcondra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Patrick's_College_of_Education,_Drumcondra) now also hosting students from the nearby [Mater Dei Institute of Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mater_Dei_Institute_of_Education) and students from the [Church of Ireland College of Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Ireland_College_of_Education) at the DCU Campus at [All Hallows College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_Hallows_College).[^cite_note-165]

The [Royal College of Surgeons in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_College_of_Surgeons_in_Ireland) (RCSI) conducts a medical school which is both a university (since 2019) and a recognised college of the NUI, and is situated at [St. Stephen's Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Stephen's_Green) in the city centre; there are also large medical schools within UCD and Trinity College. The [National College of Art and Design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_College_of_Art_and_Design) (NCAD) provides education and research in art, design, and media. The [National College of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_College_of_Ireland) (NCI) is also based in Dublin, as well as the [Economic and Social Research Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_and_Social_Research_Institute), a social science research institute, on [Sir John Rogerson's Quay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_John_Rogerson's_Quay), and the [Dublin Institute for Advanced Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Institute_for_Advanced_Studies).

The [Institute of International and European Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_International_and_European_Affairs) is also in Dublin. [Dublin Business School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Business_School) (DBS) is Ireland's largest private third-level institution, with over 9,000 students located on Aungier Street, and [Griffith College Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Griffith_College_Dublin) has its central facility in Portobello. There are also smaller specialised colleges, including [The Gaiety School of Acting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gaiety_School_of_Acting). The Irish Public Administration and Management Training Centre is based in Dublin. The Institute of Public Administration provides a range of undergraduate and postgraduate awards through the National University of Ireland and, in some instances, Queen's University Belfast.

Dublin is also home to the [Royal Irish Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Irish_Academy), whose membership is considered Ireland's highest academic honour.[^cite_note-166][^cite_note-167]

The suburban town of Dún Laoghaire is home to the [Dún Laoghaire Institute of Art, Design and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%BAn_Laoghaire_Institute_of_Art,_Design_and_Technology) (IADT), which supports training and research in art, design, business, psychology, and media technology. Dublin joined the UNESCO Global Network of Learning Cities in 2019.[^cite_note-UNESCO_UIL-GNLC-168]

The City of Dublin is the area administered by [Dublin City Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_City_Council). The traditional [County Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Dublin) includes the city and the administrative counties of [Dún Laoghaire–Rathdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%BAn_Laoghaire%E2%80%93Rathdown), [Fingal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fingal), and [South Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Dublin). In the [2022 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_census_of_Ireland), the population of the City of Dublin was 592,713.[^cite_note-2022mapdcc-7] The population of Dublin city and suburbs was 1,263,219.[^cite_note-2022mapdcands-17] County Dublin had a population of 1,458,154.[^cite_note-2022mapcodub-185] The population of the Greater Dublin Area was 2,082,605.[^cite_note-\:1-186][^cite_note-187]

Of the population of Dublin city and its suburbs, 62.9% (794,925) were born in Dublin, 26.6% (336,021) were born outside of Ireland, while the remaining 10.5% (132,273) were born in a county other than Dublin.[^cite_note-188]

After [World War II](32927.md), Italians were by far the largest immigrant group in both Dublin and Ireland, and became synonymous with the catering and restaurant landscape.[^cite_note-rteArchives1972-189][^cite_note-190] Since the late 1990s, Dublin has had a significant level of net immigration, with the greatest numbers coming from the European Union, especially the United Kingdom, Poland and Lithuania.[^cite_note-191] There is also immigration from outside Europe, including from Pakistan, Brazil, the Philippines, China, India and Nigeria. Dublin is home to a greater proportion of newer arrivals than any other part of Ireland. Sixty percent of Ireland's Asian population lives in Dublin.[^cite_note-192]

Dublin attracts the largest proportion of non-Catholic migrants from other countries. Increased secularisation in Ireland has prompted a drop in regular Catholic church attendance in Dublin from over 90 percent in the mid-1970s, down to 14 percent in a 2011 survey, and less than 2% in some areas[^cite_note-193][^cite_note-194] In the [2016 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_census_of_Ireland), 68.2% of Dublin's population identified as Catholic, 12.7% as other stated religions, with 19.1% having no religion or no religion stated.[^cite_note-195]

In the 2022 census, the population of [County Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Dublin) self-identified as 80.4% white (68.0% white Irish, 12.0% other white, and 0.4% Irish traveller), 5.8% Asian, 3.0% mixed backgrounds, 2.2% [Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_people_in_Ireland), and 8.5% not stated.[^cite_note-196] In the same census, the ethnic makeup of Dublin city was 76.81% white (including 64.23% white Irish and 12.19% other white people), 12.98% not stated, 5.11% Asian, 3.50% other and 1.58% black.[^cite_note-197]

As of December 2024, 1,467 families were living in emergency accommodation in the Dublin region. This is a decrease of 57 from the November 2024 number and an increase of 67 compared with December 2023, when there were 1,400 families in emergency accommodation.[^cite_note-198]

## Culture



The [National Museum of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Ireland)

### The arts

See also: [Irish literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_literature), [Irish theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_theatre), and [Irish art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_art)

Dublin has a significant literary history and produced many literary figures, including [Nobel laureates](23385442.md) [William Butler Yeats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Butler_Yeats), [George Bernard Shaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Bernard_Shaw), and [Samuel Beckett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Beckett). Other influential writers and playwrights include [Oscar Wilde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Wilde), [Jonathan Swift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Swift) and the creator of [Dracula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dracula), [Bram Stoker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bram_Stoker). It is also the location of key and notable works of [James Joyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joyce), including *[Ulysses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulysses_\(novel\))*, which is set in Dublin and includes much topical detail. *[Dubliners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dubliners)* is a collection of short stories by Joyce about incidents and typical characters of the city during the early 20th century. Other notable writers include [J. M. Synge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._M._Synge), [Seán O'Casey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Se%C3%A1n_O'Casey), [Brendan Behan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brendan_Behan), [Maeve Binchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maeve_Binchy), [John Banville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Banville) and [Roddy Doyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roddy_Doyle). Ireland's biggest libraries and literary museums are found in Dublin, including the [National Print Museum of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Print_Museum_of_Ireland) and [National Library of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Library_of_Ireland). In July 2010, Dublin was named as a UNESCO [City of Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Literature), joining [Edinburgh](9602.md), [Melbourne](17306237.md) and [Iowa City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iowa_City) with the permanent title.[^cite_note-199]



The [Book of Kells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Kells)

[Handel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handel)'s oratorio [Messiah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messiah_\(Handel\)) was first performed at [Neal's Music Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neale's_Musick_Hall,_Dublin), in [Fishamble Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fishamble_Street), on 13 April [1742](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1742_in_Ireland).

There are several theatres within the city centre. Various well-known actors have emerged from the Dublin theatrical scene, including [Noel Purcell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noel_Purcell_\(actor\)), [Michael Gambon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Gambon), [Brendan Gleeson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brendan_Gleeson), [Stephen Rea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Rea), [Colin Farrell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_Farrell), [Colm Meaney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colm_Meaney), and [Gabriel Byrne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel_Byrne). The best known theatres include the [Gaiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaiety_Theatre,_Dublin), [Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbey_Theatre), [Olympia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympia_Theatre,_Dublin), [Gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gate_Theatre), and [Grand Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canal_Theatre). The Gaiety specialises in musical and operatic productions, and also opens its doors after the evening theatre production to host a variety of live music, dancing, and films. The Abbey was founded in 1904 by a group that included [Yeats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Butler_Yeats) to promote indigenous literary talent. It went on to provide a breakthrough for some of the city's most famous writers, such as [Synge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._M._Synge), Yeats himself, and George Bernard Shaw. The Gate was founded in 1928 to promote European and American avant-garde works. The Grand Canal Theatre is a newer 2,111-capacity theatre that opened in 2010 in the [Grand Canal Dock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canal_Dock) area.

Apart from being the focus of the country's literature and theatre, Dublin is the focal point for much of [Irish art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_art) and the Irish artistic scene. The [Book of Kells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Kells), a world-famous manuscript produced by Celtic monks in AD 800 and an example of [Insular art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insular_art), is on display in [Trinity College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College_Dublin). The [Chester Beatty Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chester_Beatty_Library) houses a collection of manuscripts, miniature paintings, prints, drawings, rare books, and [decorative arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decorative_art) assembled by American mining millionaire (and honorary Irish citizen) [Sir Alfred Chester Beatty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Alfred_Chester_Beatty) (1875–1968). The collections date from 2700 BCE onwards and are drawn from Asia, the Middle East, North Africa, and Europe.



[Irish Museum of Modern Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Museum_of_Modern_Art)

Public [art galleries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_gallery) are found across the city and are free to visit, including the [Irish Museum of Modern Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Museum_of_Modern_Art), the [National Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Gallery_of_Ireland), the [Hugh Lane Municipal Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Lane_Municipal_Gallery), [the Douglas Hyde Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Douglas_Hyde_Gallery), the [Project Arts Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Arts_Centre) and the exhibition space of the [Royal Hibernian Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Hibernian_Academy). Private galleries in Dublin include Green on Red Gallery, [Kerlin Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerlin_Gallery), Kevin Kavanagh Gallery and Mother's Tankstation.[^cite_note-200]

Three branches of the [National Museum of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Ireland) are located in Dublin: [Archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Ireland_%E2%80%93_Archaeology) in [Kildare Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kildare_Street), [Decorative Arts and History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Ireland_%E2%80%93_Decorative_Arts_and_History) in [Collins Barracks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collins_Barracks) and [Natural History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_History_Museum_\(Ireland\)) in [Merrion Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merrion_Street).[^cite_note-201] Dublin is home to the [National College of Art and Design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_College_of_Art_and_Design), which dates from 1746, and [Dublin Institute of Design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Institute_of_Design), founded in 1991. [Dublinia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublinia) is a [living history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Living_history) attraction showcasing the city's Viking and Medieval history.

Dublin has long had an 'underground' arts scene, with Temple Bar hosting artists in the 1980s and spaces such as the Project Arts Centre serving as hubs for collectives and new exhibitions. *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)* noted that Dublin's independent and underground arts flourished during the economic recession of c. 2010.[^cite_note-202] Dublin also has many dramatic, musical and operatic companies, including Festival Productions, Lyric Opera Productions, the Pioneers' Musical & Dramatic Society, [Rathmines and Rathgar Musical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rathmines_and_Rathgar_Musical_Society), the Glasnevin Musical Society, Third Day Chorale, Second Age Theatre Company, and [Irish National Opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_National_Opera).

Dublin was shortlisted to be [World Design Capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Design_Capital) 2014.[^cite_note-203] [Taoiseach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoiseach) [Enda Kenny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enda_Kenny) was quoted to say that Dublin "would be an ideal candidate to host the World Design Capital in 2014".[^cite_note-204]

In October 2021, Dublin was shortlisted for the [European Commission](9974.md)'s 2022 European Capital of Smart Tourism award along with [Bordeaux](4097.md), [Copenhagen](5166.md), [Florence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence), [Ljubljana](42374.md), [Palma de Mallorca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palma_de_Mallorca) and [Valencia](63861.md).[^cite_note-205]

### Entertainment



[Temple Bar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_Bar,_Dublin)

Dublin has an active nightlife and is reputedly one of Europe's most youthful cities; in 2009 it was estimated that 50% of its citizens were younger than 25.[^cite_note-axdqer-206][^cite_note-207] There are many pubs in the area around [St. Stephen's Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Stephen's_Green) and [Grafton Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grafton_Street), including [Harcourt Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harcourt_Street), [Camden Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camden_Street), [Wexford Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wexford_Street) and [Leeson Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leeson_Street), the location of many nightclubs and pubs.

A well known area for nightlife is [Temple Bar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_Bar,_Dublin), south of the River Liffey. The area has become popular among tourists, including [stag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_party) and [hen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelorette_party) parties from the UK.[^cite_note-208] It was developed as Dublin's cultural quarter and does retain this spirit as a centre for small arts productions, photographic and artists' studios, and in the form of street performers and small music venues; however, it has been criticised as being overpriced and dirty by Lonely Planet.[^cite_note-209]

### Sport

#### GAA



[Croke Park](322713.md)

[Croke Park](322713.md) is the largest sports stadium in Ireland. The headquarters of the [Gaelic Athletic Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_Athletic_Association) has a capacity of 82,300. It is the third-largest stadium in Europe after [Nou Camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nou_Camp) in Barcelona and [Wembley Stadium](8913012.md) in London.[^cite_note-217] It hosts the premier [Gaelic football](37688.md) and [hurling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurling) games, [international rules football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_rules_football) and irregularly other sporting and non-sporting events including concerts. Muhammad Ali fought there in 1972, and it hosted the opening and closing ceremonies of the 2003 [Special Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Olympics). It also has conference and banqueting facilities. There is a GAA Museum there, and tours of the stadium are offered, including a rooftop walk. During the redevelopment of [Lansdowne Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lansdowne_Road), Croke Park played host to the [Irish Rugby Union Team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireland_national_rugby_union_team) and [Republic of Ireland national football team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Ireland_national_football_team) as well as hosting the [Heineken Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heineken_Cup) rugby [2008–09 semi-final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008%E2%80%9309_Heineken_Cup#Semi-finals) between Munster and Leinster, which set a world record attendance for a club rugby match.[^cite_note-218] The [Dublin GAA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_GAA) team plays most of their home league hurling games at [Parnell Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parnell_Park).

#### Rugby Union



[Aviva Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviva_Stadium)

[IRFU Stadium Lansdowne Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lansdowne_Road) was laid out in 1874. This was the venue for home games of both the Irish Rugby Union Team and the Republic of Ireland national football team. A joint venture between the [Irish Rugby Football Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Rugby_Football_Union), the [FAI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Football_Association_of_Ireland) and the Government saw it redeveloped into a new state-of-the-art 50,000-seat [Aviva Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviva_Stadium), which opened in May 2010.[^cite_note-219] Lansdowne Road/Aviva Stadium hosted the [Heineken Cup final](40682953.md) in 1999, 2003, 2013, and 2023. [Rugby union](25405.md) team [Leinster Rugby](985961.md) play their competitive home games in the [RDS Arena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RDS_Arena) and the Aviva Stadium. At the same time, [Donnybrook Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donnybrook_Stadium) hosts its friendlies and A games, Ireland A and Women, Leinster Schools and Youths, and the home club games of [All Ireland League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AIB_League) clubs [Old Wesley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Wesley) and [Bective Rangers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bective_Rangers). [County Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Dublin) is home to 13 of the senior rugby union clubs in Ireland, including 5 of the 10 sides in the top division 1A.[^cite_note-220]

#### Association football

Dublin is home to five [League of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Ireland) association football clubs: [Bohemian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohemian_F.C.), [Shamrock Rovers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shamrock_Rovers_F.C.), [Shelbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shelbourne_F.C.), [St Patrick's Athletic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Patrick's_Athletic_F.C.) and [University College Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_Dublin_A.F.C.). The first Irish side to reach the group stages of a European competition ([2011–12 UEFA Europa League group stage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011%E2%80%9312_UEFA_Europa_League_group_stage)) is Shamrock Rovers, who play at [Tallaght Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tallaght_Stadium) in [South Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Dublin). Bohemian F.C play at [Dalymount Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalymount_Park), the oldest football stadium in the country, and home ground for the [Ireland football team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Ireland_national_football_team) from 1904 to the 1970s. St Patrick's Athletic play at [Richmond Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richmond_Park_\(football_ground\)); University College Dublin at the [UCD Bowl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UCD_Bowl) in [Dún Laoghaire–Rathdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%BAn_Laoghaire%E2%80%93Rathdown); and Shelbourne at [Tolka Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tolka_Park). Tolka Park, Dalymount Park, UCD Bowl and Tallaght Stadium, along with the [Carlisle Grounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlisle_Grounds) in [Bray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bray,_County_Wicklow), hosted all Group 3 games in the intermediary round of the [2011 UEFA Regions' Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_UEFA_Regions'_Cup). The [Aviva Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviva_Stadium) hosted the [2011 UEFA Europa League final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_UEFA_Europa_League_final) and the [2024 UEFA Europa League final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_UEFA_Europa_League_final).[^cite_note-221]

#### Cricket

Dublin has two [ODI cricket grounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_One_Day_International_cricket_grounds) in [Castle Avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castle_Avenue,_Dublin) (Clontarf Cricket Club) and [Malahide Cricket Club Ground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malahide_Cricket_Club_Ground). [College Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_Park,_Dublin) has Test status and hosted Ireland's first Test cricket match, a women's match against Pakistan in 2000.[^cite_note-222] The men's Irish cricket team also played their first [Test match](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Test_cricket) against [Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan_national_cricket_team) at Malahide Cricket Club Ground during 2018.[^cite_note-223] [Leinster Lightning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leinster_Lightning) play their home [inter-provincial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cricket_in_Ireland#Inter-Provincial_Series) matches in Dublin at College Park.[^cite_note-224]

### Cuisine

See also: [Irish cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_cuisine)

As of the 2024 [Michelin Guide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelin_Guide), seven Dublin restaurants shared ten [Michelin stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelin_star) – including [Restaurant Patrick Guilbaud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restaurant_Patrick_Guilbaud), [Liath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liath_\(restaurant\)) and [Chapter One](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapter_One_\(restaurant\)) with two.[^cite_note-229] Irish-born [Kevin Thornton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevin_Thornton_\(chef\)) was awarded two Michelin stars in 2001 – though his restaurant, [Thornton's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thornton's_Restaurant), closed in 2016.[^cite_note-230] The [Dublin Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Institute_of_Technology) commenced a bachelor's degree in culinary skills in 1999.[^cite_note-231]

Historically, Irish coffee houses and cafes were associated with media professionals.[^cite_note-MacConIomaire-232] Since the beginning of the 21st century, with the growth of apartment living in the city, Dublin's cafés attracted younger patrons looking for an informal gathering place and an ad hoc office.[^cite_note-MacConIomaire-232] Cafés became more popular in the city, and Irish-owned coffee chains like Java Republic, Insomnia, and O'Brien's Sandwich Bars now compete internationally. In 2008, Irish barista Stephen Morrissey won the title of World Barista Champion.[^cite_note-233]

Immigrant groups, such as [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overseas_Chinese), [Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_diaspora) and [Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_diaspora) émigrés, have also opened restaurants around Dublin.[^cite_note-rteArchives1972-189][^cite_note-234] A number of South-East Asians immigrated from places such as [Hong Kong](13404.md), [Malaysia](3607937.md) and [Mainland China](55079.md) to Dublin during the 1960s and opened restaurants featuring their cuisines.[^cite_note-235] Modern Irish adaptations of Chinese cuisine include the [Spice bag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spice_bag), a [takeaway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takeaway_food) dish consisting mainly of chicken, chips, and vegetables.[^cite_note-236][^cite_note-237] In 2020, it was voted 'Ireland's Favourite Takeaway Dish' in the [Just Eat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Just_Eat) National Takeaway Awards.[^cite_note-238]

## English and Irish languages

Further information: [Hiberno-English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiberno-English) and [Irish language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_language)

Dublin was traditionally a city of two languages, English and [Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_language), a situation found also in the area around it, [the Pale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pale_\(Ireland\)). The Irish of County Dublin represented the easternmost extension of a broad central dialect area that stretched between Leinster and [Connacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connacht), but it also had its own local characteristics. It may also have been influenced by the east [Ulster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster) dialect of [County Meath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Meath) and [County Louth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Louth) to the north.[^cite_note-239]

In the words of a 16th-century English administrator, [William Gerard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Gerard) (1518–1581): "All Englishe, and the most part with delight, even in Dublin, speak Irishe".[^cite_note-240] The [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normans_in_Ireland) historian [Richard Stanihurst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Stanihurst) (1547–1618) wrote as follows: "When their posteritie became not altogither so warie in keeping, as their ancestors were valiant in conquering, the Irish language was free dennized in the English Pale: this canker tooke such deep root, as the bodie that before was whole and sound, was by little and little festered, and in manner wholly putrified".[^cite_note-241]

English authorities during the Cromwellian period recognized that Irish was widely spoken in the city and its surroundings. In 1655, several local dignitaries were ordered to oversee a lecture in Irish to be given in Dublin. In March 1656, a converted Catholic priest, Séamas Corcy, was appointed to preach in Irish at Bride's parish every Sunday, and was also ordered to preach at [Drogheda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drogheda) and [Athy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athy).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBerresford_Ellis1975156-242] In 1657, the English colonists in Dublin presented a petition to the Municipal Council complaining that in Dublin itself "there is Irish commonly and usually spoken".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBerresford_Ellis1975193-243]

In early 18th-century Dublin, Irish was the language of a group of poets and scribes led by Seán and Tadhg Ó Neachtain.[^cite_note-244] Scribal activity in Irish persisted in Dublin right through the 18th century.[^cite_note-245] There were still native Irish speakers in County Dublin at the time of the 1851 census.[^cite_note-246]

Though the number of Irish speakers declined throughout Ireland in the 19th century, the end of the century saw a [Gaelic revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_revival), centred in Dublin and accompanied by renewed literary activity.[^cite_note-247] This was the harbinger of a steady renewal of urban Irish, though with new characteristics of its own.[^cite_note-schism-248][^cite_note-249]

### Current era

The native language of most Dubliners today is English, and several local dialects are subsumed under the label [Dublin English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_English). Dublin also has many thousands of habitual Irish speakers, with the [2016 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_census_of_Ireland) showing that daily speakers (outside the education system) numbered 14,903. They form part of an urban Irish-speaking cohort that is generally better-educated than monoglot English speakers.[^cite_note-250]

Several Irish-medium schools support the Dublin Irish-speaking cohort. There are 12,950 students in the Dublin region attending 34 [gaelscoileanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelscoil) (Irish-language primary schools) and 10 [gaelcholáistí](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelchol%C3%A1iste) (Irish-language secondary schools).[^cite_note-251]

Two Irish language radio stations, [Raidió Na Life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raidi%C3%B3_Na_Life) and [RTÉ Raidió na Gaeltachta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT%C3%89_Raidi%C3%B3_na_Gaeltachta), have studios in the city, and the online station [Raidió Rí-Rá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raidi%C3%B3_R%C3%AD-R%C3%A1) broadcasts from studios in the city. Several Irish language agencies are also located in the capital. *[Conradh na Gaeilge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conradh_na_Gaeilge)* offers language classes and serves as a meeting place for various groups.[^cite_note-252] The closest Gaeltacht to Dublin is the [County Meath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Meath) [Gaeltacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaeltacht) of [Ráth Cairn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%A1th_Cairn) and [Baile Ghib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baile_Ghib) which is 55 km (34 mi) away.

## International relations

Dublin City Council has an International Relations Unit, established in 2007. It works on hosting international delegations, staff exchanges, international promotion of the city, twinning and partnerships, working with multi-city organisations such as [Eurocities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurocities), economic partnerships, and advising other Council units.[^cite_note-DCC_IntlRels-253]
