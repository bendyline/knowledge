# Fertilizer

"Plant food" redirects here. For the drug term, see [Bath salts (drug)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bath_salts_\(drug\)). For other uses, see [Plant food (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_food_\(disambiguation\)).



A [farmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farmer) spreading [manure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manure) to improve [soil fertility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_fertility)



Fertilizer use in kg per hectare of arable land

A **fertilizer** or **fertiliser** is any material of natural or synthetic origin that is applied to [soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil) or to [plant tissues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_tissue) to supply [plant nutrients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_nutrition). Fertilizers may be distinct from [liming materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liming_\(soil\)) or other non-nutrient [soil amendments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_amendments). Many sources of fertilizer exist, both natural and [industrially](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agrochemical) produced.[^cite_note-Scherer2009-1] For most modern [agricultural practices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_practice), fertilization focuses on three main [macronutrients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macronutrient): [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen) (N), [phosphorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphorus) (P), and [potassium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium) (K) with occasional addition of supplements like [rock flour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_flour) for [micronutrients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micronutrient). Farmers apply these fertilizers in a variety of ways: through dry or [pelletized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelletized) or liquid application processes, using large [agricultural equipment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_equipment), or [hand-tool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hand_tool) methods.

Historically, fertilization came from natural or organic sources: [compost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compost), [animal manure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manure), [human manure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_waste), harvested [minerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineral), [crop rotations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop_rotation), and byproducts of human-nature industries (e.g. [fish processing waste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish_meal), or [bloodmeal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_meal) from [animal slaughter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_slaughter)).[^cite_note-2] However, starting in the 19th century, after innovations in [plant nutrition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_nutrition) following [Justus von Liebig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justus_von_Liebig)'s discoveries, an [agricultural industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_agriculture) developed around synthetically created [agrochemical fertilizers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agrochemical).[^cite_note-3] This transition was important in transforming the [global food system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_system) towards larger-scale [industrial agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intensive_farming) with large crop yields in [monocultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoculture).[^cite_note-4]



A farmer broadcasting solid fertilizer into his field in [Janakkala, Finland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janakkala) in 1960

The invention of [Haber process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haber_process) for producing [ammonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonia) for nitrogen in the 20th century combined with amplified [chemical production capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Productive_capacity) created during [World War II](32927.md) led to a boom in using [nitrogen fertilizers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_fertilizer).[^cite_note-5] In the latter half of the 20th century, increased use of nitrogen fertilizers (800% increase between 1961 and 2019) has been a crucial component of the increased productivity of [conventional food systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conventional_food_systems) as part of the so-called "[Green Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Revolution)".[^cite_note-Mbow2019-6]

Fertilizers, especially when applied excessively, can have environmental consequences such as [water pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_pollution)[^cite_note-7] and [eutrophication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eutrophication)[^cite_note-8] due to nutrient [runoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runoff_\(hydrology\)).[^cite_note-9] Additionally, the chemical process for creating the fertilizers results in byproducts, including [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_emissions) and other emissions.[^cite_note-10] In some cases [contamination and pollution of soil](4229946.md) result by accumulation of [heavy metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_metals) contained in some fertilizers collected through mining.[^cite_note-11] [Sustainable agricultural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_agriculture) practices, such as [reduced tillage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduced_tillage) and planting [buffer strips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buffer_strip), can minimize these adverse environmental effects.[^cite_note-12]

Management of [soil fertility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_fertility) has preoccupied farmers since the beginning of agriculture. [Middle Eastern](19323.md),[^cite_note-16] [Chinese](5405.md),[^cite_note-17] [Mesoamerican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerica),[^cite_note-18] and [cultures of the Central Andes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andean_civilizations)[^cite_note-19] were all early adopters of agriculture. This is thought to have led to their cultures growing faster in population which allowed an exportation of culture to neighboring hunter-gatherer groups.[^cite_note-20] Fertilizer use along with agriculture allowed some of these early societies a critical advantage over their neighbors, leading them to become dominant cultures in their respective regions.[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-22] [Egyptians](31912046.md), [Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_people), [Babylonians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonia), and early [Germans](152735.md) are all recorded as using [minerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineral) or [manure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manure) to enhance the [productivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Productivity) of their farms.[^cite_note-Scherer2009-1] The scientific research of [plant nutrition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_nutrition) started well before the work of German chemist [Justus von Liebig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justus_von_Liebig) although his name is most mentioned as the "father of the fertilizer industry".[^cite_note-23] [Nicolas Théodore de Saussure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Th%C3%A9odore_de_Saussure) and scientific colleagues at the time were quick to disprove the simplifications of von Liebig.[^cite_note-24] Prominent scientists whom von Liebig drew were [Carl Ludwig Sprenger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Ludwig_Sprenger) and [Hermann Hellriegel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Hellriegel).[^cite_note-25] In this field, a 'knowledge erosion' took place,[^cite_note-26] partly driven by an intermingling of economics and research.[^cite_note-27] [John Bennet Lawes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Bennet_Lawes), an English [entrepreneur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entrepreneur), began experimenting on the effects of various [manures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manure) on plants growing in pots in 1837, and a year or two later the experiments were extended to crops in the field. One immediate consequence was that in 1842 he patented a manure formed by treating [phosphates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphate) with [sulfuric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfuric_acid), and thus was the first to create the artificial manure industry. In the succeeding year, he enlisted the services of [Joseph Henry Gilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Henry_Gilbert); together they performed crop experiments at the [Institute of Arable Crops Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rothamsted_Research).[^cite_note-28]

The [Birkeland–Eyde process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birkeland%E2%80%93Eyde_process) was one of the competing industrial processes at the beginning of nitrogen-based fertilizer production.[^cite_note-29] This process was used to fix atmospheric [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen) (N<sub>2</sub>) into [nitric acid](21655.md) (HNO<sub>3</sub>), one of several chemical processes called [nitrogen fixation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_fixation). The resultant nitric acid was then used as a source of [nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrate) (NO<sub>3</sub><sup>−</sup>). A factory based on the process was built in [Rjukan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rjukan) and [Notodden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notodden_\(town\)) in Norway and large [hydroelectric power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroelectric_power) facilities were built.[^cite_note-30]

The 1910s and 1920s witnessed the rise of the [Haber process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haber_process) and the [Ostwald process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ostwald_process). The Haber process produces [ammonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonia) (NH<sub>3</sub>) from [methane](18582230.md) (CH<sub>4</sub>) ([natural gas](22131.md)) and [molecular nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_nitrogen) (N<sub>2</sub>) from the air. The ammonia from the Haber process is then partially converted into [nitric acid](21655.md) (HNO<sub>3</sub>) in the [Ostwald process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ostwald_process).[^cite_note-31] It is estimated that a third of annual global food production uses ammonia from the [Haber–Bosch process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haber%E2%80%93Bosch_process) and that this supports nearly half the world's population.[^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33] After [World War II](32927.md), nitrogen production plants that had ramped up for wartime bomb manufacturing were pivoted towards agricultural uses.[^cite_note-34] The use of synthetic nitrogen fertilizers has increased steadily over the last 50 years of the 20th century, rising almost 20-fold to a rate of 100 million [tonnes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonnes) of nitrogen per year in 2003.[^cite_note-35]

The development of synthetic nitrogen fertilizers has significantly supported global [population growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_growth). It has been estimated that almost half the people on the Earth are currently fed due to synthetic nitrogen fertilizer use.[^cite_note-36] The use of phosphate fertilizers has also increased from 9 million tonnes per year in 1960 to 40 million tonnes per year in 2000, but future phosphorus fertilizer availability is now a critical issue.[^cite_note-Blackwell2019-37]

Agricultural use of inorganic fertilizers in 2021 was 195 million tonnes of nutrients, of which 56% was nitrogen.[^cite_note-FAO2023-38] Asia represented 53% of the world's total agricultural use of inorganic fertilizers in 2021, followed by the Americas (29%), Europe (12%), Africa (4%) and Oceania (2%). This ranking of the regions is the same for all nutrients. The main users of inorganic fertilizers are, in descending order, China, India, Brazil, and the United States of America (see Table 15), with China the largest user of each nutrient.[^cite_note-FAO2023-38] Agricultural use of inorganic fertilizers in 2023 was 190 million tonnes of nutrients, of which 112 million tonnes (58%) was nitrogen.[^cite_note-39]

A [maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize) crop yielding 6–9 tonnes of grain per [hectare](24919513.md) (2.5 acres) requires 31–50 kilograms (68–110 lb) of [phosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphate) fertilizer to be applied; [soybean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soybean) crops require about half, 20–25 kg per hectare.[^cite_note-40] [Yara International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yara_International) is the world's largest producer of nitrogen-based fertilizers.[^cite_note-41]

## Mechanism

Fertilizers enhance the growth of plants. This goal is met in two ways, the traditional one being additives that provide [nutrients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutrient). The second mode by which some fertilizers act is to enhance the effectiveness of the soil by modifying its water-holding capacity and aeration.[^cite_note-42] This article, like many on fertilizers, emphasizes the nutritional aspect.

Fertilizers typically provide, in varying [proportions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proportionality_\(mathematics\)):[^cite_note-Dittmar2009-43]

- [Three main macronutrients (NPK)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labeling_of_fertilizer):&#x20;

  - [Nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen) (N): leaf growth and stems;

    [^cite_note-44]

  - [Phosphorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphorus) (P): development of roots, flowers, seeds and fruit;

    [^cite_note-45]

  - [Potassium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium) (K): strong stem growth, movement of water in plants, promotion of [flowering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flowering) and [fruiting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fruiting);

    [^cite_note-46]

- Micronutrients: [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper) (Cu), [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_fertilisation) (Fe), [manganese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manganese) (Mn), [molybdenum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molybdenum) (Mo), [zinc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinc) (Zn), and [boron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boron) (B).

  [^cite_note-47]

  &#x20;Of occasional significance are [silicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon) (Si), [cobalt](24580536.md) (Co), and [vanadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanadium) (V).

  [^cite_note-48]

Although calcium uptake by plant roots has long-time been considered as luxury consumption,[^cite_note-49] it is now considered as an essential element for its various roles in the maintenance of the integrity of plant [cell walls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_wall) and [cell membranes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_membrane).[^cite_note-50] [Liming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liming_\(soil\)) has also a decisive and positive influence on [crop yields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop_yield) by couteracting [soil acidification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_acidification),[^cite_note-soil_acidification_and-51] a side-effect of [plant growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_growth)[^cite_note-52] and nutrient export by crops,[^cite_note-53] improving [soil structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_structure), and thus [soil aeration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_aeration),[^cite_note-54] and increasing soil biological activity, thereby [soil fertility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_fertility), in particular through increased [nitrification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrification).[^cite_note-55]

The [nutrients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutrient) required for healthy plant life are classified according to the [elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_element), but the elements are not used as fertilizers. Instead, [compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_compound) containing these elements are the basis of fertilizers. Macro-nutrients are present in plant tissue in quantities from 0.15% to 6.0% on a [dry matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dry_matter) (DM) (0% moisture) basis but they are sometimes consumed in larger quantities than required (luxury consumption), in particular when fertilizers are used in excess of plant requirements ([overfertilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Over_fertilization)).[^cite_note-56] Plants are made up of four main elements: [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen), [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen), [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon), and [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen). Carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen are widely available respectively in [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) and in [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water). Although nitrogen makes up most of the [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere), it is in a form that is unavailable to plants. Nitrogen is the most important fertilizer since nitrogen is present in [proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein) ([amide bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amide_bond) between [amino acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amino-acid)), [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA) ([puric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purine) and [pyrimidic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrimidine) bases), and other components (e.g., [tetrapyrrolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porphyrin) [heme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heme) in [chlorophyll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorophyll)). It is common tenet that nitrogen is absent from the [parent rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parent_rock) and thus cannot be obtained from [mineral weathering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineral_weathering), although recent studies point to the equal importance of the atmosphere and bedrock as nitrogen sources.[^cite_note-57] Nitrogen is made available to plants through [weathering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weathering) of nitrogen-containing [parent rocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parent_rock)[^cite_note-58] and [nitrogen fixation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_fixation). Only some free-living (e.g. *[Clostridium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clostridium)*) and [symbiotic bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbiotic_bacteria) living in root systems of [host plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Host_plant) (notably [legumes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legume)) can fix atmospheric nitrogen (N<sub>2</sub>) by converting it to [ammonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonia) (NH<sub>3</sub>) and [amino acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amino_acid) which are available to plants.[^cite_note-59][^cite_note-60] [Phosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphate) (PO3−4) is required for the production of [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deoxyribonucleic_acid) ([genetic code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_code)) and [ATP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate), the main energy carrier in [cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_\(biology\)), as well as certain [lipids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipid) ([phospholipids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phospholipid), the main components of the [lipidic double layer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liposome) of the [cell membranes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_membrane)).[^cite_note-61]

### Microbiological considerations

Two sets of [enzymatic reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzymatic_reaction) are highly relevant to the efficiency of nitrogen-based fertilizers.&#x20;

Urease

The first is the [hydrolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrolysis) (reaction with water) of [urea](31734.md) (CO(NH<sub>2</sub>)<sub>2</sub>). Many [soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil) [bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteria) possess the enzyme [urease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urease), which [catalyzes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalysis) the conversion of urea to [ammonium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonium) ion (NH+4) and [bicarbonate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicarbonate) [ion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion) (HCO−3).[^cite_note-62]

Ammonia oxidation

[Ammonia-oxidizing bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonia-oxidizing_bacteria) (AOB), such as species of *[Nitrosomonas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrosomonas)*, [oxidize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox) ammonia (NH<sub>3</sub>) to [nitrite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrite) (NO−2), a process termed [nitrification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrification).[^cite_note-63] [Nitrite-oxidizing bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrite-oxidizing_bacteria), especially *[Nitrobacter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrobacter)*, oxidize [nitrite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrite) (NO−2) to [nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrate) (NO−3), which is extremely [soluble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solubility) and mobile, being [leached](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaching_\(agriculture\)) easily to [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater) then to rivers then to the sea, and is a major cause of [eutrophication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eutrophication)[^cite_note-64] and [algal blooms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algal_bloom) in rivers, lakes and sea shores.[^cite_note-65]

## Classification

Fertilizers are classified in several ways. They are classified according to whether they provide a single nutrient (e.g., K, P, or N), in which case they are classified as *straight fertilizers*. *Multinutrient fertilizers* (or *complex fertilizers*) provide two or more nutrients, for example, N and P combined. Fertilizers are also sometimes classified as inorganic (the topic of most of this article) versus organic. Inorganic fertilizers exclude carbon-containing materials except [urea](31734.md). Organic fertilizers are usually (recycled) plant- or animal-derived matter (e.g. [compost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compost), [manure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manure), respectively). Inorganic are sometimes called synthetic fertilizers since various chemical treatments are required for their manufacture.[^cite_note-66]

NPK fertilizers are three-component fertilizers providing nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium. There exist two types of NPK fertilizers: compound and blends. Compound NPK fertilizers contain chemically bound ingredients, while blended NPK fertilizers are physical mixtures of single nutrient components.

[NPK rating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NPK_rating) is a rating system describing the amount of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium in a fertilizer. NPK ratings consist of three numbers separated by dashes (e.g., 10-10-10 or 16–4–8) describing the chemical content of fertilizers.[^cite_note-68] The first number represents the percentage of nitrogen in the product; the second number, P<sub>2</sub>O<sub>5</sub>; the third, K<sub>2</sub>O. Fertilizers do not actually contain P<sub>2</sub>O<sub>5</sub> or K<sub>2</sub>O, but the system is a conventional shorthand for the amount of phosphorus (P) or potassium (K) in a fertilizer. A 50-pound (23 kg) bag of fertilizer labeled 16-4-8 contains 8 lb (3.6 kg) of nitrogen (16% of the 50 pounds), an amount of phosphorus equivalent to that in 2 pounds of P<sub>2</sub>O<sub>5</sub> (4% of 50 pounds), and 4 pounds of K<sub>2</sub>O (8% of 50 pounds). Most fertilizers are labeled according to this N-P-K convention, although Australian convention, following an N-P-K-S system, adds a fourth number for sulfur, and uses elemental values for all values including P and K.[^cite_note-69]

### Micronutrients

[Micronutrients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micronutrients) are consumed in smaller quantities and are present in plant tissue on the order of [parts-per-million](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parts-per_notation) (ppm), ranging from 0.15 to 400 ppm or less than 0.04% dry matter.[^cite_note-70][^cite_note-71] These elements are often required as [co-enzymes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Co-enzymes) for [enzymes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzyme) essential to the plant's [metabolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolism). Because these elements enable [catalysts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalysis) (enzymes), their impact far exceeds their [concentration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentration). Typical micronutrients are [boron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boron), [zinc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinc), [molybdenum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molybdenum), [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron), and [manganese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manganese).[^cite_note-Dittmar2009-43] These elements are provided as water-soluble salts. Iron presents special problems because it converts from soluble ([ferrous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferrous)) to insoluble bio-unavailable ([ferric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferric)) compounds at moderate soil pH and phosphate concentrations.[^cite_note-72] For this reason, iron is often administered as a [chelate complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chelation), e.g., the [EDTA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethylenediaminetetraacetic_acid) or [EDDHA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EDDHA) derivatives. The micronutrient needs depend on the plant and the environment. For example, [sugar beets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_beet) appear to require [boron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boron), and [legumes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legume) require [cobalt](24580536.md),[^cite_note-Scherer2009-1] while environmental conditions such as heat or drought make boron less available for plants, causing [boron deficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boron_deficiency_\(plant_disorder\)).[^cite_note-73]

## Production

The production of synthetic, or inorganic, fertilizers require prepared chemicals,[^cite_note-74] whereas organic fertilizers are derived from the [biological processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_process) of plant and animal remains or [excreta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excreta) (e.g. [urine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urine), [feces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feces)).[^cite_note-75]

### Nitrogen fertilizers



Nitrogen fertilizer application per hectare of cropland



Total nitrogenous fertilizer consumption per region, measured in tonnes of total nutrient per year.

Nitrogen fertilizers are made from [ammonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonia) (NH<sub>3</sub>) [produced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonia_production) by the [Haber–Bosch process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haber_process).[^cite_note-Smil2004-67] In this [energy-intensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy-intensive) process, [natural gas](22131.md) (CH<sub>4</sub>) [usually](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_production) [supplies the hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_reforming), and the nitrogen (N<sub>2</sub>) is [derived from the air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen#Production). This ammonia is used as a [feedstock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feedstock) for all other nitrogen fertilizers, such as [anhydrous ammonium nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonium_nitrate) (NH<sub>4</sub>NO<sub>3</sub>) and [urea](31734.md) (CO(NH<sub>2</sub>)<sub>2</sub>).

Deposits of [sodium nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_nitrate) (NaNO<sub>3</sub>) ([Chilean saltpeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chilean_saltpeter)) are also found in the [Atacama Desert](18952975.md) in [Chile](5489.md) and were one of the original (1830) nitrogen-rich fertilizers used.[^cite_note-76] It is still mined for fertilizer use.[^cite_note-77] Nitrates are also produced from ammonia by the [Ostwald process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ostwald_Process).

### Phosphate fertilizers



Phosphate application per hectare of cropland



An apatite mine for phosphates in [Siilinjärvi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siilinj%C3%A4rvi_carbonatite), Finland

Phosphate fertilizers are obtained by extraction from [phosphate rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphate_rock), which contains two principal phosphorus-containing minerals, [fluorapatite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorapatite) Ca<sub>5</sub>(PO<sub>4</sub>)<sub>3</sub>F (CFA) and [hydroxyapatite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroxyapatite) Ca<sub>5</sub>(PO<sub>4</sub>)<sub>3</sub>OH. Billions of kg of phosphate rock are mined annually, but the size and quality of the remaining ore is decreasing.[^cite_note-Blackwell2019-37] These minerals are converted into water-soluble phosphate salts by treatment with [acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid).[^cite_note-78] The large production of [sulfuric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfuric_acid) is primarily motivated by this application.[^cite_note-79] In the [nitrophosphate process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrophosphate_process) or Odda process (invented in 1927), [phosphate rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphate_rock) with up to a 20% phosphorus (P) content is dissolved with [nitric acid](21655.md) (HNO<sub>3</sub>) to produce a mixture of [phosphoric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphoric_acid) (H<sub>3</sub>PO<sub>4</sub>) and [calcium nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium_nitrate) (Ca(NO<sub>3</sub>)<sub>2</sub>). This mixture can be combined with a potassium fertilizer to produce a *compound fertilizer* with the three macronutrients N, P and K in easily dissolved form.[^cite_note-80]

### Potassium fertilizers



Potash fertilizer application per hectare of cropland

[Potash](56509.md) is a mixture of potassium minerals used to make potassium (chemical symbol: K) fertilizers. Potash is soluble in water, so the main effort in producing this nutrient from the ore involves some purification steps, e.g., to remove [sodium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_chloride) (NaCl) (common [salt](1605200.md)).[^cite_note-81] Sometimes potash is referred to as K<sub>2</sub>O, as a matter of convenience to those describing the potassium content. In fact, potash fertilizers are usually [potassium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium_chloride), [potassium sulfate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium_sulfate), [potassium carbonate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium_carbonate), or [potassium nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium_nitrate).[^cite_note-Gowariker2009-82]

### Organic fertilizers

Main article: [Organic fertilizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_fertilizer)



Compost bin for small-scale production of organic fertilizer



A large commercial compost operation

*Organic fertilizers* can describe those fertilizers with a biological origin, i.e. derived from living or formerly living materials. *Organic fertilizers* can also describe commercially available and frequently packaged products that strive to follow the expectations and restrictions adopted by [organic farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_farming) and [environmentally friendly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmentally_friendly) gardening, i.e. related systems of food and plant production that significantly limit or strictly avoid the use of synthetic fertilizers and [pesticides](48340.md). The *organic fertilizer products* typically contain both some organic materials as well as acceptable additives such as nutritive rock powders, ground [seashells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seashell) ([crab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crab), [oyster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oyster), etc.), other prepared products such as seaweed meal or [kelp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelp), and cultivated [microorganisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microorganism) and derivatives.[^cite_note-83]

Fertilizers of an organic origin (the first definition) include [animal wastes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manure), plant wastes from agriculture, [seaweed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seaweed_fertilizer), [compost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compost), and treated [sewage sludge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sewage_sludge) ([biosolids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biosolid)). Beyond manures, animal sources can include products from [animal slaughters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_slaughter): [bloodmeal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloodmeal), [bone meal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bone_meal), [feather meal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feather_meal), [hides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hide_\(skin\)), [hoofs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoof), and [horns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horn_\(anatomy\)) all are typical components.[^cite_note-Dittmar2009-43] Organically derived materials available to industry such as [sewage sludge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sewage_sludge) may not be acceptable components of [organic farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_farming) and [gardening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_horticulture), because of factors ranging from residual contaminants[^cite_note-84] to public perception.[^cite_note-85] No matter the definition nor composition, most of these products contain less-concentrated nutrients, and the nutrients are not as easily quantified.[^cite_note-86] They can offer soil-building advantages as well as be appealing to those who are trying to farm or garden more naturally.[^cite_note-87]

In terms of volume, [peat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peat) is the most widely used packaged organic [soil amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_amendment). It is an immature form of [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal) which improves [soil aeration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_aeration) and [moisture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_moisture) and thus soil biological activity but confers no direct [nutritional value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutritional_value) to the plants. It is therefore not a fertilizer as defined in the beginning of the article, but rather an [amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_conditioner).[^cite_note-88] However, peat is avoided by many gardeners because its extraction destroys habitats, releases carbon dioxide, and increases flood risk.[^cite_note-89] [Coir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coir) (derived from [coconut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coconut) [husks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Husk)), [bark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bark_\(botany\)), and [sawdust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sawdust) are mainly applied as [mulch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mulch) and protects the soil from [desiccation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desiccation)[^cite_note-90] while preventing the development of [weeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weed)[^cite_note-91] and improving soil structure,[^cite_note-92] but do not confer any [nutritional value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutritional_value) to the soil. Some organic additives can even have a reverse effect on nutrients. Fresh [sawdust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sawdust) can consume soil nutrients as it breaks down and is colonized by [wood-decay fungi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood-decay_fungus), causing [nitrogen deficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_deficiency) (nitrogen drawdown) in the absence of nutrient addition.[^cite_note-93] However, this property can be used to capture excess mineral nitrogen.[^cite_note-94] These same organic soil texturizers (as well as compost, etc.) may increase the availability of nutrients through improved [cation-exchange capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cation-exchange_capacity),[^cite_note-95] or through increased growth of microorganisms that in turn increase availability of certain plant nutrients.[^cite_note-96] True organic fertilizers such as [composts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compost) and [manures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manure) may be distributed locally without going into industry production, making actual consumption more difficult to quantify.

## Application



Fertilizer [sprayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sprayer)



[Drone crop fertilizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_drone)



Applying [superphosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superphosphate) fertilizer by hand, New Zealand, 1938

Fertilizers are commonly used for growing all crops, with application rates depending on [soil fertility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_fertility), usually as measured by a [soil test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_test) and according to the particular crop. [Legumes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legume), for example, [fix nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_fixation) from the atmosphere and generally do not require nitrogen fertilizer, hence their promising use for ensuring agriculture [sustainability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainability).[^cite_note-108]

### Timing and amount of application

The timing of application of fertilizers (here only mineral fertilizers will be considered) is a function of crop requirements, [weather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weather) condition and [market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_economy) opportunities. It varies in nature and amount according to the type of agriculture (e.g. [intensive farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intensive_farming) versus [conservation agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_agriculture), [grassland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grassland) versus [arable land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arable_land)), and the presence or absence of signs of [nutrient deficiencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutrient_deficiencies). Timing fertilization with peak nutrient uptake demand is essential for optimizing both yield and quality. In general, nutrient uptake rates are highest from early to midgrowing season, which is why fertilization near the time of seeding is generally very effective. With fall-planted grains (e.g. [winter wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_wheat)), fall N fertilization followed by spring [topdressing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topdressing) is likely the best combination of fertilization practices to optimize yield.[^cite_note-109] The application of a soluble mineral fertilizer (e.g. [ammonium nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonium_nitrate)) is avoided during [heavy rainfall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_rain_\(meteorology\)) because most of it would be lost for the crop[^cite_note-110] and would rapidly pollute the [aquifer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquifer).[^cite_note-111] In [conservation agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_agriculture) (including [no-till farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No-till_farming), [permaculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permaculture)) as well as other non-organic alternatives to [intensive farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intensive_farming) (e.g. [smallholding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smallholding) agriculture) the application of mineral fertilizers is reduced to the strict requirements of the cultivated plants and sometimes is quite unnecessary.[^cite_note-112]

After harvest of a main crop (e.g. [wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheat), [maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize), [potatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potato)) a [catch crop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catch_crop) can be sown, using plant species with a rapid growth rate and high demand of nitrogen (e.g. [white mustard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_mustard)).[^cite_note-113] This allows excess nitrogen, in particular the very mobile [nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrate) anion, to be taken up and transformed in plant [proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein)[^cite_note-114] before contaminating the [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater), and the associated [geochemical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geochemistry) processes, which would rapidly occur in the absence of plant cover.[^cite_note-115] The catch crop is further buried, allowing its transformation in [humus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humus) in which nitrogen is fixed according to various stable [chemical bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_bond).[^cite_note-116]

### Liquid vs solid

Fertilizers are applied to crops both as solids and as liquid. About 90% of fertilizers are applied as solids. The most widely used solid inorganic fertilizers are [urea](31734.md), [diammonium phosphate](1722958.md) and [potassium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium_chloride).[^cite_note-117] Solid fertilizer is typically granulated or powdered. Often solids are available as [prills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prill), a solid globule. Liquid fertilizers comprise aqueous solutions of [ammonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonia_solution), aqueous solutions of [ammonium nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonium_nitrate) or [urea](31734.md). These concentrated products may be diluted with water to form a concentrated liquid fertilizer (e.g., [UAN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UAN)). Advantages of liquid fertilizer are its more rapid effect and easier coverage.[^cite_note-Dittmar2009-43] The addition of fertilizer to irrigation water is called *[fertigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertigation)*.[^cite_note-Gowariker2009-82] Granulated fertilizers are more economical to ship and store, not to mention easier to apply.[^cite_note-118][^cite_note-119]

### Slow- and controlled-release fertilizers

Main article: [Controlled-release fertiliser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controlled-release_fertiliser)

A **[controlled-release fertiliser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controlled-release_fertiliser)** (**CRF**) is a [granulated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granulated) [fertiliser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertiliser) that releases [nutrients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutrient) gradually into the [soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil) (i.e., with a [controlled release](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controlled_release) period).[^cite_note-120] Controlled-release fertilizer is also known as *controlled-availability fertilizer*, *delayed-release fertilizer*, *metered-release fertilizer*, or *slow-acting fertilizer*. Usually slow- and controlled-release fertilizers refer to nitrogen-based fertilizers, e.g. [ureaform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ureaform), needing degradation by a wide spectrum of [soil organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_organisms) before nitrogen could be liberated as nitrate.[^cite_note-121] Slow-release fertilizers (SRF) and controlled-release fertilizers (CRF) offer various benefits over conventional ones, ensuring [sustainability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainability) in fertilizing practice.[^cite_note-122][^cite_note-123]

### Foliar application

Main article: [Foliar feeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foliar_feeding)

[Foliar fertilizers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foliar_feeding) are applied directly to leaves. They can reduce the total amounts of fertilizer applied and achieve high fertilizer efficiency.[^cite_note-Niu2020-124] This method is almost invariably used to apply water-soluble straight nitrogen fertilizers and used especially for high-value crops such as fruits. Urea is the most common foliar fertilizer.[^cite_note-Dittmar2009-43] However, [chelation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chelation) of the foliar-applied nutrient is recommended for improving absorption and migration of the targeted nutrient and thereby avoiding loss to the soil and the groundwater.[^cite_note-Niu2020-124]

### Chemicals that affect nitrogen uptake



N-Butylthiophosphoryltriamide, an enhanced efficiency fertilizer

Various chemicals are used to enhance the efficiency of nitrogen-based fertilizers. In this way farmers can limit the [polluting effects of nitrogen run-off](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_pollution). [Nitrification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrification) inhibitors (also known as nitrogen stabilizers) suppress the conversion of [ammonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonia) into [nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrate), an anion that is more prone to leaching. 1-Carbamoyl-3-methylpyrazole (CMP), [dicyandiamide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dicyandiamide), [nitrapyrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrapyrin) (2-chloro-6-trichloromethylpyridine) and 3,4-dimethylpyrazole phosphate (DMPP) are popular.[^cite_note-YangFang2016-125] [Urease inhibitors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urease_inhibitor) are used to slow the hydrolytic conversion of urea into ammonia catalyzed by [ureases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urease), ammonia being prone to evaporation[^cite_note-126] as well as nitrification.[^cite_note-127] A popular inhibitor of ureases is *N*-(*n*-butyl)thiophosphoric triamide ([NBPT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NBPT)).

### Overfertilization



Fertilizer burn

Careful use of fertilization technologies is important because excess nutrients can be detrimental to the cultivated plant.[^cite_note-128] [Fertilizer burn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertilizer_burn) can occur when too much fertilizer is applied, resulting in damage or even death of the plant. Fertilizers vary in their tendency to burn roughly in accordance with their [salt index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertilizer_burn#Salt_index_and_effects).[^cite_note-129][^cite_note-130]

## Environmental effects



[Runoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_runoff) of [soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil) and fertilizer during a rain storm

See also: [Environmental impact of agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_of_agriculture), [Human impact on the nitrogen cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_impact_on_the_nitrogen_cycle), [Nitrogen fertilizer § Problems with inorganic fertilizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_fertilizer#Problems_with_inorganic_fertilizer), and [Nitrogen Cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_Cycle)

Synthetic fertilizers used in agriculture have [wide-reaching environmental consequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_of_agriculture).

According to the [Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC)](15030.md) [Special Report on Climate Change and Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Report_on_Climate_Change_and_Land), production of these fertilizers and associated [land use](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_use) practices are drivers of [global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming).[^cite_note-Mbow2019-6] The use of fertilizer has also led to a number of direct environmental consequences: [agricultural runoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_runoff) which leads to downstream effects like [ocean dead zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_zone_\(ecology\))[^cite_note-131] and [waterway](174851.md) [contamination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contamination),[^cite_note-132] [soil microbiome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_microbiome) degradation,[^cite_note-133] and accumulation of [toxic compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toxic_compound) in ecosystems.[^cite_note-134] Indirect environmental impacts include: N-crop-pest cascades,[^cite_note-135] increased [greenhouse gas emissions](9528025.md) and [environmental impacts of fracking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulic_fracturing) for the [natural gas](22131.md) used in the [Haber process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haber_process),[^cite_note-136][^cite_note-137] the agricultural boom which is partially responsible for the rapid [growth in human population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_population_growth)[^cite_note-138] and large-scale industrial agricultural practices associated with it causing [pressure on biodiversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity_loss), [habitat destruction](1707053.md) and [fragmentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habitat_fragmentation),[^cite_note-139] and agricultural [soil loss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_loss).[^cite_note-140]

In order to mitigate [environmental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_concerns) and [food security](216361.md) concerns, the [international community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_community) has included [food systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_system) in [Sustainable Development Goal 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_Development_Goal_2) which focuses on creating a [climate-friendly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effects_of_climate_change_on_agriculture) and [sustainable food production system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_food_system).[^cite_note-141] Most [policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Policy) and regulatory approaches to address these issues focus on pivoting [agricultural practices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_practice) towards [sustainable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_agriculture) or [regenerative agricultural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regenerative_agriculture) practices: these use less [synthetic fertilizers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_fertilizer) (for example [sustainable agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_agriculture)), better [soil management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_management) (for example [no-till agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No-till_farming)) and more [organic fertilizers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_fertilizer) (for example [organic farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_farming), [conservation agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_agriculture), [biodynamic agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodynamic_agriculture)).



Large pile of [phosphogypsum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphogypsum) waste near [Fort Meade, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Meade,_Florida)

For each ton of [phosphoric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphoric_acid) produced by the processing of [phosphate rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphate_rock), five tons of [waste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waste) are generated. This waste takes the form of impure, useless, radioactive solid called [phosphogypsum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphogypsum). Estimates range from 100,000,000 and 280,000,000 tons of phosphogypsum waste produced annually worldwide.[^cite_note-142]

### Water

Main article: [Eutrophication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eutrophication)



Red circles show the location and size of many [dead zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_zone_\(ecology\))

Phosphorus and nitrogen fertilizers can affect soil, surface water, and [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater)[^cite_note-Kesler2015-101] due to the dispersion of minerals into [waterways](174851.md) under high rainfall,[^cite_note-Fletcher2021-143][^cite_note-144] snowmelt and can [leach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaching_\(agriculture\)) into [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater) over time.[^cite_note-145] Agricultural [run-off](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runoff_\(hydrology\)) is a major contributor to the [eutrophication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eutrophication) of [freshwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresh_water) bodies. For example, in the [US](3434750.md), about half of all the lakes surveyed by the [United States Environmental Protection Agency](58666.md) (US EPA) were [eutrophic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eutrophic) in 2007 with a further alarming increase to 80 per cent in 2012.[^cite_note-146] The main contributor to eutrophication is [phosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphate), which is normally a [limiting nutrient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limiting_nutrient), besides nitrogen; high P concentrations promote the growth of [cyanobacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanobacteria) and [algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algae), the [demise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demise) of which consumes oxygen.[^cite_note-Werner2002-147] [Cyanobacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanobacteria) blooms ('[algal blooms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algal_blooms)') can also produce harmful [toxins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eutrophication#Toxicity) that can accumulate in the [food chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_chain), and can be harmful to humans.[^cite_note-148][^cite_note-149] Fertilizer run-off can be reduced by using weather-optimized fertilization strategies.[^cite_note-Fletcher2021-143]

The nitrogen-rich compounds found in [fertilizer runoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertilizer_runoff) are the primary cause of serious [oxygen depletion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen_depletion) in many parts of [oceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean), especially in coastal zones, [lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake) and [rivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River).[^cite_note-150] The resulting lack of dissolved oxygen greatly reduces the ability of these areas to sustain oceanic [fauna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fauna).[^cite_note-151] The number of oceanic [dead zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_zone_\(ecology\)) near inhabited coastlines is increasing.[^cite_note-152]

As of 2006, the application of nitrogen fertilizer is being increasingly controlled in northwestern Europe[^cite_note-153] and the United States.[^cite_note-154][^cite_note-155] In cases where [eutrophication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eutrophication) can be reversed, it may nevertheless take decades[^cite_note-156] and need significant soil management[^cite_note-157] before the accumulated nitrates in [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater) can be broken down by natural [denitrification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denitrification).[^cite_note-158]

#### Nitrate pollution

Only a fraction of the nitrogen-based fertilizers is converted to plant matter. The remainder accumulates in the soil or is lost as run-off.[^cite_note-159] High application rates of nitrogen-containing fertilizers combined with the high [water solubility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_solubility) of nitrate leads to increased [runoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_runoff#Agricultural_issues) into [surface water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_water) as well as [leaching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaching_\(agriculture\)) into [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater), thereby causing [groundwater pollution](44413707.md).[^cite_note-160][^cite_note-161][^cite_note-162] The excessive use of nitrogen-containing fertilizers (be they synthetic or natural) is particularly damaging, as much of the nitrogen that is not taken up by plants is transformed into nitrate which is easily leached.[^cite_note-163]

Nitrate levels above 10 mg/L (10 ppm) in groundwater can cause '[blue baby syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_baby_syndrome)' (acquired [methemoglobinemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methemoglobinemia)).[^cite_note-164] The nutrients, especially nitrates, in fertilizers can cause problems for natural habitats and for human health if they are washed off into [watercourses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watercourses) or leached through the soil into [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater).[^cite_note-165] Run-off can lead to fertilizing [algal blooms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algal_bloom) that use up all the oxygen and leave huge "dead zones" behind where other fish and aquatic life can not live.[^cite_note-166]

### Soil

#### Acidification

See also: [Soil pH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_pH) and [Soil acidification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_acidification)

Soil acidification refers to the process by which the [pH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH) level of soil becomes more acidic over time. Soil pH is a measure of the soil's acidity or alkalinity and is determined on a scale from 0 to 14, with 7 being neutral. A pH value below 7 indicates [acidic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid) soil, while a pH value above 7 indicates [alkaline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkali_soil) or [basic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_\(chemistry\)) soil. Soil acidification is a significant concern in agriculture and horticulture, leading to cation leaching, unstable soil aggregate structure, metal toxicity, decreased nutrient availability, and thus affecting soil biological properties and plant performances.[^cite_note-167]

Nitrogen-containing fertilizers release [ammonium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonium) or [nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrate) ions, which can [acidify the soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_acidification) as they undergo [biochemical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemical) reactions.[^cite_note-168] When nitrogen-containing fertilizers, whether mineral or organic, are added to the soil, they increase the concentration of hydrogen ions (H+) in the soil solution, which lowers the pH of the soil.[^cite_note-169][^cite_note-170] This may lead to decrease in [nutrient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutrient) availability which may be offset by [liming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liming_\(soil\)).[^cite_note-soil_acidification_and-51]

Soil acidification occurs also through [acid rain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_rain), a man-induced [concern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_change) despite severe measures to suppress or mitigate industrial [gas emissions](9528025.md) since the 1970s.[^cite_note-171] Among reported causes of acid rain, nitrogen fertilizers (whether mineral or organic), and the subsequent emissions of [nitrous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrous_oxide) and [nitric oxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitric_oxide) are prominent in regions of intensive agriculture.[^cite_note-172] The mechanisms are complex, involving [ammonia volatilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonia_volatilization_from_urea) from manure (whether stored or spread) or urea and direct redeposition followed by [nitrification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrification) in the soil or previous oxidation to [nitrogen oxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_oxide) in the atmosphere before redeposition as [nitric acid](21655.md).[^cite_note-173]

#### Accumulation of toxic elements

##### Cadmium

The concentration of [cadmium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cadmium) in phosphorus-containing fertilizers varies considerably and can be problematic.[^cite_note-174] For example, [mono-ammonium phosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoammonium_phosphate) fertilizer may have a cadmium content of as low as 0.14 mg/kg or as high as 50.9 mg/kg.[^cite_note-175] The [phosphate rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphate_rock) used in their manufacture can contain as much as 188 mg/kg cadmium[^cite_note-176] Examples are deposits on [Nauru](21302.md)[^cite_note-177] and the [Christmas Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christmas_Island).[^cite_note-178] Continuous use of high-cadmium fertilizer can contaminate soils[^cite_note-179] and [plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phytotoxicity).[^cite_note-Chaney2012-180] Limits to the cadmium content of phosphate fertilizers have been considered by the [European Commission](9974.md).[^cite_note-181][^cite_note-182][^cite_note-183] Producers of phosphorus-containing fertilizers now select phosphate rock based on the cadmium content.[^cite_note-Werner2002-147]

##### Fluoride

Phosphate rocks contain high levels of [fluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluoride) in the form of [fluorapatite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorapatite).[^cite_note-184] Consequently, the widespread use of phosphate fertilizers has increased soil fluoride concentrations.[^cite_note-Chaney2012-180] It has been found that [food contamination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_contamination) from fertilizer is of little concern as plants accumulate little fluoride from the soil; of greater concern is the possibility of fluoride toxicity to livestock that ingest contaminated soil.[^cite_note-Loganathan2008-185][^cite_note-Cronin2000-186] Also of possible concern are the effects of fluoride on [soil microorganisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_microorganism).[^cite_note-Loganathan2008-185][^cite_note-Cronin2000-186][^cite_note-187]

##### Radioactive elements

The [radioactive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_decay) content of the fertilizers varies considerably and depends both on their concentrations in the parent mineral and on the fertilizer production process.[^cite_note-Chaney2012-180][^cite_note-188] [Uranium-238](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-238) concentrations can range from 7 to 100 pCi/g ([picocuries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picocuries) per gram) in phosphate rock[^cite_note-189] and from 1 to 67 pCi/g in phosphate fertilizers.[^cite_note-190][^cite_note-NCRP1987-191][^cite_note-192] Where high annual rates of phosphorus fertilizer are used, this can result in [uranium-238](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-238) concentrations in soils and [drainage waters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage_system_\(geomorphology\)) that are several times greater than are normally present.[^cite_note-NCRP1987-191][^cite_note-193] However, the impact of these increases on the [risk to human health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sievert#Dose_examples) from radionuclidic contamination of foods is very small (less than 0.05 m[Sv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sievert)/y).[^cite_note-NCRP1987-191][^cite_note-194][^cite_note-195]

##### Other metals

[Steel industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel_industry) wastes such as steel [slag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slag) are often recycled as a [soil amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_amendment) or for the production of fertilizers due to their high Ca and Mg content and various [trace elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trace_element) necessary for plant growth.[^cite_note-196] However, they can also include [toxic metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toxic_heavy_metal).[^cite_note-Wilson1997-197] Among them are [arsenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arsenic),[^cite_note-He2020-198] [cadmium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cadmium),[^cite_note-He2020-198] [chromium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromium),[^cite_note-199] and [nickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel),[^cite_note-200] while steel slag amendment rather contribute to immobilize [lead](17747.md) in the soil and thus to decrease its toxicity to the cultivated plant.[^cite_note-201] The most common toxic elements in this type of fertilizer are [mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_\(element\)), lead, and arsenic.[^cite_note-202][^cite_note-203][^cite_note-204] Given the high cost of removing potentially harmful properties from steel slags,[^cite_note-205] a better solution is to [immobilize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immobilization_\(soil_science\)) them. The incorporation of [biochar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochar) to steel slag make even the mixture a good amendement for the ([passivation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passivation_\(chemistry\))) of [heavy metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_metals) in agricultural soil.[^cite_note-206] Highly pure fertilizers are widely available and perhaps best known as the highly water-soluble fertilizers containing blue dyes used around [households](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Household), such as [Miracle-Gro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miracle-Gro). These highly water-soluble fertilizers are used in the [plant nursery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_nursery) business and are available in larger packages at significantly less cost than [retail](183515.md) quantities. Some inexpensive retail granular garden fertilizers are made with high purity ingredients.[^cite_note-207]

#### Trace mineral depletion

Attention has been addressed to the decreasing concentrations of [micronutrients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micronutrient) such as [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron), [zinc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinc), [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper) and [magnesium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium) in many foods over the last 50–60 years.[^cite_note-Davis2004-208][^cite_note-Thomas2007-209] [Intensive farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intensive_farming) practices, including the use of synthetic fertilizers, are frequently suggested as reasons for these declines and [organic farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_farming) is often suggested as a solution.[^cite_note-Thomas2007-209] Although improved crop yields resulting from NPK fertilizers are known to dilute the concentrations of other nutrients in plants,[^cite_note-Davis2004-208][^cite_note-210] much of the measured decline can be attributed to the use of progressively higher-yielding crop varieties that produce foods with lower mineral concentrations than their less-productive ancestors.[^cite_note-Davis2004-208][^cite_note-211][^cite_note-212] It is, therefore, unlikely that [organic farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_farming) or reduced use of fertilizers (e.g. [conservation agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_agriculture)) will solve the problem; foods with high nutrient density are posited to be achieved using older, lower-yielding varieties or the development of new high-yield, nutrient-dense varieties.[^cite_note-Davis2004-208][^cite_note-Saltzman2013-213]

Fertilizers are, in fact, more likely to solve trace mineral deficiency problems than cause them: in Western Australia deficiencies of [zinc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinc), [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper), [manganese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manganese), [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron) and [molybdenum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molybdenum) were identified as limiting the growth of [broad-acre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadacre) crops and pastures in the 1940s and 1950s.[^cite_note-Moore2001-214] Soils in Western Australia are very old, highly weathered and deficient in many of the major nutrients and trace elements.[^cite_note-Moore2001-214] Since this time these trace elements are routinely added to fertilizers used in agriculture in this state.[^cite_note-Moore2001-214] Many other soils around the world are deficient in zinc, leading to deficiency in both plants and humans,[^cite_note-215] and zinc fertilizers are widely used to solve this problem.[^cite_note-216]

#### Changes in soil biology

Further information: [soil biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_biology)

High levels of fertilizer may cause the breakdown of the [symbiotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbiosis) relationships between plant roots and [mycorrhizal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycorrhiza) fungi,[^cite_note-217] in particular when in excess of plant requirements.[^cite_note-218] It is still debated whether and how fertilizers affect [soil animals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_animals). Both organic and organic–mineral fertilizers increase the abundance of soil fauna whereas mineral fertilizers had no such effect, with [idiosyncratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idiosyncrasy) responses of soil animal groups masking overall effects.[^cite_note-219] Chemical fertilizers stimulates the growth of [microbial populations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microbial_population_biology) but did not change their richness and diversity, while decreases in [enzymatic activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzymatic_activity) have been registered.[^cite_note-220]

The combination of [soil acidification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_acidification) and high nitrogen content was probably unknown to most soil organisms before the advent of [industrial agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_agriculture). More generally, soils are either acidic and nutrient-poor, or neutral to basic and nutrient-rich (nitrogen included), in relation to the pH-dependent capacity of [clay minerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clay_mineral) to hold nutrients.[^cite_note-221] This unexpected combination of excess nutrient availability and acid [stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_\(biology\)) causes a depletion in the sustainability of animal and microbial communities by weakening the linkages between aboveground and belowground components of [agroecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agroecosystem).[^cite_note-222] Other [ecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem) are also affected by nitrogen-enriched acid rains originationg from intensive agricultural practices. [Sphagnum bogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphagnum_bog) are now shifting from [carbon sinks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_sink) (thanks to the accumulation of recalcitrant sphagnum litter and [anoxic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anoxic_waters) conditions) to carbon sources under the influence of nitrogen deposition and subsequent stimulation of [decomposer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decomposer) activity.[^cite_note-223] Other nutrient-poor ecosystems are also strongly affected, such as [heathlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heathland), with a surprising combination of higher [soil organic matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_organic_matter) accumulation and higher [soil enzyme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_enzyme) activity.[^cite_note-224]

### Organic agriculture

Further information: [organic farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_farming)

Two types of agricultural management practices include [organic farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_farming) and [conventional agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conventional_agriculture). The former encourages [soil fertility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_fertility) using local resources to maximize efficiency. Organic agriculture avoids synthetic agrochemicals. Conventional agriculture uses all the components that organic agriculture does not use.[^cite_note-225]

The excess use of organic amendments (e.g. pig [slurry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slurry)) may have detrimental effects on [water quality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_quality) through contamination of the [aquifer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquifer) and subsequent [eutrophication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eutrophication) of the [water bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_bodies) it feds.[^cite_note-226] This is a societal concern in areas where [pig farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pig_farming) conflicts with [environmental protection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_protection).[^cite_note-227]

### Fossil fuel consumption and sustainability

Most synthetic fertilizer is fed from [fossil fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel), using fossil energy and [methane](18582230.md) for producing [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) used in the synthesis of ammonia by the [Haber process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haber_process), as well as various [chemical reactions](6271.md) used in the synthesis of most fertilizers (e.g. [urea](31734.md), [ammonium nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonium_nitrate), [superphosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superphosphate)).[^cite_note-228] Some alternatives have been proposed for the production of hydrogen, such as [solar energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_energy),[^cite_note-229] waste treatment,[^cite_note-230] or [electrolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrolysis).[^cite_note-231]

### Contribution to the greenhouse effect

See also: [Greenhouse gas emissions from agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_gas_emissions_from_agriculture)



Global [methane](18582230.md) concentrations (surface and atmospheric) for 2005; note distinct plumes

The amount of [greenhouse gases](21350772.md) [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide), [methane](18582230.md) and [nitrous oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrous_oxide) produced during the [manufacture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haber_process) and use of nitrogen fertilizer is estimated as around 5% of [anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropogenic_greenhouse_gas_emissions). One third is produced during the production and two thirds during the use of fertilizers.[^cite_note-232] [Nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrate) is converted by [soil bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrous_oxide#Soil) to [nitrous oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrous_oxide), a [greenhouse gas](21350772.md).[^cite_note-233] Nitrous oxide emissions by humans, most of which are from fertilizer, between 2007 and 2016 were estimated at 700 million tons of CO<sub>2</sub> equivalent.[^cite_note-234]

Nitrogen fertilizer was used at a rate of about 110 million tons (of N) per year in 2012.[^cite_note-235][^cite_note-236] [Nitrous oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrous_oxide) (N<sub>2</sub>O) is the third most important [greenhouse gas](21350772.md) after [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) and [methane](18582230.md). It has 296 times greater [greenhouse effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_effect) per ton than [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide). The 2025 emissions contributed the equivalent of 700 megatons of CO<sub>2</sub> to the atmosphere.[^cite_note-237] It also contributes to [stratospheric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratosphere) [ozone depletion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone_depletion).[^cite_note-238] Altering processes and procedures could reduce these emissions.[^cite_note-239]

[Methane emissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane_emissions) from crop fields (notably rice [paddy fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paddy_field)) are increased by the application of ammonium-based fertilizers. These emissions contribute to global climate change as methane is a potent greenhouse gas.[^cite_note-240][^cite_note-241]

## Policy

### Regulation

In Europe, problems with high nitrate concentrations in [runoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runoff_\(hydrology\)) are being addressed by the European Union's Nitrates Directive.[^cite_note-242] Within [Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Britain), farmers are encouraged to manage their land more sustainably in '[catchment-sensitive farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catchment_Sensitive_Farming)'.[^cite_note-243] In the [US](3434750.md), high concentrations of nitrate and phosphorus in runoff and [drainage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storm_drain) water are classified as nonpoint source pollutants due to their diffuse origin; this pollution is regulated at the state level.[^cite_note-244] [Oregon](26811621.md) and [Washington](13015878.md), both in the [United States](3434750.md), have fertilizer registration programs with on-line [databases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Database) listing chemical analyses of fertilizers.[^cite_note-245][^cite_note-246] [Carbon emission trading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_emission_trading) and [eco-tariffs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eco-tariff) affect the production and price of fertilizer.[^cite_note-247]

### Subsidies

In [China](5405.md), [regulations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulation) have been implemented to control the use of N fertilizers in farming. In 2008, Chinese governments began to partially withdraw fertilizer [subsidies](146719.md), including subsidies to fertilizer transportation and to electricity and natural gas use in the industry. In consequence, the price of fertilizer has gone up and large-scale farms have begun to use less fertilizer. If large-scale farms keep reducing their use of fertilizer subsidies, they have no choice but to optimize the fertilizer they have which would therefore gain an increase in both grain yield and profit.[^cite_note-248]

In March 2022, the [United States Department of Agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Agriculture) announced a new \$250M grant to promote American fertilizer production. Part of the [Commodity Credit Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodity_Credit_Corporation), the grant program will support fertilizer production that is independent of dominant fertilizer suppliers, made in America, and utilizing innovative production techniques to jumpstart future competition.[^cite_note-249]

In the [European Union](9317.md) The [Russo-Ukrainian war](42085878.md) and the subsequent strong increase in energy and mineral fertiliser prices highlighted the need for greater autonomy and efficiency in the production and use of fertilisers. The amended Temporary Crisis and Transition Framework for State Aid[^cite_note-250] enabled EU countries to provide specific support to farmers and fertiliser producers. Funds generated by measures such as the cap on the market revenues of certain [electricity generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_generator) and the solidarity contribution can also be used, subject to the applicable conditions, for purposes of national support schemes.[^cite_note-251]
