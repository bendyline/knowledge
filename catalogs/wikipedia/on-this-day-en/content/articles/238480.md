The **Senedd** ([/ˈsɛnɛð/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [*SEN-edh*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key); lit. 'parliament'  or 'senate'), officially known as the **Welsh Parliament** in [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language) and **Senedd Cymru** ([\[ˈsɛnɛð ˈkəmrɨ\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Welsh) [//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/e/eb/Senedd_Cymru.ogg/Senedd_Cymru.ogg.mp3](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/e/eb/Senedd_Cymru.ogg/Senedd_Cymru.ogg.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup>) in [Welsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_language),[^cite_note-4] is the [devolved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devolution_in_the_United_Kingdom), [unicameral](204304.md) [legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislature) of [Wales](69894.md). A democratically elected body, its role is to scrutinise the [Welsh Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Government) and legislate on devolved matters that are not reserved to the [Parliament of the United Kingdom](13964.md).[^cite_note-role-5] It is a bilingual institution, with both Welsh and English being the official languages of its business.[^cite_note-6] From its creation in May 1999 until May 2020, the Senedd was officially known as the **National Assembly for Wales** (*Cynulliad Cenedlaethol Cymru*) and was often simply called the **Welsh Assembly**.[^cite_note-7]

The Senedd comprises 96 members who are known as [members of the Senedd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Member_of_the_Senedd) (*Aelodau o'r Senedd*),[^cite_note-8] abbreviated as "MS" (Welsh: *AS*).[^cite_note-9] Between 2011 and 2026, members were elected for a five-year [term of office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Term_of_office) under the [additional-member system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Additional-member_system), in which 40 MSs represented smaller geographical divisions known as ["constituencies"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senedd_constituencies_and_electoral_regions) and were elected by [first-past-the-post voting](261709.md), and 20 MSs represented [five "electoral regions"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senedd_constituencies_and_electoral_regions) using the [D'Hondt method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D'Hondt_method) of [proportional representation](23298.md). Since May 2026, elections use [closed list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_list) proportional representation, and Senedd terms last for four years. Typically, the largest party in the Senedd forms the Welsh Government.

A National Assembly for Wales was created by the [Government of Wales Act 1998](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Wales_Act_1998), following the result of the [1997 referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997_Welsh_devolution_referendum). The Assembly initially had no powers to make [primary legislation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_legislation). Limited law-making powers were gained through the [Government of Wales Act 2006](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Wales_Act_2006). Its primary law-making powers were enhanced following a Yes vote in the [referendum on 3 March 2011](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Welsh_devolution_referendum), meaning that the [UK Parliament](13964.md) or the [Secretary of State for Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_State_for_Wales) were no longer consulted when passing [acts of the National Assembly for Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Act_of_Senedd_Cymru) related to the [20 devolved areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devolved,_reserved_and_excepted_matters).[^cite_note-10] These powers were further extended by the [Wales Act 2014](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales_Act_2014) and [Wales Act 2017](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales_Act_2017), with the latter moving the Assembly to a reserved powers model of devolution like that of the Scottish Parliament. In May 2020, the Assembly was renamed to "Senedd Cymru" or "Welsh Parliament" when section 2 of the [Senedd and Elections (Wales) Act 2020](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senedd_and_Elections_\(Wales\)_Act_2020) came into force. The Senedd's powers in economic matters are significantly restricted by the [UK Internal Market Act 2020](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_Internal_Market_Act_2020),[^cite_note-Restrict20-11] a primary purpose of which is to constrain the capacity of the devolved institutions to use their regulatory autonomy.[^cite_note-DouHu22-12] Matters devolved to the Senedd include health, education, economic development, transport, the environment, agriculture, local government and some taxes.

### Road to devolution

Main article: [Welsh devolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_devolution)

Political movements supporting Welsh self-rule began in the late nineteenth century alongside a rise in [Welsh nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_nationalism). The [Sunday Closing (Wales) Act 1881](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunday_Closing_\(Wales\)_Act_1881) was the first piece of legislation to acknowledge Wales as a separate politico-legal nation from England since the [Acts of Union 1535 and 1542](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laws_in_Wales_Acts_1535_and_1542). The late 19th century saw the formation of a number of national institutions; a national and annual cultural event, the [National Eisteddfod of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Eisteddfod_of_Wales) in 1861,[^cite_note-13] the [Football Association of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Football_Association_of_Wales) in 1876,[^cite_note-14] the [Welsh Rugby Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Rugby_Union) in 1881[^cite_note-15] and the [University of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wales) in 1893.[^cite_note-16]

In 1896, [education in Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_in_Wales) began to become distinct with the formation of the Central Welsh Board which inspected grammar schools in Wales and The [Welsh Intermediate Education Act 1889](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Intermediate_Education_Act_1889) was brought about to "make further provision for the intermediate and technical education of the inhabitants of Wales and the county of Monmouth", making the board responsible for inspection of secondary schools.[^cite_note-17][^cite_note-\:4-18] In 1907, the Welsh department of the [Board of Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Board_of_education) was formed and in the same year, a Welsh Inspectorate was established for inspection of elementary schools in Wales.[^cite_note-19]

The early 20th century also saw the continued formation of a number of Welsh national institutions; the [National Library of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Library_of_Wales) in 1911,[^cite_note-20] the [Welsh Guards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Guards) in 1915[^cite_note-21] and the Welsh Board of Health in 1919.[^cite_note-22] The [Church in Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_in_Wales) came into existence in 1920 following the disestablishment of the [Church of England](5955.md) via the [Welsh Church Act 1914](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Church_Act_1914).[^cite_note-23]

An appointed [Council for Wales and Monmouthshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_for_Wales_and_Monmouthshire) was established in 1949 to "ensure the government is adequately informed of the impact of government activities on the general life of the people of Wales". The council had 27 members nominated by local authorities in Wales, the [University of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wales), [National Eisteddfod Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Eisteddfod_of_Wales) and the [Welsh Tourist Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Tourist_Board). A post of Minister of Welsh Affairs was created in 1951 and the post of [Secretary of State for Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_State_for_Wales) and the [Welsh Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Office) were established in 1964 leading to the abolition of the Council for Wales. The establishment of the Welsh Office effectively created the basis for the territorial governance of Wales.[^cite_note-24] The [Royal Commission on the Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Commission_on_the_Constitution_\(United_Kingdom\)) (the Kilbrandon Commission) was set up in 1969 by [Harold Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Wilson)'s Labour Government to investigate the possibility of devolution for Scotland and Wales.[^cite_note-devolution-25] Its recommendations formed the basis of the 1974 [White paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_paper) *Democracy and Devolution: proposals for Scotland and Wales*,[^cite_note-devolution-25] which proposed the creation of a [Welsh Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Assembly_\(Wales_Act_1978\)). However, Welsh voters overwhelmingly rejected the proposals in a [referendum held in 1979](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1979_Welsh_devolution_referendum).[^cite_note-devolution-25][^cite_note-26]

Following the [1997 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997_United_Kingdom_general_election), the new Labour Government argued that an Assembly would be more democratically accountable than the [Welsh Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Office). For eleven years prior to 1997 Wales had been represented in the [Cabinet of the United Kingdom](162790.md) by a [Secretary of State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_State_for_Wales) who did not represent a Welsh constituency at [Westminster](13964.md).[^cite_note-27] A [referendum was held](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997_Welsh_devolution_referendum) in Wales on 18 September 1997 in which voters approved the creation of the National Assembly for Wales with a total of 559,419 votes, or 50.3% of the vote.[^cite_note-28]

In the following year, the [Government of Wales Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Wales_Act_1998) was passed by the [United Kingdom parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_parliament), establishing the Assembly. On 1 July 1999, the powers of the Secretary of State for Wales were transferred to the Assembly, and the Welsh Office ceased to exist.[^cite_note-29]

In July 2002, the [Welsh Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Government) established an [independent commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Commission), with [Lord Richard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivor_Richard) (former leader of the [House of Lords](13658.md)) as chair, to review the powers and electoral arrangements of the National Assembly to ensure that it is able to operate in the best interests of the people of Wales.[^cite_note-richardspage-30] The Richard Commission reported in March 2004. It recommended that the National Assembly should have powers to legislate in certain areas, whilst others would remain the preserve of Westminster.[^cite_note-richardspage-30] It also recommended changing the electoral system to the [single transferable vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_transferable_vote) (STV) which would produce greater [proportionality](23298.md).[^cite_note-richardspage-30]

In response, the British government, in its *Better Governance for Wales* White Paper, published on 15 June 2005, proposed a more permissive law-making system for the Welsh Assembly based on the use of Parliamentary Orders in Council.[^cite_note-31][^cite_note-32]

### Enhanced powers: The Government of Wales Act 2006

Main article: [Government of Wales Act 2006](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Wales_Act_2006)

The [Government of Wales Act 2006](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Wales_Act_2006) received [Royal assent](153067.md) on 25 July 2006. It conferred on the Assembly legislative powers similar to other [devolved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devolution) legislatures through the ability to pass [Assembly Measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_of_the_National_Assembly_for_Wales) concerning matters that are devolved. Requests for further legislative powers made through [legislative competence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislative_Competence_Order) requests were subject to the veto of the [Secretary of State for Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_State_for_Wales), [House of Commons](13828.md) or [House of Lords](13658.md).

The Act reformed the assembly to a parliamentary-type structure, establishing the [Welsh Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Government) as an entity separate from, but accountable to the National Assembly. It enables the Assembly to legislate within its devolved fields.

The Act also reforms the Assembly's electoral system. It prevents individuals from standing as candidates in both constituency and regional seats. It was supported in the Richard Commission.[^cite_note-richardspage-30]

The changes to the Assembly's powers were commenced on 4 May 2007, after the [election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_National_Assembly_for_Wales_election).[^cite_note-33]

Following a [referendum on 3 March 2011](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Welsh_devolution_referendum), the Welsh Assembly gained direct law making powers, without the need to consult Westminster.

### Reserved powers model: The Wales Act 2017

Main article: [Wales Act 2017](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales_Act_2017)

The [Conservative-Liberal coalition government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cameron%E2%80%93Clegg_coalition) created the [Commission on Devolution in Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commission_on_Devolution_in_Wales) (also known as Silk Commission), composed of members nominated by the 4 parties represented in the Welsh Assembly and several leading legal and political experts, to "create a lasting devolution settlement for Wales". Following the first set of recommendations by the Commission, the UK government announced in November 2013 that some borrowing powers are to be devolved to the Assembly along with control of landfill tax and stamp duty. Additionally the [Wales Act 2014](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales_Act_2014) provides for a referendum to be held on the Assembly's ability to set a degree of [income tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Income_tax),[^cite_note-34] though there is a proposal for the requirement for a referendum to be removed.

Both the UK and Welsh governments supported the Silk Commission (Part 2) proposal to move to a "[reserved powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devolved,_reserved_and_excepted_matters)" model of devolution (similar to that of the [Scottish Parliament](61188.md) and the [Northern Ireland Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Assembly)) where the UK government would have specific "reserved" powers and the Welsh Assembly would have control of all other matters.[^cite_note-35][^cite_note-36] This replaced the previous model where certain powers were "conferred" and all others were assumed to be powers of the UK national government. Since the passing of the [Wales Act 2017](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales_Act_2017), the power model in Wales has been in line with that of Scotland, being a reserved matter model.[^cite_note-37]

The [Wales Act 2017](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales_Act_2017), based on the second set of recommendations of the Silk Commission, proposed devolving further areas of government, including some relating to water, marine affairs (ports, harbours, conservation), energy (subsidies, petroleum extraction, construction of smaller energy-generating facilities, etc.), rail franchising and road travel.[^cite_note-38]

### Name change

In July 2016, Assembly members unanimously agreed that the name of the Assembly should reflect its constitutional status as a national parliament.[^cite_note-39] The [Assembly Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senedd_Commission) ran a public consultation on the proposal, which showed that 61% of respondents agreed or strongly agreed that the Assembly should change its name.[^cite_note-40] In 2018, the commission announced its intention to introduce legislation to change the name of the Assembly. Later that year, the *[Llywydd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llywydd_of_the_Senedd)* – the Assembly's presiding officer – wrote to all Assembly Members explaining that the name change proposed in the Bill would be the monolingual name Senedd.[^cite_note-41] In 2019, the [Senedd and Elections (Wales) Bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senedd_and_Elections_\(Wales\)_Act_2020), favouring the name Senedd, was introduced on behalf of the Assembly Commission. Following support of a subsequent amendment to the Bill which favoured a bilingual name for the institution, the Bill was passed by the Assembly on 27 November 2019 and was given Royal Assent on 15 January 2020.[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43] The Act changed the name of the Assembly to *Senedd Cymru* or the *Welsh Parliament*. Its guidance states that the institution will be commonly known as the Senedd in both languages. The name change came into effect on 6 May 2020. Members of the renamed body are known as Members of the Senedd (MS), or *Aelodau o'r Senedd* (AS) in Welsh.[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-auto-45]

### Expansion

Main article: [Senedd Cymru (Members and Elections) Act 2024](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senedd_Cymru_\(Members_and_Elections\)_Act_2024)

On 22 November 2021, [Welsh Labour and Plaid Cymru agreed a co-operation deal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Welsh_Labour%E2%80%93Plaid_Cymru_agreement)[^cite_note-46] that would see the implementation of 46 policies that the two parties shared.[^cite_note-47] One of these was the expansion of the Senedd from 60 members to between 80 and 100 members.[^cite_note-48]

At its conference on 12 March 2022, Welsh Labour unanimously approved increasing the size of the Senedd. "The expansion of the Senedd is essential because the journey of devolution is not yet complete", former First Minister [Alun Michael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alun_Michael) said. "There is more to come. And the capacity needs to be there for those backbenchers to do the job of holding to account that you rightly said, cannot be done by to smaller number of representatives."[^cite_note-49] Two weeks later, Plaid members backed the expansion proposal as well.[^cite_note-50]

On 10 May 2022, plans to increase the number of MSs from 60 to 96 were unveiled, as well as the scrapping of [first past the post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_past_the_post), which, at the time, was used to elect 40 of the 60 Members of the Senedd (MSs). Drakeford said these changes were required as "report after report" had demonstrated that the Senedd in its current form "cannot do the job in the way that people in Wales have a right to expect it to be done".[^cite_note-51] Welsh Lib Dem leader [Jane Dodds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Dodds) criticised these plans, claiming that they would disproportionately impact smaller parties.[^cite_note-52]

On 8 June 2022, the Senedd voted 40–14 in favour of expanding the number of MSs.[^cite_note-53]

In 2023, the reform bill committee heard reforms included taking 32 new constituencies created that will be used in the [2024 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_Kingdom_general_election) and pairing them to create 16 Senedd constituencies – with each returning six members in [multi-member districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-member_district).[^cite_note-54] Opposition to the proposed [closed list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_list) voting system was expressed by some Labour[^cite_note-55] and Plaid Cymru[^cite_note-56] representatives, while opposition to the overall expansion of the Senedd was expressed by some Labour representatives[^cite_note-57] and the Conservatives.[^cite_note-58]

Currently it is led by First Minister, [Rhun ap Iorwerth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhun_ap_Iorwerth), of [Plaid Cymru](53343.md) since [May 2026](68245236.md). The government between 2007 and 2011, had been a coalition between [Labour](19279158.md), led by First Minister Carwyn Jones and Plaid Cymru, led by Deputy First Minister [Ieuan Wyn Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ieuan_Wyn_Jones); and between 2016 and 2021, Labour had been in coalition with the [Liberal Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Democrats_\(UK\)) and an independent member.[^cite_note-Cabinet_members_1-61][^cite_note-News_Wales_1-62] From 2021 to early 2026, the government has been a Labour minority government. The executive and civil servants are mainly based in Cardiff's [Cathays Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathays_Park) while the MSs, the Senedd Commission and Ministerial support staff are based in [Cardiff Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Bay), where a new £67 million building, the [Senedd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senedd_building), has been built.[^cite_note-63][^cite_note-64][^cite_note-65] From May 2026, a minority Plaid Cymru government has been formed and works in the Senedd.

One important feature of the National Assembly until 2007 was that there was no legal or constitutional separation of the legislative and executive functions, since it was a single corporate entity. Compared with other [parliamentary systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_system), and arrangements for devolution in other countries of the UK, this was unusual. In practice, however, there was separation of functions, and the terms "Assembly" and "Assembly Parliamentary Service" came into use to distinguish between the two arms. The Government of Wales Act 2006 regularised the separation when it came into effect following the 2007 Assembly Election.

Initially, the Assembly did not have primary legislative or fiscal powers, as these powers were reserved by [Westminster](13964.md). The Assembly did have powers to pass [secondary legislation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_legislation) in devolved areas. Sometimes secondary legislation could be used to amend [primary legislation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_legislation), but the scope of this was very limited. For example, the first Government of Wales Act gave the Assembly power to amend primary legislation relating to the merger of certain public bodies. However, most secondary powers were conferred on the executive by primary legislation to give the executive (i.e. Ministers) more powers, and the Assembly had wider legislative powers than appearances might suggest. For example, the Assembly delayed local elections due to be held in 2003 for a year by use of secondary powers, so that they would not clash with Assembly elections. In 2001 the UK parliament used primary legislation to delay for one month local elections in England during the [Foot-and-mouth disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foot-and-mouth_disease) epidemic.

The Assembly gained limited primary legislative powers following the 2007 election and the passage of the [Government of Wales Act 2006](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Wales_Act_2006). These laws are known as [Assembly Measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_of_the_National_Assembly_for_Wales) and can be enacted in specific fields and matters within the legislative competency of the Assembly. New matters and fields can be devolved by Acts of the UK Parliament or by [LCOs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislative_Competence_Order) approved by Parliament.

Until 2015 the Assembly had no tax-varying powers, however it could influence the rate of [Council Tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_Tax) set by [local authorities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_government_in_the_United_Kingdom), which are part-funded by a grant from the Welsh government.[^cite_note-BBC-tcfwgtfct-66] It also has some discretion over charges for government services. Notable examples in which this discretion has been used to create significant differences from other areas in the UK are:

1. *Charges for NHS prescriptions in Wales* – these have now been abolished.

   [^cite_note-67]

2. *Charges for University Tuition* – are different for Welsh resident students studying at Welsh Universities, compared with students from or studying elsewhere in the UK.

   [^cite_note-68]

3. *Charging for Residential Care* – In Wales there is a flat rate of contribution towards the cost of nursing care (roughly comparable to the highest level of English Contribution) for those who require residential care.

   [^cite_note-69]

This means in reality that there is a wider definition of "nursing care" than in England and therefore less dependence on means testing in Wales than in England, so that more people are entitled to higher levels of state assistance. These variations in the levels of charges may be viewed as *de facto* tax varying powers.

This model of more limited legislative powers created in 1999 was partly because Wales has had the same legal system as England since 1536 (though a different court system until 1830), when it was [merged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laws_in_Wales_Acts_1535_and_1542) with England. [Ireland](147575.md#Union_with_Great_Britain) and [Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_of_Union_1707) were never merged with England, and so always retained some differences in their legal systems. The [Scottish Parliament](61188.md) and the [Northern Ireland Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Assembly) both have deeper and wider powers.

The Assembly inherited the powers and budget of the [Secretary of State for Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_State_for_Wales) and most of the functions of the [Welsh Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Office). It has power to vary laws passed by [Westminster](13964.md) using secondary legislation.

Following a referendum on 4 March 2011, the Welsh Assembly gained direct law-making powers (without the need to consult Westminster). On 3 July 2012, the Welsh Assembly passed its first Act, the Local Government Byelaws (Wales) Act.[^cite_note-70]

The Wales Act 2014 and Wales Act 2017 devolved the following taxes to the Welsh Assembly:

- [Non-Domestic Rates in Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_rates_in_Wales) – from 1 April 2015

- [Land Transaction Tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_Transaction_Tax) (LTT) – from 1 April 2018

- [Landfill Disposals Tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landfill_Disposals_Tax) (LDT) – from 1 April 2018

- [Welsh Rates of Income Tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Rates_of_Income_Tax) (WRIT) – from 1 April 2019

### Powers of the Senedd

Main article: [Devolved, reserved and excepted matters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devolved,_reserved_and_excepted_matters)

The Senedd has the competence to pass bills for [Acts of Senedd Cymru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Act_of_Senedd_Cymru) in all areas which are not explicitly reserved to Westminster; these 'reserved matters' are outlined in schedule 7A of the [Government of Wales Act 2006](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Wales_Act_2006).[^cite_note-71]

This means the Senedd has powers over areas such as:

- Agriculture, [fisheries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisheries), [forestry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forestry_in_Wales) and [rural development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_Resources_Wales)

- [Culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Wales)

- [Economic development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Wales)

- [Education and training](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_in_Wales)

- [Environment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environment_Agency_Wales)

- [Health and health services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NHS_Wales)

- [Highways and transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_in_Wales) (not including rail infrastructure)

- [Local government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_Government_\(Wales\)_Act_1994)

- [Tourism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism_in_Wales)

- [Welsh language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_language)

Reserved matters include subjects such as:

- Foreign affairs

- Immigration

- Police and justice

- Currency

- Most benefits

- Most taxes

- Defence

### Constraints on powers and undermining of devolution

As part of the process of leaving the European Union, the Conservative Party in power in Westminster passed the [United Kingdom Internal Market Act 2020](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_Internal_Market_Act_2020). This legislation aims to prevent internal trade barriers within the UK, and to restrict the exercise of legislative powers of the devolved administrations in economic areas.[^cite_note-Restrict20-11] It has several effects on the constitutional arrangements regarding devolved legislative powers. Principal amongst these is the effect that the market access principles will have on the practical ability of the devolved administrations to regulate economic activity.[^cite_note-Wincott21-78][^cite_note-LydgateEvi20-81] It also expressly reserves the regulation of distortive or harmful subsidies to the UK Government, and gives them spending powers in numerous policymaking areas.[^cite_note-DouganMcEwen20-72][^cite_note-Wincott21-78]

These powers undermine the authority of the Senedd to determine infrastructure priorities within its jurisdiction. The principles undermine devolved competences in two ways. These relate to its status as a protected enactment, and to the disproportionate market size and power of the economy under English jurisdiction.[^cite_note-DouganMcEwen20-72] Because the Senedd will be unable to disapply the market access principles, if they attempt to introduce new or stricter regulatory standards, they will only apply to goods produced within the devolved jurisdiction. This means that these standards will have little or no practical effect other than to disadvantage their own economy, severely restricting their ability to introduce regulatory divergence, or pursue different economic or social choices to those made in Westminster.[^cite_note-Wincott21-78][^cite_note-LydgateEvi20-81]

In 2025 the Labour government in Westminster started the Pride in Place scheme.[^cite_note-82] Through this scheme the UK Government spent £180 million across nine Welsh areas in order to invest in public services, undermining the Welsh Government's devolved responsibility of local government.[^cite_note-83] In response to this 11 Labour MSs wrote a letter to the UK Prime Minister [Keir Starmer](18681491.md) accusing the UK Government of "rolling back the existing devolution settlement".[^cite_note-84][^cite_note-85]

## Members, constituencies, and electoral system



Members of the Senedd during a [plenary session](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plenary_session) in the Siambr

See also: [List of members of the Senedd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_members_of_the_Senedd) and [List of Senedd constituencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Senedd_constituencies)

Initially, the Senedd was elected under the [Additional Member System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Additional_Member_System),[^cite_note-86][^cite_note-erf-87] with forty MSs elected from single-member constituencies on a [plurality voting system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plurality_voting_system) (or *first past the post*) basis, the constituencies being equivalent to those used for the [House of Commons](13828.md) and twenty MSs elected from regional [closed lists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_list) using an alternative party vote.[^cite_note-electionbbc-88] There were five regions: [Mid and West Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid_and_West_Wales_\(Senedd_electoral_region\)), [North Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Wales_\(Senedd_electoral_region\)), [South Wales Central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Wales_Central_\(Senedd_electoral_region\)), [South Wales East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Wales_East_\(Senedd_electoral_region\)) and [South Wales West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Wales_West_\(Senedd_electoral_region\)) (these are the same as the pre-1999 European Parliament constituencies for Wales), each of which returned four members.[^cite_note-electionbbc-88] The additional members produced a degree of proportionality within each region.[^cite_note-electionbbc-88] Voters could vote for any regional party list irrespective of their vote in the constituency election (splitting of the votes across party lists was permitted).&#x20;

List MSs were not elected independently of the constituency result. Rather, elected constituency MSs were deemed to be pre-elected list representatives for the purposes of calculating remainders in the [D'Hondt method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D'Hondt_method).[^cite_note-electionbbc-88]

Overall proportionality was limited by the small proportion of list members (they made up only 33% of the Senedd, compared with 43% in the [Scottish Parliament](61188.md), 50% in the [New Zealand House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_House_of_Representatives), and 53% in the German *[Bundestag](3768.md)*), the regionalisation of the list element, and the lack of compensation for [overhang seats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overhang_seat), over-representation produced by the FPTP.[^cite_note-richards-89] Consequently, the Senedd as a whole had a greater degree of proportionality (based on proportions in the list elections) than the plurality voting system used for British parliamentary elections, but still deviated somewhat from full proportionality.[^cite_note-richards-89]

With the [Senedd Cymru (members and elections) Act 2024](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senedd_Cymru_\(Members_and_Elections\)_Act_2024), the voting system was changed to a [closed list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_list) [proportional representation](23298.md) system using the [D’Hondt method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D'Hondt_method) with [6 seat constituencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elections_in_Wales), starting with the [2026 election](68245236.md).[^cite_note-90] Each Senedd constituency is made up of two neighboring constituencies for the House of Commons.

In April 2020 the Senedd became the first legislature in the UK to meet over the internet. Due to the consequences of the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_Wales), it held First Minister's Questions using [Zoom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoom_\(software\)) [videotelephony](2304367.md) software and the session was subsequently broadcast by Senedd.tv.[^cite_note-91]

## Composition

### Government formation

Plaid Cymru have 44 out of 96 seats, 5 seats short of a majority. On 12 May 2026 [Rhun ap Iorwerth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhun_ap_Iorwerth), the leader of Plaid Cymru was sworn in as First Minister,[^cite_note-92] on the 13th Plaid Cymru formed a [minority government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ap_Iorwerth_government).[^cite_note-\:0-93]

## Officials

### Elected officials

Main article: [Llywydd of the Senedd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llywydd_of_the_Senedd)



The Senedd's [ceremonial mace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceremonial_maces_in_the_British_Isles), *Byrllysg*, sits in front of the Presiding Officer's desk in the Senedd chamber.

After each election, the Senedd elects one Member of the Senedd to serve as [Llywydd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llywydd_of_the_Senedd) (Presiding Officer) of the Senedd, and another to serve as Deputy Presiding Officer (Dirprwy Lywydd). There is also provisions for an additional Dirprwy Lywydd in the standing orders. [Huw Irranca-Davies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huw_Irranca-Davies), [Welsh Labour](6732798.md) MS, has been Llywydd since 2026, having taken over from [Elin Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elin_Jones). The Llywydd also acts as Chair of the [Senedd Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senedd_Commission). Both the Llywydd and the Deputy Presiding Officer (Dirprwy Lywydd) typically don't vote in simple-majority votes, but can vote if there is a tie.&#x20;

### Permanent officials

The permanent administrative and support staff of the Senedd are employed by the Senedd Commission. They are not civil servants, although they enjoy similar terms and conditions of service to members of the [UK Civil Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Service_\(United_Kingdom\)). The most senior permanent official is the [chief executive and clerk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_Executive_and_Clerk_of_the_Senedd), and the position is currently held by [Manon Antoniazzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manon_Antoniazzi).

### Turnout

Voter turnout at Senedd elections has been historically been lower than UK general elections. The 2026 election turnout was for the first time in a Senedd election above 50%, reaching 51.6%, however that is still lower than recent [UK general elections in Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_Kingdom_general_election_in_Wales) (56% in 2024). In their 2004 paper *Turnout, Participation and Legitimacy in Post-Devolution Wales*, academics [Roger Awan-Scully](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Awan-Scully), [Richard Wyn Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Wyn_Jones) and [Dafydd Trystan Davies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dafydd_Trystan_Davies) identified three potential reasons for this: antipathy to the Welsh institutions, apathy to the Welsh institutions or apathy to politics more generally. They suggested apathy – in Wales and to politics in general – is the most likely reason.[^cite_note-103]

Following the 2021 election, Dr Jac Larner, a politics lecturer at [Cardiff University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_University) and an investigator for the Welsh election survey, said the lower turnout figures in Wales did not necessarily reflect a lack of perceived importance in the Senedd.[^cite_note-Grey-104] He told [BBC News](1139893.md): "We know from research that low voter turnout is actually a lot do to with people thinking they can't win in a devolved election, so they don't bother going to vote. That's different to a general election where, in Wales, Labour are still more likely to win a majority of seats, but at the UK level it's far more competitive."[^cite_note-Grey-104] He compared turnout in Wales to turnout for [Scottish Parliament](61188.md) elections, which is significantly higher: "Scotland is in quite a unique political position at the moment, where the single most salient issue and the biggest cleavage in society – the issue of [independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_independence) – basically is going to be determined by what happens at the Holyrood elections. Part of it is this idea of interest – there has always been more interest in the idea of a Scottish Parliament, the Scottish Parliament has always been more powerful than the Senedd, even going back to 1999."[^cite_note-Grey-104]

## Buildings



The [Senedd building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senedd_building)

### Senedd building

Main article: [Senedd building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senedd_building)

The [debating chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debating_chamber) in [Cardiff Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_Bay), the *[Senedd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senedd_building)* (*Senate*), was designed by the [Richard Rogers Partnership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Rogers_Partnership), and built by [Taylor Woodrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_Woodrow), with environmental, mechanical, electrical and plumbing design by BDSP Partnership. It uses traditional Welsh materials, such as slate and [Welsh oak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_oak_\(tree\)), in its construction, and the design is based around the concepts of openness and transparency. The timber ceiling and centre funnel, manufactured and installed by BCL Timber Projects (sub-contracted by Taylor Woodrow) is made from Canadian sourced Western Red Cedar.

The Senedd building



Logo of the Senedd Cymru – Welsh Parliament on the outside of the building

The Senedd houses the debating chamber known as the Siambr ([Welsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_language): *Y Siambr*)[^cite_note-105] and Committee Rooms. It was officially opened by Queen [Elizabeth II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_II) on [Saint David's Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_David's_Day), 1 March 2006.[^cite_note-opening-106]

The Senedd is designed to be environmentally friendly: it uses an Earth Heat Exchange system for heating; rainwater is collected from the roof and used for flushing toilets and cleaning windows, and the roof features a wind cowl which funnels natural light and air into the debating chamber below.[^cite_note-107]

### Telecasting&#x20;



Screenshot of the front page of **senedd.tv** in 2022

The building houses the debating chamber and committee rooms for the Senedd. When the Senedd building opened on 1 March 2006, there was regular screening of live proceedings from the Siambr on [S4C2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S4C2) and also on [internet television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_television).[^cite_note-Television-108] Coverage of the S4C2 screenings were on Tuesdays, Wednesdays and Thursdays between 9\:00 am 6\:00 pm when the Senedd was sitting.[^cite_note-Internet-109] In addition, limited screens were shown on the [BBC Two Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Two) programme *"am.pm"*, including [First Minister](602076.md)'s Questions.[^cite_note-BBC-110] These were decommissioned after S4C2 switched its scheduling to children programs and because of budget cuts.[^cite_note-S4CJan142-111] Internet television screenings are now shown on the Senedd's own website called Senedd.tv, which screens approximately 35 hours of content each week in [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language) and [Welsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_language). The service began 15 April 2008.[^cite_note-Assembly-112] Key events such as First Minister's Questions are shown live and recorded on [BBC Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Parliament) on television and on [iPlayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPlayer). Also on BBC Parliament some proceedings are shown as highlights of the week on the programme The Week in Parliament.

In October 2023, [GB News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GB_News) was banned from the Senedd's internal TV system, with a spokesperson for the presiding officer claiming the channel was "deliberately offensive, demeaning to public debate and contrary to our parliament's values".[^cite_note-113]

### Tŷ Hywel and Pierhead Building

Red brick modern six-floor building

Tŷ Hywel

Victorian red brick building with clock tower to the right

The Pierhead Building

Main articles: [Tŷ Hywel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C5%B7_Hywel) and [Pierhead Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierhead_Building)

Between 1999 and 2006, a temporary debating chamber was initially based in [Tŷ Hywel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C5%B7_Hywel), next to the site of the present building. The offices of Members are still in this building which is connected to the Senedd by a [skyway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skyway). Tŷ Hywel once again hosts plenary sessions of the Senedd between April 2025 and March 2026 whilst the debating chamber in the Senedd building undergoes refurbishment to increase its capacity in the lead up to the [next Senedd election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Next_Senedd_election) where the number of members returned is to rise to 96.

The [Senedd Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senedd_Commission) is also responsible for the [Pierhead Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierhead_Building), which is the location of "The Assembly at the Pierhead" exhibition, and is the Visitor and Education Centre for the Senedd as well as housing a small gift shop. The exhibition provides visitors with information on the history, functions and day-to-day work of the Senedd.

## Inclusivity review

In 2026, the Senedd’s Family Friendly and Inclusive Parliament Review Board, chaired by [Joyce Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joyce_Watson) MS, commissioned a review of inclusive policies at the Senedd.[^cite_note-115] The report (Parken, Hibbs, and [Minto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rachel_Minto)) found that there was a lack of representation of parents with young children, many members were juggling caring responsibilities, long working hours squeeze family time, and there was a lack of diversity in relation to socio-economic status, and ethnic minority, LGBTQ and disabled people.
