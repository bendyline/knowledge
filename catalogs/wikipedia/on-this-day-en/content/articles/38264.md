**Granada** ([/ɡrəˈnɑːdə/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [*grə-NAH-də*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key);[^cite_note-8] Spanish: [\[ɡɾaˈnaða\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Spanish) [//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/d/da/Pronunciation_of_Granada_in_Spanish.ogg/Pronunciation_of_Granada_in_Spanish.ogg.mp3](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/d/da/Pronunciation_of_Granada_in_Spanish.ogg/Pronunciation_of_Granada_in_Spanish.ogg.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup>) is the [capital city](181337.md) of the [province of Granada](143442.md), in the [autonomous community](55002.md) of [Andalusia](2736.md), [Spain](26667.md). Granada is located at the foot of the [Sierra Nevada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Nevada_\(Spain\)) mountains, at the confluence of four rivers, the [Darro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darro_\(river\)), the [Genil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genil), the [Monachil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monachil_\(river\)) and the Beiro. Ascribed to the [Vega de Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vega_de_Granada) *comarca*, the city sits at an average elevation of 738 m (2,421 ft) [above sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Above_mean_sea_level) 50 km from the [Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean) coast, the [Costa Tropical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Costa_Tropical). With a population of 233,532 as of 2024, it is the [20th-largest city in Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_municipalities_of_Spain#By_population) and is an important [wine growing region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granada_\(wine\)). It was the last Muslim stronghold in Islamic Spain.

Nearby is the [Sierra Nevada Ski Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Nevada_Ski_Station), where the [FIS Alpine World Ski Championships 1996](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FIS_Alpine_World_Ski_Championships_1996) were held. Its nearest airport is [Federico García Lorca Granada-Jaén Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federico_Garc%C3%ADa_Lorca_Airport).

The area was settled since ancient times by [Iberians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberians), [Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome), and [Visigoths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visigothic_Kingdom). The current settlement became a major city of [Al-Andalus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Andalus) in the 11th century during the [Zirid Taifa of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taifa_of_Granada).[^cite_note-\:05222-9] In the 13th century it became the capital of the [Emirate of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emirate_of_Granada) under [Nasrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasrid_dynasty) rule, the last [Muslim](6037917.md)-ruled state in the [Iberian Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_Peninsula). Granada was [conquered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granada_War) in 1492 by the [Catholic Monarchs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Monarchs_of_Spain) and progressively transformed into a [Christian](18337522.md) city over the course of the 16th century.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColeman2013-10]

The [Alhambra](30543.md), a medieval Nasrid citadel and palace, is located in Granada. It is one of the most famous monuments of [Islamic architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_architecture)[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBloom2020151-11] and one of the most visited tourist sites in Spain.[^cite_note-12][^cite_note-13] Islamic-period influence and [Moorish architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moorish_architecture) are also preserved in the [Albaicín](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albaic%C3%ADn) neighborhood and other medieval monuments in the city.[^cite_note-\:24-14] The 16th century also saw a flourishing of [Mudéjar architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mud%C3%A9jar_art) and [Renaissance architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_architecture),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColeman201370,_89-15] followed later by [Baroque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baroque_architecture) and [Churrigueresque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churrigueresque) styles.[^cite_note-16][^cite_note-17] The [University of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Granada) has an estimated 47,000 undergraduate students spread over five different campuses in the city. The [pomegranate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pomegranate) ([Spanish](26825.md): *granada*) is the [heraldic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraldry) device of Granada.

## Etymology

Granada's historical name in the [Arabic language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language) was غرناطة (*_Ġarnāṭa_*).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERingSalkinLa_Boda1995296-18][^cite_note-FOOTNOTERoom2006149-19][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDale1882-20][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEl_Hareir2011454-21] Both the name's meaning and origin are uncertain and have been debated.[^cite_note-\:5-22][^cite_note-\:13-23] The toponym definitely existed prior to the [Zirid period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taifa_of_Granada) in the 11th century.[^cite_note-\:13-23] It probably first appeared in the 9th century[^cite_note-\:5-22] and it is found in Arabic sources from the 10th century.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEViguera-Molíns202120-24]

The word *Gárnata* (or *Karnata*) possibly meant "hill of strangers".[^cite_note-25]<sup>: 41 </sup>[^cite_note-26] Another meaning might be "hill of pilgrims".[^cite_note-\:5-22] It could even have been of [Berber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berber_languages) origin.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEViguera-Molíns202120-24] Another theory is that it derived from [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) *granum* (or its plural *grana*), meaning "seed", "beads", or a "scarlet colour", which would have been adapted into Arabic as *_Ġarnāṭa_* or *_Iġranāṭa_*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEViguera-Molíns202120-24] The Arab chronicler [Al-Maqqari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Maqqari) believed that it came from the Latin word for [pomegranate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pomegranate), *granata*.[^cite_note-\:5-22] In either case, the Latin word may have been used not in its primary sense, but in the more derived sense of "red", referring to the colour of the area's soil and its buildings. This would also mirror the etymology of the name of the [Alhambra](30543.md).[^cite_note-\:13-23][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEViguera-Molíns202120-24]

## History

See also: [Granada chronology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granada_chronology)

### Pre-Umayyad history

See also: [Hispania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispania)



[Mosaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_mosaic) from a [Roman villa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_villa), dating from 1 AD, discovered in the district of Los Mondragones in Granada (now kept at the [Archaeological Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeological_Museum_of_Granada))

[^cite_note-\:5-22]

The region surrounding what today is Granada has been populated since at least 5500 BC.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERingSalkinLa_Boda1995296-18] Archeological artifacts found in the city indicate that the site of the city, including the area around the present-day avenue of *Gran Vía de Colón*, was inhabited since the [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age). The most ancient ruins found in the area belong to an [oppidum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oppidum) called *Ilturir*, founded by the [Iberian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberians) [Bastetani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bastetani) tribe around 650 BC.[^cite_note-\:5-22] The name Elibyrge is also attested in reference to this area.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERingSalkinLa_Boda1995296-18] This settlement became later known as *Iliberri* or *Iliberis*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERingSalkinLa_Boda1995296-18][^cite_note-FOOTNOTERodgersCavendish20216–10-27][^cite_note-\:5-22] In 44 BC *Iliberis* became a [Roman colony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonia_\(Roman\)) and in 27 BC it became a Roman *[municipium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Municipium)* named *Florentia Iliberritana* ('Flourishing Iliberri').[^cite_note-\:5-22][^cite_note-FOOTNOTERodgersCavendish20216–10-27]

The identification of present-day Granada with the Roman-era *Iliberis* and the historical continuity between the two settlements has long been debated by scholars.[^cite_note-\:0522-28][^cite_note-\:2-29][^cite_note-FOOTNOTERodgersCavendish20216–10-27] Modern archeological digs on the [Albaicín](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albaic%C3%ADn) hill have uncovered finds demonstrating the presence of a significant Roman town on that site.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERodgersCavendish20216–10-27] Little is known, however, about the history of the city in the period between the end of the [Roman era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispania) and the 11th century.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERodgersCavendish20216–10-27] An important Christian [synod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synod) circa 300 AD, the [Synod of Elvira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synod_of_Elvira), took place near this area (the name *Elvira* being derived from the name *Iliberri*), but there is no concrete archeological or documentary evidence establishing the exact location of the meeting. It may have taken place in the former Roman town or it may have taken place somewhere in the surrounding region, which was known as Elvira.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERodgersCavendish20216–10-27]

### Founding and early history

The [Umayyad conquest of Hispania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_conquest_of_Hispania), starting in 711 AD, brought large parts of the Iberian Peninsula under Moorish control and established [al-Andalus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Andalus). The earliest Arabic historical sources mention that a town named *Qashtīliya*, later known as *[Madīnat Ilbīra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madinat_Ilbira)* (Elvira), was located on the southern slopes of the Sierra de Elvira mountains (near present-day [Atarfe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atarfe)) and became the most important settlement in the area.[^cite_note-\:2-29][^cite_note-FOOTNOTERodgersCavendish20216–10-27] A smaller settlement and fortress (*ḥiṣn*) named *Ġarnāṭa* (also transliterated as *Gharnāṭa)* existed on the south side of the Darro River or on the site of the current Albaicín neighbourhood. The latter had a mainly [Jewish](25955086.md) population and thus was also known as *Gharnāṭat al-Yahūd* ("*Gharnāṭa* of the Jews").[^cite_note-\:05222-9][^cite_note-FOOTNOTERodgersCavendish20216–10-27] The district around the city was known as *[Kūrat Ilbīra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kura_\(al-Andalus\)#K%C5%ABra_of_Elvira)* (roughly "Province of Elvira"). After 743 the town of *Ilbīra* was settled by soldiers from the region of [Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria_\(region\)) who played a role in supporting [Abd al-Rahman I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_al-Rahman_I), the founder of the [Emirate of Córdoba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emirate_of_C%C3%B3rdoba) and a new [Umayyad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_dynasty) dynasty.[^cite_note-\:05222-9] In the late 9th century, during the reign of [Abdallah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdullah_ibn_Muhammad_al-Umawi) (r. 844–912), the city and its surrounding district were the site of conflict between *[muwallads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muwallad)* (Muslim converts) who were loyal to the central government and Arabs, led by Sawwār ibn Ḥamdūn, who resented them.[^cite_note-\:05222-9][^cite_note-30]

At the beginning of the 11th century, the area became dominated by the Zirids, a [Sanhaja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanhaja) [Berber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berbers) group and offshoot of the [Zirids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zirid_dynasty) who ruled parts of [North Africa](21714.md). This group became an important contingent in the army of [ʿAbd al-Malik al-Muẓaffar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_al-Malik_al-Muzaffar), the prime minister of Caliph [Hisham II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hisham_II) (r. 976–1009) and successor to [Ibn Abi ʿAmir al-Mansur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almanzor) (Almanzor) as *de facto* ruler of the [Caliphate of Córdoba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliphate_of_C%C3%B3rdoba). For their service, the Zirids were granted control of the province of Elvira.[^cite_note-\:05222-9] When the Caliphate collapsed after 1009 and the [Fitna (civil war)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitna_of_al-Andalus) began, the Zirid leader [Zawi ben Ziri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zawi_ben_Ziri) established an independent kingdom for himself, the [Taifa of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taifa_of_Granada). Arab sources such as [al-Idrisi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_al-Idrisi) consider him to be the founder of the city of Granada.[^cite_note-\:05222-9] His [great-grandnephew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdallah_ibn_Buluggin)'s surviving "memoirs," known as the *Tibyan* – the only ones for the Spanish "Middle Ages" – provide considerable detail for this brief period.[^cite_note-\:3-31] Because *Madīnat Ilbīra* was situated on a low plain and, as a result, difficult to protect from attacks, the ruler decided to transfer his residence to the higher situated area of *Ġarnāṭa*. According to Arabic sources *Ilbīra* was razed during the Fitna, afterwards it was not restored at its previous place and instead *Ġarnāṭa*, the former Jewish town, replaced it as the main city. In a short time this town was transformed into one of the most important cities of [al-Andalus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Andalus).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERingSalkinLa_Boda1995296-18][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDale1882-20] Until the 11th century it had a mixed population of Christians, Muslims, and Jews.[^cite_note-\:24-14]



Puerta Monaita, one of the 11th-century Zirid gates in the [Albaicin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albaic%C3%ADn)

The Zirids built their citadel and palace, known as the *al-Qaṣaba al-Qadīma* ("Old Citadel"), on the hill now occupied by the Albaicín neighborhood.[^cite_note-\:05222-9][^cite_note-\:24-14] It was connected to two smaller fortresses on the Sabika hill (site of the future Alhambra) and Mauror hill to the south.[^cite_note-\:24-14] The city around it grew during the 11th century to include the Albaicín, the Sabika, the Mauror, and a part of the surrounding plains. The city was fortified with walls encompassing an area of approximately 75 [hectares](24919513.md).[^cite_note-\:05222-9] The northern part of these walls, near the Albaicin citadel, have survived to the present day, along with two of its gates: *Bāb al-Unaydar* (now called *Puerta Monaita* in Spanish) and *Bāb al-Ziyāda* (now known as *Arco de las Pesas* or *Puerta Nueva*).[^cite_note-\:24-14][^cite_note-\:05222-9] The city and its residences were supplied with water through an extensive network of underground [cisterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cistern) and pipes.[^cite_note-\:05222-9][^cite_note-\:052232-32] On the Darro River, along the wall connecting the Zirid citadel with the Sabika hill, was a [sluice gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sluice_gate) called *Bāb al-Difāf* ("Gate of the Tambourines"), which could be closed or opened to control the flow of the river and retain water if necessary.[^cite_note-34][^cite_note-FOOTNOTERėklaitytė2021443–445-33][^cite_note-FOOTNOTERodgersCavendish202121-35] The nearby [Bañuelo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Ba%C3%B1uelo), a former [hammam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammam) (bathhouse), also likely dates from this time, as does the former [minaret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minaret) of a [mosque](19894.md) in the Albaicín, now part of the Church of San José.[^cite_note-\:24-14]

Under the Zirid kings [Habbus ibn Maksan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habbus_al-Muzaffar) and [Badis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Badis_ibn_Habus), the most powerful figure was the Jewish administrator known as [Samuel ha-Nagid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_ibn_Naghrillah) (in [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language)) or Isma'il ibn Nagrilla (in Arabic). Samuel was a highly educated member of the former elites of Cordoba, who fled that city after the outbreak of the Fitna. He eventually found his way to Granada, where Habbus ibn Maksan appointed him his secretary in 1020 and entrusted him with many important responsibilities, including tax collection. Under Badis, he even took charge of the army.[^cite_note-\:4-36] During this period, the Muslim king was looked upon as a mainly symbolic figurehead. Granada was the centre of Jewish Sephardi culture and scholarship. According to Daniel Eisenberg:

> Granada was in the eleventh century the center of Sephardic civilization at its peak, and from 1027 until 1066 Granada was a powerful Jewish state. Jews did not hold the foreigner ([dhimmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhimmi)) status typical of Islamic rule. Samuel ibn Nagrilla, recognized by Sephardic Jews everywhere as the quasi-political ha-Nagid ('The Prince'), was king in all but name. As vizier he made policy and—much more unusual—led the army. \[...] It is said that Samuel's strengthening and fortification of Granada was what permitted it, later, to survive as the last Islamic state in the Iberian peninsula. All of the greatest figures of eleventh-century Hispano-Jewish culture are associated with Granada. [Moses Ibn Ezra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses_Ibn_Ezra) was from Granada; on his invitation [Judah ha-Levi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judah_ha-Levi) spent several years there as his guest. [Ibn Gabirol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Gabirol)’s patrons and hosts were the Jewish [viziers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vizier) of Granada, Samuel ha-Nagid and his son Joseph.[^cite_note-37]

After Samuel's death, his son Joseph took over after his position but proved to lack his father's diplomacy, bringing on the [1066 Granada massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1066_Granada_massacre),[^cite_note-\:4-36] which ended the [Golden age of Jewish culture in Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_age_of_Jewish_culture_in_Spain).

From the late 11th century to the early 13th century, Al-Andalus was dominated by two successive North African Berber empires. The [Almoravids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almoravids) ruled Granada from 1090 and the [Almohads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almohad_dynasty) from 1166.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHourihane2012-38] Granada also served as an administrative capital of Almoravid rule in al-Andalus.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKennedy1996172-39] Evidence from the artistic and archeological remains of this period suggest that the city thrived under the Almoravids but declined under the Almohads.[^cite_note-\:24-14] Remnants of the Almohad period in the city include the [Alcázar Genil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alc%C3%A1zar_Genil), built in 1218–1219 (but later redecorated under the Nasrids),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEArnold2017240–241-40] and possibly the former minaret attached to the present-day Church of San Juan de los Reyes in the Albaicin.[^cite_note-41][^cite_note-43]

### Nasrid Emirate of Granada

Main article: [Emirate of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emirate_of_Granada)



Sigil of the [Nasrid dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasrid_dynasty) located in the Palacio de Comares

In 1228 [Idris al-Ma'mun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idris_al-Ma'mun), the last effective [Almohad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almohad_Caliphate) ruler in al-Andalus, left the Iberian Peninsula. As Almohad rule collapsed local leaders and factions emerged across the region. With the *[Reconquista](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconquista)* in full swing, the Christian kingdoms of [Castile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Castile) and [Aragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Aragon) – under kings [Ferdinand III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_III_of_Castile) and [James I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_I_of_Aragon), respectively – made major conquests across al-Andalus. Castile captured [Cordoba in 1236](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_C%C3%B3rdoba_\(1236\)) and [Seville in 1248](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Seville). Meanwhile, the ambitious [Ibn al-Ahmar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_I_of_Granada) (Muhammad I) established what became the last and longest reigning [Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim) dynasty in the Iberian peninsula, the [Nasrids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasrid_dynasty), who ruled the [Emirate of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emirate_of_Granada). On multiple occasions Ibn al-Ahmar aligned himself with Ferdinand III, eventually agreeing to become his vassal in 1246.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKennedy1996276-44] Granada thereafter became a [tributary state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tributary_state) to the Kingdom of Castile, although this was often interrupted by wars between the two states.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKennedy1996-45][^cite_note-\:05222-9] The political history of the emirate was turbulent and intertwined with that of its neighbours. The Nasrids sometimes provided refuge or military aid to Castilian kings and noblemen, even against other Muslim states, while in turn the Castilians provided refuge and aid to some Nasrid emirs against other Nasrid rivals. On other occasions the Nasrids attempted to leverage the aid of the North African [Marinids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marinid_Sultanate) to ward off Castile, although Marinid interventions in the Peninsula ended after [Battle of Rio Salado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_R%C3%ADo_Salado) (1340).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKennedy1996-45][^cite_note-\:8-46][^cite_note-\:05222-9]

The population of the emirate was also swollen by Muslim refugees from the territories newly conquered by Castile and Aragon, resulting in a small yet densely populated territory which was more uniformly Muslim and Arabic-speaking than before.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKennedy1996277-47][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarvey199014–15-48] The city itself expanded and new neighbourhoods grew around the Albaicín (named after refugees from [Baeza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baeza,_Spain)) and in Antequeruela (named after refugees from [Antequera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antequera) after 1410).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKennedy1996277-47] A new set of walls was constructed further north during the 13th–14th centuries, with *Bab Ilbirah* (present-day *Puerta de Elvira*) as its western entrance.[^cite_note-\:05222-9][^cite_note-\:24-14] A major Muslim cemetery existed outside this gate.[^cite_note-49] The city's heart was its Great Mosque (on the site of the present-day [Granada Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granada_Cathedral)) and the commercial district known as the *qaysariyya* (the [Alcaicería](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcaicer%C3%ADa_of_Granada)).[^cite_note-\:05222-9][^cite_note-\:24-14] Next to this was the only major [madrasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madrasa) built in al-Andalus, the *Madrasa al-Yusufiyya* (known today as the [Palacio de la Madraza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madrasa_of_Granada)), founded in 1349.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBloom2020151–173-50][^cite_note-\:32-51] Other monuments from this era include the *al-Funduq al-Jadida* ("New Inn" or [caravanserai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caravanserai), now known as the [Corral del Carbón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corral_del_Carb%C3%B3n)), built in the early 14th century,[^cite_note-Capt2-52] the [Maristan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maristan_of_Granada) (hospital), built in 1365–1367 and demolished in 1843,[^cite_note-\:9-53] and the main mosque of the Albaicín, dating from the 13th century.[^cite_note-55]

&#x20;When Ibn Al-Ahmar established himself in the city he moved the royal palace from the old Zirid citadel on the Albaicín hill to the Sabika hill, beginning construction on what became the present Alhambra.[^cite_note-\:05222-9][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBloom2020151-11] The Alhambra acted as a self-contained palace-city, with its own mosque, hammams, fortress, and residential quarters for workers and servants. The most celebrated palaces that survive today, such as the [Comares Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_the_Myrtles) and the [Palace of the Lions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_the_Lions), generally date from the reigns of Yusuf I (r. 1333–1354) and his son Muhammad V (r. 1354–1391, with interruptions).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBloom2020151–173-50] Some smaller examples of Nasrid palace architecture in the city have survived in the [Cuarto Real de Santo Domingo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuarto_Real_de_Santo_Domingo) (late 13th century) and the [Dar al-Horra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dar_al-Horra) (15th century).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEArnold2017242–244,_290-56]



Map showing the Emirate of Granada by Ottoman cartographer [Piri Reis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piri_Reis)

Partly due to the heavy tributary payments to Castile, Granada's economy specialized in the trade of high-value goods.[^cite_note-\:05222-9] Integrated within the European mercantile network, the ports of the kingdom fostered intense trading relations with the [Genoese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Genoa), but also with the Catalans, and to a lesser extent, with the Venetians, the Florentines, and the Portuguese.[^cite_note-57] It provided connections with Muslim and Arab trade centres, particularly for [gold](12240.md) from [sub-Saharan Africa](27067.md) and the [Maghreb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maghreb), and exported [silk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silk) and [dried fruits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dried_fruit) produced in the area.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKennedy1996277-47]

Despite its frontier position, Granada was also an important Islamic intellectual and cultural centre, especially in the time of Muhammad V, with figures such as [Ibn Khaldun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Khaldun) and [Ibn al-Khatib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_al-Khatib) serving in the Nasrid court.[^cite_note-\:1-42][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKennedy1996291-58] [Ibn Battuta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Battuta), a famous traveller and historian, visited the Emirate of Granada in 1350. He described it as a powerful and self-sufficient kingdom in its own right, although frequently embroiled in skirmishes with the Kingdom of Castile. In his journal, Ibn Battuta called Granada the "metropolis of Andalusia and the bride of its cities."[^cite_note-59]

### End of Muslim rule and 16th-century changes



*[The Surrender of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Surrender_of_Granada)* by F. Padilla: [Muhammad XII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_XI_of_Granada) before [Ferdinand II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_II_of_Aragon) and [Isabella I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isabella_I_of_Castile) (circa 1882)

On 2 January 1492, the last Muslim ruler in Iberia, Emir [Muhammad XI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_XI_of_Granada), known as "Boabdil" to the Spanish, surrendered complete control of the [Emirate of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emirate_of_Granada) to the [Catholic Monarchs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Monarchs) ([Ferdinand II of Aragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_II_of_Aragon) and [Isabella I of Castile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isabella_I_of_Castile)), after the last episode of the [Granada War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granada_War).

The 1492 capitulation of the Kingdom of Granada to the Catholic Monarchs is one of the most significant events in Granada's history. It brought the demise of the last Muslim-controlled polity in the Iberian Peninsula. The terms of the surrender, outlined in the [Treaty of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Granada_\(1491\)) at the end of 1491, explicitly allowed the Muslim inhabitants, known as *[mudéjares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mud%C3%A9jar)*, to continue unmolested in the practice of their faith and customs. This had been a traditional practice during Castilian (and [Aragonese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_of_Aragon)) conquests of Muslim cities since the takeover of [Toledo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toledo,_Spain) in the 11th century.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColeman201336-60] The terms of the surrender pressured Jewish inhabitants (then numbering 110 households[^cite_note-61]) to convert or leave within three years,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarvey1990321-62] but this provision was quickly superseded by the [Alhambra Decree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alhambra_Decree), issued only a few months later on 31 March, which instead forced all Jews in Spain to convert or be expelled within four months.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColeman201338-63][^cite_note-64] Those who converted became known as *[conversos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Converso)* (converts). This move, along with the progressive erosion of other guarantees provided by the surrender treaty, raised tensions and fears within the remaining Muslim community during the 1490s.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColeman201337–38-65] Many of the city's affluent Muslims and its traditional ruling classes emigrated to North Africa in the early years after the conquest, but these early emigrants numbered only a few thousand, with the rest of the population unable to afford leaving.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColeman201338-63][^cite_note-66]

By 1499, Cardinal [Francisco Jiménez de Cisneros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_Jim%C3%A9nez_de_Cisneros) grew frustrated with the slow pace of the efforts of the first [archbishop of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishopric_of_Granada), [Hernando de Talavera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hernando_de_Talavera), to convert non-Christians and undertook a program of forced [baptisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptism), creating the *converso* class for Muslims and Jews. Cisneros's new strategy, which was a direct violation of the terms of the treaty, provoked the [Rebellion of the Alpujarras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebellion_of_the_Alpujarras_\(1499%E2%80%931501\)) (1499–1501) centred in the rural [Alpujarras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpujarras) region southeast of the city. The rebellion lasted until 1500 in Granada and continued until 1501 in the Alpujarras.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColeman201343-67] Responding to the rebellion of 1501, the [Crown of Castile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_of_Castile) rescinded the Treaty of Granada,[^cite_note-68] and mandated that Granada's Muslims convert or emigrate. Many of the remaining Muslim elites subsequently emigrated to North Africa. The majority of the Granada's *mudéjares* converted (becoming the so-called *[moriscos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morisco)* or Moorish) so that they could stay. Both populations of converts were subject to persecution, execution, or exile, and each had cells that practiced their original religion in secrecy (the so-called *[marranos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marranos)* in the case of the *conversos* accused of the charge of [crypto-Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crypto-Judaism)).



16th-century view of the city, as depicted in [Georg Braun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Braun)'s *Civitates orbis terrarum*

Over the course of the 16th century, Granada took on an ever more Catholic and Castilian character, as immigrants arrived from other regions of Castile, lured by the promise of economic opportunities in the newly conquered city.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColeman201315-69] At the time of the city's surrender in 1492 it had a population of 50,000 which included only a handful of Christians (mostly captives), but by 1561 (the year of the first royal [census](6889.md) of the city) the population was composed of over 30,000 Christian immigrants and approximately 15,000 *moriscos*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColeman201315-69] After 1492 the city's first churches had been installed in some converted mosques.[^cite_note-70] The vast majority of the city's remaining mosques were subsequently converted into churches during and after the mass conversions of 1500.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColeman201396–97-71] In 1531, [Charles V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_V,_Holy_Roman_Emperor) founded the [University of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Granada) on the site of the former madrasa built by Yusuf I.[^cite_note-\:5-22]

Granada's Town Council did not fully establish until almost nine years after the Castilian conquest,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPeinado_Santaella2013213-72] upon the concession of the so-called 'Constitutive Charter' of the Ayuntamiento of Granada on 23 September 1500.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPeinado_Santaella2013221-73] From then on, the municipal institution became a crucible for the "[Old Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Christian)" and the converted *morisco* elites, resulting in strong factionalism, particularly after 1508.[^cite_note-74] The new period also saw the creation of a number of other new institutions such as the Cathedral Cabildo, the [Captaincy–General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Captaincy%E2%80%93General_of_Granada?action=edit\&redlink=1), the Royal Chapel and the [Royal Chancellery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Chancellery_of_Granada).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPeinado_Santaella2013214-75] For the rest of the 16th century the Granadan ruling oligarchy featured roughly a 40% of (Jewish) *conversos* and about a 31% of *[hidalgos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hidalgo_\(nobility\))*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPeinado_Santaella2013215-76] From the 1520s onward, the mosque structures themselves began to be replaced with new church buildings, a process which continued for most of the century.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColeman201396–97-71] In December 1568, during a period of renewed persecution against *moriscos*, the [Second Morisco Rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebellion_of_the_Alpujarras_\(1568%E2%80%931571\)) broke out in the Alpujarras.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColeman20137–8-77] Although the city's *morisco* population played little role in the rebellion, King [Philip II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_II_of_Spain) ordered the expulsion of the vast majority of the *morisco* population from the [Kingdom of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Granada_\(Crown_of_Castile\)), with the exception of those artisans and professionals judged essential to the economy. The expelled population was redistributed to other cities throughout the Crown of Castile. The [final expulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expulsion_of_the_Moriscos) of all *moriscos* from Castile and Aragon was carried out between 1609 and 1614.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColeman20137–8-77]

### Later history and present day

| --- | --- |



Early 17th-century map of Granada

During the 17th century, despite the importance of immigration,[^cite_note-78] the population of the city stagnated at about 55,000, contrary to the trend of population increase experienced in the rural areas of the [Kingdom of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Granada_\(Crown_of_Castile\)),[^cite_note-79] where the hammer of depopulation caused by the expulsion of the *moriscos* had taken a far greater toll in the previous century. The 17th-century demographic stagnation in the city and overall steady population increase in the wider kingdom went in line with the demographic disaster experienced throughout the century in the rest of the Crown of Castile.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGarrido_García200660–61-80] The city was overshadowed in importance by other cities including [Seville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seville) and the capital, [Madrid](41188263.md).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERingSalkinLa_Boda1995298-81]

Between 1810 and 1812 Granada was occupied by [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon)'s army during the [Peninsular War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peninsular_War).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERingSalkinLa_Boda1995298-81] The French troops occupied the Alhambra as a fortified position and caused significant damage to the monument. Upon evacuating the city, they attempted to dynamite the whole complex, successfully blowing up eight towers before the remaining fuses were disabled by Spanish soldier José Garcia, thus saving what remains today.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELópez2011301-82] In 1830 [Washington Irving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_Irving) lived in Granada and wrote his *[Tales of the Alhambra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tales_of_the_Alhambra)*, which revived some international interest in southern Spain and in its Islamic-era monuments.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERingSalkinLa_Boda1995299-83]

In the 1930s the tensions that eventually divided Spain were evident in Granada, with frequent riots and friction between landowners and peasants. When the [Spanish Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Civil_War) broke out in 1936, Granada was one of the cities that joined the [Nationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalist_faction_\(Spanish_Civil_War\)) uprising.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERingSalkinLa_Boda1995298–299-84][^cite_note-85] There was local resistance against the Nationalists, particularly from the working classes in the Albaicín, which was violently repressed.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERingSalkinLa_Boda1995299-83] During the 1950s and 1960s, under the [Franco regime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francoist_Spain), the province of Granada was one of the poorest areas in Spain.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERingSalkinLa_Boda1995299-83] In recent decades tourism has become a major industry in the city.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERingSalkinLa_Boda1995299-83]

## Geography

### Location



A satellite view centred on Granada displaying [Sierra Nevada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Nevada_\(Spain\)) to the east and the rest of the [Vega of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vega_of_Granada), including a number of the municipalities part of the urban area of Granada to the west

The city of Granada sits on the [Vega de Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vega_de_Granada), one of the depressions or plains located within the [Baetic mountain ranges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baetic_System), continued to the west by those of Archidona and [Antequera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoya_of_Antequera) and to the East by those of [Guadix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoya_of_Guadix), [Baza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoya_of_Baza) and Huéscar.[^cite_note-86]

The fertile soil of the Vega, apt for agriculture, is irrigated by the water streams originated in [Sierra Nevada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Nevada_\(Spain\)), as well as minor secondary ranges, all drained by the catchment basin of the [Genil River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genil_River),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMenor_Toribio1997192-87] that cuts across the city centre following an east to west direction. The [Monachil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monachil_\(river\)), a left-bank tributary of the former, also passes through the city, discharging into the Genil to the west of the city centre.

### Climate

Granada has a [hot-summer Mediterranean climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hot-summer_Mediterranean_climate) (*Csa*) close to a cold [semi-arid climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-arid_climate) (*BSk*). Summers are hot and dry with daily temperatures averaging 34 °C (93 °F) in the hottest month (July); however, temperatures reaching over 40 °C (104 °F) are not uncommon in the summer months. Winters are cool and damp, with most of the rainfall concentrated from November through to January. The coldest month is January with daytime temperatures hovering at 13 °C (55 °F) and dropping to around 1 °C (34 °F) during the night. Frost is common as temperatures usually reach below-freezing in the early morning. Snow is rare and occurs once every few years. Spring and autumn are unpredictable, with temperatures ranging from mild to warm. Early summer in 2017 confronted the city with two massive heat waves that broke long-standing record temperatures starting on 13 June 2017, with a new maximum high for the month at 40.6 °C (old record 40.0), which was topped three times within the span of four days at 40.9 °C on 14 June, 41.3 (15 June) and, eventually, 41.5 (17 June). The second extreme surge in temperatures followed roughly a month later when readings soared to 45.7 and 45.3 °C on 12 and 13 July, respectively, surpassing the old July record by almost 3 degrees.

### Metropolitan area of Granada

Granada's metropolitan area consists of about fifty municipalities and the capital. Although it is not formally constituted as a political and administrative body, there are several public services that are combined. The arrival of many inhabitants of the capital and other towns in the province, influences a large [population growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_growth). Despite the fact that the capital loses inhabitants, who move to neighboring towns. The main causes of the exodus towards the towns of the metropolitan area are, mainly, the difficulty of accessing a home in the capital because of the high prices it has and labor reasons, because in the towns of the periphery the majority are being located of industrial estates.

### Capital of Granada Province

The city of Granada is the capital of the province of the same name, thus all administrative entities of provincial scope dependent on the regional government and the state are located there. There is a provincial delegation from each of the governmental departments to the [Junta de Andalucía](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_Government_of_Andalusia), coordinated by a government delegate under the Ministry of the Interior. [The national government of Spain](1306113.md) has a [sub-delegation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Delegation_\(Spain\)) in Granada, subordinate to the government delegate in the autonomous community.

### Judicial administration

The headquarters of the Superior Court of Justice of Andalusia, [Ceuta](6443.md) and [Melilla](20226.md) is in Granada, located in Plaza Nueva, in the building of the historic [Royal Chancery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Court_of_Justice_of_Andalusia), as well as the Superior Prosecutor's Office of Andalusia, located in the building of the Bank of Spain. It has a Provincial Court, located in Corteza del Carmen Street, and is also head of the Judicial Party No. 3 of the province, whose demarcation includes the city and 49 towns, some of them very populated, in the metropolitan area region.

Most of the courts are located in two administrative buildings, in Plaza Nueva and Avenida del Sur. The set of judicial bodies is as follows:

- Superior Court of Justice: President. Civil-Criminal Chamber. Contentious-Administrative Room. Social room.

- Provincial Court: President. Criminal: 2; Civil: 3.

### Municipal organization

Main article: [Ayuntamiento de Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayuntamiento_de_Granada)



[Marifrán Carazo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marifr%C3%A1n_Carazo), mayor of Granada since 2023

Its political administration is carried out through a City Council, of democratic management, whose components are chosen every four years by universal suffrage. The electoral roll is composed of all residents registered in Granada over 18 years of age, of [Spanish](26667.md) nationality and of the other member countries of the European Union. According to the provisions of the General Electoral Regime Law, which establishes the number of eligible councilors according to the population of the municipality, the Municipal Corporation of Granada is made up of 27 councilors.

In the municipal elections held in 2019 the constitution of the City Council was eleven councilors belonging to the [Popular Party](404727.md), eight to the Socialist Party, four belonging to Citizens Party, three to Vamos, Granada and one to the United Left-Socialist Alternative-For the People. [Luis Salvador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luis_Salvador_\(politician\)), leader of [Citizens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizens_\(Spanish_political_party\)), became mayor with support from the PP and Vox.[^cite_note-97] Salvador resigned in 2021, having lost the support of the PP.[^cite_note-pais-98] He was succeeded by the Socialist [Francisco Cuenca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_Cuenca) who had previously served as mayor from 2016 to 2019.[^cite_note-EP-99] In the [2023 Spanish local elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_Spanish_local_elections) Cuenca was ousted by [Marifrán Carazo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marifr%C3%A1n_Carazo) of the PP. Carazo increased the PP's number of seats from 7 to 14, gaining her a slim majority and making her the first female mayor of the city.[^cite_note-100]

### Realejo

Realejo has been described as a Jewish district in the time of the Nasrid Granada.[^cite_note-101][^cite_note-102] Today, the district is made up of many villas, with gardens opening onto the streets, called *Los Cármenes*.

### Cartuja

This district contains the Carthusian monastery of the same name: Cartuja. This is an old monastery started in a late Gothic style with Baroque exuberant interior decorations. In this district also, many buildings were created with the extension of the [University of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Granada).

### Bib-Rambla

The toponym derives from a gate (Bab al-Ramla, or the [Gate of the Ears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gate_of_the_Ears)) that was built when Granada was controlled by the [Nasrid dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasrid_dynasty). Nowadays, Bib-Rambla is a high point for gastronomy, especially in its terraces of restaurants, open on beautiful days. The Arab [bazaar](556904.md), the *[Alcaicería](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcaicer%C3%ADa_of_Granada)*, is made up of several narrow streets, which start from this place and continue as far as the cathedral.

### Sacromonte

Main article: [Sacromonte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacromonte)

The Sacromonte neighborhood is located on the extension of the hill of Albaicín, along the Darro River. This area, which became famous by the nineteenth century for its predominantly [Gitano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gitanos) inhabitants, is characterized by cave houses, which are dug into the hillside. The area has a reputation as a major centre of flamenco song and dance, including the [*Zambra Gitana*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zambra_\(dance\)), an Andalusian dance originating in the Middle East. The zone is a protected cultural environment under the auspices of the *Centro de Interpretación del Sacromonte*, a cultural centre dedicated to the preservation of Gitano cultural forms.

### Zaidín

Main article: [Zaidín (Granada)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaid%C3%ADn_\(Granada\))

This formerly blue collar but now upmarket neighborhood houses 100,000 residents of Granada, making it the largest neighborhood or 'barrio'. Traditionally populated by [Romani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gitanos) people, now many residents are from North and West Africa, China, and many South American countries. Every Saturday morning it hosts a large outdoor market or "mercadillo", where many people come and sell their wares of fruits and vegetables, clothes and shoes, and other bits and bobs.

## Economy

Granada produces agricultural products such as barley, wheat, sugarcane and tobacco. Besides these crops, olive trees as well as oranges, lemons, figs, almonds, and pomegranates are also commonly grown. For manufacturing, sugar refining is also made in the city.[^cite_note-103] Vineyards and wines are also a significant contributor to the city and region.[^cite_note-104] In 2020, Granada exported a total of \$1.3 billion products.[^cite_note-105]

### Education

#### University

The [University of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Granada) (UGR), founded in 1531 by the Holy Roman Emperor [Charles V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_V,_Holy_Roman_Emperor), meant the continuation of higher studies in La Madraza, when the city was the capital of the last [Nasrid Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasrid_Kingdom_of_Granada). The university has become internationally recognised in many university fields: teaching, research, cultural and services to its members and its surroundings. It is therefore one of the destinations that receives more exchange students from the Erasmus Program13 and the fourth Spanish university in number of students, after the Complutense University of Madrid, and the [University of Seville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Seville). The university has around 47,000 undergraduate students.[^cite_note-106]

#### Obligatory education

In the city there are a total of 69 compulsory secondary education centres. [Infant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_childhood_education) and [primary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_education) [education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education) is taught in 104 centres, distributed among private, concerted and public centres. There are also five adult education centres.

### Health

Granada schools public health system is the exclusive competence of the autonomous community, which provides two types of attention: primary, which constitutes the first level of access to the system; and the specialized one. Health centres and offices constitute the offer of primary care, differing from each other in the level of care they provide.

#### Hospital network

Its hospital network is basically composed of public hospitals managed by the [Andalusian Health Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andalusian_Health_Service) and other smaller private management centres. This network covers the needs of the city and its Metropolitan Area. In total there are 2047 beds.

- The Hospital of the Health Campus, was inaugurated completely in July 2016 and is the second largest in Spain – after La Paz Hospital – and is the reference centre in the city for the areas of Allergology, Pathological Anatomy, Hematology, Internal Medicine, [Mental Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_health), Pulmonology, Radiotherapy or Urology, among others; Orthopedic and Traumatological Surgery; Rehabilitation and Physical Medicine; [Neurology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurology), Neurosurgery, Neurophysiology, Maxillofacial Surgery, [Plastic Surgery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_surgery), Dermatology, Ophthalmology, Otorhino, Rheumatology, Endocrinology and Vascular Surgery.

- The Virgen de las Nieves Regional Hospital (popularly known as Ruiz de Alda Hospital), is formed by a general hospital, a maternal and child hospital, all located in the same enclosure, and is maintained as a nerve centre of Digestive, Respiratory, Oncology, Hematology and Transplants. It includes the San Juan de Dios Hospital, a peripheral centre of specialties and a therapeutic unit for the mental illnesses.

- The Hospital of San Rafael, has a hospitalization capacity of 190 beds and as a day hospital has 45 places of geriatrics and 15 of Alzheimer's. It is owned by the Hospital Order of [San Juan de Dios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Juan_de_Dios_Hospital_\(Granada\)). It is especially dedicated to the comprehensive care of the elderly, possessing a large number of analysis and rehabilitation services. The hospital activity is arranged with the Ministry of Health of the Junta de Andalucía.

- La Inmaculada Clinic is a private hospital belonging to the ASISA insurance company born as a concerted hospital of the Spanish Health System in 1975. Through an agreement with the University of Granada, the centre also participates in the training of students of the Faculty of Medicine.

- Nuestra Señora de la Salud Clinic is a private hospital that has 71 rooms and belongs to the medical insurance company Adeslas. Serves insured private medical companies. It currently has a new location with a newly built site. It is very close to the Alhambra, near the Serrallo Tunnels, being its previous location on the Avenue of the Armed Forces (Granada).

#### Health centres

The local primary care network currently consists of eight health centres distributed by the different districts of the city and are the following: Zaidín Sur, Zaidín Centro-Este, Realejo, Las Flores, La Caleta, Góngora, Doctores, Cartuja and Albaycín.

#### Municipal competitions

Article 42 of the General Health Law provides that municipalities, without prejudice to the powers of other public administrations, will have the following minimum responsibilities in matters related to health.

- Sanitary control of the environment: air pollution, water supply, sewage sanitation and urban and industrial waste.

- Sanitary control of industries, activities and services, transport, noise and vibration.

- Sanitary control of buildings and places of housing and human coexistence, especially of food centres, hairdressers, saunas and centres of personal hygiene, hotels and residential centres, schools, tourist camps and areas of physical activity sports and recreation.

- Sanitary control of the distribution and supply of perishable food, beverages and other products, directly or indirectly related to human use or consumption, as well as the means of transport.

- Sanitary control of cemeteries and mortuary health police.

#### Citizen security

The coordination between the state security forces ([National Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Police_Corps), [Civil Guard](325008.md), [Local Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Municipal_police) and Autonomous Police) and the City of Granada is carried out by the local Citizen Security Board. This body allows for proper communication of the security forces in matters of security within its competence, the prevention of crime, road safety and proper procedure.

The daily citizen security is in charge of the state and local security forces and bodies (Spain) according to the powers that each estate has, trying to act in a coordinated and collaborative way in the prosecution and resolution of all types of crimes.

#### Social services

The City of Granada has the Social Services Area to provide the necessary help and advice that the most disadvantaged and needy groups and people may need. For this reason, the Social Welfare Delegation of the City of Granada has several municipal community social services centres, one for each local district, coordinated by Los Mondragones Municipal Administration located on the Fuerzas Armadas Avenue. In its internal organization the services provided are organized around each municipal centre and therefore of each municipal district but also around groups.

### Taxi

### Airport

The nearest civil airport is [Federico García Lorca Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federico_Garc%C3%ADa_Lorca_Airport), about 15 km (9 mi) west of Granada. The [Armilla Air Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armilla_Air_Base?action=edit\&redlink=1) was the first civil airport serving the city and its surroundings but it was replaced by the former airport in the 1970s and repurposed for military uses.

### Granada Public Transportation statistics

The average amount of time people spend commuting with public transit in Granada, for example to and from work, on a weekday is 42 min. 9% of public transit riders, ride for more than 2 hours every day. The average amount of time people wait at a stop or station for public transit is 10 min, while 8% of riders wait for over 20 minutes on average every day. The average distance people usually ride in a single trip with public transit is 2.7 km, while 0% travel for over 12 km in a single direction.[^cite_note-109]

#### Alhambra

Main article: [Alhambra](30543.md)



*[Muqarnas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muqarnas)* ceiling in the Palace of the Lions, one of the Nasrid palaces

The Alhambra is a fortified palace complex located on the Sabika hill, an outcrop of the Sierra Nevada which overlooks the city of Granada. It is one of the most famous monuments of [Islamic architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_architecture) and one of the best-preserved palaces of the historic [Islamic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_world), in addition to containing notable examples of Spanish [Renaissance architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_architecture).[^cite_note-UNESCO-110][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBloom2020151-11][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEArnold2017234-111] It is one of Spain's major tourist attractions and a [UNESCO](21786641.md) [World Heritage Site](44940.md) since 1984.[^cite_note-UNESCO-110]

The complex was begun in 1238 by Muhammad I Ibn al-Ahmar, the first Nasrid emir, on the site of earlier fortresses and of the 11th-century palace of vizier Samuel ibn Naghrillah.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEArnold2017234-111][^cite_note-\:05222-9][^cite_note-\:24-14] Later Nasrid rulers continuously modified the site, most notably during the reigns of Yusuf I and Muhammad V in the 14th century.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBloom2020152-112][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEArnold2017234–237-113] During this era, the Alhambra was a self-contained city separate from the rest of Granada below.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBloom2020152-112] It contained most of the amenities of a Muslim city such as a [Friday mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congregational_mosque), [hammams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammam) (public baths), roads, houses, artisan workshops, a [tannery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanning_\(leather\)), and a sophisticated water supply system.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELópez2011201–215-114][^cite_note-\:05223-115] As a royal city and citadel, it contained at least six major palaces, most of them located along the northern edge where they commanded views over the [Albaicín](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albaic%C3%ADn) quarter.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBloom2020152-112] The most famous and best-preserved are the [Mexuar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexuar), the Comares Palace, the Palace of the Lions, and the [Partal Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partal_Palace), which form the main attraction to visitors today. The other palaces are known from historical sources and from modern excavations.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELópez2011-116][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEArnold2017-117] At the Alhambra's western tip is the [Alcazaba fortress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcazaba_of_the_Alhambra), the centrepiece of its defensive system. The architecture of the Nasrid palaces reflects the tradition of [Moorish architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moorish_architecture) developed over previous centuries.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBloom2020-118][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEArnold2017-117] Decoration is focused on the inside of the building and was executed primarily with [tile mosaics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zellij) on lower walls and carved [stucco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stucco) on the upper walls. [Geometric patterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_geometric_patterns), [vegetal motifs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabesque), and [Arabic calligraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_calligraphy) were the main types of decorative motifs. Additionally, "stalactite"-like sculpting, known as *[muqarnas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muqarnas)*, was used for three-dimensional features like [vaulted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vault_\(architecture\)) ceilings.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELópez2011-116][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBloom2020164-119]

After the conclusion of the *Reconquista* in 1492, the site became the Royal Court of Ferdinand and Isabella (where [Christopher Columbus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Columbus) received royal endorsement for his expedition), and the palaces were partially altered. In 1526, [Charles V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_V,_Holy_Roman_Emperor) commissioned a new Renaissance-style palace, now known as the [Palace of Charles V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_Charles_V), in direct juxtaposition with the Nasrid palaces, but it was left uncompleted in the early 17th century.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELópez201165–70-120] Other notable Renaissance additions from the reign of Charles V include the Emperor's Chambers and the *Peinador de la Reina* ('Queen's Robing Room'), which are connected to the former Nasrid Palaces.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELópez2011149–159-121]

In the 18th century the Alhambra fell into severe neglect and parts of it were demolished by French troops in 1812.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELópez2011299–301-122] Through the course of the 19th century, the Alhambra was "rediscovered" by British, American, and other European [Romantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism) travelers and intellectuals. The most influential of them was [Washington Irving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_Irving), whose *[Tales of the Alhambra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tales_of_the_Alhambra)* (1832) brought international attention to the site.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELópez2011303-123] The Alhambra became one of the first Islamic monuments to become the object of modern scientific study and has been the subject of numerous restorations since the 19th century, a process which continues today.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEArnold2017238–239-124][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBloom2020153–155-125]

#### Generalife

Main article: [Generalife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalife)



The Generalife Palace

The [Generalife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalife) is a Nasrid-era country estate or summer palace located just east of the Alhambra, on a sloping site above it. It was first built by [Muhammad II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_II_of_Granada) and [Muhammad III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_III_of_Granada) in the late 13th and early 14th centuries.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERuggles2000155–156-126][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEArnold2017257-127] This palace features several rectangular garden courtyards with decorated pavilions at either end and it was originally linked to the Alhambra by a walled corridor that crosses the valley between the two. It underwent modifications and redecoration under multiple later Nasrid rulers.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELópez2011219–237-128]

The Generalife also underwent major alterations by Christian Spanish builders in the 16th century, which imparted Renaissance elements on the palace.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELópez2011219–237-128] In the 20th century a large area of modern landscaped gardens, known as the *Jardines Nuevos* ('New Gardens'), were added to the south of the Nasrid palace and form the main approach to it today. These date in large part to the work of [Leopoldo Torres Balbás](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopoldo_Torres_Balb%C3%A1s) and Francisco Prieto Moreno, between 1931 and 1951, who incorporated both [Italian](14532.md) and Moorish influences in their design.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELópez2011219–237-128][^cite_note-\:62-129] An open-air auditorium was also added in 1952.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELópez2011219–237-128] Along with the Alhambra, the Generalife is a major tourist attraction and was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1984.[^cite_note-UNESCO-110]

#### Cathedral

Main article: [Cathedral of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_of_Granada)



[Cathedral of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_of_Granada), south portal

The [cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral) of Granada is built over the Nasrid Great [Mosque](19894.md) of Granada, in the centre of the city. Its construction began during the [Spanish Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Renaissance) in the early 16th century, shortly after the [conquest of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granada_War) by the [Catholic Monarchs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Monarchs), who commissioned the works to [Juan Gil de Hontañón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Gil_de_Honta%C3%B1%C3%B3n) and Enrique Egas. Numerous grand buildings were built in the reign of [Charles V, Holy Roman Emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_V,_Holy_Roman_Emperor), so that the cathedral is contemporary to the [Christian palace of the Alhambra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_Charles_V), the [University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Granada) and the Real Chancillería (Supreme Court).

The church was conceived on the model of the [Cathedral of Toledo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_of_Toledo), for what initially was a [Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_architecture) architectural project, as was customary in Spain in the early decades of the 16th century. However, Egas was relieved by the Catholic hierarchy in 1529, and the continuation of the work was assigned to [Diego Siloe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diego_Siloe), who built upon the example of his predecessor, but changed the approach towards a fully Renaissance aesthetic.[^cite_note-130]

The architect drew new Renaissance lines for the whole building over the gothic foundations, with an ambulatory and five naves instead of the usual three. Over time, the bishopric continued to commission new architectural projects of importance, such as the redesign of the main façade, undertaken in 1664 by [Alonso Cano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alonso_Cano) (1601–1667) to introduce Baroque elements. In 1706 Francisco de Hurtado Izquierdo and later his collaborator José Bada built the current tabernacle of the cathedral.

Highlights of the church's components include the main [chapel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapel), where may be found the praying statues of the [Catholic Monarchs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Monarchs), which consists of a series of [Corinthian columns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corinthian_order) with the [entablature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entablature) resting on their [capitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_\(architecture\)), and the [vault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vault_\(architecture\)) over all. The spaces of the walls between the columns are perforated by a series of windows. The design of the [tabernacle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_tabernacle) of 1706 preserves the classic proportions of the church, with its multiple columns crossing the forms of Diego de Siloé.[^cite_note-131]

#### Royal Chapel

Main article: [Royal Chapel of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Chapel_of_Granada)



[Isabelline-Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isabelline_\(architectural_style\)) [Royal Chapel of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Chapel_of_Granada)

The [Catholic Monarchs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Monarchs) chose the city of Granada as their burial site by a royal decree dated 13 September 1504. The Royal Chapel of Granada, built over the former terrace of the Great Mosque, ranks with other important Granadan buildings such as the Lonja and the Catedral e Iglesia del Sagrario. In it are buried the [Catholic Monarchs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Monarchs), their daughter [Joanna of Castile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joanna_of_Castile) (*Juana la Loca*) and her husband [Felipe I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_I_of_Castile) (*Felipe el Hermoso*). Construction of the Chapel started in 1505, directed by its designer, Enrique Egas. Built in several stages, the continuing evolution of its design joined [Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_architecture) construction and decoration with Renaissance ideals, as seen in the tombs and the 17th and 18th century Granadan art in the Chapel of Santa Cruz. Over the years the church acquired a treasury of works of art, liturgical objects and [relics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relic).

The Royal Chapel was declared a [Historic Artistic Monument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bien_de_Inter%C3%A9s_Cultural) on 19 May 1884, taking consideration of BIC ([Bien de Interés Cultural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bien_de_Inter%C3%A9s_Cultural)) status in the current legislation of Spanish Historical Heritage (Law 16/1985 of 25 June). The most important parts of the chapel are its main [retable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retable), grid and [vault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vault_\(architecture\)). In the Sacristy-Museum is the legacy of the Catholic Monarchs. Its art gallery is highlighted by works of the Flemish, Italian and Spanish schools.[^cite_note-132]

#### Albayzín

Main article: [Albaicín](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albaic%C3%ADn)



The view of the Albaicín from the Alhambra

The historic neighbourhood, located on the hillsides north of the Alhambra, still retains much of its medieval street plan dating back to the Nasrid period, although it has undergone physical and demographic changes since then.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERodgersCavendish20214-133] Archeological evidence indicates that Iberians inhabited the main hill of the present-day Albaicin towards the 7th century BC and that a significant town was located here during the Roman period.[^cite_note-\:10-134][^cite_note-FOOTNOTERodgersCavendish20216–10-27] During the early Islamic period, up to the 10th century, the location hosted only the small settlement of *Gharnāṭa* (Granada), until in the 11th century the creation of the Zirid Taifa kingdom transformed the location into one of the most important cities in Al-Andalus.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERodgersCavendish20216–10-27][^cite_note-\:2-29][^cite_note-\:10-134] During this period, the city's main citadel and palace, the *al-Qaṣaba al-Qadīma* ("Old Citadel"), was located on the top of this hill.[^cite_note-\:05222-9][^cite_note-\:24-14] During the Nasrid period, the citadel was moved to the Alhambra and the old district became known as *al-Bayyāzīn* ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language): ٱلْبَيّازِينْ), from which its present name derives.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKennedy1996277-47][^cite_note-135][^cite_note-136] Among the various Nasrid-period houses and other structures, one notable establishment was the Maristan, a type of historic hospital ([bimaristan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bimaristan)) which also took care of the mentally ill.[^cite_note-\:9-53][^cite_note-\:12-137]

Through the course of the 16th century, after the surrender of Granada to the Catholic Monarchs in 1492, the district underwent some of the some changes that occurred throughout the city. Mosques were replaced with new parish churches, particularly after 1501.[^cite_note-138][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColeman2013-10] These new churches were often built in a mix of [Mudéjar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mud%C3%A9jar_art) and Renaissance styles, such as the Church of Santa Ana, the Church of San Cristóbal, and the Church of San Miguel Bajo, among others.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColeman201370-139][^cite_note-140][^cite_note-141] Some, like the churches of [San Salvador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_San_Salvador_\(Granada\)), San José, and San Juan de los Reyes, preserve parts of former mosques.[^cite_note-\:052232-32][^cite_note-\:24-14] New civic institutions were also built in this area, such as the [Royal Chancellery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Chancellery_of_Granada) (*Real Chancillería*), which overlooks Plaza Nueva, a wide public square expanded during the 16th century.[^cite_note-142]

During the 16th century most of the city's *morisco* population was concentrated in the Albaicín, but after the 1568 rebellion and their subsequent expulsion, the district was depopulated.[^cite_note-\:02-143][^cite_note-\:11-144] As a result, many of the remaining Christian residents expanded their residences to form *carmen*s, traditional semi-rural houses with gardens or orchards, many examples of which survive today. Some of these houses and mansions, such as Casa de Zafra and the Dar al-Horra, date back to the Nasrid period.[^cite_note-\:02-143] In 1994 the UNESCO World Heritage Site encompassing the Alhambra was extended to incorporate the Albaicín as well.[^cite_note-\:11-144][^cite_note-UNESCO-110] In addition to its historic houses and mansions, churches, and the Royal Chancellery, the district also preserves sections of the 11th-century Zirid city walls (part of the former Zirid citadel), the 14th-century Nasrid walls, the Bañuelo (a former Islamic-era bathhouse), and numerous cisterns from the Islamic period that were part of a water supply system providing water to most of the city's houses.[^cite_note-\:5-22][^cite_note-\:052232-32][^cite_note-145]

#### Sacromonte

Main article: [Sacromonte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacromonte)

The Sacromonte neighbourhood is located on the Valparaíso hill, one of several hills that make up Granada. This neighborhood is known as the old neighbourhood of the [Romani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romani_people), who settled in Granada after the conquest of the city. It is one of the most picturesque neighborhoods, full of whitewashed caves cut into the rock and used as residences. The sound of strumming guitars may still be heard there in the performance of flamenco *[cantes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flamenco#Cante_\(song\))* and *quejíos*, so that over time it has become one of the most popular tourist attractions in Granada.

At the top of this hill is the Abbey of Sacromonte and the College of Sacromonte, founded in the 17th century by the then Archbishop of Granada [Pedro de Castro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedro_de_Castro,_1st_Duke_of_la_Conquista). The Abbey of Sacromonte was built to monitor and guard the alleged relics of the evangelists of Baetica. Those are of questionable authenticity, but since their finding the area has been a religious pilgrimage destination.[^cite_note-146]

The abbey complex consists of the catacombs, the abbey (17th–18th centuries), the Colegio Viejo de San Dionisio Areopagita (17th century) and the Colegio Nuevo (19th century). The interior of the church is simple and small but has numerous excellent works of art, which accentuate the size and rich carving of the *Crucificado de Risueño*, an object of devotion for the Romani people, who sing and dance in the procession of Holy Week. The facilities also include a museum, which houses the works acquired by the Foundation.[^cite_note-147]

#### Charterhouse

Main article: [Granada Charterhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granada_Charterhouse)



Tabernacle Dome, [Granada Charterhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granada_Charterhouse)

The Charterhouse of Granada is a monastery of cloistered monks, located in what was a farm or Muslim almunia called *Aynadamar* ("fountain of tears") that had an abundance of water and fruit trees. The initiative to build the monastery in that place was begun by [Gonzalo Fernández de Córdoba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonzalo_Fern%C3%A1ndez_de_C%C3%B3rdoba), known as [El Gran Capitán](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonzalo_Fern%C3%A1ndez_de_C%C3%B3rdoba). The charterhouse was founded in 1506; construction started ten years later, and continued for the following 300 years.

The Monastery suffered heavy damage during the [Peninsular War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peninsular_War) and lost considerable property in 1837 as a result of the [confiscations of Mendizábal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesiastical_Confiscations_of_Mendiz%C3%A1bal). Currently, the monastery belongs to the [Carthusians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carthusian_Order), reporting directly to the [Archdiocese of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Granada).[^cite_note-148]

The street entrance to the complex is an ornate arch of [Plateresque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plateresque) style. Through it one reaches a large courtyard, at the end which is a wide staircase leading to the entrance of the church. The church, of early 16th century style and plan, has three entrances, one for the faithful and the other two for monks and clergy. Its plan has a single [nave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nave) divided into four sections, highlighting the [retables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retable) of [Juan Sánchez Cotán](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_S%C3%A1nchez_Cot%C3%A1n) and the [chancel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancel)'s glass doors, adorned with [mother-of-pearl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mother-of-pearl), [silver](27119.md), rare woods, and [ivory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory). The [presbytery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbytery_\(architecture\)) is covered by [elliptical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellipse) [vaulting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vault_\(architecture\)). The main altar, between the chancel arch and the [church tabernacle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_tabernacle), is gilded wood.

The church's [tabernacle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tabernacle) and sancta sanctorum are considered a masterpiece of [Baroque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baroque) Spanish art in its blend of architecture, painting and sculpture. The dome that covers this area is decorated with frescoes by the Córdoba artist [Antonio Palomino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_Palomino) (18th century) representing the triumph of the Church Militant, faith, and religious life.

The courtyard, with galleries of arches on [Doric order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doric_order) columns opening on it, is centred by a fountain. The Chapter House of Legos is the oldest building of the monastery (1517). It is rectangular and covered with groin vaulting.[^cite_note-149]

### Mosque of Granada

Main article: [Granada Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granada_Mosque)

The new mosque was inaugurated in 2003 on the summit of the neighborhood of Albayzin. The mosque was built near the Church of San Salvador and the Church of San Nicolás. The Church of San Salvador was built on the site of the Great Mosque of Albayzin. The Society for the Return of Islam in Spain purchased the site in 1981, but it took many years for the plans to be approved. The mosque's initial funding was supplied by [Shaykh 'Abdalqadir as-Sufi al-Murabit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaykh_Abdalqadir_as-sufi_al-Murabit) who envisioned providing Granada's new Spanish Muslim community with a mosque. Additional funding came from Malaysia, Morocco and the United Arab Emirates. In 1991 the CIE (*Comunidad Islámica en España*) hired the architect Renato Ramirez Sanchez to design the mosque. In the 1990s, there was a heated debate pertaining to the design of the minaret. Construction eventually began in 2001. The mosque now serves about 500 people.[^cite_note-150]

#### Palace of the Marqués de Salar

The **Palace of the Marqués de Salar** was built in one of the most emblematic streets of Granada, the **Carrera del Darro**, at number 5. This place is an architectural example of the classical Granada during the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_architecture) transformation of the XVI<sup>th</sup> century. It was built by the Marqués de Salar, great-grandson of both Hernán Pérez del Pulgar (known by the name of *El de la Hazañas* \[The One of the Valiant Deeds]) and [Gonzalo Fernández de Córdoba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonzalo_Fern%C3%A1ndez_de_C%C3%B3rdoba) (*El Gran Capitán* \[The Great Captain]), Captain-General of the Castilian-Aragonese forces that concluded the Reconquest of the peninsula. The palace is now the museum of perfumes El Patio de los Perfumes, with 1,500 square metres (16,000 sq ft) of floor space on two floors and 130 square metres (1,400 sq ft) of patio to relax surrounded by flowers and perfumes.

### Cuisine

| icon | This section **does not [cite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia\:Citing_sources) any [sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia\:Verifiability)**. Please help [improve this section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special\:EditPage/Granada) by [adding citations to reliable sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Referencing_for_beginners). Unsourced material may be challenged and [removed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia\:Verifiability#Burden_of_evidence). *(February 2022)*_&#x20;([Learn how and when to remove this message](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Maintenance_template_removal))_ |
| --- | --- |

The [gastronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastronomy) of Granada is part of the Arabic-Andalusian cuisine tradition, with a strong Arab and Jewish heritage, which is reflected in its condiments and spices, such as [cumin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumin), [coriander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coriander), [nutmeg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutmeg), cinnamon, raisins, almonds or honey. The writer Miguel Alcobendas, author of the *traditional cuisine of Granada*, says that it has its origin in living together, from the thirteenth to the fifteenth century (when Granada surrendered to the [Catholic Monarchs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Monarchs)), of Muslims, Jews and Christians in the [Nasrid Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasrid_Kingdom_of_Granada) from Granada. Subsequently, there was a miscegenation with the kitchen of the Christians, in which pork acquired an importance in the kitchen of Granada more than in the rest of Spain, since its consumption allowed its eaters to demonstrate a certain distance from the persecuted religions, since [both Muslims and Jews have it banned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_restrictions_on_the_consumption_of_pork).

The climatic differences of the different regions of the province, from the coast to the peaks of the [Sierra Nevada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Nevada_\(Spain\)) propitiates a great variety of raw materials: vegetables, meats and sausages, and fish that are combined in a multitude of dishes and recipes for soups and stews.

The famous and reputed Trevélez ham comes from the Sierra of Granada, to which other pork derivatives are added, sausages such as [chorizo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chorizo), [black pudding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_pudding) and pork tenderloin.

Ham and beans, two products of the land, are combined in one of its most typical dishes, beans with ham; Other known dishes are the Sacromonte tortilla, which among other ingredients must have cooked brains and veal crustaillas, chopped and sauteed before mixing with the egg. It is also worth mentioning "papas a lo pobre", potatoes which are usually served with egg and fried peppers, as well as with pieces of pork or ham.

Among the stews and potajes, the pot of San Antón stands out, which is eaten mainly towards the second half of January; cabbage stew, which combines vegetables and legumes; the stew of green beans and fennel; The thistle and pumpkin casserole, with noodles and aromatic herbs, or gypsy pottery are other dishes of the land.

[Confectionery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confectionery) is well represented in the gastronomy of Granada, for sweets prepared by the nuns can be purchased in the numerous convents of the city: the *pestiños* of Vélez or those of the *Encarnación*, the [puff pastries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puff_pastry) of San Jerónimo, the ovos moles of San Antón, the Zafra biscuit, sweet potato rolls, *cocas*, *roscos* from Santo Tomas and *mantecados*. Aljojábanas, honey and cheese dumplings and some of the fritters called *almohados*, as well as fig bread, Moorish *roscos* and an almond cake called *soyá* are all of Arabic heritage.

Unlike in other provinces of Andalusia, in Granada *[tapas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tapas)* (appetizers or snacks) are usually free in bars and restaurants. "Bar hopping" (*Ir de tapas*) and eating tapas in the time between finishing work and having dinner is a deeply rooted traditional activity among the people of Granada. There are different tapas routes around the city.

### Leisure and entertainment

In Granada there is a wide program of leisure and entertainment, which covers a large number of fields, available to both visitors and citizens themselves. Of the leisure activities carried out, the following can be highlighted:

- The zambras of Sacromonte. Old bridal parties held by the gypsies of the city, and that disappeared for years before their current vindication. They develop in the caves of the Sacromonte neighborhood and have a unique character in the world of flamenco. There are also more classic flamenco shows in the Albaicín. These flamenco shows, usually linked to restaurants, are one of the city's cultural attractions.

- Festivals. Granada has a very complete range of events: International Music and Dance Festival, International Jazz Festival, Granada Festival South Cinemas and International Tango Festival, among others.

- Concerts. Throughout the year there is a stable program of concerts in the Manuel de Falla Auditorium and theater and opera performances in the Congress Palace.

- Parties. Throughout the year several parties are held on significant dates for religious, civil or cultural reasons.

During the Roman period, there was a township named Iliberris on the slopes of the Sierra de Elvira, where archaeological remains of Roman, Visigothic, and Arab origin have been found. The existence of a Roman settlement in the same place as Granada—or rather, the identification of Granada with Iliberis—has been a bone of contention among historians of Granada since the 10th century. In the era of the so-called Christian "reconquest" of southern Spain, the notion of such continuity allowed the conquerors to speak of a "restoration" of Christianity in Granada. Some experts argue strongly for continuity between the Roman city and Granada, as against the possibility (supported by the Arabic sources) that Granada was, in fact, a Muslim foundation: archaeological evidence has not been decisive either way.

[^cite_note-:2-29]: &#x20;Carvajal López, José C. (2020). ["Material culture"](https://books.google.com/books?id=MureDwAAQBAJ\&dq=%22Medina+Elvira%22\&pg=PT526). In Fierro, Maribel (ed.). *The Routledge Handbook of Muslim Iberia*. Routledge. pp. 490, 505 (see note 18). [ISBN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISBN_\(identifier\)) [978-1-317-23354-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special\:BookSources/978-1-317-23354-1). The location of the town of Ilbīra was a burning issue in Granada since the 16th century. The Catholic propaganda after 1492 pushed the identification of Ilbīra with Granada, and also with the Roman municipium Illiberis, where the first Church Council of Iberia took place in the early 4th century. If Granada was indeed Illiberis, the town was associated with one important early Christian centre and the right of the kings of Spain to take it over was providentially justified. However, there were enough mentions in the written sources, not only in Arabic, and even material evidence that suggested that Ilbīra, the first Islamic capital, was located near the village of Atarfe, about 15 kilometres north-west of Granada. The similarity in names suggested that Illiberis had been in Ilbīra and that Granada was a town founded by Muslims, ideas which implicitly supported the notion that Islam was an integral part of Spain rather than merely an enemy against which the nation had been built. After a debate four centuries long, M. Gómez Moreno compiled cases of the material evidence collected around the area of Atarfe by spoilers and even undertook an excavation in 1872 in which he found the mosque of the town, finally proving without any doubt that Ilbīra was located there. \[p. 490] (...) See Gómez Moreno, Medina Elvira for the description of the debate and of the different interventions. It is interesting to note that this solves the question of the location of Ilbīra, but not of Illiberis, at least in the time of the Council. Excavations in Granada have uncovered strong evidence that suggests that the Iberian and Roman towns of the same name were located there (e.g. Sotomayor Muro, "¿Donde estuvo Iliberri?"). It seems that at some point in late Antiquity, possibly before the Islamic conquest, the town of Illiberis was moved to the location where it later became Madīna Ilbīrah (e.g. Adroher et al., "Discusión", pp. 202–206). To a certain extent, the debate still continues. \[p. 505, note 18]
