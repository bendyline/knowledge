It is made up of 348 senators (*sénateurs* and *sénatrices*)[^cite_note-1] elected by part of the country's [local councillors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territorial_collectivity) in [indirect elections](652583.md). Senators have six-year terms, with half of the seats up for election every three years. They represent France's [departments](38523.md) (328),[^cite_note-2] [overseas collectivities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overseas_France) (8)[^cite_note-3] and [citizens abroad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_senators_of_French_citizens_living_abroad) (12).[^cite_note-4]

Senators' [mode of election varies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Senate_elections) upon their constituency's population size: in the less populated constituencies (one or two seats), they are elected individually, whereas in more populated ones (three seats or more), they are elected on lists. It is common for senators to hold [dual mandates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_mandate), such as in a [regional council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_council_\(France\)) or [departmental council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Departmental_council_\(France\)).

The Senate enjoys less prominence than the National Assembly, which is elected on [direct universal ballot](652563.md) and upon the majority of which the [Government](3398091.md) relies: in case of disagreement, the Assembly can in many cases have the last word, although the Senate keeps a role in some key procedures, such as [constitutional amendments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_France). However, following the [2024 snap legislative election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_French_legislative_election) and the ensuing political fragmentation in the Assembly, the newly-outed [Barnier government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnier_government) held a working majority in the Senate but not in the Assembly, a first under the [Fifth Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Fifth_Republic),[^cite_note-5] marking "a form of strengthening of the Senate".[^cite_note-6]

[Bicameralism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicameralism) was introduced in France amid the [French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution) in [1795](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_the_Year_III); as in many countries, it assigned the upper chamber with the role of moderating the lower, although for a long time as an ally of the [executive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_branch). The present selection mode of the Senate dates back to the start of the [Third Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_French_Republic), when it was turned into what [Léon Gambetta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%A9on_Gambetta) famously called a "grand council of the [communes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communes_of_France) of France". Over time, it developed a sense of independence as a "guardian of the institutions" and "guardian of liberties", favoured by the fact that senators are on average older than [deputies](29334549.md) in the Assembly, joining the Senate in the later part of their career. Debates in the Senate tend to be less tense and generally receive less media coverage. As a result of its election relying on what is often summed up as rural mayors, it has had a right-wing majority since 1958, with only a three-year exception in 2011–2014.

The [President of the Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_presidents_of_the_Senate_of_France) is to step in as acting [President of France](24899.md) in case of an incapacitation or a vacancy, which last happened in 1974. The officeholder also appoints three members of the [Constitutional Council](1141259.md). Since 2014, [Gérard Larcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%A9rard_Larcher) has been President of the Senate.

The Senate is housed inside the [Luxembourg Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luxembourg_Palace) in the [6th arrondissement of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/6th_arrondissement_of_Paris). Like the National Assembly, it is guarded by [Republican Guards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_Guard_\(France\)). In front of the building lies the Senate's garden, the [Jardin du Luxembourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jardin_du_Luxembourg), open to the public.

## History

France's first experience with an upper house was under the [Directory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Directory) from 1795 to 1799, when the [Council of Ancients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Ancients) was the upper chamber. There were Senates in both the [First](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_French_Empire) and [Second Empires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_French_Empire) (the former being known as the *[Sénat conservateur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%A9nat_conservateur)*, the latter as the French Senate), but these were only nominally legislative bodies – technically they were not legislative, but rather advisory bodies on the model of the [Roman Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Senate).



The Luxembourg Palace in Paris, where the Senate meets

With the [Restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourbon_Restoration_in_France) in 1814, a new [Chamber of Peers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamber_of_Peers_\(France\)) was created, on the model of the British [House of Lords](13658.md).[^cite_note-7] At first it contained hereditary peers, but following the [July Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/July_Revolution) of 1830, it became a body whose members were appointed for life. The [Second Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Second_Republic) returned to a [unicameral](204304.md) system after 1848, but soon after the establishment of the Second Empire in 1852, a Senate was established as the upper chamber.[^cite_note-8] In the [Fourth Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Fourth_Republic), the Senate was replaced by the [Council of the Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_the_Republic_\(France\)), but its function was largely the same. With the new Constitution of the [Fifth Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Fifth_Republic) which came into force on 4 October 1958, the older name of Senate was restored.

In [2011](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_French_Senate_election), the [Socialist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_Party_\(France\)) won control of the Senate for the first time since the foundation of the Fifth Republic.[^cite_note-9] In [2014](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_French_Senate_election), the centre-right [Gaullists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaullism) and their allies won back the control of the Senate; they retained their majority in [2017](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_French_Senate_election).

## Powers

Under the [Constitution of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_France), the Senate has nearly the same powers as the [National Assembly](299616.md). [Bills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_\(proposed_law\)) may be submitted by the government (*projets de loi*) or by either house of Parliament (*propositions de loi*). Because both houses may amend the bill, it may take several readings to reach an agreement between the National Assembly and the Senate. When the Senate and the National Assembly cannot agree on a bill, the administration can decide, after a procedure called *commission mixte paritaire*, to give the final decision to the National Assembly, whose majority is normally on the government's side, but as regarding the constitutional laws the administration must have the Senate's agreement. This does not happen frequently; usually the two houses eventually agree on the bill, or the administration decides to withdraw it. This power however gives the National Assembly a prominent role in the law-making process, especially since the administration is necessarily of the same side as the Assembly, for the Assembly can dismiss the administration through a motion of censure.

The power to pass a vote of censure, or vote of no confidence, is limited. As was the case in the Fourth Republic's constitution, new cabinets do not have to receive a vote of confidence. Also, a vote of censure can occur only after 10 per cent of the members sign a petition; if rejected, those members that signed cannot sign another petition until that session of Parliament has ended. If the petition gets the required support, a vote of censure must gain an absolute majority of all members, not just those voting. If the Assembly and the Senate have politically distinct majorities, the Assembly will in most cases prevail; open conflict between the two houses is uncommon.

As per article 24 of the Constitution, the Senate is the representative of the territories and defends the local elected officials. The Senate also serves to monitor the administration's actions by publishing many reports each year on various topics.

## Composition

Until September 2004, the Senate had 321 members, each elected to serve for a nine-year term. In that month, the term was reduced to six years, while – to reflect a growth in the country's population – the number of senators was set to increase progressively, to reach 348 by 2011.[^cite_note-10] Senators had been elected in thirds every three years; this was also changed to one half of their number every three years.[^cite_note-11]

### President

Further information: [List of presidents of the Senate of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_presidents_of_the_Senate_of_France)



Gérard Larcher has been President of the Senate since 2014.



Then-Senate President [Alain Poher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alain_Poher) served as Acting President of France twice: in 1969 and 1974.

The president of the Senate is elected by senators from among their members. The current incumbent is [Gérard Larcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%A9rard_Larcher). The President of the Senate is, under the Constitution of the Fifth Republic, first in the line of succession—in case of death, resignation or removal from office (only for health reasons)—to the [presidency of the French Republic](24899.md), becoming Acting President of the Republic until a new election can be held. This happened twice for [Alain Poher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alain_Poher)—once at the resignation of [Charles de Gaulle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_de_Gaulle) and once at the death of [Georges Pompidou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Pompidou).

The President of the Senate also has the right to designate three of the nine members of the [Constitutional Council](1141259.md), serving for nine years.

### Election

Main article: [French Senate elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Senate_elections)

Senators are [elected indirectly](652583.md) by approximately 150,000 officials, known as the *grands électeurs*, including regional councillors, department councillors, mayors, municipal councillors in large communes, as well as members of the National Assembly. However, 90% of the electors are delegates appointed by councillors. This system introduces a bias in the composition of the Senate favoring rural areas. As a consequence, while the political majority changes frequently in the National Assembly, the Senate has remained politically right, with one brief exception (2011–2014), since the foundation of the [Fifth Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Fifth_Republic), much to the displeasure of the [Socialists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_Party_\(France\)).[^cite_note-12]

This has spurred controversy, especially after the [2008 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_French_Senate_election)[^cite_note-13] in which the Socialist Party, despite controlling all but two of France's [regions](58846.md), a majority of [departments](38523.md), as well as [communes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communes_of_France) representing more than 50% of the population, still failed to achieve a majority in the Senate. The Senate has also been accused of being a "refuge" for politicians that have lost their seats in the [National Assembly](299616.md). The left, led by the Socialist Party, gained control of the Senate for the first time since 1958 during the [2011 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_French_Senate_election), leading to the election of [Jean-Pierre Bel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Pierre_Bel) at its presidency. This proved a short-lived win, as the right, led by the [Union for a Popular Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_for_a_Popular_Movement), regained the Senate three years later.

As an indirectly elected house, the Senate is often criticised by political parties such as [La France Insoumise](52520313.md) on the left and the [National Rally](67119.md) on the right as not being sufficiently popularly representative.[^cite_note-15] Although both La France Insoumise and the National Rally enjoy significant representation in the National Assembly, they are far less represented in the Senate, as they do not have a large number of local councillors affiliated with them, who in turn elect the senators.

The left has historically opposed the very existence of a second chamber, while the right has defended it; controversies over the Senate's role are revived from time to time. The common phrase "a senator's pace" (*un train de sénateur*) mocks the upper house's perceived slow rhythm and readiness to let new legislation die.
