A **nautical mile** is a [unit of length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_of_length) used in air, marine, and space [navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigation), and for the definition of [territorial waters](496983.md).[^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5] Historically, it was defined as the [meridian arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_arc) length corresponding to one [minute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minute_of_arc) (⁠1/60⁠ of a degree) of [latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latitude) at the [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator), so that [Earth's polar circumference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_circumference) is very near to 21,600 nautical miles (that is 60 minutes × 360 degrees). Today the international nautical mile is defined as exactly 1,852 metres (about 6,076 ft; 1.151 mi).[^cite_note-6] The derived unit of speed is the [knot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knot_\(unit\)), one nautical mile per hour.

The nautical mile is not part of the [International System of Units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units) (SI). However, it is still in common use globally in air, marine, and space contexts due to its correspondence with [geographic coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographic_coordinate).

## History



Visual comparison of a kilometre, [statute mile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mile#Statute) and nautical mile

The word *mile* is from the Latin phrase for a thousand paces: *[mille passus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mile#Roman_mile)*. Navigation at sea was done by eye[^cite_note-\:0-13] until around 1500 when navigational instruments were developed and cartographers began using a [coordinate system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_system) with [parallels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_of_latitude) of [latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latitude) and [meridians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_\(geography\)) of [longitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude).

The earliest reference of 60 miles to a degree is a map by [Nicolaus Germanus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaus_Germanus) in a 1482 edition of [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy)'s [*Geography*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_\(Ptolemy\)) indicating one degree of longitude at the Equator contains "*milaria 60*".[^cite_note-14] An earlier manuscript map by Nicolaus Germanus in a previous edition of *Geography* states "*unus gradus longitudinis et latitudinis sub equinoctiali continet stadia 500 que faciunt miliaria 62⁠1/2⁠*" ("one degree longitude and latitude under the equator forms 500 [stadia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stadion_\(unit\)), which make 62⁠1/2⁠ miles").[^cite_note-Germanus_1460-15] Whether a correction or convenience, the reason for the change from 62⁠1/2⁠ to 60 miles to a degree is not explained. Eventually, the ratio of 60 miles to a degree appeared in English in a 1555 translation of [Pietro Martire d'Anghiera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pietro_Martire_d'Anghiera)'s Decades: "\[Ptolemy] assigned likewise to every degree three score miles."[^cite_note-16]

By the late 16th century English geographers and navigators knew that the ratio of distances at sea to degrees was constant along any [great circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_circle) (such as the [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator), or any meridian), assuming that Earth was a sphere. In 1574, [William Bourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Bourne_\(mathematician\)) stated in *A Regiment for the Sea* the "rule to raise a degree" practised by navigators: "But as I take it, we in England should allowe 60 myles to one degrée: that is, after 3 miles to one of our Englishe leagues, wherefore 20 of oure English leagues shoulde answere to one degrée."[^cite_note-Bourne-17] Likewise, [Robert Hues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Hues) wrote in 1594 that the distance along a great circle was 60 miles per degree.[^cite_note-Waters-18] However, these referred to the old English mile of 5000 feet and [league](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_\(unit\)) of 15,000 feet, relying upon Ptolemy's underestimate of the [Earth's circumference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_circumference).[^cite_note-Robert_Hues-19] In the early seventeenth century, English geographers started to acknowledge the discrepancy between the angular measurement of a degree of latitude and the linear measurement of miles. In 1624 [Edmund Gunter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Gunter) suggested 352,000 feet to a degree (5866⁠2/3⁠ feet per [arcminute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcminute)).[^cite_note-Gunther-20][^cite_note-Waters-18] In 1633, [William Oughtred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Oughtred) suggested 349,800 feet to a degree (5830 feet per arcminute).[^cite_note-Oughtred-21] Both Gunter and Oughtred put forward the notion of dividing a degree into 100 parts, but their proposal was generally ignored by navigators. The ratio of 60 miles, or 20 leagues, to a degree of latitude remained fixed while the length of the mile was revised with better estimates of the Earth's circumference. In 1637, Robert Norwood proposed a new measurement of 6120 feet for an arcminute of latitude, which was within 44 feet of the currently accepted value for a nautical mile.[^cite_note-Richard_Norwood-22]

Since the Earth is not a perfect sphere but is an [oblate spheroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oblate_spheroid) with slightly flattened poles, a minute of latitude is not constant, but about 1,862 metres at the poles and 1,843 metres at the Equator.[^cite_note-rasc-23] France and other metric countries state that in principle a nautical mile is an arcminute of a meridian at a latitude of 45°, but that is a modern justification for a more mundane calculation that was developed a century earlier. By the mid-19th century, France had defined a nautical mile via the original 1791 [definition of the metre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definition_of_the_metre), one ten-millionth of a [quarter meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarter_meridian).[^cite_note-Annuaires1933-24][^cite_note-Annuaries1848-25] So ⁠10,000,000 m/90 × 60⁠ = 1,851.85 m ≈ 1,852 m became the metric length for a nautical mile. France made it legal for the French Navy in 1906, and many metric countries voted to sanction it for international use at the 1929 International Hydrographic Conference.

Both the United States and the United Kingdom used an average arcminute—specifically, a minute of arc of a great circle of a sphere having the same surface area as the [Clarke 1866 ellipsoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_ellipsoid).[^cite_note-Galzebrook-26] The [*authalic* (equal area) radius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authalic_radius) of the Clarke 1866 ellipsoid is 6,370,997.2 metres (20,902,222 ft).[^cite_note-Snyder-27] The resulting arcminute is 1,853.2480 metres (6,080.210 ft). The United States chose five significant digits for its nautical mile, 6,080.2 [feet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foot_\(unit\)), whereas the United Kingdom chose four significant digits for its Admiralty mile, 6,080 feet.

In 1929 the international nautical mile was defined by the First International Extraordinary Hydrographic Conference in [Monaco](19261.md) as exactly 1,852 metres (which is 6,076.12 ft).[^cite_note-BIPM-2] The United States did not adopt the international nautical mile until 1954.[^cite_note-28] Britain adopted it in 1970,[^cite_note-29] but legal references to the obsolete unit are now converted to 1,853 metres (which is 6,079.40 ft).[^cite_note-30]

## Similar definitions

The [metre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre) was originally defined as 1⁄10,000,000 of the length of the meridian arc from the [North Pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Pole) to the equator,[^cite_note-32] thus one kilometre of distance corresponded to one [centigrad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gradian) of latitude (1% of a [centesimal degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gradian), also known as a centesimal arc-minute). The Earth's circumference is therefore approximately 40,000 km. The equatorial circumference is slightly longer than the polar circumference –  the measurement based on this (⁠40,075.017 km/360 × 60⁠ = 1,855.3 metres) is known as the [geographical mile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographical_mile).

Using the definition ⁠1/60⁠ of a degree of latitude on [Mars](14640471.md), a Martian nautical mile equals to 983 m (1,075 yd). This is potentially useful for [celestial navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_navigation) on a [human mission to the planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_mission_to_Mars), as both a shorthand and a quick way to roughly determine the location.[^cite_note-33]
