The **forint**[^cite_note-1] ([sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency_sign) **Ft**; [code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_4217) **HUF**) is the [currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency) of [Hungary](13275.md). It was formerly divided into 100 [fillér](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fill%C3%A9r), but fillér coins are no longer in circulation. The introduction of the forint on 1 August 1946 was a crucial step in the post-[World War II](32927.md) stabilisation of the [Hungarian economy](13428.md), and the currency remained relatively stable until the 1980s. Transition to a [market economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_economy) in the early 1990s adversely affected the value of the forint; inflation peaked at 35% in 1991. Between 2001 and 2022, inflation was in single digits, and the forint has been declared fully [convertible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convertibility).[^cite_note-2] In May 2022, inflation reached 10.7% amid the [Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine) and economic uncertainty.[^cite_note-3] As a member of the [European Union](9317.md), the long-term aim of the Hungarian government may be to [replace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungary_and_the_euro) the forint with the [euro](9472.md), although under the [Orbán](750582.md) governments there was no target date for adopting the euro.[^cite_note-4]

Forint of [Louis I of Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_I_of_Hungary) (1342–1382). Reverse: LODOVICVS DEI GRACIA REX. Obverse: S\[ANCTVS] IOHANNES B\[APTISTA].



Forint of [Matthias Corvinus of Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthias_Corvinus_of_Hungary) (1458–1490). Obverse: S\[ANCTVS] [LADISLAVS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ladislaus_I_of_Hungary) REX. Reverse: MATHIAS D\[EI] G\[RATIA] R\[EX] VNGARIE.

The forint's name comes from the Italian city of [Florence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence), where gold coins called *[fiorino d'oro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florin)* were minted from 1252. In Hungary, the *florentinus* (later *forint*), also a gold-based currency, was used from 1325 under [Charles Robert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_I_of_Hungary), with several other countries following Hungary's example.[^cite_note-5]

Between 1868 and 1892, the forint was the name used in [Hungarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_language) for the currency of the [Austro-Hungarian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Hungarian_Empire), known in German as the [*Gulden*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Hungarian_florin). It was subdivided into 100 [krajczár](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krajczar) (*krajcár* in modern Hungarian orthography; cf German *Kreuzer*).[^cite_note-6]

The forint was reintroduced on 1 August 1946, after the [pengő](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_peng%C5%91) was rendered worthless in 1945–46 by the most severe [hyperinflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperinflation) ever recorded. This was brought about by a mixture of the high demand for reparations from the [USSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USSR), Soviet plundering of Hungarian industries, and the holding of Hungary's gold reserves in the [United States](3434750.md).[^cite_note-7] The different parties in the government had different plans to solve this problem. To the [Independent Smallholders' Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_Smallholders'_Party)–which had won a large majority in the [1945 Hungarian parliamentary election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1945_Hungarian_parliamentary_election)–as well as the [Social Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_Democratic_Party_of_Hungary), outside support was essential. However, the Soviet Union and its local supporters in the [Hungarian Communist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_Communist_Party) were opposed to raising loans in the West, and thus the Communist Party masterminded the procedure using exclusively domestic resources. The Communist plan called for tight limits on personal spending, as well as the concentration of existing stocks in state hands.[^cite_note-8]

When the forint was introduced, its value was defined on the basis of 1 kilogram of fine gold being 13,210 Ft (or 1 Ft = 75.7 mg fine gold). Therefore, given that gold was fixed at £8 8s sterling (£8.40 in modern decimal notation) per troy ounce, one pound sterling was at that time worth about 49 forints. When the forint was introduced, it dropped 29 zeroes from the value of the pengő. As a measure of how badly inflated the pengő was, total value of pengő notes in circulation at the time was now equivalent to less than 0.2 fillér.

## Coins

Main article: [Coins of the Hungarian forint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coins_of_the_Hungarian_forint)

In 1946, coins were introduced in denominations of 2, 10, 20 fillérs and 1, 2, 5 forints. The silver 5 forint coin was reissued only in the next year; later it was withdrawn from circulation. Five and 50 fillérs coins were issued in 1948. In 1967, a 5 forint coin was reintroduced, followed by a 10 forint in 1971 and 20 forint in 1982.

In 1992, a new series of coins was introduced in denominations of 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100 and (a somewhat different, 500[‰](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millesimal_fineness) silver) 200 forint. Production of the 2 and 5 fillér coins ceased in 1992, with all fillér coins withdrawn from circulation by 1999. From 1996, a [bicolor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bi-metallic_coins) 100 forint coin was minted to replace the 1992 version, since the latter was considered too big and ugly, and could easily be mistaken for the 20 forint coin.

Silver 200 forint coins were withdrawn in 1998 (as their nominal value was too low compared to their precious metal content); the 1 and 2 forint coins remained legal tender until 29 February 2008.[^cite_note-9] For cash purchases, the total price is now [rounded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash_rounding) to the nearest multiple of 5 forint (to 0 or to 5).[^cite_note-10] A new 200 forint coin[^cite_note-11] made of base metal alloy was introduced in place of the 200 forint banknote on 15 June 2009.

## Banknotes

Main article: [Banknotes of the Hungarian forint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banknotes_of_the_Hungarian_forint)

In 1946, 10 and 100 forint notes were introduced by the [Hungarian National Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_National_Bank). A new series of higher quality banknotes (in denominations of 10, 20 and 100 forints) were introduced in 1947 and 1948. 50 forint notes were added in 1953, 500 forint notes were introduced in 1970, followed by 1,000 forints in 1983, and 5,000 forints in 1991.

A completely redesigned new series of banknotes in denominations of 200, 500, 1,000, 2,000, 5,000, 10,000 and 20,000 forints was introduced gradually between 1997 and 2001. Each banknote depicts a famous Hungarian leader or politician on the obverse and a place or event related to him on the reverse. All of the banknotes are [watermarked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watermark), contain an embedded vertical security strip, an [invisible ink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invisible_ink) design and are suitable for visually impaired people. The 1,000 forints and higher denominations are protected by an interwoven [holographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holographic) security strip. The notes share the common size of 154 mm × 70 mm (6.1 in × 2.8 in). The banknotes are printed by the Hungarian Banknote Printing Corp. in Budapest on paper manufactured by the Diósgyőr Papermill in [Miskolc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miskolc).

Commemorative banknotes have also been issued: 1,000 and 2,000 forint notes to commemorate the millennium (in 2000) and a 500-forint note to commemorate the 50th anniversary of the [1956 revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1956_revolution) (in 2006).

The 200-forint banknote was withdrawn from circulation in 2009, as its value inflated over time.[^cite_note-12] Also due to the embarrassment caused by the fact that the model of the portrait on the banknote, purported to be of King Charles Robert (of whom no authentic contemporary depictions survived), turned out to be of an acquaintance of the designer, the managing director of the security company that supplied the banknote printing company. The banknote was replaced by a bimetallic 200-forint coin featuring the iconic [Széchenyi Chain Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sz%C3%A9chenyi_Chain_Bridge).[^cite_note-13]

Forgery of forint banknotes is not significant. However, forged 20,000 forint notes printed on the paper of 2,000 forint notes after dissolving the original ink might come up and are not easy to recognize. Another denomination preferred by counterfeiters was the 1,000 forint note until improved security features were added in 2006.

Worn banknotes no longer fit for circulation are withdrawn, destroyed and turned into [briquettes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Briquette) which are donated to public benefit (charitable) organizations to be used as heating fuel.[^cite_note-14]

In 2014, a new revised version of the 1997 banknote series was gradually put into circulation beginning with the 10,000 Ft banknote in 2014 and completed with the 500 Ft banknote in 2019.

In 2022 after [Russia's invasion of Ukraine](79400268.md), the EUR-HUF exchange rate breached the 400 forints per 1 euro line for the first time, but rates at that level or higher lasted until the end of 2022. Some time later, the forint also depreciated against the US dollar, breaching the same line.[^cite_note-WK-15][^cite_note-16] The forint is still more than 400 forints per 1 euro in May 2025.[^cite_note-WK-15] Against the US dollar the forint strengthened more markedly, returning to levels below 400 in November 2022 and below 350 in April 2023.
