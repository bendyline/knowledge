On January 20, 2025, during the first day of [his second term](78298108.md), [United States president](24113.md) [Donald Trump](4848272.md) granted blanket [clemency](288593.md) to all people, nearly 1,600, convicted of or awaiting trial or sentencing for offenses related to the [January 6 United States Capitol attack](66307490.md) that occurred near the end of his [first presidential term](52231341.md). Most of them received full pardons, while the sentences of 14 members of the [Oath Keepers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oath_Keepers) and the [Proud Boys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proud_Boys) were commuted. More than 600 rioters had been convicted of or pleaded guilty to assault of or obstructing law enforcement officers and 170 of using a deadly weapon.[^cite_note-1]

Multiple January 6 defendants have since been involved in additional crimes after being pardoned including [homicide](123451.md), [burglary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burglary), [theft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theft), [stalking](25272420.md), [child pornography](25211885.md), [sexual assault](218407.md) on minors, and [driving under the influence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Driving_under_the_influence). By June 2026, at least 97 defendants had been arrested and charged with other crimes, accounting for approximately 1 in every 16 Capitol attack defendants.[^cite_note-2]

## Background

Main articles: [January 6 United States Capitol attack](66307490.md), [criminal proceedings in the January 6 United States Capitol attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_proceedings_in_the_January_6_United_States_Capitol_attack), and [attempts to overturn the 2020 United States presidential election](65905202.md)

In the aftermath of his [2020 presidential election](48410011.md) loss to [Joe Biden](145422.md), Trump repeatedly made [false claims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_or_misleading_statements_by_Donald_Trump) that widespread [electoral fraud](382165.md) had occurred and that only he himself had legitimately won the election. Although most resulting lawsuits were either dismissed or ruled against by numerous courts,[^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5] Trump nonetheless [conspired](3406102.md) with his [campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump_2020_presidential_campaign) team to [submit documents in several states](71378172.md) (all of which had been won by Biden) which [falsely claimed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Making_false_statements) to be legitimate electoral certificates for President Trump and Vice President [Mike Pence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mike_Pence).[^cite_note-Breuninger_6/22/2022-6] After the submission of these documents, the Trump campaign intended that the [presiding officer of the United States Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presiding_Officer_of_the_United_States_Senate), either [President of the Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_Senate_of_the_United_States) Pence or [President pro tempore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_pro_tempore_of_the_United_States_Senate) [Chuck Grassley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuck_Grassley), would claim to have the unilateral power to reject [electors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Electoral_College#Electors) during the [January 6, 2021, vote counting session](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_United_States_Electoral_College_vote_count); the presiding officer would reject all electors from the several states in which the Trump campaign had submitted false documents, leaving 232 votes for Trump and 222 for Biden, thereby [overturning the election results](65905202.md) in favor of Trump.[^cite_note-NYT-20230808-7][^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9] The plans for January 6 failed to come to fruition after Pence refused to follow the campaign's proposals.[^cite_note-Showdown-10][^cite_note-11]

Trump nevertheless urged his supporters on January 6, 2021, to march to the [Capitol](31979.md) while the joint session of Congress was assembled there to count electoral votes and formalize Biden's victory, culminating with [hundreds storming](66307490.md) the building and interrupting the electoral vote count.[^cite_note-2021-01-11-ap-sinister-12]

By the end of 2021, 725 people had been charged with federal crimes.[^cite_note-insider.com-13][^cite_note-14] That number rose to 1,000 by the second anniversary of the attack,[^cite_note-insider.com-13] to 1,200 by the third anniversary (three-quarters of whom had by then been found guilty)[^cite_note-15][^cite_note-16] and to 1,500 before the fourth anniversary.[^cite_note-17] The [Justice Department](52563.md) documented assaults on over 140 [police officers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_officer) and property damage exceeding US\$2.8 million to the Capitol building and grounds. Approximately 170 defendants had been accused of using deadly or dangerous weapons against law enforcement officers, including [fire extinguishers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_extinguisher) and [bear spray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bear_spray).[^cite_note-CBS_2025-01-20-18]

Bodycam video taken at the U.S. Capitol on January 6, 2021

Throughout the [Biden administration](65732174.md), Trump characterized the January 6 defendants as "[political prisoners](37307.md)" and "hostages."[^cite_note-CBS_2025-01-20-18] He promoted a [revisionist history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_revisionism) of the event by downplaying the severity of the violence and spreading conspiracy theories.[^cite_note-19][^cite_note-20] House Republicans also spread a fringe conspiracy that the FBI orchestrated the attack.[^cite_note-21] On January 29, 2022, when over 760 people had been charged,[^cite_note-insider.com-13] Trump said at a Texas rally that he would be inclined to pardon the rioters if reelected in 2024,[^cite_note-TrumpPardon-22] which he repeated at a Tennessee rally in June 2022.[^cite_note-23] In November, four days before the [midterm elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_United_States_elections), he said: "Let them all go now!"[^cite_note-24] On May 10, 2023, he said he would be "inclined to pardon many of them" while hedging by saying "a couple of them, probably, they got out of control".[^cite_note-25] On September 15, 2023, he said in an interview that aired two days later: "I'm going to look at them, and I certainly might \[pardon them] if I think it's appropriate."[^cite_note-26]

Then-representative [Adam Schiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Schiff), who served on the [House committee that investigated the attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_House_Select_Committee_on_the_January_6_Attack) and was the lead manager during [Trump's first impeachment trial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_impeachment_trial_of_Donald_Trump), told MSNBC in February 2022 that Trump's offer of pardons suggested that he "condoned" the violence.[^cite_note-27] Representative [Pete Aguilar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pete_Aguilar), who was also on the committee, told CNN the same day that he considered Trump's offer to be [witness tampering](1864064.md).[^cite_note-28]

On December 8, 2024, as president–elect, Trump said in an interview with [Kristen Welker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kristen_Welker) on [Meet the Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meet_the_Press), that he would pardon the rioters on his "first day" in office except for any he might deem to be "radical, crazy."[^cite_note-29] Then-[vice president–elect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vice_President-elect_of_the_United_States) [JD Vance](53396477.md) stated that pardons should be given to those who "protested peacefully", and not those who did so violently.[^cite_note-30] Vance initially advocated for a blanket pardon in private but thought Trump would not want to pursue one for political reasons, and was reportedly "100% behind" Trump's decision to grant clemency to all rioters.[^cite_note-Caputo_1222025-31] A week following the pardon, Vance told *[Face the Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_the_Nation)* that he and Trump perceived a "massive denial of due process of liberty" and that the pardon was the "right decision".[^cite_note-2025-01-26_HuffPost-32]

## Presidential clemency

On January 20, 2025, Trump issued a [proclamation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proclamation) titled "Granting Pardons and Commutation of Sentences for Certain Offenses Relating to the Events at or Near the United States Capitol on January 6, 2021". The proclamation categorized prior criminal proceedings as a "grave national injustice" against the American people, and positioned the pardons as beginning a "process of national reconciliation".[^cite_note-press-release-33] Two inside sources stated that Trump made the decision to give blanket pardons at the "last minute" just days before the inauguration,[^cite_note-34] with one advisor saying Trump said "Fuck it: Release 'em all".[^cite_note-Caputo_1222025-31]



Members and associates of Oath Keepers with some identified inside the U.S. Capitol

Citing [Article II, Section 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_Two_of_the_United_States_Constitution#Section_2:_Presidential_powers) of the [Constitution of the United States](31644.md), the proclamation established two distinct categories of clemency for individuals involved in the events at the [United States Capitol](31979.md) on January 6, 2021. The first category of clemency consisted of sentence commutations to time served for fourteen named individuals. These commutations applied to prominent figures in the January 6 events, including [Oath Keepers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oath_Keepers) leader [Stewart Rhodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stewart_Rhodes) with members [Kelly Meggs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelly_Meggs) and [Roberto Minuta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roberto_Minuta), [Proud Boys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proud_Boys) leaders [Ethan Nordean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethan_Nordean), [Jeremy Bertino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Bertino), and [Joseph Biggs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Biggs), and Proud Boy member [Dominic Pezzola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominic_Pezzola), who was the first rioter to breach the Capitol building, all of whom had their sentences reduced to [time served](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_served) "as of January 20, 2025".[^cite_note-press-release-33][^cite_note-MSNBC-35] The second category consisted of "full, complete, and unconditional" pardons granted to every other defendant convicted in relation to the events of January 6.[^cite_note-press-release-33]

The [Attorney General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attorney_General_for_the_District_of_Columbia) was directed to immediately issue pardon certificates to all eligible individuals and ensure the release of any incarcerated persons affected by the pardons. Additionally, the [United States Department of Justice](52563.md) was ordered to dismiss "with [prejudice](649139.md)" all pending indictments related to January 6 conduct, and the [Federal Bureau of Prisons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Bureau_of_Prisons) received explicit orders to implement all Justice Department instructions regarding both the releases and the dismissal of pending cases.[^cite_note-press-release-33]

### Further pardons

On May 13, 2025, [Ed Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ed_Martin_\(Missouri_politician\)) announced he would serve as the [DOJ Pardon Attorney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_the_Pardon_Attorney).[^cite_note-weaponization-36] On May 22, Peter Ticktin of the American Rights Alliance recommended that Martin pursue full pardons for Rhodes, Biggs, Nordean, Rehl and Pezzola, as well as for two other January 6 defendants (Dan Wilson and Elias Costianes) who were in prison for unrelated charges.[^cite_note-37]

On November 9, 2025, Martin announced that Trump had also pardoned the "Alternate Electors of 2020". The list of 77 people who were pardoned included [Rudy Giuliani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudy_Giuliani), [Sidney Powell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidney_Powell), [John Eastman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Eastman), [Jenna Ellis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jenna_Ellis), [Kenneth Chesebro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Chesebro), [Mark Meadows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Meadows), [Boris Epshteyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boris_Epshteyn) and [Jeffrey Clark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeffrey_Clark). As none of the 77 were facing federal charges, the pardon was symbolic.[^cite_note-38]

#### Gained positions

- Jared Lane Wise, who had been an FBI supervisory agent from 2004 through 2017, was arrested in Oregon on misdemeanor charges in May 2023 related to the attack on the Capitol. According to an FBI agent's affidavit, Wise repeatedly shouted "Kill 'em!" as he watched rioters assault officers outside the Capitol. Wise entered the Capitol through the Senate wing door and remained in the building for around nine minutes. [Police body camera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_body_camera) footage captured Wise berating officers outside the Capitol, shouting: "Shame on you! I'm former law enforcement. You're disgusting. You are the [Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazism). You are the [Gestapo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gestapo). You can't see it." After his pardon, he became a counselor to Justice Department pardon attorney [Ed Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ed_Martin_\(Missouri_politician\)) who was overseeing the new [Weaponization Working Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weaponization_Working_Group) investigating the FBI.

## Analysis

Trump's grant of clemency was described by then-Attorney General Merrick Garland as encouraging future political violence,[^cite_note-100][^cite_note-\:0-101] and Trump later suggested the Proud Boys and Oath Keepers may have a place in the political conversation.[^cite_note-102]

Employees in the Justice Department and legal scholars called the pardons an unprecedented and dangerous use of the pardon that created a mockery of federal law enforcement, their work, and the US justice system.[^cite_note-\:0-101] An anonymous senior official in the Justice Department called the pardons an act of personal retribution that gave a "green light" to political violence and indicated approval of the January 6 attacks.[^cite_note-\:0-101]

US District Judge [Royce Lamberth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royce_Lamberth), who was appointed by [Ronald Reagan](25433.md), said during a case on January 25, 2025, that during his time on the bench, he could never recall "such meritless justifications of criminal activity".[^cite_note-TOA_1-103][^cite_note-USDC-104] Randall Eliason, a former federal prosecutor and professor at [George Washington University Law School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington_University_Law_School), called the pardons an abuse of power by Trump, and said they signaled that crimes committed on Trump's behalf would not be punished.[^cite_note-\:0-101] [Bruce Ackerman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Ackerman), a law and political science professor at [Yale Law School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_Law_School), echoed that sentiment, calling the action "a president pardoning his allies for their participation in a violent [coup d'etat](25914154.md)".[^cite_note-\:0-101]

Many of the pardoned rioters had prior convictions and outstanding charges for [rape](7980471.md), child sexual exploitation, [domestic violence](18978563.md), [manslaughter](7397035.md), [drug trafficking](218449.md), and other crimes.[^cite_note-105][^cite_note-106] The Justice Department has made the argument, albeit somewhat inconsistently,[^cite_note-\:1-68] that Trump's pardons may extend to cases involving weapons or other crimes uncovered in January 6-related searches.[^cite_note-107]

## Reactions

A [PBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PBS)/[Marist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marist_Poll) poll conducted a month before the pardons found that 89% of Democrats, 62% of independents, and 30% of Republicans disapproved of them.[^cite_note-Lopez_1222025-108] [Reuters](18998750.md)/[Ipsos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ipsos) polling conducted shortly before and during the pardons found that 58% of people believed Trump should not pardon all those involved in the riot.[^cite_note-109] A month later, in late February, 83% opposed pardoning violent participants.[^cite_note-110] Groups as diverse as the [Cato Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cato_Institute)[^cite_note-111] and [Southern Poverty Law Center](27164.md)[^cite_note-112] also condemned the pardons. [NPR](29697232.md) reported that some Trump voters disapproved of the sweeping pardons, but that "Trump's staunchest supporters \[...] refuse to believe that fellow backers of the president were violent that day" and cited conspiracy theories they read on social media.[^cite_note-Lopez_1222025-108]

### Trump

After Trump issued the pardons, he answered affirmatively when a reporter asked whether he agreed "that it's never acceptable to assault a police officer". When asked to reconcile that opinion with his having pardoned someone who "drove a stun gun into the neck of a D.C. police officer" (this attack was against Officer [Michael Fanone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Fanone)), Trump replied, "Well, I don't know. Was it a pardon? We're looking at commutes. We're looking at pardons." When the reporter confirmed that the person had been pardoned, Trump added, "OK, we'll take a look at everything." He continued speaking for another minute, adding: "These people have already served a long period of time, and I made a decision to grant a pardon."[^cite_note-113][^cite_note-114]

On July 31, 2026, at a cabinet meeting at Camp David, Trump said of the rioters generally: "They went down for an innocent journey."[^cite_note-115]

### Prosecutors

In early June 2025, Greg Rosen, a top federal prosecutor, resigned from the Department of Justice. Within the U.S. Attorney's Office for the District of Columbia, Rosen had led the Capitol Siege section before Trump disbanded it. Rosen told CBS: "The message that \[the pardons] send is that political violence towards a political goal is acceptable in a modern democratic society. That, from my perspective, is anathema to a constitutional republic."[^cite_note-116]

On June 27, 2025, the Justice Department fired two supervising attorneys who had overseen the January 6 prosecutions and a line attorney who had prosecuted the cases.[^cite_note-117]

### Pardoned people

[Norm Pattis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norm_Pattis), defense lawyer for the [Proud Boys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proud_Boys) organizer [Joe Biggs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Biggs), called the pardons "wonderful" and expressed gratitude that Biggs would have his prison sentence cut short by 13 to 14 years.[^cite_note-apnews_update-118] On June 6, 2025, Biggs, Nordean, Rehl, and Pezzola (who had had their sentences commuted) and Tarrio (who had been pardoned) sued the federal government. They sought \$100 million in restitution for what they claimed had been, under the Biden administration, "egregious and systemic abuse of the legal system and the United States Constitution to punish and oppress political allies of President Trump."[^cite_note-119]

[Jacob Chansley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_Chansley)'s immediate reaction to being pardoned was to post to X that he would buy guns.[^cite_note-120] Stewart Rhodes told reporters that his actions on January 6 were justified and called for the prosecution of the Capitol police who testified against him at his trial and the Justice Department lawyers involved in his case.[^cite_note-121] Tarrio and Rhodes said they wanted Trump to seek revenge on their behalf.[^cite_note-122] On January 22, Rhodes appeared on Capitol Hill and delivered a speech defending his actions.[^cite_note-123]

[Pamela Hemphill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pamela_Hemphill) objected to the pardons, saying: "We were wrong that day. We broke the law. There should be no pardons", that accepting the pardon would "contribute to their gaslighting and false narrative" in an attempt to "rewrite history", and that the Justice Department was not weaponized against Trump supporters.[^cite_note-124][^cite_note-CNN:_A_slap_in_the_face-125] Hemphill officially rejected her pardon in 2025 and has since been speaking out against the disinformation surrounding the 2020 election and events of January 6.[^cite_note-126]

[Jason Riddle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jason_Riddle) also objected to his pardon, telling [ABC News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABC_News_\(United_States\)) in January: "I am guilty of the crimes I have committed and accept the consequences. It is thanks to those consequences I now have a happy and fruitful existence." He also expressed resentment toward Trump, stating: "I don't need to obsess over a [narcissistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narcissism) bully to feel better about myself. Trump can shove his pardon up his ass."[^cite_note-127] To help him formally reject the pardon, the office of Senator [Maggie Hassan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maggie_Hassan) contacted the federal pardon office.[^cite_note-128]

Rebecca Lavrenz, known on social media as the "J6 Praying Grandma", also declined her pardon, saying she planned to appeal her case and get her criminal record cleared. Lavrenz was convicted of four misdemeanor charges and sentenced to a year of probation, including six months of house arrest, and fined \$103,000.[^cite_note-129]

In December 2025, personal injury attorney [Mark McCloskey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_McCloskey) submitted claims on behalf of about 400 former rioters, some who were seeking millions of dollars. "They can't get jobs, because soon as they apply for a job, they get Googled", McCloskey said.[^cite_note-130]

Tarrio repeated his position at a march on the Capitol on January 6, 2026, saying that the pardons were "not enough" and that the Justice Department should prosecute those who had prosecuted January 6 defendants.[^cite_note-131]

### Republicans

Some Republican leaders, including Senators [Lindsey Graham](363279.md), [Thom Tillis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thom_Tillis), [James Lankford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Lankford), [Lisa Murkowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisa_Murkowski), [Bill Cassidy](9386382.md), and [Susan Collins](375195.md), also expressed disagreement with the mass pardon.[^cite_note-144][^cite_note-145][^cite_note-146]

At the confirmation hearing of Acting U.S. Attorney General [Todd Blanche](74006584.md) on July 15, 2026, Senator [Dick Durbin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dick_Durbin) (a Democrat) asked Blanche for his opinion on the pardon. Blanche replied: "The Constitution gives the president the full power to pardon anybody for any reason he wants, and so I don’t question President Trump's authority or his decision to do so."[^cite_note-151]

## Defendants involved in further crimes and incidents

Multiple January 6 defendants have been involved in additional crimes before or after being pardoned,[^cite_note-152] including [homicide](123451.md), [burglary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burglary), [theft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theft), [stalking](25272420.md), [child pornography](25211885.md), [sexual assault](218407.md) on minors, and [driving under the influence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Driving_under_the_influence).

[Sedition Hunters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedition_Hunters), an [online community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_community) of [open-source intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-source_intelligence) investigators who identified hundreds of participants in the Capitol attack, has an incomplete database of rioters with post-January 6 arrests or convictions.[^cite_note-153]

In December 2025, the watchdog organization [Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizens_for_Responsibility_and_Ethics_in_Washington) reported that at least 33 defendants had been rearrested, charged, or sentenced for other crimes since the Capitol attack—four of whose alleged offenses occurred in 2025 after the pardon—while another, days after the pardon, was fatally shot during a [traffic stop](1430646.md) when he [resisted arrest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resisting_arrest) while armed.[^cite_note-154] Former special counsel [Jack Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Smith_\(lawyer\)) mentioned this in his public testimony before Congress in January 2026, saying it was "reasonable" to expect that the pardoned rioters would continue to commit crime.[^cite_note-155]

In June 2026, the nonprofit online multimedia publication *[Lawfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawfare_\(website\))* reported that at least 97 of the individuals granted clemency by Trump had been arrested for, charged with, or convicted of crimes subsequent to the events of January 6. The publication assessed that nearly 1 in 16 Capitol attack participants had re-offended after receiving clemency, with at least 14 being charged with sex crimes or crimes related to child sexual abuse material, six facing domestic violence charges, and at least 20 being charged with driving under the influence or public intoxication.[^cite_note-156]
