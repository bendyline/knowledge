The **Second Congo War**,[^cite_note-9] also known as **Africa's World War[^cite_note-10]** or the **Great War of Africa**, was a major conflict that began on 2 August 1998 in the [Democratic Republic of the Congo](76762.md) (DRC), just over a year after the [First Congo War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Congo_War). The war initially erupted when Congolese president [Laurent-Désiré Kabila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurent-D%C3%A9sir%C3%A9_Kabila) turned against his former allies from [Rwanda](25645.md) and [Uganda](31816.md), who had helped him seize power.[^cite_note-'I_Will_Not_Leave_This_Place'-11] The conflict expanded as Kabila rallied a coalition of other countries to his defense. The war drew in nine African nations and approximately 25 armed groups, making it one of the largest wars in African history.[^cite_note-Bowers2-12]

Although a peace agreement was signed in 2002, and the war officially ended on 18 July 2003 with the establishment of the [Transitional Government of the Democratic Republic of the Congo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transitional_Government_of_the_Democratic_Republic_of_the_Congo) and the raising of the DRC's flag in the rebel-controlled city of [Goma](1210483.md),[^cite_note-13] violence has persisted in various regions, particularly in the east,[^cite_note-\:0-14] through ongoing conflicts such as the [Lord's Resistance Army insurgency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord's_Resistance_Army_insurgency) and the [Kivu](14687290.md) and [Ituri conflicts](1585788.md).

The Second Congo War and its aftermath caused an estimated 5.4 million deaths, primarily due to disease, malnutrition and war crimes,[^cite_note-15] making it the deadliest conflict since [World War II](32927.md), according to a 2008 report by the [International Rescue Committee](196452.md).[^cite_note-reuters-16] The conflict also [displaced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Displaced_person) approximately 2 million people, forcing them to flee their homes or seek [asylum](45547.md) in neighboring countries.[^cite_note-\:0-14] Additionally, the war was heavily influenced and funded by the trade of [conflict minerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conflict_minerals_law), which continues to fuel violence in the region.[^cite_note-'I_Will_Not_Leave_This_Place'-11][^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18]

## Background

### First Congo War

Main article: [First Congo War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Congo_War)

The [First Congo War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Congo_War) began in 1996, as [Rwanda](25645.md) increasingly expressed concern that Hutu members of [Republican Rally for Democracy in Rwanda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rassemblement_D%C3%A9mocratique_pour_le_Rwanda) (RDR) militias were carrying out cross-border raids from what was then [Zaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaire), and planning an invasion of Rwanda.[^cite_note-19] These mostly [Hutu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutu) militias had entrenched themselves in [refugee camps](438046.md) in eastern Zaire, where many had fled to escape the [Tutsi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tutsi)-dominated [Rwandan Patriotic Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rwandan_Patriotic_Front) (RPF) in the aftermath of 1994's [Rwandan genocide](676659.md).[^cite_note-20]

The [Tutsi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tutsi)-dominated RPF government of Rwanda, which had gained power in July 1994, protested this as a violation of Rwandan territorial integrity, and began arming the ethnically Tutsi [Banyamulenge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banyamulenge) of eastern Zaire.[^cite_note-21] The [Mobutu regime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobutu_government) of Zaire vigorously denounced this intervention, though it possessed neither the military capability to halt it, nor the [political capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_capital) to attract international assistance.

### Kabila's march to Kinshasa

With active support from [Uganda](31816.md), Rwanda, and [Angola](701.md), the Tutsi forces of [Laurent-Désiré Kabila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurent-D%C3%A9sir%C3%A9_Kabila) moved methodically down the [Congo River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congo_River), encountering only light resistance from the poorly trained, ill-disciplined forces of [Mobutu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobutu_Sese_Seko)'s crumbling regime. The bulk of Kabila's fighters were Tutsi – many of them veterans of various conflicts in the continent's [Great Lakes region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Great_Lakes). Kabila himself provided credibility, largely reflecting his long-time political opposition to Mobutu, and his role as a follower of [Patrice Lumumba](49240.md).[^cite_note-Kettle-22]

Lumumba had been the first prime minister of the independent Congo, who had been executed by a combination of internal and external forces in a murder ordered by the [CIA](5183633.md),[^cite_note-Kettle-22] in January 1961, ultimately being replaced by Mobutu in 1965.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-23] Kabila had declared himself a [Marxist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxist), and an admirer of [Mao Zedong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mao_Zedong). He had been waging armed rebellion in eastern Zaire for more than three decades (though [Che Guevara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Che_Guevara) – in his account of the early years of the conflict – portrayed him as an uncommitted and uninspiring leader).[^cite_note-ReferenceA-23]

In December 1996, near the end of the [Great Lakes refugee crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Lakes_refugee_crisis), Kabila's army began a slow movement west, taking control of border towns and [mines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineral_industry_of_Africa) and solidifying control. There were reports of massacres and of brutal repression by the rebel army. A UN human-rights investigator published statements from witnesses who claimed that Kabila's Alliance of Democratic Forces for the Liberation of Congo ([ADFLC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ADFLC)) had committed massacres, with the advancing army killing as many as 60,000 civilians. The ADFLC strenuously denied this claim.[^cite_note-Essays-24] Roberto Garreton stated that his investigation in the town of [Goma](1210483.md) turned up allegations of disappearances, torture, and killings; he quoted [Moïse Nyarugabo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mo%C3%AFse_Nyarugabo?action=edit\&redlink=1), an aide to Mobutu, as saying that such casualties should be expected in wartime.

Kabila's forces, with support of Rwanda, launched an offensive in earlier October 1996 in South Kivu, and demanded that the Kinshasa government surrender. The rebels took [Kasenga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kasenga) on 27 March 1997. The government denied the rebels' success, starting a long pattern of false statements from the defense minister on the progress and conduct of the war. Negotiations were proposed in late March, and on 2 April a new [Prime Minister of Zaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_minister_of_the_Democratic_Republic_of_the_Congo), [Étienne Tshisekedi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89tienne_Tshisekedi)—a longtime rival of Mobutu—was installed.[^cite_note-Essays-24] Kabila, by this point in control of roughly one-quarter of the country, dismissed this as irrelevant, and threatened Tshisekedi that if he accepted the post he would have no part in a new government.

The ADFLC made consistent progress in its advance from the east throughout April 1997, and by May its troops had reached the outskirts of [Kinshasa](45773.md).[^cite_note-dvr-25] Mobutu fled Kinshasa on 16 May, and the "libérateurs" (i. e., liberators) entered the capital without serious resistance.[^cite_note-dvr-25] Mobutu fled the country, and died in exile in [Morocco](19291.md), four months later. Kabila proclaimed himself president on 17 May 1997, immediately ordering a violent crackdown to restore order, and began attempting a reorganisation of the nation.

### Unwelcome support from other African nations

When Kabila gained control of the capital, in May 1997, he faced substantial obstacles to governing the country. He renamed the state, from Zaire, to [Democratic Republic of Congo](76762.md).[^cite_note-26] In addition to political jostling among various groups seeking to gain power, and an enormous external debt, his foreign backers proved unwilling to leave when asked. The conspicuous Rwandan presence in the capital rankled many Congolese, who began to see Kabila as a pawn of foreign powers.[^cite_note-28]

Tensions reached new heights on 14 July 1998, when Kabila dismissed his Rwandan chief of staff [James Kabarebe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Kabarebe), replacing him with a native Congolese, [chief of staff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_of_Staff_of_the_Armed_Forces_of_the_Democratic_Republic_of_the_Congo) [Célestin Kifwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C%C3%A9lestin_Kifwa?action=edit\&redlink=1).[^cite_note-29] Although this move chilled what was already a troubled relationship with Rwanda, Kabila softened the blow by making Kabarebe the military adviser to his successor.

Two weeks later, Kabila reversed to his previous decision, thanking Rwanda for its help, and ordering all Rwandan and Ugandan military forces to leave the country.[^cite_note-30] Within 24 hours, Rwandan military advisers living in Kinshasa were unceremoniously flown out. Those most alarmed by this order were the [Banyamulenge Tutsi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banyamulenge) of eastern Congo; their tensions with neighboring ethnic groups had been a contributing factor in the genesis of the [First Congo War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Congo_War), and they were also used by Rwanda to affect events across the border in the DRC.

## Course of the war

### 1998–1999

Further information: [Operation Kitona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Kitona)



The situation in the Congo in August 1998, by the time of [Operation Kitona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Kitona):\

**Red** – [Rwanda](25645.md)'s allies\
**Yellow** – [Uganda](31816.md)'s allies

On 2 August 1998, the Banyamulenge in Goma erupted into rebellion. Rwanda offered them immediate assistance, and early in August a well-armed rebel group, the [Rally for Congolese Democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rally_for_Congolese_Democracy) (RCD)—composed primarily of Banyamulenge and backed by Rwanda and Uganda—emerged. This group quickly came to dominate the resource-rich eastern provinces, and based its operations in Goma. The RCD quickly took control of the towns of [Bukavu](448570.md) and [Uvira](2774222.md) in the [Kivus](1200745.md).[^cite_note-Clark_2002_128-31]

The Tutsi-led Rwandan government allied with Uganda. Burundi also retaliated, occupying a portion of northeastern Congo. To help remove the occupying Rwandans, President Kabila enlisted the aid of refugee Hutu in eastern Congo and began to agitate public opinion against the Tutsi, resulting in several public [lynchings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynching) in the streets of Kinshasa.[^cite_note-Clark_2002_128-31] On 12 August a loyalist army major broadcast a message urging resistance from a radio station in [Bunia](1925095.md) in eastern Congo: "People must bring a machete, a spear, an arrow, a hoe, spades, rakes, nails, truncheons, electric irons, barbed wire, stones, and the like, in order, dear listeners, to kill the Rwandan Tutsis."[^cite_note-BBC_hate_messages-32]

The Rwandan government also challenged current borders by claiming a substantial part of eastern Congo as "historically Rwandan". The Rwandans alleged that Kabila was organising a [genocide](12441.md) against their Tutsi brethren in the [Kivu](1200745.md) region. The degree to which Rwandan intervention was motivated by a desire to protect the Banyamulenge, as opposed to using them as a smokescreen for its regional aspirations after ousting Mobutu, is still being debated.[^cite_note-33]

In a bold move, Rwandan soldiers under the command of James Kabarebe [hijacked three planes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Kitona) and flew them to the government base of [Kitona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitona) on the Atlantic coast.[^cite_note-34] The planes landed in the middle of the Kitona base, but the motley collection of troops there (ex-FAZ, but also Angolan [UNITA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNITA) elements and former [Pascal Lissouba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal_Lissouba) militiamen from [Brazzaville](52710.md)) were in poor condition and in no condition to fight unless given food and weapons.[^cite_note-35] They were quickly won over to the Rwandan side.[^cite_note-Cornell_University_Press-36]

More towns in the east and around Kitona fell in rapid succession, as the combined RCD, Rwandan and rebel soldiers overwhelmed government forces amid a flurry of ineffectual diplomatic efforts by various African nations. By 13 August, less than two weeks after the revolt had begun, rebels held the [Inga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inga_dams) [hydroelectric](381399.md) station that provided power to Kinshasa as well as the port of [Matadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matadi) through which most of Kinshasa's food passed. The diamond center of [Kisangani](588207.md) fell into rebel hands on 23 August and forces advancing from the east had begun to threaten Kinshasa by late August.[^cite_note-Cornell_University_Press-36] Uganda, while retaining joint support of the RCD with Rwanda, also created a rebel group that it supported exclusively, the [Movement for the Liberation of Congo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Movement_for_the_Liberation_of_Congo) (MLC).[^cite_note-37]

Despite the movement of the front lines, fighting continued throughout the country. Even as rebel forces advanced on Kinshasa, government forces continued to battle for control of towns in the east of the country. The Hutu militants with whom Kabila was co-operating were also a significant force in the east. Nevertheless, the fall of the capital and of Kabila, who had spent the previous weeks desperately seeking support from various African nations and [Cuba](5042481.md), seemed increasingly certain.

The rebel offensive was abruptly reversed as Kabila's diplomatic efforts bore fruit. The first African countries to respond to Kabila's request for help were fellow members of the [Southern African Development Community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_African_Development_Community) (SADC). While officially the SADC members are bound to a mutual defence treaty in the case of outside aggression, many member nations took a neutral stance to the conflict. However, the governments of [Namibia](21292.md), [Zimbabwe](34399.md) and [Angola](701.md) supported the Kabila government after a meeting in [Harare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harare), Zimbabwe, on 19 August.[^cite_note-38] Two more nations joined the conflict for Kabila in the following weeks: [Chad](5488.md) and [Sudan](27421.md).

A multisided war began. In September 1998, Zimbabwean forces flown into Kinshasa held off a rebel advance that reached the outskirts of the capital, while Angolan units attacked northward from its borders and eastward from the Angolan territory of [Cabinda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinda_\(province\)), against the besieging rebel forces. This intervention by various nations saved the Kabila government and pushed the rebel front lines away from the capital. However, it was unable to defeat the rebel forces, and the advance threatened to escalate into direct conflict with the national armies of Uganda and Rwanda.[^cite_note-Scherrer_2002_274–275-39]

In November 1998 a new Ugandan-backed rebel group, the [Movement for the Liberation of Congo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Movement_for_the_Liberation_of_Congo), was reported in the north of the country. On 6 November Rwandan President [Paul Kagame](393593.md) admitted for the first time that Rwandan forces were assisting the RCD rebels for security reasons, apparently after a request by [Nelson Mandela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nelson_Mandela) to advance peace talks.[^cite_note-Scherrer_2002_274–275-39] On 18 January 1999, Rwanda, Uganda, Angola, Namibia and Zimbabwe agreed on a ceasefire at a summit at [Windhoek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windhoek), [Namibia](21292.md) but the RCD was not invited. Fighting thus continued.

Outside of Africa, most states remained neutral, but urged an end to the violence.

### 1999–2000

Main articles: [Ituri conflict](1585788.md), [Lusaka Ceasefire Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lusaka_Ceasefire_Agreement), and [Six-Day War (2000)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six-Day_War_\(2000\))

On 5 April 1999, tensions within the RCD about the dominance of the Banyamulenge reached a boiling point when RCD leader [Ernest Wamba dia Wamba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Wamba_dia_Wamba) moved his base from Goma to Uganda-controlled Kisangani to head a breakaway faction known as RCD-Kisangani, which later became The [Forces for Renewal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forces_for_Renewal).[^cite_note-40] A further sign of a break occurred when President [Yoweri Museveni](293409.md) of Uganda and Kabila signed a ceasefire accord on 18 April in [Sirte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sirte), [Libya](17633.md), following the mediation of Libyan leader [Muammar al-Gaddafi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muammar_al-Gaddafi), but both the RCD and Rwanda refused to take part.[^cite_note-41][^cite_note-42] which was retracted and took part with Chad and Sudan.&#x20;

On 16 May Wamba was ousted as head of the RCD in favour of a pro-Rwanda figure, Dr. [Emile Ilunga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emile_Ilunga).[^cite_note-43] Seven days later the various factions of the RCD clashed over control of Kisangani. On 8 June rebel factions met to try to create a common front against Kabila. Despite these efforts, the occupying armed forces of Uganda recreated the province of [Ituri](1545860.md), based on the province of Kibali-Ituri which previously existed from 1962 to 1966 in the Republic of the Congo. Perceived favoritism to the ethnic Hema by the Ugandan forces sparked the ethnic clash of the [Ituri conflict](1585788.md), sometimes referred to as a "war within a war".[^cite_note-44]

Nevertheless, diplomatic circumstances contributed to the first ceasefire of the war. In July 1999 the [Lusaka Ceasefire Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lusaka_Ceasefire_Agreement) was signed by the six warring countries (Democratic Republic of Congo, Angola, Namibia, Zimbabwe, Rwanda and Uganda) and, on 1 August, the MLC (the RCD refused to sign until 31 August).[^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46] Under the terms of the agreement, forces from all sides, under a Joint Military Commission, would co-operate in tracking, disarming and documenting all armed groups in the Congo, especially those forces identified with the 1994 Rwandan genocide.

Few provisions were made to actually disarm the militias. The United Nations [Security Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UN_Security_Council) deployed about 90 [liaison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liaison_officer) personnel in August 1999 to support the [ceasefire](592591.md). However, in the following months all sides accused the others of repeatedly breaking the cease-fire, and it became clear that small incidents could trigger attacks.

Tensions between Uganda and Rwanda escalated as units of the [Uganda People's Defense Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uganda_People's_Defense_Force) and the [Rwandan Patriotic Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rwandan_Patriotic_Army) [clashed in Kisangani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six-Day_War_\(2000\)) on the morning of 7 August.[^cite_note-47][^cite_note-\:7-48][^cite_note-\:9-49] Fighting broke out again between the two armies on the evening of 14 August; fighting occurred throughout much of Kisangani, including in the airport and on major roads.[^cite_note-\:7-48][^cite_note-\:9-49] The conflict lasted until 17 August, when a ceasefire was called that day.[^cite_note-\:7-48] Both sides used heavy weapons during these clashes.[^cite_note-\:9-49] As reported by the [International Crisis Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Crisis_Group), the fighting was a result of differences over the objectives and strategies used during the war.[^cite_note-50]<sup>B</sup>

In November, government-controlled television in Kinshasa claimed that Kabila's army had been rebuilt and was now prepared to fulfil its "mission to liberate" the country. Rwandan-supported rebel forces launched a major offensive and approached Kinshasa but were eventually repelled.

By 24 February 2000, the UN authorised a force of 5,537 troops, the United Nations Organization Mission in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (known by the French [acronym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acronym), [MONUC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MONUC)), to monitor the cease-fire.[^cite_note-51] Fighting continued between rebels and government forces and between Rwandan and Ugandan forces. Numerous clashes and offensives occurred throughout the country, most notably heavy fighting between Uganda and Rwanda in Kisangani in May and June 2000.

On 9 August 2000 a government offensive in Equateur Province along the [Ubangi River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ubangi_River) was repulsed near [Libenge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libenge) by MLC forces.[^cite_note-\:7-48] Military operations and diplomatic efforts made by the UN, [African Union](10634933.md) and [Southern African Development Community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_African_Development_Community) failed to make any headway.

### 2001

#### Assassination of Laurent-Désiré Kabila

Main article: [Assassination of Laurent-Désiré Kabila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assassination_of_Laurent-D%C3%A9sir%C3%A9_Kabila)



Rebel soldiers photographed in [Gbadolite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gbadolite).

On 16 January 2001, [Laurent-Désiré Kabila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurent-D%C3%A9sir%C3%A9_Kabila) was shot and killed at the [Palais de Marbre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palais_de_Marbre) in Kinshasa.[^cite_note-52] The government initially stated that Kabila was wounded but still alive when he was flown to [Zimbabwe](34399.md) for intensive care.[^cite_note-kabassass-53] The circumstances of his assassination are unclear, and have been the subject of considerable rumours and disagreements. The consensus is that Kabila was shot by one of his bodyguards, the 18-year-old [Rashidi Mizele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashidi_Mizele), a *[kadogo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_soldiers_in_the_Democratic_Republic_of_the_Congo)* (child soldier). Mizele had previously been misidentified as Rashidi Kasereka.[^cite_note-54]

Two days later the government announced on state television that the desperate attempt by Zimbabwean medical personnel to save Kabila had failed and that Kabila had died from his wounds.[^cite_note-55] His remains were returned for a state funeral on 26 January 2001.

Shortly after the assassination, the French newspaper *[Le Monde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Monde)* published a story in which self-identified assassination plotters revealed documents and details of the plot to murder Kabila.[^cite_note-\:10-56] The plotters were mainly *kadogos* who had been under Kabila's command since 1996, and were aggrieved over their poor treatment.[^cite_note-\:11-57] The catalyst for the assassination appeared to be the execution of 47 *kadogos* accused of plotting against Kabila.[^cite_note-\:11-57] The execution, witnessed by Kabila, took place the day before his assassination.[^cite_note-\:10-56]

While the role of the disgruntled *kadogos* is relatively well-established, there have been some attempts to argue that they were under the influence of external actors who sought to overthrow Kabila. Some Congolese officials attempted to implicate their principal enemies by alleging that the Rwandans masterminded the operation.[^cite_note-\:13-58] Some observers have lent credibility to these allegations, including [Al-Jazeera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Jazeera_English)'s documentary, "[Murder in Kinshasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murder_in_Kinshasa)", which alleges that a Lebanese diamond dealer allegedly organised the logistics of the organisation.[^cite_note-59][^cite_note-60] Others have speculated that the Angolans (due to Kabila's complicity in helping the Angolan rebel group [UNITA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNITA) channel funds through the DRC) or even the Americans were involved in the assassination.[^cite_note-\:13-58] There is, as yet, no proof that Mizele or the *kadogos* were acting under orders from an external source.

#### Joseph Kabila becomes president

By unanimous vote of the Congolese parliament, his son, [Joseph Kabila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Kabila), was sworn in as president to replace him. That he won the election was largely due to Robert Mugabe's backing, and the fact that most parliamentarians had been handpicked by the elder Kabila.[^cite_note-61] In February, the new president met [Rwandan](25645.md) President [Paul Kagame](393593.md) in the United States.[^cite_note-62] Rwanda, [Uganda](31816.md), and the rebels agreed to a [UN](31769.md) pullout plan. Uganda and Rwanda began pulling troops back from the front line.[^cite_note-63] Joseph Kabila has been described as "a more adept political leader than his father".[^cite_note-\:0-14] As Chris Talbot notes, an article in *[The Washington Post](102226.md)* "favourably contrasted Joseph Kabila – Western-educated and English-speaking – with his father."[^cite_note-\:4-64] The author of the *Washington Post* article writes that Joseph Kabila gave diplomats "hope that things have changed", in contrast to Laurent-Désiré Kabila, who "stood as the major impediment to a peaceful settlement of the war launched in August 1998 to unseat him."[^cite_note-65] A peace accord Laurent signed in the summer of 1999, the [Lusaka Ceasefire Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lusaka_Ceasefire_Agreement), "remained unfulfilled largely because he kept staging new offensives while blocking deployment of U.N. peacekeepers in government-held territory." To compare, according to an analyst from the London-based [Economist Intelligence Unit](15303116.md), "The only obstruction had been Kabila because the \[Lusaka] accord called for the government's democratic transition and that was a threat to his power."[^cite_note-\:4-64]

#### UN investigates illegal exploitation of minerals

In April 2001, a UN panel of experts investigated the illegal exploitation of diamonds, [cobalt](24580536.md), [coltan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coltan), gold and other lucrative resources in the Congo. The report accused Rwanda, Uganda and Zimbabwe of systematically exploiting Congolese resources and recommended the Security Council impose [sanctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_sanctions).[^cite_note-66]

### 2002



An estimate of territory held by factions in 2001 to 2003.

In 2002, Rwanda's situation in the war began to worsen. Many members of the RCD either gave up fighting or decided to join Kabila's government. Moreover, the [Banyamulenge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banyamulenge), the backbone of Rwanda's militia forces, became increasingly tired of control from [Kigali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kigali) and the unending conflict.[^cite_note-\:1-67] A number of them mutinied, leading to violent clashes between them and Rwandan forces.[^cite_note-\:1-67]

At the same time, the western Congo was becoming increasingly secure under the younger Kabila. [International aid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_aid) was resumed as inflation was brought under control.

In March, the RCD-Goma faction captured [Moliro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moliro), a town located on the coast of [Lake Tanganyika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Tanganyika), from government forces. According to said faction, the town was shelled by government gunboats the following day in response. The capture was seen as a violation of the Lusaka Ceasefire Agreement.[^cite_note-\:8-68]

#### Peace agreements (April–December 2002)

Under the leadership of [South Africa](17416221.md), peace talks held in that country between April and December 2002 led to the signing of a "comprehensive peace agreement."[^cite_note-\:0-14] The [Sun City Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_City_Agreement) was formalised on 19 April 2002.[^cite_note-69] It was a framework for providing the Congo with a unified, multiparty government and democratic elections. However, critics noted that there were no stipulations regarding the unification of the army, which weakened the effectiveness of the agreement. There were several reported breaches of the Sun City agreement, but it had seen a reduction in the fighting. The agreement came about due to the military stalemate rather than any actual desire for peace. The rebels, who lacked ideology, large civilian constituency, and a unified cause, apart from their hopes of profiting politically and economically from the dissolution of the Congo, were stuck in a perpetual war, especially following the inauguration of the [Bush administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidency_of_George_W._Bush), which had turned a cold-shoulder to Rwanda. The [Clinton administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinton_administration) had, throughout 1996 and 2000, supported or at least tolerated the invasions of the Congo,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPrunier2009305-70] as the country felt deeply embarrassed by its inaction during the [Rwandan genocide](676659.md).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPrunier200934-71] Under [George W. Bush](3414021.md), this support began to wane as the United States grew tired of Rwanda exploiting its "genocide guilt credit" to justify what increasingly appeared to be a policy of deliberate deception in the Congo.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPrunier2009293-72] For the exhausted Congolese government, whose weak 120,000 man army included a large number of [ghost soldiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost_soldiers), the statement by the Angolan government that its support was about to end, whom along with Zimbabwe (whose support it feared might also wear out over time), it desperately relied on, raised fears in the government about the possibility of the government's armed forces collapsing from lack of support.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPrunier2009305–306-73]

On 30 July 2002, Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of Congo signed a peace deal known as the [Pretoria Accord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pretoria_Accord) after five days of talks in [Pretoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pretoria), South Africa.[^cite_note-74] The talks centered on two issues. One was the withdrawal of the estimated 20,000 Rwandan soldiers in the Congo.[^cite_note-\:2-75] The other was the rounding up of the ex-Rwandan soldiers and the dismantling of the Hutu militia known as [Interahamwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interahamwe), which took part in Rwanda's 1994 genocide and continues to operate out of eastern Congo.[^cite_note-\:2-75] Rwanda had previously refused to withdraw until the Hutu militias were dealt with.

Signed on 6 September, the [Luanda Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luanda_Agreement) formalised peace between Congo and Uganda.[^cite_note-\:3-76] The treaty aimed to get Uganda to withdraw its troops from [Bunia](1925095.md) and to improve the relationship between the two countries,[^cite_note-\:3-76] but implementation proved troublesome. Eleven days later, the first Rwandan soldiers were withdrawn from the eastern DRC. On 5 October Rwanda announced the completion of its withdrawal; [MONUC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MONUC) confirmed the departure of over 20,000 Rwandan soldiers.[^cite_note-77]

On 21 October, the UN published its expert panel's report on the pillage of natural resources by armed groups. Both Rwanda and Uganda rejected accusations that senior political and military figures were involved in illicit trafficking of plundered resources. Zimbabwe Defense Minister [Sydney Sekeramayi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Sekeramayi) says the Zimbabwean military withdrew from the DRC in October 2002, but in June 2006 reporters said a 50-man force had stayed in the DRC to protect Kabila.[^cite_note-zim-78]

On 17 December 2002, the Congolese parties of the Inter Congolese Dialogue (the national government, the MLC, the RCD, the RCD-ML, the RCD-N, the domestic political opposition, representatives of civil society and the Mai Mai) signed the Global and All-Inclusive Agreement. The agreement described a plan for transitional governance that would have resulted in legislative and presidential elections within two years of its signing and marked the formal end of the Second Congo War.

### Pygmy genocide (2002–2003)

Main article: [Effacer le tableau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effacer_le_tableau)

At the end of 2002 through January 2003, around 60,000 [Pygmy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pygmy_peoples) civilians and 10,000 combatants were killed in an extermination campaign known as "[Effacer le tableau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effacer_le_tableau)" by the [Movement for the Liberation of Congo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Movement_for_the_Liberation_of_Congo).[^cite_note-SeshadriICE1-79] Human rights activists have made demands for the massacre to be recognized as a [genocide](12441.md).[^cite_note-80]

### 2003 onwards: Transitional Government

Main article: [Transitional Government of the Democratic Republic of the Congo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transitional_Government_of_the_Democratic_Republic_of_the_Congo)

On 18 July 2003, the [Transitional Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transitional_Government_of_the_Democratic_Republic_of_the_Congo) came into being as specified in the Global and All-Inclusive Agreement out of the warring parties. The agreement obliges the parties to carry out a plan to reunify the country, disarm and integrate the warring parties and hold elections. There were numerous problems, resulting in continued instability in much of the country and a delay in the scheduled national elections from June 2005 to July 2006.

The main cause for the continued weakness of the Transitional Government is the refusal by the former warring parties to give up power to a centralised and neutral national administration. Some belligerents maintained administrative and military [command-and-control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Command_and_Control_\(Military\)) structures separate from that of the Transitional Government, but as the [International Crisis Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Crisis_Group) has reported, these have gradually been reduced. A high level of official corruption siphoning money away from civil servants, soldiers and infrastructure projects causes further instability.

On 30 July 2006 the first elections were held in the DRC after the populace approved a new constitution.[^cite_note-81] A second round was held on 30 October.[^cite_note-82]

## Foreign supporters of the Congo government

### Zimbabwe



Belligerents of the Second Congo War:\
**Violet** – Democratic Republic of the Congo\
**Orange** – anti-DRC coalition\
**Dark blue** – pro-DRC coalition\
**Light blue** – DRC allies, not directly involved in the war.\
**Green** – DRC supporters politically

[Robert Mugabe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Mugabe)'s administration dispatched elements of the [Zimbabwe National Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zimbabwe_National_Army) to the Democratic Republic of the Congo in 1998.[^cite_note-zim-78] Mugabe, perhaps the most ardent supporter of intervention on Kabila's behalf, was the only major player involved in the conflict able to marshal a reasonably modern and experienced air force. Zimbabwe's military was also regarded as being one of the more well-equipped and professional of the region; it was the decisive factor in the outcome of the war and exhibited outstanding tactical proficiency.[^cite_note-83]

Zimbabwean strategy revolved around defending the person of Laurent Kabila only with allied forces, as Congolese forces were thought to be unreliable, then retake important settlements, and expel the rebels from the Kinshasa region. Mugabe's initial buildup in Kinshasa consisted of special forces along with some paratroops, reportedly numbering between 600 and 1,000. By August 1998, two more [battalions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battalion) had been dispatched. They were accompanied by some [Soviet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union)-manufactured [T-54/55](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-54/55) tanks, [Crocodile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crocodile_Armoured_Personnel_Carrier) armoured personnel carriers, and [EE-9 Cascavel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EE-9_Cascavel) scout cars flown into the capital on [Angolan Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angolan_Air_Force) planes. The contingent grew to 3,800 around November, and peaked at 12,000 in January 2001. The Zimbabweans began leaving in 2002 and had completely withdrawn by the end of the year.[^cite_note-84] Prior to this deployment, Zimbabwe had built up an apparently potent brigade-sized, [combined arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combined_arms), reaction force with efficient air support and professional competence; however the prolonged operations in the Congo are said to have damaged its credibility.[^cite_note-Zim1-85]

The [Air Force of Zimbabwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Force_of_Zimbabwe) made particularly effective use of its air power, hitting rebel and Rwandan offensives on [Mbuji-Mayi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mbuji-Mayi) with repeated strikes by [BAE Hawks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BAE_Hawk) and [Hawker Hunters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawker_Hunter). It also sustained heavy losses during the conflict, including three of its six [Mil Mi-24 Hind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mil_Mi-24_Hind) helicopters, a transport aircraft, and an unidentified interceptor, probably a Chinese [Chengdu J-7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chengdu_J-7).[^cite_note-BBC1-86] Despite the effectiveness of its highly mobile, big-gunned [Eland-90](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eland_Mk7) and Cascavel armoured cars, Zimbabwean ground forces also lost a significant number, either captured or destroyed by the rebel coalition.[^cite_note-87]

### Namibia

Under the direction of President [Sam Nujoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Nujoma), Namibia became involved in the Congo on behalf of its commitment to the [Southern African Development Community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_African_Development_Community). Nujoma, a longtime ally of Kabila, claimed he could not refuse the requests for military assistance from Zimbabwe and Angola. In February 1999, [Namibian Defence Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Namibian_Defence_Force) personnel in the Congo were organised around an infantry [battalion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battalion) group with staff, artillery, logistics support and a flight of Alouette helicopters and Y-12 transport aircraft from the [Namibian Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Namibian_Air_Force).[^cite_note-Zim1-85] Between 2000 and 2001, this figure may have fluctuated between 1,600 and 2,000[^cite_note-Scramble-88] by which point 30 servicemen had been killed in action[^cite_note-Nam-89] and the war effort was costing Namibia \$150,000 a day.[^cite_note-90]

The Namibian intervention was greeted with intense criticism by opposition parties, as well as neighboring [South Africa](17416221.md) and several Western donors. The [European Union](9317.md) expressed concern that Nujoma was misusing his country's development funds for the Congo expedition, and individual member states—including [Finland](10577.md)—cut off financial aid. A furious diplomatic row also ensued with South African authorities after they suspended all outgoing military exports to the NDF.[^cite_note-Nam-89]

### Angola

The [Angolan](701.md) government had fought against [Mobutu Sésé Seko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobutu_S%C3%A9s%C3%A9_Seko) in the [First Congo War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Congo_War) because of his support for rebel [UNITA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNITA) in the [Angolan Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angolan_Civil_War).[^cite_note-91] The Angolan government wanted to eliminate UNITA operations in southern Congo, which [exchanged diamonds extracted from rebel-held Angola for foreign weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conflict_diamond#Angola). Angola had no confidence that a new president would be more effective than Kabila and feared that continued fighting would lead to a [power vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_vacuum) that could only help UNITA. The intervention of the experienced Angolan forces was essential in deciding the outcome of both wars.

### Chad

Kabila had originally discounted the possibility of support from *[Françafrique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7afrique)* ([Francophone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francophonie) Africa) but after a summit meeting in [Libreville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libreville), [Gabon](12027.md), on 24 September, [Chad](5488.md) agreed to send 2,000 troops. France had encouraged Chad to join as a means of regaining influence in a region where the French had retreated after the 1994 [genocide committed against Tutsi in Rwanda](676659.md).[^cite_note-ICG_Nov98-92] Nevertheless, Chadian intervention resulted in a fiasco. Its [forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_of_Chad) were accused of serious human rights violations and looting, virtually from their arrival in the country. They withdrew very quickly under international and national pressure and opprobrium.

### Sudan

Unconfirmed reports in September 1999 indicated that forces of the government of [Sudan](27421.md) were fighting rebels in [Orientale Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientale_Province), close to the Sudanese and Ugandan borders. However, Sudan did not establish a significant military presence inside the DRC, though it continued to offer extensive support to three Ugandan rebel groups—the [Lord's Resistance Army](17873743.md), the [Uganda National Rescue Front II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uganda_National_Rescue_Front_II) and the [Allied Democratic Forces](1258240.md)—in retaliation for Ugandan support for the [Sudan People's Liberation Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudan_People's_Liberation_Army).[^cite_note-HRW_1999WR-93]

### Areas of continuing conflict



A meeting of victims of [sexual violence in the Democratic Republic of the Congo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_violence_in_the_Democratic_Republic_of_the_Congo).

The fragility of the state has allowed continued violence and human rights abuses in the east. There are three significant centres of conflict:

- North and South Kivu, where a weakened FDLR continues to threaten the Rwandan border and the [Banyamulenge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banyamulenge), where Rwanda supports [RCD-Goma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RCD-Goma) rebels against Kinshasa (see [Kivu conflict](14687290.md)), and where local conflicts continue to fuel violence;

- [Ituri](1545860.md), where MONUC / MONUSCO has proved unable to contain the numerous militia and groups driving the [Ituri conflict](1585788.md);

- Northern [Katanga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katanga_Province), where [Mai-Mai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mai-Mai) Militias slipped out of the control of Kinshasa (see [Katanga insurgency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katanga_insurgency)).

The ethnic violence between [Hutu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutu)- and [Tutsi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tutsi)-aligned forces has been a driving impetus for much of the conflict, with people on both sides fearing their annihilation. The [Kinshasa](45773.md) and Hutu-aligned forces enjoyed close relations as their interests in expelling the armies and proxy forces of Uganda and Rwanda dovetailed.

While the Uganda and Rwanda-aligned forces worked closely together to gain territory at the expense of Kinshasa, competition over access to resources created a fissure in their relationship. There were reports that Uganda permitted Kinshasa to send arms to the Hutu FDLR via territory held by Uganda-backed rebels as Uganda, Kinshasa and the Hutu are all seeking, in varying degrees, to check the influence of Rwanda and its affiliates.

### Rwanda and Ugandan backing of rebels

See also: [Democratic Republic of the Congo–Rwanda relations](74945445.md)

Rwanda backed rebels due to fears of Hutu rebels on its border. The Kinshasa government was suspicious of [Kigali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kigali)'s influence over the region, as Rwanda has occupied the area numerous times and some witnesses confirm that Rwanda has profited from the looting of Congolese minerals. Consequently, Rwanda allegedly supported the [continuing rebellion of General Nkunda](14687290.md) in Congo. [Nkunda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurent_Nkunda) was, however, arrested by Rwandan police in 2009.[^cite_note-94] The DRC sought assurance that Kigali aligned forces have no conflict-mineral or territorial interests in eastern Congo.[^cite_note-95]

On 19 December 2005 the United Nations [International Court of Justice](14918.md) ruled that the DRC's sovereignty had been violated by Uganda, and that the DRC had lost billions of dollars' worth of resources.[^cite_note-96] The DRC government has asked for \$10 billion in compensation. Though the ICJ has taken many steps to ensure that war crimes and crimes against humanity will be prosecuted, the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank rewarded both Uganda and Rwanda with debt relief packages for improving their economic health during a time when much of their increased revenue was partially a direct result of illegally importing conflicted minerals from the DRC. In this case, international institutions such as the IMF and WB are allegedly at odds with international laws and charters. Both the IMF and WB have been accused of helping to facilitate the conflict in the DRC by rewarding combatants.[^cite_note-97]

### Economic impact

The deteriorated infrastructure and constant threat of attacks made overland travel impossible, causing internal trade in Congo to come to a standstill. On the Congo River, the most important remaining transport route, there was no ship traffic between the different power regions for years. Isolated villages were cut off from urban markets, while cities suffered from food shortages. In Kinshasa, food prices quadrupled within a year. International aid arrived only sparingly. Initially, it was mainly limited to Kinshasa and it was not until 2001 that it reached the actual war zones in the east.[^cite_note-\:18-98]

The impoverishment of the population worsened further. A 2006 study by the Congolese government, three years after the end of the war, revealed that 76% of the population could not send their children to school, 79% were malnourished, 81% did not have adequate housing, and 82% had no access to medical care. Overall, 71% of the population lived in absolute poverty, 85% in the poorest province of [Équateur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89quateur_\(former_province\)). According to aid organizations, in 2000, 31% of Kinshasa's residents were chronically malnourished and only 10% could afford three meals a day. In 2003, the average food intake of the population was 1300 kilocalories per day. The health system was largely destroyed and, reportedly, 500,000 people died of malaria each year, more than a third of them children under five years old.[^cite_note-\:18-98]

#### Hyperinflation in Zimbabwe

Main article: [Hyperinflation in Zimbabwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperinflation_in_Zimbabwe)

Zimbabwean involvement in the war had a significant negative impact on the country's economy. As hardware losses multiplied, Western donors—including the [International Monetary Fund](15251.md) and the [World Bank](36752.md)—placed their aid programmes to Harare under review,[^cite_note-BBC1-86] denying Mugabe the foreign currency he needed to buy spare parts valued at \$600 million for fighting vehicles then employed in the Congo.[^cite_note-99] Between January and June 2000, the *[Financial Times](136566.md)* reported (via a leaked memo from Minister of Finance [Herbert Murerwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Murerwa)) that Zimbabwe spent \$166 million on its military ventures there.[^cite_note-100][^cite_note-101][^cite_note-\:19-102] These factors contributed to the country's severe economic instability during the 2000s, culminating in a [period of hyperinflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperinflation_in_Zimbabwe) between 2007 and 2009.[^cite_note-\:19-102][^cite_note-object.cato.org-103]

### Environmental impact

The war has caused substantial environmental damage. The forests of Congo are a major biodiversity hotspot, housing The [Congo Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congo_Basin) which is widely known as the second greatest tropical rain forest in the world and the largest forest in Africa.

#### Animals and bushmeat trade

On account of the war the Democratic Republic of Congo saw their elephant population halve in size, their hippo population go from 22,000 to 900 and their great ape population decrease by 77–93% between 1998 and 2015.[^cite_note-\:5-104][^cite_note-\:6-105]

With up to 3.4 million people being displaced in Congo as a result of the civil war, many moved into Congo's forests, where they hunted [bonobos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonobo), [gorillas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gorilla), elephants and more as [bushmeat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bushmeat) for survival and cleared forest land that were significant habitats for these many animals.[^cite_note-\:12-106] Many of these individuals were either from the city where the hunting of certain animals was not a taboo or locals who had their tradition slowly broken down because of their need to survive.[^cite_note-LINGOMO-107] One such example had been the [Bongando](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bongando_people) people in which a 2009 study by Kyoto University had observed a disintegration of their cultural interdiction of eating [bonobos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonobo) as influenced by the civil war.[^cite_note-LINGOMO-107] During the conflict many of the front lines were located within [bonobo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonobo) reserves, further contributing to the decline in their population.

Government instability and the lack of government enforcement allowed new illegal militia mining camps to form, who located themselves deep within Congo's forest. There, [bushmeat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bushmeat) became the main source of food.[^cite_note-\:6-105] On top of that, the mass number of people entering the forests in conjunction with the mines' unsanitary conditions introduced an increased risk of disease for animals such as gorillas.[^cite_note-\:6-105] The government instability and the dangers of war also ceased much of the conservation and protection efforts occurring in Congo at the time, enabling the numerous rampant rebel groups present in Congo to openly partake in [poaching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poaching).[^cite_note-\:5-104]

Military assistance having been obstructed by surrounding African countries, Congo was pushed into entering numerous lumber leases with German, Chinese and Malaysian corporations to uphold their military activities.[^cite_note-108] The roads emerging to support the new logging efforts opened nearby villages to receive easier means of transportation and access to deeper parts of Congo's wildlife reserves.[^cite_note-\:14-109] The result was a significant increase in [bushmeat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bushmeat) sales in villages near these logging sites.[^cite_note-\:14-109]

The potential threat of rebel forces during the war caused many military officers controlling urban trade to leave their post.[^cite_note-\:15-110] This created an open access market in the [bushmeat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bushmeat) and pet trade that was then undertaken by exploitative lower ranked soldiers looking for profit.[^cite_note-\:15-110] The result was an observed quintuple increase of protected animal sales in urban markets in north-eastern parts of Congo around [Garamba National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garamba_National_Park).[^cite_note-\:15-110] Meanwhile, in other northern parts of Congo, researchers in other studies have found a 23% increase in protected animal sales with the overall pattern being a significant increase in protected animal sales.[^cite_note-\:16-111]

#### Deforestation

The war resulted in a loss of 1.3% of Congo's forests, a size comparable to Belgium.[^cite_note-112]

The millions of displaced individuals led to large-scale logging and deforestation to create arable land. These activities had an outsized effect due to the [edge effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_effects) as a result of the [forest fragmentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habitat_fragmentation).[^cite_note-\:16-111]

The [Virunga National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virunga_National_Park), Africa's oldest national park, became the first endangered UN World Heritage Site.[^cite_note-\:17-113] Its flora was cleared during both the first and second Congo war to make way for both the Rwandan and Congolese army.[^cite_note-\:17-113]

### Dispute over death toll

The [Human Security Report Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Security_Report_Project) (HSRP) of [Simon Fraser University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Fraser_University) has challenged the toll of 5.4 million war-related deaths between 1998 and 2008. It states that of the IRC's five periodic estimates, two that cover a period from 1998 to 2001 are flawed, and the reported 2.6 million deaths therein should not be included in the total death toll. The other three periodic IRC estimates cover a period from May 2001 – April 2007, and in which 2.83 million of the total 5.4 million deaths were reported. The HSRP argued that the estimates were built on a general death rate that was far too low for Congo, and that most of those people would have most likely died anyway. Thus, the IRC figure should be revised to 860,000 total war-related excess deaths.[^cite_note-114][^cite_note-115]

In response to the criticism from HSRP, one of the authors of the IRC report acknowledged small statistical discrepancies in the original study, but argued that the report had been widely peer-reviewed and was judged to be an accurate estimate of the war-related excess deaths.[^cite_note-\:20-116]
