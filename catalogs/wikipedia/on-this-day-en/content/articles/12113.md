[Greece](12108.md) is a [developed country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developed_country) with a [mixed economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_economy).[^cite_note-WEO_groups-2][^cite_note-World_Bank-3] The Greek economy is the [50th-largest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_\(nominal\)) by nominal [gross domestic product](12594.md) (GDP) and [54th-largest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_\(PPP\)) by [purchasing power parity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purchasing_power_parity) (PPP).[^cite_note-WEO_database-4] It is the [16th-largest economy](1598533.md#Economies_of_member_states) in the [European Union](9317.md) and eleventh largest in the [eurozone](184391.md).[^cite_note-Eurostat_GDP-38] Greece is a [welfare state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welfare_state) and ranks relatively highly among [OECD](33853117.md) nations in terms of [social spending](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_social_welfare_spending), which stood at 23.7% of GDP in 2024.[^cite_note-39] It has the highest [debt-to-GDP ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debt-to-GDP_ratio) in the EU, standing at 146% in 2025.[^cite_note-40] The economy is based on the [service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertiary_sector) (78%), [industrial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_sector) (18%) and [agricultural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_sector) (4%) [sectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_sector).[^cite_note-ELSTAT_Economy-9]

The Greek economy was devastated by [World War II](32927.md) (1939–1945), but expanded rapidly from 1950 to 1980 in what is commonly known as the [Greek economic miracle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_economic_miracle).[^cite_note-Economic_miracle-41] Greece also enjoyed a sustained period of growth rates above the EU average from the mid-1990s to the mid-2000s, peaking at 6.4% in 2006.[^cite_note-WEO_database-4][^cite_note-ELSTAT_growth_2023-42] The subsequent [Great Recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Recession) and [Greek government-debt crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_government-debt_crisis), a central focus of the wider [euro area crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_area_crisis), plunged the economy into a sharp downturn in the late 2000s and early 2010s. The [tourism](159622.md) boom of recent years has helped drive a [strong recovery](#Post-COVID_recovery),[^cite_note-IMF_recovery-43][^cite_note-ECB_recovery-44][^cite_note-Reuters_recovery-45] with Greece setting new records in arrivals (38 million) and revenue (€23.6 billion) in 2025 as one of the [most visited countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Tourism_rankings) in the world.[^cite_note-46]

Significant Greek industries include tourism and [shipping](25159995.md). The [Greek merchant navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Merchant_Marine) is the largest in the world, with Greek-owned vessels accounting for 21% of global [deadweight tonnage](1346384.md) as of 2021; the total capacity of the Greek-owned fleet has increased by 45.8% compared to 2014.[^cite_note-2021ShippingData-47][^cite_note-UN_shipping-48][^cite_note-49] Greece has the largest economy in [Southeast Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Europe) and contributes extensively to regional investment.[^cite_note-50][^cite_note-Keridis-51] Greece has [free trade agreements](2533562.md) with many nations and is a founding member of the [OECD](33853117.md) (1961), the [European single market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_single_market) (1993) and the [World Trade Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Trade_Organization) (1995), among others.

Historical GDP per capita development

The evolution of the Greek economy during the 19th century (a period that transformed a large part of the world because of the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution)) has been little researched. Recent research from 2006[^cite_note-52] examines the gradual development of industry and further development of shipping in a predominantly agricultural economy, calculating an average rate of per capita GDP growth between 1833 and 1911 that was only slightly lower than that of the other Western European nations. Industrial activity, (including heavy industry like shipbuilding) was evident, mainly in [Ermoupolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ermoupolis) and [Piraeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piraeus).[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-GrM-54] Nonetheless, Greece faced economic hardships and defaulted on its external loans in 1843, 1860 and 1893.[^cite_note-55]

Other studies support the above view on the general trends in the economy, providing comparative measures of standard of living. The per capita income (in purchasing power terms) of Greece was 65% that of France in 1850, 56% in 1890, 62% in 1938,[^cite_note-56][^cite_note-57] 75% in 1980, 90% in 2007, 96.4% in 2008 and 97.9% in 2009.[^cite_note-58][^cite_note-59]

During [World War II](32927.md), Greece experienced one of history's most extreme [hyperinflation crises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperinflation_in_Greece) during the [Axis occupation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_occupation_of_Greece), peaking in November 1944 with monthly rates reaching roughly 13,800 percent.

The country's post-World War II development has largely been connected with the [Greek economic miracle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_economic_miracle).[^cite_note-Economic_miracle-41] During that period, Greece saw growth rates second only to those of [Japan](15573.md), while ranking first in Europe in terms of GDP growth.[^cite_note-Economic_miracle-41] It is indicative that between 1960 and 1973 the Greek economy grew by an average of 7.7%, in contrast to 4.7% for the EU15 and 4.9% for the [OECD](33853117.md).[^cite_note-Economic_miracle-41] Also during that period, exports grew by an average annual rate of 12.6%.[^cite_note-Economic_miracle-41]

Greece entered the [Eurozone](184391.md) in 2001

Greece was accepted into the [Economic and Monetary Union of the European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_and_Monetary_Union_of_the_European_Union) by the [European Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Council) on 19 June 2000, based on a number of [criteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_convergence_criteria) (inflation rate, budget deficit, public debt, long-term interest rates, exchange rate) using 1999 as the reference year. After an audit commissioned by the incoming [New Democracy](424225.md) government in 2004, [Eurostat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurostat) revealed that the statistics for the budget deficit had been under-reported.[^cite_note-63] However, even after all corrections that followed, the reference year budget deficit did not exceed the allowable upper limit (3%) according to the Eurostat accounting method in force at the time of application, and thus Greece had still met all critiera for Eurozone entry (details given below).[^cite_note-65]

Most of the differences in the revised budget deficit numbers were due to a temporary change of accounting practices by the new government, i.e., recording expenses when military material was ordered rather than received.[^cite_note-66] However, it was the retroactive application of ESA95 methodology (applied since 2000) by Eurostat, that finally raised the reference year (1999) budget deficit to 3.38% of GDP, thus exceeding the 3% limit. This led to claims that Greece (similar claims have been made about other European countries like Italy)[^cite_note-67] had not actually met all five accession criteria, and the common perception that Greece entered the Eurozone through "falsified" deficit numbers.

In the 2005 OECD report for Greece,[^cite_note-OECD_Greece_2005-68] it was clearly stated that "the impact of new accounting rules on the fiscal figures for the years 1997 to 1999 ranged from 0.7 to 1 percentage point of GDP; this retroactive change of methodology was responsible for the revised deficit exceeding 3% in 1999, the year of \[Greece's] EMU membership qualification". The above led the Greek minister of finance to clarify that the 1999 budget deficit was below the prescribed 3% limit when calculated with the ESA79 methodology in force at the time of Greece's application, and thus the criteria had been met.[^cite_note-69]

The original accounting practice for military expenses was later restored in line with Eurostat recommendations, theoretically lowering even the ESA95-calculated 1999 Greek budget deficit to below 3% (an official Eurostat calculation is still pending for 1999).

An error sometimes made is the confusion of discussion regarding Greece's Eurozone entry with the controversy regarding usage of derivatives' deals with U.S. Banks by Greece and other Eurozone countries to artificially reduce their reported budget deficits. A currency swap arranged with [Goldman Sachs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldman_Sachs) allowed Greece to "hide" 2.8 billion Euros of debt, however, this affected deficit values after 2001 (when Greece had already been admitted into the Eurozone) and is not related to Greece's Eurozone entry.[^cite_note-70]

A study of the period 1999–2009 by forensic accountants has found that data submitted to Eurostat by Greece, among other countries, had a statistical distribution indicative of manipulation; "Greece with a mean value of 17.74, shows the largest deviation from [Benford's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benford's_law) among the members of the eurozone, followed by Belgium with a value of 17.21 and Austria with a value of 15.25".[^cite_note-71][^cite_note-72]

#### Historical Debt

Greece, like other European nations, had faced [debt crises in the 19th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_sovereign_debt_crises), as well as a similar crisis in 1932 during the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression). In general, however, during the 20th century it enjoyed one of the highest GDP growth rates on the planet [^cite_note-Angus_Maddison-73] (for a quarter century from the early 1950s to mid 1970s, [second in the world after Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_economic_miracle)). Average Greek government debt-to-GDP for the entire century before the crisis (1909–2008) was lower than that for the UK, Canada, or France,[^cite_note-Debt_Past-61][^cite_note-Historical_Debt_IMF-62] while for the 30-year period (1952–1981) until entrance into the [European Economic Community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Economic_Community), the Greek government [debt-to-GDP ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debt-to-GDP_ratio) averaged only 19.8%.[^cite_note-Historical_Debt_IMF-62]

Between 1981 and 1993 Greece's government debt-to-GDP ratio steadily rose, surpassing the average of what is today the Eurozone in the mid-1980s (see chart right). For the next 15 years, from 1993 to 2007 (i.e., before the [2008 financial crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_financial_crisis)), Greece's government debt-to-GDP ratio remained roughly unchanged (the value was not affected by the [2004 Athens Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Summer_Olympics)), averaging 102%[^cite_note-Historical_Debt_IMF-62][^cite_note-Debt_1980-74] - a value lower than that for Italy (107%) and Belgium (110%) during the same 15-year period,[^cite_note-Historical_Debt_IMF-62] and comparable to that for the U.S. or the OECD average in 2017.

During the latter period, the country's annual budget deficit usually exceeded 3% of GDP, but its effect on the debt-to-GDP ratio was counterbalanced by high GDP growth rates. The debt-to-GDP values for 2006 and 2007 (about 105%) were established after [audits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_financial_audits,_2009%E2%80%9310) resulted in corrections according to Eurostat methodology, of up to 10 percentage points for the particular years (as well as similar corrections for the years 2008 and 2009). These corrections, although altering the debt level by [a maximum of about 10%](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_financial_audits,_2009%E2%80%9310), resulted in a popular notion that "Greece was previously hiding its debt".

#### Evolution of the debt crisis

The Greek crisis was triggered by the turmoil of the [Great Recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Recession), which led the budget deficits of several Western nations to reach or exceed 10% of GDP.[^cite_note-Debt_Past-61] In Greece's case, the high budget deficit (which, after several corrections, was revealed that it had been allowed to reach 10.2% and 15.1% of GDP in 2008 and 2009, respectively) was coupled with a high public debt to GDP ratio (which, until then, was relatively stable for several years, at just above 100% of GDP - as calculated after all corrections[^cite_note-Debt_Past-61]). Thus, the country appeared to lose control of its public debt to GDP ratio, which already reached 127% of GDP in 2009.[^cite_note-Debt_2009_2017_Eurostat-75] In addition, being a member of the Eurozone, the country had essentially no autonomous [monetary policy flexibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_and_Monetary_Union_of_the_European_Union#Monetary_policy_inflexibility). Finally, there was an effect of controversies about Greek statistics (due the aforementioned drastic budget deficit revisions which lead to an increase in the calculated value of the Greek public debt by [about 10%](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_financial_audits,_2009%E2%80%9310), i.e., a public debt to GDP of about 100% until 2007), while there have been arguments about a possible effect of [media reports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_government-debt_crisis#Criticism_of_the_role_of_news_media_and_stereotyping). Consequently, Greece was "punished" by the markets which increased borrowing rates, making impossible for the country to finance its debt since early 2010, exacerbating the [euro area crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_area_crisis).

The Greek economy faced its most-severe crisis since the restoration of democracy in 1974 as the Greek government revised its deficit forecasts from 3.7% in early 2009 and 6% in September 2009, to 12.7% of gross domestic product (GDP) in October 2009.[^cite_note-76][^cite_note-77]

The aforementioned budget deficit and debt revisions were connected with findings that, through the assistance of [Goldman Sachs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldman_Sachs), [JPMorgan Chase](231001.md) and numerous other banks, financial products were developed which enabled the governments of Greece, Italy and many other European countries to hide parts of their borrowing.[^cite_note-Rehn-78][^cite_note-Goldman-79] Dozens of similar agreements were concluded across Europe whereby banks supplied cash in advance in exchange for future payments by the governments involved; in turn, the liabilities of the involved countries were "kept off the books".[^cite_note-Goldman-79][^cite_note-80][^cite_note-Bloomberg-81][^cite_note-The_Telegraph-82][^cite_note-The_Guardian-83][^cite_note-Der_Spiegel-84]

According to *[Der Spiegel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Der_Spiegel)*, credits given to European governments were disguised as "swaps" and consequently did not get registered as debt because Eurostat at the time ignored statistics involving financial derivatives. A German derivatives dealer had commented to *Der Spiegel* that "The Maastricht rules can be circumvented quite legally through swaps," and "In previous years, Italy used a similar trick to mask its true debt with the help of a different US bank."[^cite_note-Der_Spiegel-84] These conditions had enabled Greek as well as many other European governments to spend beyond their means, while meeting the deficit targets of the European Union and the [monetary union guidelines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_convergence_criteria).[^cite_note-Europe_derivatives_1-85][^cite_note-Europe_derivatives_2-86][^cite_note-87][^cite_note-88][^cite_note-89][^cite_note-Italy_swaps-90][^cite_note-FT_Italy_derivatives-91][^cite_note-Goldman-79][^cite_note-92] In May 2010, the Greek government deficit was again revised and estimated to be 13.6%[^cite_note-93] which was among the highest relative to GDP, with [Iceland](14531.md) in first place at 15.7% and the [United Kingdom](31717.md) third with 12.6%.[^cite_note-94] Public debt was forecast, according to some estimates, to hit 120% of GDP during 2010.[^cite_note-95]

As a consequence, there was a crisis in international confidence in Greece's ability to repay its sovereign debt, as reflected by the rise of the country's borrowing rates (although their slow rise – the 10-year government bond yield only exceeded 7% in April 2010 – coinciding with a large number of negative articles, has led to arguments about the role of international news media in the evolution of the crisis). In order to avert a default (as high borrowing rates effectively prohibited access to the markets), in May 2010 the other Eurozone countries, and the IMF, agreed to a "rescue package" which involved giving Greece an immediate €45 billion in bail-out loans, with more funds to follow, totaling €110 billion.[^cite_note-96][^cite_note-97] In order to secure the funding, Greece was required to adopt harsh austerity measures to bring its deficit under control.[^cite_note-98] Their implementation was to be monitored and evaluated by the [European Commission](9974.md), the [European Central Bank](9474.md) and the IMF.[^cite_note-99][^cite_note-100]

The [2008 financial crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_financial_crisis) – particularly the [austerity](684037.md) package put forth by the EU and the IMF – has been met with anger by the Greek public, leading to [riots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riots) and [social unrest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_unrest), while there have been theories about the effect of [international media](https://www.academia.edu/6655991/Media_Coverage_of_the_2010_Greek_Debt_Crisis_Inaccuracies_and_Evidence_of_Manipulation). Despite - others say because of - the long range of austerity measures, the government deficit has not been reduced accordingly, mainly, according to many economists, because of the subsequent recession.[^cite_note-101][^cite_note-102][^cite_note-103][^cite_note-104][^cite_note-105]

Public sector workers have come out on strike in order to resist job cuts and reductions to salaries as the government promises that a large scale privatisation programme will be accelerated.[^cite_note-106] Immigrants are sometimes treated as scapegoats for economic problems by far-right extremists.[^cite_note-107]

In 2013, Greece became the first [developed market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developed_market) whose stock market was reclassified as an [emerging market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emerging_markets) by different financial rating companies,[^cite_note-108][^cite_note-109][^cite_note-110] only starting to be reclassified back as a developed market, first by FTSE Russell in October 2025, followed by other rating companies.[^cite_note-GCT-111][^cite_note-LSEG-112]

By July 2014 there were still anger and protests about the austerity measures, with a 24-hour strike among government workers timed to coincide with an audit by inspectors from the International Monetary Fund, the European Union and European Central Bank in advance of a decision on a second bailout of one billion euros (\$1.36 billion), due in late July.[^cite_note-GreekStrike-113]

Greece exited its six-year recession in the second quarter of 2014,[^cite_note-114] but the challenges of securing political stability and debt sustainability remained.[^cite_note-115]

A third bailout was agreed in July 2015, after a confrontation with the newly elected leftist government of Alexis Tsipras. In June 2017, news reports indicated that the "crushing debt burden" had not been alleviated and that Greece was at the risk of defaulting on some payments.[^cite_note-116] The [International Monetary Fund](15251.md) stated that the country should be able to borrow again "in due course". At the time, the Euro zone gave Greece another credit of \$9.5-billion, \$8.5 billion of loans and brief details of a possible debt relief with the assistance of the IMF.[^cite_note-117] On 13 July, the Greek government sent a letter of intent to the IMF with 21 commitments it promised to meet by June 2018. They included changes in labour laws, a plan to cap public sector work contracts, to transform temporary contracts into permanent agreements and to recalculate pension payments to reduce spending on social security.[^cite_note-118]

Greece's bailouts successfully ended (as declared) on 20 August 2018.[^cite_note-Bailout_exit-119]

In June 2026, Greece repaid a further €6.9 billions from its first bailout package ahead of schedule as part of a broader effort to improve its financial standing and reduce debt.[^cite_note-120] This follows previous earlier repayments to EU creditors, as well as a full repayment of Greece’s loans to the International Monetary Fund two years ahead of schedule in 2022.[^cite_note-121][^cite_note-122] The early debt repayments were seen as part of the country's remarkable recovery following the end of the crisis in 2018[^cite_note-123][^cite_note-124][^cite_note-125], further evidenced by dramatically reduced borrowing costs for the country, with its benchmark 10-year government bond yields falling below Italy's on November 8, 2019[^cite_note-126] and France's on November 29, 2024.[^cite_note-127]

As a result of its improved finances, Greece is expected to have a lower [debt-to-GDP ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debt-to-GDP_ratio) than Italy by the end of 2026, three years earlier than previously forecast.[^cite_note-repayment-128] Noting that Greece was recording the fastest debt reduction in the world, the [finance minister](4025366.md) and [Eurogroup](21533929.md) president [Kyriakos Pierrakakis](61389979.md) said that the government aimed to reduce the debt burden below 120% of GDP by 2029.[^cite_note-129]

#### Effects of the bailout programmes on the debt crisis

There was a 25% drop in Greece's GDP, connected with the bailout programmes.[^cite_note-Tsipras_Bloomberg-130][^cite_note-Marianne-131] This had a critical effect: the Debt-to-GDP ratio, the key factor defining the severity of the crisis, would jump from its 2009 level of 127%[^cite_note-Eurostat_Debt_to_GDP-132] to about 170%, solely due to the GDP drop (i.e., for the same Debt). Such a level is considered unsustainable.[^cite_note-133] Reports show that, for government debt, the threshold for unhealthy Debt-to-GDPT ratio is around 85%.[^cite_note-134] In a 2013 report, the IMF admitted that it had underestimated the effects of so extensive tax hikes and budget cuts on the country's GDP and issued an informal apology.[^cite_note-IMF_Guardian-135][^cite_note-IMF_Reuters-136][^cite_note-IMF_Telegraph-137][^cite_note-Marianne-131] In 2009, [real](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_gross_domestic_product) GDP growth rates hit −4.1% with −5.7% in 2010, −9.9% in 2011, −8.3% in 2012 and −2.3% in 2013.[^cite_note-ELSTAT_growth_2023-42][^cite_note-ELSTAT_growth-138] In 2011, the country's [government debt](695460.md) reached €356 billion (172% of nominal GDP).[^cite_note-139] After negotiating the biggest [debt restructuring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debt_restructuring) in history with the [private sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_sector_involvement), which sustained losses in the order of €100 billion for private [bond](60737.md) investors,[^cite_note-140] Greece reduced its sovereign debt burden to €280 billion (137% of GDP) in the first quarter of 2012.[^cite_note-141] Greece achieved a real GDP growth rate of 0.8% in 2014—after five consecutive years of economic decline—but the economy contracted by 0.2% in 2015 and recorded zero growth in 2016.[^cite_note-ELSTAT_growth-138][^cite_note-142] The country returned to modest growth rates of 1.5% in 2017, 2.1% in 2018 and 2.3% in 2019.[^cite_note-ELSTAT_growth-138]

### COVID-19 recession

The [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_Greece) had a severe impact on all sectors of the economy, and tourism in particular. As a result, GDP contracted by 9.2% in 2020 during the [COVID-19 recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_recession).[^cite_note-ELSTAT_growth-138] In June 2021, the [European Commission](9974.md) agreed to disburse approximately €30 billion in COVID-related economic aid (€12 billion in loans and €18 billion in grants).[^cite_note-143]

### Post-COVID recovery

Greece has recovered significantly since the early 2020s,[^cite_note-IMF_recovery-43][^cite_note-ECB_recovery-44][^cite_note-Reuters_recovery-45] approaching its pre-crisis levels of economic output with growth rates well above the [eurozone](184391.md) average: 8.7% in 2021, 5.5% in 2022 and 2.1% in 2023, 2024 and 2025.[^cite_note-ELSTAT_growth-138][^cite_note-ELSTAT_growth_2025-7] On 20 August 2022, Greece formally exited the EU's "enhanced surveillance framework", which had been in place since the conclusion of the [third bailout programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Economic_Adjustment_Programme_for_Greece) exactly four years earlier.[^cite_note-ESF-144] On 2 December 2022, Berlin-based [credit rating agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credit_rating_agency) Scope assigned a positive outlook to Greece's BB+ rating, presaging the country's return to [investment grade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bond_credit_rating#Investment_grade).[^cite_note-145] On 31 July 2023, Greece's investment-grade status was restored by Japanese credit rating agency R\&I.[^cite_note-146][^cite_note-147] Scope, [DBRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morningstar_DBRS), [S\&P](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S\&P_Global_Ratings) and [Fitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitch_Ratings) followed suit on 4 August,[^cite_note-Scope-148][^cite_note-149] 8 September,[^cite_note-150][^cite_note-151] 20 October and 1 December 2023 respectively,[^cite_note-S\&P-152][^cite_note-153][^cite_note-154] but [Moody's](1705862.md) delayed doing so until 14 March 2025.[^cite_note-155] *[The Economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Economist)* ranked Greece the world's top economic performer for 2022 and 2023, citing significant improvements in five key economic and financial indicators.[^cite_note-156][^cite_note-157]

Greece enjoys a high [standard of living](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_of_living) and very high [Human Development Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Development_Index), being ranked 32nd in the world in 2019.[^cite_note-HDI-158] However, the severe recession of recent years saw GDP per capita fall from 94% of the EU average in 2009 to 67% between 2017 and 2019.[^cite_note-Eurostat_PPS_2011-159][^cite_note-Eurostat_PPS_2019-160] During the same period, Actual Individual Consumption (AIC) per capita fell from 104% to 78% of the EU average.[^cite_note-Eurostat_PPS_2011-159][^cite_note-Eurostat_PPS_2019-160]

Greece's main industries are tourism, shipping, industrial products, food and tobacco processing, textiles, chemicals, metal products, mining and petroleum. Greece's GDP growth has also, as an average, since the early 1990s been higher than the EU average. However, the Greek economy continues to face significant problems, including high unemployment levels, inefficient public sector [bureaucracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureaucracy), [tax evasion](2256795.md), [corruption](3608404.md) and low global [competitiveness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Competition_\(companies\)).[^cite_note-161][^cite_note-162]

Greece is ranked 56th in the world, and 21st among [EU member states](10890716.md), on the [Corruption Perceptions Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corruption_Perceptions_Index).[^cite_note-CPI-15] Thus, it has roughly returned to its ranking prior to the 2010–2018 debt crisis.[^cite_note-Corruption_2008-163] However, Greece still has the EU's lowest [Index of Economic Freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_of_Economic_Freedom) and second lowest [Global Competitiveness Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Competitiveness_Report), ranking 113th and 59th in the world respectively.[^cite_note-164][^cite_note-165]

After fourteen consecutive years of economic growth, Greece went into recession in 2008.[^cite_note-WEO_database-4] By the end of 2009, the Greek economy faced the highest [budget deficit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budget_deficit) and [government debt](695460.md)-to-GDP ratio in the EU. After several upward revisions, the 2009 budget deficit is now estimated at 15.7% of GDP.[^cite_note-166] This, combined with rapidly rising debt levels (127.9% of GDP in 2009) led to a precipitous increase in borrowing costs, effectively shutting Greece out of the global financial markets and resulting in a severe economic crisis.[^cite_note-167]

Greece was accused of trying to cover up the extent of its massive budget deficit during the [2008 financial crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_financial_crisis).[^cite_note-168] The allegation was prompted by the massive revision of the 2009 budget deficit forecast by the new [PASOK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panhellenic_Socialist_Movement) government [elected in October 2009](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_legislative_election,_2009), from "6–8%" (estimated by the previous [New Democracy](424225.md) government) to 12.7% (later revised to 15.7%). However, the accuracy of the revised figures has also been questioned, and in February 2012 the [Hellenic Parliament](2449826.md) voted in favor of an official investigation following accusations by a former member of the [Hellenic Statistical Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenic_Statistical_Authority) that the deficit had been artificially inflated in order to justify harsher austerity measures.[^cite_note-169][^cite_note-170]

The Greek labor force, which amount around 5 million workers, average 2,032 hours of work per worker annually in 2011, is ranked fourth among [OECD](33853117.md) countries, after [Mexico](3966054.md), [South Korea](27019.md) and [Chile](5489.md).[^cite_note-OECDavrgAHWork-172] The [Groningen Growth & Development Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Groningen) has published a poll revealing that between 1995 and 2005, Greece was the country whose workers have the most hours/year work among European nations; Greeks worked an average of 1,900 hours per year, followed by Spaniards (average of 1,800 hours/year).[^cite_note-173]

As a result of the ongoing economic crisis, industrial production in the country went down by 8% between March 2010 and March 2011,[^cite_note-ELSTAT_industry-174] The volume of building activity saw a reduction of 73% in 2010.[^cite_note-ELSTAT_building-175] Additionally, the turnover in retail sales saw a decline of 9% between February 2010 and February 2011.[^cite_note-ELSTAT_retail-176]

Between 2008 and 2013 unemployment skyrocketed, from a generational low of 7.2% in the second and third quarters of 2008 to a high of 27.9% in June 2013, leaving over a million jobless.[^cite_note-177][^cite_note-178][^cite_note-179] Youth unemployment peaked at 64.9% in May 2013.[^cite_note-180] Unemployment figures have steadily improved in recent years, with the overall rate falling to 7.4% in August 2026 and youth unemployment dropping to 14.3% in December 2025.[^cite_note-ELSTAT_unemployment-16][^cite_note-Eurostat_unemployment-18][^cite_note-181] Male and female unemployment stood at 4.9% and 10.3% in August 2026, the twelfth-lowest and second-highest in the European Union respectively.[^cite_note-ELSTAT_unemployment-16][^cite_note-Eurostat_unemployment-18]

Greece has a significant shipbuilding and ship maintenance industry. The six shipyards around the port of Piraeus are among the largest in Europe.[^cite_note-193] In recent years, Greece has become a leader in the construction and maintenance of luxury yachts.[^cite_note-194]

23.2% of the world's total [merchant fleet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merchant_fleet) is owned by Greek companies, making it the largest in the world. They are ranked in the top 5 for all kinds of ships, including first for tankers and bulk carriers.

Shipping has traditionally been a key sector in the Greek economy since ancient times.[^cite_note-historyshipping-195] In 1813, the Greek merchant navy was made up of 615 ships.[^cite_note-Istoria_ton_Ellinon-196] Its total tonnage was 153,580 tons and was crewed with 37,526 sailors and 5,878 cannons.[^cite_note-Istoria_ton_Ellinon-196] In 1914 the figures stood at 449,430 tons and 1,322 ships (of which 287 were steam boats).[^cite_note-Greek_fleet-197]

During the 1960s, the size of the Greek fleet nearly doubled, primarily through the investment undertaken by the shipping magnates [Onassis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle_Onassis), [Vardinoyannis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vardis_Vardinogiannis), [Livanos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Livanos) and [Niarchos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stavros_Niarchos).[^cite_note-slate-198] The basis of the modern Greek maritime industry was formed after [World War II](32927.md) when Greek shipping businessmen were able to amass surplus ships sold to them by the United States Government through the Ship Sales Act of the 1940s.[^cite_note-slate-198]

Greece has the largest [merchant navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merchant_navy) in the world, accounting for more than 15% of the world's total [deadweight tonnage](1346384.md) (dwt) according to the [United Nations Conference on Trade and Development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Conference_on_Trade_and_Development).[^cite_note-UN_shipping-48] The Greek merchant navy's total dwt of nearly 245 million is comparable only to [Japan](15573.md)'s, which is ranked second with almost 224 million.[^cite_note-UN_shipping-48] Additionally, Greece represents 39.52% of all of the European Union's dwt.[^cite_note-ECSA-199] However, today's fleet roster is smaller than an all-time high of 5,000 ships in the late 1970s.[^cite_note-historyshipping-195]

Greece is ranked third in the world by number of ships, with 4,709, behind Japan, with 5,974, and China, which leads with 7,114 owned vessels.[^cite_note-\:2-200] A [European Community Shipowners' Associations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Community_Shipowners'_Associations) report for 2011–2012 reveals that the [Greek flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_Greece) is the seventh-most-used internationally for shipping, while it ranks second in the [EU](9317.md).[^cite_note-ECSA-199]

In terms of ship categories, Greek companies have 22.6% of the world's tankers[^cite_note-ECSA-199] and 16.1% of the world's bulk carriers (in dwt).[^cite_note-ECSA-199] An additional equivalent of 27.45% of the world's tanker dwt is on order,[^cite_note-ECSA-199] with another 12.7% of bulk carriers also on order.[^cite_note-ECSA-199] Shipping accounts for an estimated 6% of Greek GDP,[^cite_note-ECSA2011-201] employs about 160,000 people (4% of the workforce),[^cite_note-nbg-202] and represents 1/3 of the country's trade deficit.[^cite_note-nbg-202] Earnings from shipping amounted to €14.1 billion in 2011,[^cite_note-ECSA-199] while between 2000 and 2010 Greek shipping contributed a total of €140 billion[^cite_note-ECSA2011-201] (half of the country's public debt in 2009 and 3.5 times the receipts from the European Union in the period 2000–2013).[^cite_note-ECSA2011-201] The 2011 ECSA report showed that there are approximately 750 Greek shipping companies in operation.[^cite_note-ECSA2011-201]

The latest available data from the Union of Greek Shipowners show that "the Greek-owned ocean-going fleet consists of 3,428 ships, totaling 245 million deadweight tonnes in capacity. This equals 15.6 percent of the carrying capacity of the entire global fleet, including 23.6 percent of the world tanker fleet and 17.2 percent of dry bulk".[^cite_note-TimeWorldGrShipping-203]

Counting shipping as quasi-exports and in terms of monetary value, Greece ranked 4th globally in 2011 having exported shipping services worth 17,704.132 million \$; only [Denmark](76972.md), Germany and [South Korea](27019.md) ranked higher during that year.[^cite_note-WTO-ITC-SeaTransportRankExports-204] Similarly counting shipping services provided to Greece by other countries as quasi-imports and the difference between exports and imports as a trade balance, Greece in 2011 ranked in the latter second behind Germany, having imported shipping services worth 7,076.605 million US\$ and having run a trade surplus of 10,712.342 million US\$.[^cite_note-WTO-ITC-SeaTransportRankImports-205][^cite_note-WTO-ITC-SeaTransportRankTradeBalance-206] In 2022, Greek used vessel sales were second only to China's.[^cite_note-\:2-200]

### Education

Main article: [Education in Greece](420676.md)

This section is an excerpt from [Education in Greece](420676.md).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Education_in_Greece\&action=edit)]





The central building of Athens University (left), and the building of a high school in Argos, right.

[Education in Greece](420676.md) is centralized and governed by the [Ministry of Education, Religious Affairs, and Sports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Education_and_Religious_Affairs) (Greek: Υπουργείο Παιδείας, Θρησκευμάτων και Αθλητισμού, Υ.ΠΑΙ.Θ.Α.) at all grade levels throughout elementary, middle school, and high school.[^cite_note-208][^cite_note-209][^cite_note-210][^cite_note-211] The Ministry exercises control over public schools, formulates and implements legislation, administers the budget, coordinates national level university entrance examinations, sets up the [national curriculum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_curriculum), appoints public school teaching staff, and coordinates other services.

The Ministry of Education and Religious Affairs[^cite_note-212] is also in charge of which classes are necessary for general education. They have implemented mandatory courses such as religion in required grade levels (1st-9th grades). Students can only be exempt if their guardians fill out a declaration excluding them from religious lessons.[^cite_note-213]

The national supervisory role of the Ministry is exercised through [Regional Unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_units_of_Greece) Public Education Offices, which are named Regional Directorates of Primary and Secondary School Education.[^cite_note-214][^cite_note-215][^cite_note-216] Public schools and their supply of [textbooks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textbook) are funded by the government. Public schools in Greece are tuition-free and students on a state approved list are provided textbooks at no cost.

About 25% of postgraduate programmes are tuition-fee, while about 30% of students are eligible to attend programmes tuition-free based on individual criteria.[^cite_note-Education_in_Greece_4485law2017-217]

[Formal education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_education) in [Greece](12108.md) consists of three [educational stages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Educational_stage). The first stage of formal education is the primary stage, which lasts for six years starting aged six and ending at the age of 12, followed by the secondary stage, which is separated into two sub-stages: the compulsory [middle school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnasium_\(school\)), which lasts three years starting at age 12, and non-compulsory [Lyceum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyceum), which lasts three years starting at 15. The third stage involves higher education.

School holidays in Greece include Christmas, [Greek Independence Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Independence_Day), Easter, [National Anniversary Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohi_Day), a three-month summer holiday, [National Public Holidays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_holidays_in_Greece), and local holidays, which vary by region such as the local [patron saint](68055.md)'s day.

In addition to schooling, the majority of students attend extracurricular private classes at private tutoring centres called "frontistiria" (*φροντιστήρια*, *[frontistiria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frontistiria)*), or one-to-one tuition. These centres prepare students for higher education admissions, like the [Pan-Hellenic Examinations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apolytirion), and/or provide foreign language education.

It is forbidden by law for students to [use mobile phones](49478366.md) while on the school premises. Taking or making phone calls, texting, or the use of other camera, video or other recording devices or medium that have image and audio processing ability like [smartwatches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smartwatch) is forbidden. Students must switch off their mobile phones or set them to silent mode and keep them in their bags while on the school premises.[^cite_note-218][^cite_note-219][^cite_note-220][^cite_note-221] However, especially at high schools, the use of mobile phones is widespread, especially at breaks and sometimes in the class.

### Healthcare

Main article: [Healthcare in Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Healthcare_in_Greece)

This section is an excerpt from [Healthcare in Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Healthcare_in_Greece).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Healthcare_in_Greece\&action=edit)]



Logo of the [Ministry for Health and Social Solidarity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Health_\(Greece\)).

Healthcare in [Greece](12108.md) consists of a [universal health care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_health_care) system provided through [national health insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_health_insurance), and private health care. According to the 2011 budget, the Greek healthcare system was allocated 6.1 billion [euro](9472.md), or 2.8% of GDP.[^cite_note-Healthcare_in_Greece_Budget_2011-222] In a 2000 report by the [World Health Organization](33583.md), the Greek healthcare system was ranked 14th worldwide in the overall assessment, above other countries such as [Germany](11867.md) (25) and the [United Kingdom](31717.md) (18), while ranking 11th at level of service.[^cite_note-Healthcare_in_Greece_WHO_report-223]

Healthcare in Greece is provided by the [National Healthcare Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Healthcare_Service?action=edit\&redlink=1) (ESY) ([Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language): Εθνικό Σύστημα Υγείας, ΕΣΥ) (or National Health System, ESY).

### Telecommunications

Main article: [Telecommunications in Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunications_in_Greece)

See also: [Internet in Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_in_Greece)



[OTE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OTE) headquarters in Athens

Between 1949 and the 1980s, [telephone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephone) communications in Greece were a [state monopoly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_monopoly) by the [Hellenic Telecommunications Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OTE), better known by its acronym, OTE. Despite the liberalization of telephone communications in the country in the 1980s, OTE still dominates the Greek market in its field and has emerged as one of the largest telecommunications companies in [Southeast Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Europe).[^cite_note-OTE-224] Since 2011, the company's major [shareholder](146081.md) is [Deutsche Telekom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsche_Telekom) with a 40% stake, while the Greek state continues to own 10% of the company's shares.[^cite_note-OTE-224] OTE owns several subsidiaries across in [Southeast Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Europe), including [Cosmote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmote), Greece's top mobile telecommunications provider, [Cosmote Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmote_Romania) and [Albanian Mobile Communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albanian_Mobile_Communications).[^cite_note-OTE-224]

Other [mobile telecommunications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_telecommunications) companies active in Greece are [Wind Hellas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_Hellas) and [Vodafone Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vodafone_Greece). The total number of active [cellular phone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_phone) accounts in the country in 2009 based on statistics from the country's mobile phone providers was over 20 million,[^cite_note-Eleftherotypia_syndeseis-225] a penetration of 180%.[^cite_note-Eleftherotypia_syndeseis-225] Additionally, there are 5.745 million active landlines in the country.[^cite_note-CIAFactBook-1]

Greece has tended to lag behind its European Union partners in terms of [Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet) use, with the gap closing rapidly in recent years. The percentage of households with [Internet access](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_access) more than doubled between 2006 and 2013, from 23% to 56% respectively (compared with an EU average of 49% and 79%).[^cite_note-Eurostat_Internet_2011-226][^cite_note-Eurostat_Internet_2013-227] At the same time, there was a massive increase in the proportion of households with a [broadband](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadband) connection, from 4% in 2006 to 55% in 2013 (compared with an EU average of 30% and 76%).[^cite_note-Eurostat_Internet_2011-226][^cite_note-Eurostat_Internet_2013-227] By 2025, the percentage of Greek households with Internet access had reached 88.7%.[^cite_note-228]

### Tourism

Main article: [Tourism in Greece](159622.md)



[Porto Carras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porto_Carras) resort, [Chalkidiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalkidiki)



The island of [Santorini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santorini), popular tourist destination.

Tourism in the modern sense only started to flourish in Greece in the years post-1950,[^cite_note-Tourism_1-229][^cite_note-Tourism_2-230] although tourism in ancient times is also documented in relation to religious or sports festivals such as the [Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Olympic_Games).[^cite_note-Tourism_2-230] Since the 1950s, the tourism sector saw an unprecedented boost as arrivals went from 33,000 in 1950 to 11.4 million in 1994.[^cite_note-Tourism_1-229]

Greece attracts more than 16 million tourists each year, thus contributing 18.2% to the nation's GDP in 2008 according to an [OECD](33853117.md) report.[^cite_note-OECD_report-231] The same survey showed that the average tourist expenditure while in Greece was \$1,073, ranking Greece 10th in the world.[^cite_note-OECD_report-231] The number of jobs directly or indirectly related to the tourism sector were 840,000 in 2008 and represented 19% of the country's total labor force.[^cite_note-OECD_report-231] In 2009, Greece welcomed over 19.3 million tourists,[^cite_note-Eurostat_Tourism_table-232] a major increase from the 17.7 million tourists the country welcomed in 2008.[^cite_note-Eurostat_Tourism-233] With 31.3 million international tourists in 2019, Greece was the 7th-most-visited country in the European Union and 13th in the world,[^cite_note-234] marking a steady increase from 18 million tourists in 2013.[^cite_note-World_Tourism_Organization-235]

Among the member states of the European Union, Greece was the most popular destination for residents of [Cyprus](5593.md) and [Sweden](5058739.md) in 2011.[^cite_note-236]

The ministry responsible for tourism is the [Ministry of Culture and Tourism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Culture_and_Tourism_\(Greece\)), while Greece also owns the [Greek National Tourism Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_National_Tourism_Organization) which aims in promoting tourism in Greece.[^cite_note-OECD_report-231]

In recent years a number of well-known tourism-related organizations have placed Greek destinations in the top of their lists. In 2009 [Lonely Planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lonely_Planet) ranked [Thessaloniki](40471.md), the country's second-largest city, the world's fifth best "Ultimate Party Town", alongside cities such as [Montreal](7954681.md) and [Dubai](211583.md),[^cite_note-237] while in 2011 the island of [Santorini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santorini) was voted as the best island in the world by [Travel + Leisure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travel_+_Leisure).[^cite_note-Best_Islands-238] The neighbouring island of [Mykonos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mykonos) was ranked as the 5th best island Europe.[^cite_note-Best_Islands-238] [Thessaloniki](40471.md) was the [European Youth Capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Youth_Capital) in 2014.

### Foreign investment

Since the fall of communism, Greece has invested heavily in neighbouring countries in [Southeast Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Europe). Between 1997 and 2009, 12.11% of [foreign direct investment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_direct_investment) capital in [North Macedonia](23564616.md) was Greek, ranking fourth. Greece invested €1.38 billion in [Bulgaria](3415.md) between 2005 and 2007[^cite_note-Sofia_Echo-244] and many important companies (including [Bulgarian Postbank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgarian_Postbank), [United Bulgarian Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Bulgarian_Bank) Coca-Cola Bulgaria) are owned by Greek financial groups.[^cite_note-Sofia_Echo-244] In [Serbia](29265.md), 250 Greek companies are active with a total investment of over €2 billion.[^cite_note-Kathimerini_Serbia-245] [Romanian](25445.md) statistics from 2016 show that Greek investment in the country exceeded €4 billion, ranking Greece fifth or sixth among foreign investors.[^cite_note-246] Greece has been the largest investor in [Albania](738.md) since the fall of communism with 25% of foreign investments in 2016 coming from Greece, in addition business relations between both are extremely strong and continuously rising.[^cite_note-247]

During the [debt crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_government-debt_crisis) and subsequent [COVID-19 recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_recession), Greece's negative [balance of trade](4810.md) decreased significantly–from €44.3 billion in 2008 to €18.15 billion in 2020–due to a substantial drop in imports.[^cite_note-Imports_Exports_2008_Excel_files-248][^cite_note-249] However, the trade boom of recent years has seen the balance approach pre-crisis levels. Exports increased by 30.9% in 2021 and 38.3% in 2022, while imports rose by 34.6% and 43.6% during the same period.[^cite_note-250] This was followed by a correction in 2023, with exports and imports decreasing by 8.5% and 12.1% respectively.[^cite_note-251] In 2024, exports decreased by 2% and imports increased by 3%.[^cite_note-ELSTAT_trade-23] In 2025, exports and imports decreased by 2.7% and 3.5% respectively.[^cite_note-ELSTAT_trade-23]

Greece is also the largest import partner of [Cyprus](5593.md) (18.0%)[^cite_note-252] and the largest export partner of [Palau](23372.md) (82.4%).[^cite_note-253]

## Infrastructure

### Transport

Main article: [Transport in Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_in_Greece)

See also: [National roads in Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_roads_in_Greece), [Highways in Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highways_in_Greece), [Rail transport in Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_transport_in_Greece), [List of airlines of Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_airlines_of_Greece), [List of airports in Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_airports_in_Greece), and [List of ports in Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_ports_in_Greece)



The [Egnatia Odos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A2_motorway_\(Greece\)), part of [European route E90](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_route_E90).



[Corinth Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corinth_Canal)



[OSE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenic_Railways_Organisation) HQs

As of 2012, Greece had a total of 82 airports,[^cite_note-CIAFactBook-1] of which 67 were paved and six had runways longer than 3,047 meters.[^cite_note-CIAFactBook-1] Of these airports, two are classified as "international" by the [Hellenic Civil Aviation Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenic_Civil_Aviation_Authority),[^cite_note-HCAA-255] but 15 offer international services.[^cite_note-HCAA-255] Additionally Greece has 9 heliports.[^cite_note-CIAFactBook-1] Greece does not have a [flag carrier](465816.md), but the country's airline industry is dominated by [Aegean Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegean_Airlines) and its subsidiary [Olympic Air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Air).

Between 1975 and 2009, [Olympic Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Airlines) (known after 2003 as Olympic Airlines) was the country's state-owned flag carrier, but financial problems led to its privatization and relaunch as Olympic Air in 2009. Both Aegean Airlines and Olympic Air have won awards for their services; in 2009 and 2011, Aegean Airlines was awarded the "Best regional airline in Europe" award by [Skytrax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skytrax),[^cite_note-Aegean_Awards-256] and also has two gold and one silver awards by the [ERA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Regional_Airlines_Association),[^cite_note-Aegean_Awards-256] while Olympic Air holds one silver ERA award for "Airline of the Year"[^cite_note-ERA10-11-257] as well as a *"[Condé Nast Traveller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cond%C3%A9_Nast_Traveller) 2011 Readers Choice Awards: Top Domestic Airline"* award.[^cite_note-258]

The Greek road network is made up of 116,986 km of roads,[^cite_note-CIAFactBook-1] of which 1863 km are [highways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highways), ranking 24th worldwide, as of 2016.[^cite_note-CIAFactBook-1] Since the entry of Greece to the [European Community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Community) (now the European Union), a number of important projects (such as the [Egnatia Odos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A2_motorway_\(Greece\)) and the [Attiki Odos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attiki_Odos)) have been co-funded by the organization, helping to upgrade the country's road network. In 2007, Greece ranked 8th in the European Union in goods transported by road at almost 500 million tons.

Greece's [rail network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railways_of_Greece) is estimated to be at 2,548 km.[^cite_note-CIAFactBook-1] Rail transport in Greece is operated by [TrainOSE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TrainOSE), a current subsidiary of the [Ferrovie dello Stato Italiane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferrovie_dello_Stato_Italiane) after the [Hellenic Railways Organisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenic_Railways_Organisation) had sold its 100% stake on the operator. Most of the country's network is [standard gauge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_gauge) (1,565 km),[^cite_note-CIAFactBook-1] while the country also has 983 km of [narrow gauge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narrow_gauge).[^cite_note-CIAFactBook-1] A total of 764 km of rail are electrified.[^cite_note-CIAFactBook-1] Greece has rail connections with [Bulgaria](3415.md), [North Macedonia](23564616.md) and [Turkey](11125639.md). A total of three [suburban railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suburban_railway) systems ([Proastiakos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proastiakos)) in [Athens](1216.md), [Thessaloniki](40471.md) and [Patras](470691.md), and two [metro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metro_\(rapid_transit\)) systems ([Athens Metro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens_Metro), [Thessaloniki Metro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thessaloniki_Metro)) are in operation.

According to [Eurostat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurostat), Greece's largest port by tons of goods transported in 2010 is the port of [Aghioi Theodoroi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aghioi_Theodoroi), with 17.38 million tons.[^cite_note-Eurostat_ports-259] The [Port of Thessaloniki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Thessaloniki) comes second with 15.8 million tons,[^cite_note-Eurostat_ports-259] followed by the [Port of Piraeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Piraeus), with 13.2 million tons,[^cite_note-Eurostat_ports-259] and the port of [Eleusis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleusis), with 12.37 million tons.[^cite_note-Eurostat_ports-259] The total number of goods transported through Greece in 2010 amounted to 124.38 million tons,[^cite_note-Eurostat_ports-259] a considerable drop from the 164.3 million tons transported through the country in 2007.[^cite_note-Eurostat_ports-259] Since then, Piraeus has grown to become the Mediterranean's third-largest port thanks to heavy investment by [Chinese](5405.md) logistics giant [COSCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COSCO). In 2013, Piraeus was declared the fastest-growing port in the world.[^cite_note-260]

In 2010 Piraeus handled 513,319 [TEUs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twenty-foot_equivalent_unit),[^cite_note-OLP_Statistical_Data-261] followed by Thessaloniki, which handled 273,282 TEUs.[^cite_note-OLTH_Statistical_Data-262] In the same year, 83.9 million people passed through Greece's ports,[^cite_note-Eurostat_passengers_ports-263] 12.7 million through the port of [Paloukia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paloukia) in [Salamis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salamis_Island),[^cite_note-Eurostat_passengers_ports-263] another 12.7 through the port of [Perama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perama),[^cite_note-Eurostat_passengers_ports-263] 9.5 million through [Piraeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piraeus)[^cite_note-Eurostat_passengers_ports-263] and 2.7 million through [Igoumenitsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igoumenitsa).[^cite_note-Eurostat_passengers_ports-263] In 2013, Piraeus handled a record 3.16 million TEUs, the third-largest figure in the Mediterranean, of which 2.52 million were transported through Pier II, owned by COSCO and 644,000 were transported through Pier I, owned by the Greek state.

### Energy

Further information: [Energy in Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_in_Greece) and [Renewable energy in Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renewable_energy_in_Greece)



Electricity generation in Greece - percentage share

Energy production in Greece is dominated by the [Public Power Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Power_Corporation_of_Greece) (known mostly by its acronym ΔΕΗ, or in English DEI). In 2009 DEI supplied for 85.6% of all energy demand in Greece,[^cite_note-DEI_2010-264] while the number fell to 77.3% in 2010.[^cite_note-DEI_2010-264] Almost half (48%) of DEI's power output is generated using [lignite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lignite), a drop from the 51.6% in 2009.[^cite_note-DEI_2010-264] Another 12% comes from Hydroelectric power plants[^cite_note-Invest_in_Greece_energy-265] and another 20% from [natural gas](22131.md).[^cite_note-Invest_in_Greece_energy-265] Between 2009 and 2010, independent companies' energy production increased by 56%,[^cite_note-DEI_2010-264] from 2,709 [Gigawatt hour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilowatt_hour) in 2009 to 4,232 GWh in 2010.[^cite_note-DEI_2010-264]

In 2008 renewable energy accounted for 8% of the country's total energy consumption,[^cite_note-Eurostat_renewable_energy-266] a rise from the 7.2% it accounted for in 2006,[^cite_note-Eurostat_renewable_energy-266] but still below the EU average of 10% in 2008.[^cite_note-Eurostat_renewable_energy-266] 10% of the country's renewable energy comes from [solar power](13690575.md),[^cite_note-Eurostat_Sustainable-184] while most comes from [biomass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomass) and waste recycling.[^cite_note-Eurostat_Sustainable-184] In line with the [European Commission](9974.md)'s Directive on Renewable Energy, Greece aims to get 18% of its energy from renewable sources by 2020.[^cite_note-Directive-267] In 2013 and for several months, Greece produced more than 20% of its electricity from renewable energy sources and hydroelectric power plants.[^cite_note-268] Greece currently does not have any [nuclear power plants](207347.md) in operation, however in 2009 the [Academy of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Athens_\(modern\)) suggested that research in the possibility of Greek nuclear power plants begin.[^cite_note-Academy_of_Athens_nuclear_power-269]

Greece had 10 million [barrels](594043.md) of proven [oil reserves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_reserves) as of 1 January 2012.[^cite_note-CIAFactBook-1] [Hellenic Petroleum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenic_Petroleum) is the country's largest oil company, followed by [Motor Oil Hellas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motor_Oil_Hellas). Greece's oil production stands at 1,751 barrels per day (bbl/d), ranked 95th worldwide,[^cite_note-CIAFactBook-1] while it exports 19,960 bbl/d, ranked 53rd,[^cite_note-CIAFactBook-1] and imports 355,600 bbl/d, ranked 25th.[^cite_note-CIAFactBook-1]

In 2011 the Greek government approved the start of oil exploration and drilling in three locations within Greece,[^cite_note-Invest_in_Greece_oil-270] with an estimated output of 250 to 300 million barrels over the next 15 to 20 years.[^cite_note-Invest_in_Greece_oil-270] The estimated output in [euros](9472.md) of the three deposits is €25 billion over a 15-year period,[^cite_note-Invest_in_Greece_oil-270] of which €13–€14 billion will enter state coffers.[^cite_note-Invest_in_Greece_oil-270] Greece's dispute with Turkey [over the Aegean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegean_dispute) poses substantial obstacles to oil exploration in the [Aegean Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegean_Sea).

In addition to the above, Greece is also to start oil and gas exploration in other locations in the [Ionian Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionian_Sea), as well as the [Libyan Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libyan_Sea), within the Greek [exclusive economic zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exclusive_economic_zone), south of [Crete](6591.md).[^cite_note-Skai_oil-271][^cite_note-Skai_oil_2-272] The [Ministry of the Environment, Energy and Climate Change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_the_Environment,_Energy_and_Climate_Change_\(Greece\)) announced that there was interest from various countries (including [Norway](21241.md) and the [United States](3434750.md)) in exploration,[^cite_note-Skai_oil_2-272] and the first results regarding the amount of oil and gas in these locations were expected in the summer of 2012.[^cite_note-Skai_oil_2-272] In November 2012, a report published by Deutsche Bank estimated the value of natural gas reserves south of [Crete](6591.md) at €427 billion.[^cite_note-273]

A number of [oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_pipeline) and [gas pipelines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_pipeline) are currently under construction or under planning in the country. Such projects include the *Interconnector Turkey-Greece-Italy* (ITGI) and *South Stream* gas pipelines.[^cite_note-Invest_in_Greece_energy-265]

[EuroAsia Interconnector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EuroAsia_Interconnector) will electrically connect Attica and [Crete](6591.md) in Greece with [Cyprus](5593.md) and [Israel](9282173.md) with 2000 MW [HVDC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-voltage_direct_current) [undersea power cable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_power_cable).[^cite_note-EA-274][^cite_note-mirror35197-275] [EuroAsia Interconnector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EuroAsia_Interconnector) is specially important for isolated systems, like Cyprus and Crete. [Crete](6591.md) is energetically isolated from mainland Greece and Hellenic Republic covers for Crete electricity costs difference of around €300 million per year.[^cite_note-CRE-276]

In April 2026 following the energy crisis, due to the [2026 Iran war](82533582.md), the government introduced a €300 million fuel subsidy package. It aimed to help households and farmers with the rising energy prices. The package includes cuts to diesel prices, fuel vouchers for consumers, and financial support for farmers and ferry operators. By this the hope to ease the cost of living within Greece.[^cite_note-277]

### Water supply and sanitation

Main article: [Water supply and sanitation in Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_supply_and_sanitation_in_Greece)

This section is an excerpt from [Water supply and sanitation in Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_supply_and_sanitation_in_Greece).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Water_supply_and_sanitation_in_Greece\&action=edit)]

[Water supply and sanitation in Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_supply_and_sanitation_in_Greece) is characterised by diversity. While [Athens](1216.md) receives its water from a series of [reservoirs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reservoir), some of which are located 200 km away, some small islands are supplied with water from [tankers](2506366.md). Greeks have suffered from repeated [droughts](37335.md), the most recent one occurring in 2007. The [EU](9317.md) supported the construction of numerous [wastewater treatment plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wastewater_treatment_plant) since the 1990s, in order to achieve EU environmental standards. While the wastewater discharge of the biggest cities is now in compliance with these standards, some smaller towns still lag behind.

Two state utilities, [EYDAP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EYDAP) in [Athens](1216.md) and [EYATH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EYATH?action=edit\&redlink=1) in [Thessaloniki](40471.md), are in charge of water supply and sanitation for about half the population. These publicly traded companies post profits despite low tariffs, partly due to investment subsidies from the state. Outside the two largest cities, 230 different municipal utilities are in charge of water supply and sanitation. As part of the so-called [Kallikratis plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kallikratis_plan) for local government reform, smaller municipalities and municipal utilities are to be merged into larger units. The [Greek financial crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_financial_crisis) is making it difficult for Greek utilities to increase tariffs, to enforce payment of arrears and to service their debt.

## Taxation and government finances

Main article: [Taxation in Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxation_in_Greece)



Revenues of Greece between 1999 and 2010 as a percentage of GDP, compared to the EU average.

Greece has a tiered tax system based on [progressive taxation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_taxation). Greek law recognizes six categories of taxable income:[^cite_note-Eurostat_Taxation-278] [immovable property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immovable_property), [movable property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Movable_property) (investment), income from agriculture, business, employment, and income from professional activities. Greece's [personal income tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_income_tax) rate, until recently, ranged from 0% for annual incomes below €12,000[^cite_note-Eurostat_Taxation-278] to 45% for annual incomes over €100,000.[^cite_note-Eurostat_Taxation-278] Under the new 2010 tax reform, [tax exemptions](960403.md) have been abolished.[^cite_note-Eurostat_Taxation-278]

Also under the new austerity measures and among other changes, the personal income tax-free ceiling has been reduced to €5,000 per annum[^cite_note-New_Tax_Free_Ceiling-279] while further future changes, for example abolition of this ceiling, are already being planned.[^cite_note-New_Tax_Bill-280]

Greece's [corporate tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_tax) dropped from 40% in 2000[^cite_note-Eurostat_Taxation-278] to 20% in 2010.[^cite_note-Eurostat_Taxation-278] For 2011 only, corporate tax was at 24%.[^cite_note-Eurostat_Taxation-278] [Value added tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_added_tax) (VAT) has gone up in 2010 compared to 2009: 23% as opposed to 19%.[^cite_note-Eurostat_Taxation-278]

The lowest VAT possible is 6.5% (previously 4.5%)[^cite_note-Eurostat_Taxation-278] for newspapers, periodicals and cultural event tickets, while a tax rate of 13% (from 9%)[^cite_note-Eurostat_Taxation-278] applies to certain [service sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Service_sector) professions. Additionally, both employers and employees have to pay [social contribution taxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax#Social-security_contributions), which apply at a rate of 16%[^cite_note-Eurostat_Taxation-278] for [white collar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White-collar_worker) jobs and 19.5%[^cite_note-Eurostat_Taxation-278] for [blue collar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_collar) jobs, and are used for [social insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_insurance). In 2017 the VAT tax rate was 24%[^cite_note-Eurostat_Taxation2-281] with minor exceptions, 13% reduced for some basic foodstuffs which will be soon abolished and everything, as it seems, will soon go to 24% in order to fight the phantom of tax evasion.

The Ministry of Finance expected tax revenues for 2012 to be €52.7 billion (€23.6 billion in direct taxes and €29.1 billion in indirect taxes),[^cite_note-Budget_2012-282] an increase of 5.8% from 2011.[^cite_note-Budget_2012-282] In 2012, the government was expected to have considerably higher tax revenues than in 2011 on a number of sectors, primarily housing (an increase of 217.5% from 2011).[^cite_note-Budget_2012-282]

### Tax evasion

Greece suffers from very high levels of [tax evasion](2256795.md). In the last quarter of 2005, tax evasion reached 49%,[^cite_note-Ethnos_Forodiafygi-283] while in January 2006 it fell to 41.6%.[^cite_note-Ethnos_Forodiafygi-283] It is worth noting that the newspaper [Ethnos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnos_\(newspaper\)) which published these figures went bankrupt; it is no longer published and some sources suggest that the information it had published was highly debatable.[^cite_note-284] A study by researchers from the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago) concluded that tax evasion in 2009 by self-employed professionals alone in Greece (accountants, dentists, lawyers, doctors, personal tutors and independent financial advisers) was €28 billion or 31% of the budget deficit that year.[^cite_note-285]

Greece's "shadow economy" was estimated at 24.3% of GDP in 2012, compared with 28.6% for Estonia, 26.5% for Latvia, 21.6% for Italy, 17.1% for Belgium, 14.7% for Sweden, 13.7% for Finland, and 13.5% for Germany, and is certainly related to the fact that the percentage of Greeks that are self-employed is more than double the EU average (2013 est.).[^cite_note-Debt_Past-61]

The [Tax Justice Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_Justice_Network) estimated in 2011 that there were over 20 billion euros in [Swiss](26748.md) bank accounts held by Greeks.[^cite_note-Skai_TJN-286] The former Finance Minister of Greece, Evangelos Venizelos, was quoted as saying, "Around 15,000 individuals and companies owe the taxman 37 billion euros".[^cite_note-287] Additionally, the TJN put the number of Greek-owned [off-shore companies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offshore_investment) at over 10,000.[^cite_note-Ta_Nea_TJN-288]

In 2012, Swiss estimates suggested that Greeks had some 20 billion euros in Switzerland of which only one percent had been declared as taxable in Greece.[^cite_note-289] Estimates in 2015 were even more dramatic. They indicated that the amount due to the government of Greece from Greeks' accounts in Swiss banks totaled around 80 billion euros.[^cite_note-The_Local_ch-290][^cite_note-Swissinfo.ch-291]

A mid-2017 report indicated Greeks have been "taxed to the hilt" and many believed that the risk of penalties for tax evasion were less serious than the risk of bankruptcy. One method of evasion is the so-called black market, grey economy or shadow economy: work is done for cash payment which is not declared as income; as well, VAT is not collected and remitted.[^cite_note-292] A January 2017 report[^cite_note-293] by the DiaNEOsis think-tank indicated that unpaid taxes in Greece at the time totaled approximately 95 billion euros, up from 76 billion euros in 2015, much of it was expected to be uncollectable. Another early 2017 study estimated that the loss to the government as a result of tax evasion was between 6% and 9% of the country's GDP, or roughly between 11 billion and 16 billion euros per annum.[^cite_note-keeptalkinggreece.com-294]

The shortfall in the collection of VAT (sales tax) is also significant. In 2014, the government collected 28% less than was owed to it; this shortfall was about double the average for the EU. The uncollected amount that year was about 4.9 billion euros.[^cite_note-295] The DiaNEOsis study estimated that 3.5% of GDP is lost due to VAT fraud, while losses due to smuggling of alcohol, tobacco and petrol amounted to approximately another 0.5% of the country's GDP.[^cite_note-keeptalkinggreece.com-294]

#### Planned solutions

Following similar actions by the [United Kingdom](31717.md) and [Germany](11867.md), the Greek government was in talks with [Switzerland](26748.md) in 2011, attempting to force Swiss banks to reveal information on the back accounts of Greek citizens.[^cite_note-Skai_Elvetia-296] The [Ministry of Finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Finance_\(Greece\)) stated that Greeks with Swiss bank accounts would either be required to pay a tax or reveal information such as the identity of the bank account holder to the Greek internal revenue services.[^cite_note-Skai_Elvetia-296] The Greek and Swiss governments were to reach a deal on the matter by the end of 2011.[^cite_note-Skai_Elvetia-296]

The solution demanded by Greece still had not been effected as of 2015. That year, estimates indicated that the amount of evaded taxes stored in Swiss banks was around 80 billion euros. By then, however, a tax treaty to address this issue was under serious negotiation between the Greek and Swiss governments.[^cite_note-The_Local_ch-290][^cite_note-Swissinfo.ch-291] An agreement was finally ratified by Switzerland on 1 March 2016 creating a new tax transparency law that would allow for a more effective battle against tax evasion. Starting in 2018, banks in both Greece and Switzerland will exchange information about the bank accounts of citizens of the other country to minimize the possibility of hiding untaxed income.[^cite_note-297]

In 2016 and 2017, the government was encouraging the use of [credit cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credit_card) or [debit cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debit_card) to pay for goods and services in order to reduce cash only payments. By January 2017, taxpayers were only granted tax-allowances or deductions when payments were made electronically, with a "paper trail" of the transactions that the government could easily audit. This was expected to reduce the problem of businesses taking payments but not issuing an invoice;[^cite_note-298] that tactic had been used by various companies to avoid payment of VAT (sales) tax as well as income tax.[^cite_note-299][^cite_note-300]

By 28 July 2017, numerous businesses were required by law to install a point of sale device to enable them to accept payment by credit or debit card. Failure to comply with the electronic payment facility can lead to fines of up to 1,500 euros. The requirement applied to around 400,000 firms or individuals in 85 professions. The greater use of cards was one of the factors that had already achieved significant increases in VAT collection in 2016.[^cite_note-301]

### Corruption

Main article: [Corruption in Greece](37482000.md)

This section is an excerpt from [Corruption in Greece](37482000.md).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Corruption_in_Greece\&action=edit)]

[Corruption in Greece](37482000.md) is considered to be significant,[^cite_note-Corruption_in_Greece_guardian.co.uk-302][^cite_note-Corruption_in_Greece_Ethnos_Forodiafygi-303] with the country ranking as one of the most corrupt in the [European Union](9317.md) according to the [Corruption Perception Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corruption_Perception_Index). [Transparency International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transparency_International) stated in 2012 that corruption had played a major role in causing the [Greek financial crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_financial_crisis).[^cite_note-304] [Tax evasion](2256795.md) was described by Greek politicians as "a national sport"—with up to €30 billion per year going uncollected, according to a 2012 estimate.[^cite_note-305] A 2016 estimate indicated that between €11 billion and €16 billion per annum were not collectable.[^cite_note-Corruption_in_Greece_keeptalkinggreece.com-306] Other significant amounts were uncollected due to VAT ([sales tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sales_tax)) fraud[^cite_note-307] and smuggling.[^cite_note-Corruption_in_Greece_auto-308] In 2016, the [OECD](33853117.md), Greece and the [European Commission](9974.md) launched a project to increase integrity and reduce corruption in Greece through the technical empowerment of the Greek authorities for the implementation of Greece's National Anti-Corruption Action Plan (NACAP).[^cite_note-309]

[Political corruption](66220.md) is also acknowledged as a significant problem by many observers.

The government's anti-corruption efforts have been evaluated as ineffective, according to several sources, which has been attributed to poor enforcement of anti-corruption legislation and the ineffectiveness of anti-corruption agencies. Anti-corruption agencies have been hindered by excessive political influence and continuous replacement of staff. Recent involvement of high-ranking public officials in corruption cases has been reported in the media.[^cite_note-310][^cite_note-311]

## Living standards and social welfare

Living standards in Greece have been shaped by long-term trends in wages, poverty, social exclusion, and demographic change. These factors continue to influence the economic well-being of households across the country.

### Wages and purchasing power

Nominal [wages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wages) in Greece have increased in recent years, although real incomes remain below the [European Union](9317.md) average. According to the [ERGANI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ERGANI_\(labour_market_system\)?action=edit\&redlink=1) labour-market information system, the average gross monthly salary rose from €1,046 in 2019 to €1,342 in 2024.[^cite_note-OTwages2024-312] The median annual income for full-time employees was approximately €17,954 in 2024, ranking among the lowest in the EU.[^cite_note-OTmedian2024-313] Rising [living costs](522934.md), particularly for [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) and essential goods, have limited improvements in households’ [purchasing power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purchasing_power).[^cite_note-Ekath2025-314]

### Poverty, social exclusion and demographic pressures

Despite economic recovery, levels of [poverty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poverty) and [social exclusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_exclusion) remain significant. The [Hellenic Statistical Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenic_Statistical_Authority) reported that 26.9 percent of the population, about 2.74 million people, were at risk of poverty or social exclusion in 2024, a slight increase from 2023.[^cite_note-ProtoPoverty2025-315] Severe material and social deprivation affected 14.0 percent of the population in the same year.[^cite_note-ProtoPoverty2025-315] High housing and energy costs have been identified as major contributing factors.[^cite_note-Ekath2025-314]

Demographic trends place additional pressure on social welfare systems. Greece’s population declined from about 11.1 million in 2010 to 10.4 million in 2024, while the proportion of residents aged 65 and over rose to 23.3 percent.[^cite_note-Oxan2024-316] These developments have implications for pensions, [healthcare services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Healthcare_in_Greece), and the country’s [labour force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_force), with potential effects on future living standards and inequality.

## Labour force

### Working hours

In 2011, 53.3 percent of employed persons worked more than 40 to 49 hours a week and 24.8 percent worked more than 50 hours a week, totaling up to 78.1 percent of employed persons working 40 or more hours a week.[^cite_note-\:0-320] When accounting for varying age groups, the percentage of employees working 40 to 49 hours a week peaked in the 25 to 29 age range.[^cite_note-\:0-320]  As workers got older, they gradually decreased in percentage working 40 to 49 hours, but increased in working 50+ hours, suggesting a correlation that as employees grow older, they work more hours. Of different occupation groups, skilled agricultural, forestry, and fishery workers and managers were the most likely to work 50+ hours; however, they do not take up a significant portion of the labor force, only 14.3 percent.[^cite_note-321]  In 2014, the average number of [working hours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Working_time) for Greek employees was 2124 hours, ranking as the third highest among [OECD](33853117.md) countries and the highest in the [Eurozone](184391.md).[^cite_note-\:1-322]

Recent trends in employment indicate that the number of working hours will decrease in the future due to the rise of part-time work. Since 2011, average working hours have decreased.[^cite_note-\:1-322] In 1998, Greece passed legislation introducing part-time employment in [public services](1291782.md) with the goal of reducing unemployment, increasing the total, but decreasing the average number of hours worked per employee.[^cite_note-323] Whether the legislation was successful in increasing public-sector part-time work, labor market trends show that part-time employment has increased from 7.7 percent in 2007 to 11 percent in 2016 of total employment.[^cite_note-324] Both men and women have had the part-time share of employment increase over this period. While women still constitute a majority of part-time workers, recently men have been taking a larger share of part-time employment.[^cite_note-325]

## Currency

Between 1832 and 2002 the currency of Greece was the [drachma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_drachma). After signing the [Maastricht Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maastricht_Treaty), Greece applied to join the [eurozone](184391.md). The two main [convergence criteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_convergence_criteria) were a maximum budget deficit of 3% of GDP and a declining public debt if it stood above 60% of GDP. Greece met the criteria as shown in its 1999 annual public account. On 1 January 2001, Greece joined the eurozone, with the adoption of the euro at the fixed exchange rate ₯340.75 to €1.[^cite_note-EUprofileGreece-326] However, in 2001 the euro only existed electronically, so the physical exchange from drachma to euro only took place on 1 January 2002. This was followed by a ten-year period for eligible exchange of drachma to euro, which ended on 1 March 2012.[^cite_note-Bank_of_Greece_drachma-327][^cite_note-Drachma_exchange_rate-328]

Prior to the adoption of the euro, 64% of Greek citizens viewed the new currency positively,[^cite_note-Public_Issue_Evro-329] but in February 2005 this figure fell to 26% and by June 2005 it fell further to 20%.[^cite_note-Public_Issue_Evro-329] Since 2010 the figure has risen again, and a survey in September 2011 showed that 63% of Greek citizens viewed of the euro positively.[^cite_note-Public_Issue_Evro-329]

## Poverty rate

As a result of the recession sparked by the public debt crisis, [poverty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poverty) has increased. The rate of people at risk of poverty or [social exclusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_exclusion) reached a high of 36% in 2014, before subsiding over the following years to 26.1% in 2023.[^cite_note-331] The rate of [extreme poverty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_poverty) rose to 15% in 2015, up from 8.9% in 2011 and a huge increase from 2009 when it did not exceed 2.2%.[^cite_note-diaNEOsis-332] In 2015, the rate among children aged 0–17 was 17.6% and for young people aged 18–29 the rate was 24.4%.[^cite_note-diaNEOsis-332] With [unemployment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unemployment) on the rise, those without jobs between 2012 and 2015 were at the highest risk of poverty (70–75%), up from less than 50% in 2011.[^cite_note-diaNEOsis-332] Those out of work lose their [health insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_insurance) after two years, further exacerbating the poverty rate. Younger unemployed people tend to rely on the older generations of their families for financial support. However, long-term unemployment has depleted [pension funds](43615.md) due to fewer workers making social security contributions, resulting in higher poverty rates in intergenerational households reliant on the reduced [pensions](43613.md) received by their [retired](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retirement) members. Over the course of the economic crisis, Greeks have endured significant job losses and [wage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wage) cuts, as well as deep cuts to [workers' compensation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Workers'_compensation) and [welfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welfare_spending) benefits. From 2008 to 2013, Greeks became 40% poorer on average, and in 2014 saw their [disposable household income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disposable_and_discretionary_income) drop below 2003 levels.[^cite_note-Reuters-333]

By the late 2010s and early 2020s, Greece gradually recovered from the worst effects of the debt crisis, with unemployment and poverty rates declining compared to their peak levels. Economic growth, tourism recovery, and improving public finances contributed to a reduction in social exclusion, although living standards for many households remained below pre-crisis levels. However, new challenges emerged following the [COVID-19 pandemic](62750956.md) and the [global inflation surge of 2021–2023](69605317.md), which increased the cost of housing, energy, and basic goods. In 2023, 26.1% of the population remained at risk of poverty or social exclusion, one of the highest rates in the European Union.[^cite_note-334] Severe material and social deprivation also remained comparatively high in Greece, affecting 13.5% of the population in 2023.[^cite_note-335] Despite economic recovery and falling unemployment, many Greeks continued to face financial insecurity, particularly young people, pensioners, and low-income households, reflecting the long-term social consequences of the debt crisis.
