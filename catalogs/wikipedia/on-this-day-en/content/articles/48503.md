**Provence**[^cite_note-1] is a geographical, [cultural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture) region and [historical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History) province of southeastern [France](5843419.md), which stretches from the left bank of the lower [Rhône](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rh%C3%B4ne) to the west to the [Italian border](47329029.md) to the east; it is bordered by the [Mediterranean Sea](19006.md) to the south. It largely corresponds with the modern administrative [region](58846.md) of [Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provence-Alpes-C%C3%B4te_d'Azur) and includes the [departments](38523.md) of [Var](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Var_\(department\)), [Bouches-du-Rhône](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bouches-du-Rh%C3%B4ne), [Alpes-de-Haute-Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpes-de-Haute-Provence), as well as parts of [Alpes-Maritimes](80989.md) and [Vaucluse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaucluse).[^cite_note-Petit_Robert_1988-2] The largest city of the region and its modern-day capital is [Marseille](40888948.md).

The [Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome) made the region the first Roman province beyond the Alps and called it *Provincia Romana*, which evolved into the present name. Until 1481 it was ruled by the [counts of Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_rulers_of_Provence) from their capital in Aquae Sextiae (today [Aix-en-Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aix-en-Provence)), then became a province of the [kings of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kings_of_France).[^cite_note-Petit_Robert_1988-2] It also hosted the [Avignon papacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avignon_papacy) in the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), when the Pope and his Curia fled Rome. While the region has been part of France for more than 500 years, it still retains a distinct cultural and linguistic identity, particularly in the interior of the region.[^cite_note-3]

## Etymology

The region got its name in Roman times, when it was known as *Provincia Romana*, simply "the Roman province". This name eventually was shortened to Provincia (the province),[^cite_note-4] and as the language evolved from Latin to [Provençal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proven%C3%A7al_dialect), so did the pronunciation and spelling.

## History

Main article: [History of Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Provence)

### Prehistoric Provence



The entrance to the [Cosquer Cave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosquer_Cave), decorated with paintings of [auks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auk), [bison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bison), [seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinniped) and outlines of hands dating to 27,000 to 19,000 BC, is located 37 metres under the surface of the [Calanque de Morgiou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calanque_de_Morgiou) in [Marseille](40888948.md).

The coast of Provence has some of the earliest known sites of human habitation in Europe. Primitive [stone tools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stone_tool) dating back 1 to 1.05 million years BC have been found in the [Grotte du Vallonnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grotte_du_Vallonnet) near [Roquebrune-Cap-Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roquebrune-Cap-Martin), between [Monaco](19261.md) and [Menton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menton).[^cite_note-5] More sophisticated tools, worked on both sides of the stone and dating to 600,000 BC, were found in the Cave of Escale at [Saint-Estève-Janson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Est%C3%A8ve-Janson); tools from 400,000 BC and some of the first fireplaces in Europe were found at [Terra Amata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terra_Amata_\(archaeological_site\)) in Nice.[^cite_note-6] Tools dating to the [Middle Paleolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Paleolithic) (300,000 BC) and [Upper Paleolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Paleolithic) (30,000–10,000 BC) were discovered in the Observatory Cave, in the [Jardin Exotique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jardin_Exotique_de_Monaco) of [Monaco](19261.md).[^cite_note-7]

The [Paleolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleolithic) period in Provence saw great changes in the climate. Two [ice ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_age) came and went, and the [sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level) changed dramatically. At the beginning of the Paleolithic, the sea level in western Provence was 150 meters higher than today. By the end of the Paleolithic, it had dropped to 100 to 150 meters below the [sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level) today. The cave dwellings of the early inhabitants of Provence were regularly flooded by the rising sea or left far from the sea and swept away by [erosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erosion).[^cite_note-ReferenceA-8]



The [Dolmen of the Fairy Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolmen_of_the_Fairy_Stone) (2500 to 2000 BC) near [Draguignan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Draguignan)

The changes in the sea level led to one of the most remarkable discoveries of signs of early man in Provence. In 1985, a diver named Henri Cosquer discovered the mouth of a submarine cave 37 metres below the surface of the [Calanque de Morgiou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calanque_de_Morgiou) near Marseille. The entrance led to a cave above sea level. Inside, the walls of the [Cosquer Cave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosquer_Cave) are decorated with drawings of [bison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bison), seals, [auks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auk), [horses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horse) and outlines of human hands, dating to between 27,000 and 19,000 BC.[^cite_note-9]



Provençal landscape near [Vaison-la-Romaine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaison-la-Romaine)

The end of the Paleolithic and beginning of the [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic) period saw the sea settle at its present level, a warming of the climate and the retreat of the forests. The disappearance of the forests and the deer and other easily hunted game meant that the inhabitants of Provence had to survive on [rabbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabbit), [snails](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snail) and wild sheep. In about 6000 BC, the Castelnovian people, living around [Châteauneuf-les-Martigues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C3%A2teauneuf-les-Martigues), were among the first people in Europe to domesticate wild sheep, and to cease moving constantly from place to place. Once they settled in one place they were able to develop new industries. Inspired by pottery from the eastern Mediterranean, in about 6000 BC they created the first pottery made in France.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-8]

Around 6000 BC, a wave of new settlers from the east, the [Chasséens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chass%C3%A9en_culture), arrived in Provence. They were farmers and warriors, and gradually displaced the earlier pastoral people from their lands. They were followed about 2500 BC by another wave of people, also farmers, known as the Courronniens, who arrived by sea and settled along the coast of what is now the Bouches-du-Rhône.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-8] Traces of these early civilisations can be found in many parts of Provence. A Neolithic site dating to about 6,000 BC was discovered in Marseille near the [Saint-Charles railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gare_de_Marseille_Saint-Charles). and a [dolmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolmen) from the [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age) (2500–900 BC) can be found near [Draguignan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Draguignan).

### Ligures and Celts in Provence

Between the 10th and 4th century BC, the [Ligures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ligures) were found in Provence from [Massilia](40888948.md) as far as modern [Liguria](71864.md). They were of uncertain origin; they may have been the descendants of the indigenous Neolithic peoples.[^cite_note-J.R._Palanque,_Pg._33-10] According to [Strabo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strabo), the Ligurians, living in proximity to numerous Celtic mountain tribes, were a different people (ἑτεροεθνεῖς), but "were similar to the Celts in their modes of life".[^cite_note-11] They did not have their own alphabet, but their language remains in place names in Provence ending in the suffixes -*asc*, -*osc*, -*inc*, -*ates*, and -*auni*.[^cite_note-J.R._Palanque,_Pg._33-10] The ancient geographer [Posidonius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posidonius) wrote of them: "Their country is savage and dry. The soil is so rocky that you cannot plant anything without striking stones. The men compensate for the lack of wheat by hunting... They climb the mountains like goats."[^cite_note-12] They were also warlike; they invaded Italy and went as far as Rome in the 4th century BC, and they later aided the passage of [Hannibal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hannibal), on his way to attack Rome (218 BC). Traces of the Ligures remain today in the [dolmens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolmens) and other megaliths found in eastern Provence, in the primitive stone shelters called 'Bories' found in the [Luberon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luberon) and [Comtat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comtat_Venaissin), and in the rock carvings in the [Valley of Marvels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valley_of_Marvels) near [Mont Bégo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mont_B%C3%A9go) in the Alpes-Maritimes, at an altitude of 2,000 meters.[^cite_note-R._Palanque,_Pg._34-13]

Between the 8th and 5th centuries BC, tribes of Celtic peoples, probably coming from Central Europe, also began moving into Provence. They had weapons made of iron, which allowed them to easily defeat the local tribes, who were still armed with bronze weapons. One tribe, called the [Segobriga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Segobriga), settled near modern-day Marseille. The Caturiges, Tricastins, and Cavares settled to the west of the [Durance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durance) river.[^cite_note-14]

[Celts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celts) and Ligurians spread throughout the area and the Celto-Ligures eventually shared the territory of Provence, each tribe in its own alpine valley or settlement along a river, each with its own king and dynasty. They built hilltop forts and settlements, later given the Latin name *[oppidum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oppidum)*. Today the traces of 165 *oppida* are found in the Var, and as many as 285 in the Alpes-Maritimes.[^cite_note-R._Palanque,_Pg._34-13] They worshipped various aspects of nature, establishing sacred woods at Sainte-Baume and Gemenos, and healing springs at Glanum and Vernègues. Later, in the 5th and 4th centuries BC, the different tribes formed confederations; the Voconces in the area from the [Isère](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Is%C3%A8re) to the [Vaucluse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaucluse); the Cavares in the Comtat; and the Salyens, from the [Rhône](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rh%C3%B4ne) river to the Var. The tribes began to trade their local products, iron, silver, alabaster, marble, gold, resin, wax, honey and cheese; with their neighbours, first by trading routes along the Rhône river, and later [Etruscan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etruscan_civilization) traders visited the coast. Etruscan [amphorae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphorae) from the 7th and 6th centuries BC have been found in Marseille, Cassis, and in hilltop *oppida* in the region.[^cite_note-R._Palanque,_Pg._34-13]

### Greeks in Provence

Main article: [Greeks in pre-Roman Gaul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greeks_in_pre-Roman_Gaul)



Remains of the ancient harbour of [Massalia](40888948.md), near the Old Port of Marseille

Traders from the island of Rhodes were visiting the coast of Provence in the 7th century BC. Rhodes pottery from that century has been found in Marseille, near [Martigues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martigues) and Istres, and at Mont Garou and Evenos near [Toulon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toulon). The traders from Rhodes gave their names to the ancient town of Rhodanousia ([Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language): 'Ροδανουσίαν) (now [Trinquetaille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinquetaille), across the Rhône river from Arles), and to the main river of Provence, the Rhodanos, today known as the Rhône.[^cite_note-15]

The first permanent Greek settlement was [Massalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massalia), established at modern-day Marseille in about 600 BC by colonists coming from [Phocaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phocaea) (now [Foça](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fo%C3%A7a), on the [Aegean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegean_Sea) coast of [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_Minor)). A second wave of colonists arrived in about 540 BC, when Phocaea was destroyed by the [Persians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_Empire).[^cite_note-R._Palanque,_pg._41-16]

Massalia became one of the major trading ports of the ancient world. At its height, in the 4th century BC, it had a population of about 6,000 inhabitants, living on about fifty hectares surrounded by a wall. It was governed as an aristocratic republic, by an assembly of the 600 wealthiest citizens. It had a large temple of the cult of [Apollo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo) of Delphi on a hilltop overlooking the port, and a temple of the cult of [Artemis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis) of Ephesus at the other end of the city. The Drachma coins minted in Massalia were found in all parts of Ligurian-Celtic Gaul. Traders from Massalia ventured inland deep into France on the Rivers [Durance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durance) and Rhône, and established overland trade routes deep into Gaul, and to Switzerland and Burgundy, and as far north as the Baltic Sea. They exported their own products; local wine, salted pork and fish, aromatic and medicinal plants, coral and cork.[^cite_note-R._Palanque,_pg._41-16]

The Massalians also established a series of small colonies and trading posts along the coast; which later became towns; they founded *Citharista* ([La Ciotat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Ciotat)); *Tauroeis* (Le Brusc); *Olbia* (near [Hyères](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hy%C3%A8res)); *Pergantion* (Breganson); *Caccabaria* ([Cavalaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavalaire)); *Athenopolis* ([Saint-Tropez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Tropez)); *Antipolis* ([Antibes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibes)); *Nikaia* ([Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nice)), and *Monoicos* ([Monaco](19261.md)). They established inland towns at *Glanum* ([Saint-Rémy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-R%C3%A9my-de-Provence)) and *Mastrabala* ([Saint-Blaise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Blaise,_Alpes-Maritimes)).

The most famous citizen of Massalia was the mathematician, astronomer and navigator [Pytheas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pytheas). Pytheas made mathematical instruments which allowed him to establish almost exactly the latitude of Marseille, and he was the first scientist to observe that the tides were connected with the phases of the moon. Between 330 and 320 BC he organised an expedition by ship into the Atlantic and as far north as England, and to visit [Iceland](14531.md), Shetland, and Norway. He was the first scientist to describe drift ice and the midnight sun. Though he hoped to establish a sea trading route for tin from [Cornwall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornwall), his trip was not a commercial success, and it was not repeated. The Massalians found it cheaper and simpler to trade with Northern Europe over land routes.[^cite_note-17]

### Roman Provence (2nd century BC to 5th century AD)



[Triumphal Arch of Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triumphal_Arch_of_Orange), first century AD

In the 2nd century BC the people of Massalia appealed to Rome for help against the Ligures. Roman legions entered Provence three times; first in 181 BC the Romans suppressed Ligurian uprisings near Genoa; in 154 BC the Roman Consul Optimus defeated the [Oxybii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxybii) and the [Deciates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deciates), who were attacking Antibes; and in 125 BC, the Romans put down an uprising of a confederation of Celtic tribes.[^cite_note-18] After this battle, the Romans decided to establish permanent settlements in Provence. In 122 BC, next to the Celtic town of Entremont, the Romans built a new town, *Aquae Sextiae*, later called [Aix-en-Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aix-en-Provence). In 118 BC they founded *Narbo* ([Narbonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narbonne)).

The Roman general [Gaius Marius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaius_Marius) crushed the last serious resistance in 102 BC by defeating the [Cimbri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cimbri) and the [Teutons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teutons). He then began building roads to facilitate troop movements and commerce between Rome, Spain and Northern Europe; one from the coast inland to [Apt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apt,_Vaucluse) and [Tarascon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarascon), and the other along the coast from Italy to Spain, passing through [Fréjus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fr%C3%A9jus) and Aix-en-Provence.



The [Roman arena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arles_Amphitheatre) at [Arles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arles) (2nd century AD)

In 49 BC, Massalia had the misfortune to choose the wrong side in the power struggle between [Pompey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompey) and [Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar). Pompey was defeated, and Massalia lost its territories and political influence. Roman veterans, in the meantime, populated two new towns, Arles and Fréjus, at the sites of older Greek settlements.

In 8 BC the Emperor [Augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus) built a triumphal monument at [La Turbie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Turbie) to commemorate the pacification of the region, and he began to Romanize Provence politically and culturally. Roman engineers and architects built monuments, theatres, baths, villas, fora, arenas and [aqueducts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_aqueduct), many of which still exist. (See [Architecture of Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture_of_Provence).) Roman towns were built at [Cavaillon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavaillon); [Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange,_Vaucluse); [Arles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arles); [Fréjus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fr%C3%A9jus); [Glanum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glanum) (outside [Saint-Rémy-de-Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-R%C3%A9my-de-Provence)); [Carpentras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpentras); [Vaison-la-Romaine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaison-la-Romaine); [Nîmes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N%C3%AEmes); [Vernègues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vern%C3%A8gues); [Saint-Chamas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Chamas) and [Cimiez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cimiez) (above Nice). The Roman province, which was called [Gallia Narbonensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallia_Narbonensis), for its capital, Narbo (modern Narbonne), extended from Italy to Spain, from the [Alps](981.md) to the [Pyrenees](24707.md).

The [Pax Romana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pax_Romana) in Provence lasted until the middle of the 3rd century. [Germanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_peoples) tribes invaded Provence in 257 and 275. At the beginning the 4th century, the court of Roman Emperor [Constantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_the_Great) (ca. 272–337) was forced to take refuge in Arles. By the end of the 5th century, Roman power in Provence had vanished, and an age of invasions, wars, and chaos began.

### Arrival of Christianity (3rd–6th centuries)



The [baptistery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptistery) of [Fréjus Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fr%C3%A9jus_Cathedral) (5th century) is still in use

There are many legends about the earliest Christians in Provence, but they are difficult to verify. It is documented that there were organised churches and bishops in the Roman towns of Provence as early as the 3rd and 4th centuries; in [Arles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arles) in 254; Marseille in 314; [Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange,_Vaucluse), [Vaison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaison) and [Apt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apt,_Vaucluse) in 314; [Cavaillon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavaillon), [Digne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digne), [Embrun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embrun,_Hautes-Alpes), [Gap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gap,_Hautes-Alpes), and [Fréjus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fr%C3%A9jus) at the end of the 4th century; [Aix-en-Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aix-en-Provence) in 408; [Carpentras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpentras), [Avignon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avignon), [Riez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riez), [Cimiez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cimiez) (today part of [Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nice)) and [Vence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vence) in 439; [Antibes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibes) in 442; [Toulon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toulon) in 451; [Senez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senez) in 406, [Saint-Paul-Trois-Châteaux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Paul-Trois-Ch%C3%A2teaux) in 517; and [Glandèves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gland%C3%A8ves) in 541.[^cite_note-19]

The oldest Christian structure still surviving in Provence is the baptistery of the [Fréjus Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fr%C3%A9jus_Cathedral), dating from the 5th century. At about the same time, the first two monasteries in Provence were founded: [Lérins Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%A9rins_Abbey), on the island of [Saint-Honorat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%8Ele_Saint-Honorat) near Cannes, and [Abbey of St Victor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbey_of_St_Victor,_Marseille) in Marseille.

### Germanic invasions, Merovingians and Carolingians (5th–9th centuries)



[King Boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boso_of_Provence) and [San Stephen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_protomartyrs) (fragment of fresco at [Charlieu Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlieu_Abbey))

Beginning in the second half of the 5th century, as Roman power waned, successive waves of Germanic tribes entered Provence: first the [Visigoths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visigoths) (480), then the [Ostrogoths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ostrogoths), then the [Burgundians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burgundians), finally, the [Franks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franks) in the 6th century. [Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab) invaders and [Berber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berber_people) pirates came from North Africa to the Coast of Provence in the early 7th century.

During the late 7th and the early 8th centuries, Provence was formally subject to the Frankish kings of the [Merovingian dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merovingian_dynasty), but it was in fact ruled by its own regional nobility of Gallo-Roman stock, which ruled themselves according to Roman, not Frankish, law. The region actually enjoyed more prestige than the northern Franks had, but the local aristocracy feared [Charles Martel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Martel)'s expansionist ambitions.[^cite_note-rogercollins-20] In 737, Charles Martel headed down the Rhône Valley after subduing Burgundy. He attacked [Avignon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Avignon) and [Arles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arles), garrisoned by the [Umayyads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_invasion_of_Gaul), and came back in 739 to capture for a second time Avignon and chase the duke [Maurontus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurontus) to his stronghold of Marseille.[^cite_note-rogercollins-20] The city was brought to heel and the duke had to flee to an island. The region was thereafter under the rule of [Carolingian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carolingian) kings, descended from Charles Martel, and then was part of the empire of [Charlemagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlemagne) (742–814).

In 879, after the death of the Carolingian ruler [Charles the Bald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_the_Bald), [Boso of Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boso_of_Provence) (also known as Boson), his brother-in-law, broke away from the Carolingian kingdom of [Louis III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_the_Younger) and was elected the first ruler of an independent state of Provence.

### Fraxinet and the Counts of Provence (9th–13th centuries)



[Ramon Berenguer III, Count of Barcelona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramon_Berenguer_III,_Count_of_Barcelona), who through marriage became Raymond Berenguer I, Count of Provence, depicted at the Castle of Fos. Painting by [Marià Fortuny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mari%C3%A0_Fortuny) ([Reial Acadèmia Catalana de Belles Arts de Sant Jordi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reial_Acad%C3%A8mia_Catalana_de_Belles_Arts_de_Sant_Jordi), on deposit at the [Generalitat of Catalonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalitat_of_Catalonia), Barcelona)

During the Middle Ages, Provence was ruled by three successive dynasties of counts and became a focal point in the rivalries between the [Crown of Aragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_of_Aragon), the [kings of Burgundy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kings_of_Burgundy), the rulers of the [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire), and the [Angevin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Valois-Anjou) kings of France.&#x20;



Coat of arms of the [Crown of Aragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_of_Aragon), used by the [House of Aragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Aragon) when ruling Provence (late 12th century–1246)



Coat of arms of the counts of Provence of the [Angevin dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Valois-Anjou), rulers of Provence from 1246 until its incorporation into France in 1486

The [Bosonids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosonids) (879–1112), descendants of Boson, the first king of Provence, held the county in the early medieval period. His son, [Louis the Blind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_the_Blind) (r. 890–928), lost his sight during his attempt to claim the Italian throne. He was succeeded in influence by his cousin [Hugh of Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_of_Italy) (d. 947), who became duke of Provence and [count of Vienne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Count_of_Vienne). Hugh transferred the capital from Vienne to [Arles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arles) and recognised the overlordship of [Rudolph II of Burgundy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolph_II_of_Burgundy), making Provence a [fief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fief) of Burgundy.

In the 9th and 10th centuries, Provence was subject to invasions by [Normans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normans) and [Saracens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saracens) (a term used in medieval sources for Muslim raiders). While the Normans withdrew, the Saracens established a permanent base at [Fraxinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraxinet) in 887. In 973 they captured [Maieul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maieul), [abbot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbot) of [Cluny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cluny_Abbey), prompting a coordinated response by local forces. Under [Count William I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_I_of_Provence), the Provençals defeated the Saracens at the [Battle of Tourtour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Tourtour), near [La Garde-Freinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Garde-Freinet). Surviving Saracens were either killed, enslaved, or left the region.

Dynastic conflicts persisted. A dispute between [Rudolph III of Burgundy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolph_III_of_Burgundy) and the German emperor [Conrad II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conrad_II) resulted in Provence becoming a [fiefdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiefdom) of the [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire) in 1032, a status it retained until 1246.

In 1112, [Douce I, Countess of Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douce_I,_Countess_of_Provence), the last Bosonid heiress, married [Ramon Berenguer III, Count of Barcelona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramon_Berenguer_III,_Count_of_Barcelona), initiating the rule of the Barcelona dynasty in Provence. From 1112 to 1131 he governed as Count of Provence. Following the dynastic union of the [County of Barcelona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_of_Barcelona) and the [Kingdom of Aragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Aragon) (1137), later known as the [Crown of Aragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_of_Aragon), Provençal succession passed to the [House of Aragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Aragon), whose members ruled until 1246. The traditional coat of arms of Provence—four red vertical bars on a gold field—originates from this period of Aragonese rule.[^cite_note-21]

In 1125, Provence was divided: territories north and west of the [Durance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durance) passed to the [Count of Toulouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Count_of_Toulouse), while the lands between the Durance, the [Mediterranean Sea](19006.md), the [Rhône](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rh%C3%B4ne), and the Alps remained under the counts of Provence. The capital shifted from [Arles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arles) to [Aix-en-Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aix-en-Provence) and later to [Brignoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brignoles).[^cite_note-22]

Under the [House of Aragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Aragon), the 12th century saw significant ecclesiastical construction in Provence in the [Romanesque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanesque_architecture) style, combining elements of the [Gallo-Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallo-Roman) tradition of the Rhône valley with the [Lombard](43807.md) style of the Alps. [Aix Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aix_Cathedral) was built on the site of the Roman forum and later partially rebuilt in [Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_architecture) style. The [Church of St. Trophime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_St._Trophime) in Arles became a major Romanesque monument, while [Montmajour Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montmajour_Abbey) developed into an important pilgrimage site.

The 12th century also witnessed the foundation of major [Cistercian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cistercian) monasteries in remote areas of Provence, including [Sénanque Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%A9nanque_Abbey) (1148–1178) in the [Luberon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luberon), [Thoronet Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thoronet_Abbey) near [Draguignan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Draguignan) (1160), and [Silvacane Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silvacane_Abbey) at [La Roque-d'Anthéron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Roque-d'Anth%C3%A9ron) (1175).

In the 13th century, the French Capetian monarchy extended its influence into southern France through dynastic marriages. [Alphonse, Count of Poitou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphonse,_Count_of_Poitou), son of King [Louis VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_VIII_of_France), married [Joan, Countess of Toulouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan,_Countess_of_Toulouse). King [Louis IX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_IX_of_France) married [Marguerite of Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marguerite_of_Provence). In 1246, [Charles of Anjou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_I_of_Naples), the youngest son of Louis VIII, married [Beatrice of Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_of_Provence), heiress to the county. Provence subsequently became linked to the [Angevin dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capetian_House_of_Anjou) and the Kingdom of Naples.[^cite_note-23]

### The popes in Avignon (14th century)

Main article: [Avignon papacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avignon_papacy)



The façade of the [Palais des Papes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palais_des_Papes)

In 1309, [Pope Clement V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Clement_V), who was originally from Bordeaux, moved the [Papal Curia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papal_Curia) to Avignon, a period known as the [Avignon Papacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avignon_Papacy).[^cite_note-24] From 1309 until 1377, seven popes reigned in Avignon before the [Schism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Schism) between the Roman and Avignon churches, which led to the creation of rival popes in both places. After that, three [antipopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antipope) reigned in Avignon until 1423, when the papacy finally returned to Rome. Between 1334 and 1363 the old and new Papal Palaces of Avignon were built by Popes [Benedict XII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedict_XII) and [Clement VI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clement_VI) respectively; together the [Palais des Papes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palais_des_Papes) was the largest Gothic palace in Europe.[^cite_note-25]

The 14th century was a terrible time in Provence, and all of Europe: the population of Provence had been about 400,000 people; the [Black Death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death) (1348–1350) killed fifteen thousand people in Arles, half the population of the city, and greatly reduced the population of the whole region. The defeat of the French Army during the [Hundred Years' War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hundred_Years'_War) forced the cities of Provence to build walls and towers to defend themselves against armies of former soldiers who ravaged the countryside.

The Angevin rulers of Provence also had a difficult time. An assembly of nobles, religious leaders, and town leaders of Provence was organised to resist the authority of Queen [Joan I of Naples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_I_of_Naples) (1343–1382). She was murdered in 1382 by her cousin and heir, [Charles of Durazzo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_III_of_Naples), who started a new war, leading to the separation of [Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nice), [Puget-Théniers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puget-Th%C3%A9niers) and [Barcelonnette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barcelonnette) from Provence in 1388, and their attachment to the [County of Savoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_of_Savoy). From 1388 up to 1526, the area acquired by the Savoy was known as *Terres Neuves de Provence*; after 1526 it officially took on the name [County of Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_of_Nice).

### Good King René, the last ruler of Provence

The 15th century saw a series of wars between the kings of [Aragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_of_Aragon) and the counts of Provence. In 1423 the army of [Alphonse of Aragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfonso_V_of_Aragon) captured Marseille, and in 1443 they captured Naples, and forced its ruler, King [René I of Naples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ren%C3%A9_I_of_Naples), to flee. He eventually settled in one of his remaining territories, Provence.

History and legend has given René the title "Good King René of Provence", though he only lived in Provence in the last ten years of his life, from 1470 to 1480, and his political policies of territorial expansion were costly and unsuccessful. Provence benefitted from population growth and economic expansion, and René was a generous patron of the arts, sponsoring painters [Nicolas Froment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Froment), [Louis Bréa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Br%C3%A9a), and other masters. He also completed one of the finest castles in Provence at [Tarascon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarascon_castle), on the Rhône river.

When René died in 1480, his title passed to his nephew [Charles du Maine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_IV_of_Anjou). One year later, in 1481, when Charles died, the title passed to King [Louis XI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_XI). Provence was legally incorporated into the French royal domain in 1486.

### 1486 to 1789

Soon after Provence became part of France, it became involved in the [Wars of Religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Wars_of_Religion) that swept the country in the 16th century. Between 1493 and 1501, many Jews were expelled from their homes and sought sanctuary in the region of Avignon, which was still under the direct rule of the Pope. In 1545, the [Parlement of Aix-en-Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parlement_of_Aix-en-Provence) ordered the destruction of the villages of Lourmarin, Mérindol, Cabriéres in the Luberon, because their inhabitants were [Vaudois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waldensians), of Italian [Piedmontese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piedmontese) origin, and were not considered sufficiently orthodox Catholics. Most of Provence remained strongly Catholic, though Protestants controlled the [Principality of Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principality_of_Orange), an enclave ruled by William of the [House of Orange-Nassau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Orange-Nassau) of the Netherlands, who inherited it in 1544 and which was not incorporated into France until 1673. An army of the [Catholic League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_League_\(French\)) laid siege to the Protestant town of Mėnerbes in the [Vaucluse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaucluse) between 1573 and 1578. The wars did not stop until the end of the 16th century, with the consolidation of power in Provence by the [Bourbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Bourbon) kings.



View of Toulon Harbour around 1750, by [Joseph Vernet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Vernet)

The semi-independent Parliament of Provence in Aix and some of the cities of Provence, particularly Marseille, continued to rebel against the authority of the Bourbon king. After uprisings in 1630–31 and 1648–1652, the young King [Louis XIV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_XIV) had two large forts, fort St. Jean and Fort St. Nicholas, built at the harbour entrance to control the city's unruly population.

At the beginning of the 17th century, [Cardinal Richelieu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_Richelieu) began to build a naval arsenal and dockyard at Toulon to serve as a base for a new French Mediterranean fleet. The base was greatly enlarged by [Jean-Baptiste Colbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Colbert), the minister of Louis XIV, who also commissioned his chief military engineer [Vauban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vauban) to strengthen the fortifications around the city.

At the beginning of the 17th century, Provence had a population of about 450,000 people.[^cite_note-Bastié,_pg._35-26] It was predominantly rural, devoted to raising wheat, wine, and olives, with small industries for tanning, pottery, perfume-making, and ship and boat building. [Provençal quilts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proven%C3%A7al_quilts), made from the mid-17th century onwards, were successfully exported to England, Spain, Italy, Germany and the Netherlands.[^cite_note-isa-27] There was considerable commerce along the coast, and up and down the Rhône river. The cities: Marseille, [Toulon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toulon), Avignon and Aix-en-Provence, saw the construction of boulevards and richly decorated private houses.



Marseille in 1754, by Vernet

At the beginning of the 18th century, Provence suffered from the economic malaise of the end of the reign of Louis XIV. The [Great Plague of Marseille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Plague_of_Marseille) struck the region between 1720 and 1722, beginning in Marseille, killing some 40,000 people. Still, by the end of the century, many artisanal industries began to flourish; making [perfumes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfumes) in [Grasse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grasse); olive oil in Aix and the [Alpilles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpilles); textiles in Orange, Avignon and Tarascon; and [faience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faience) pottery in Marseille, Apt, [Aubagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aubagne), and [Moustiers-Sainte-Marie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moustiers-Sainte-Marie). Many immigrants arrived from Liguria and the Piedmont in Italy. By the end of the 18th century, Marseille had a population of 120,000 people, making it the third largest city in France.[^cite_note-Bastié,_pg._35-26]

### During the French Revolution

Main article: [French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution)

Most of Provence, with the exception of Marseille, Aix and Avignon, was rural, conservative and largely royalist, and the Revolution was as violent and bloody in Provence as it was in other parts of France. On 30 April 1790, [Fort Saint-Nicolas in Marseille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Saint-Nicolas_in_Marseille) was besieged, and many of the soldiers inside were massacred. On 17 October 1791, a massacre of royalists and religious figures took place in the ice storage rooms (*glacières*) of the prison of the [Palace of the Popes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palais_des_Papes) in Avignon.



[La Marseillaise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Marseillaise), 1792

When the radical [Montagnards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montagnard_\(French_Revolution\)) seized power from the [Girondins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girondins) in May 1793, a counter-revolution broke out in Avignon, Marseille and Toulon. A revolutionary army under [General Carteaux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Fran%C3%A7ois_Carteaux) recaptured Marseille in August 1793 and [renamed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_renaming) it "City without a Name" (*[Ville sans Nom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ville-sans-nom)*.) In Toulon, the opponents of the Revolution handed the city to a British and Spanish fleet on 28 August 1793. A Revolutionary Army laid siege to the British positions for four months (see the [Siege of Toulon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Toulon_\(1793\))) and finally, the enterprise of the young commander of artillery, [Napoleon Bonaparte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon), defeated the British and drove them out in December 1793. About 15,000 royalists escaped with the British fleet, but five to eight hundred of the 7,000 who remained were shot on the [Champ de Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Champ_de_Mars_\(Toulon\)?action=edit\&redlink=1), and Toulon was renamed *Port la Montagne*.

The fall of the Montagnards in July 1794 was followed by a new [White Terror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_White_Terror) aimed at the revolutionaries. Calm was not restored until the rise of the [Directory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Directory) to power in 1795.

Provence produced some memorable figures in the French Revolution; both moderates such as the [comte de Mirabeau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comte_de_Mirabeau) and figures of the far left such as the [Marquis de Sade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marquis_de_Sade); there was also the military figure [Charles Barbaroux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Barbaroux) and the theorist [Emmanuel-Joseph Sieyès](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmanuel-Joseph_Siey%C3%A8s) (1748–1836), who instigated the coup of [18 Brumaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/18_Brumaire) which brought [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon) to power. The revolutionary anthem *[La Marseillaise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Marseillaise)* despite its origins on the [Rhine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Army_of_the_Rhine) got its name because revolutionary volunteers from Marseille sang it on the streets of Paris.

### Under Napoleon

[Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon) restored the belongings and power of the families of the [Ancien Régime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancien_R%C3%A9gime) in Provence. The British fleet of Admiral [Horatio Nelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horatio_Nelson) blockaded Toulon, and almost all maritime commerce was stopped, causing hardship and poverty. When Napoleon was defeated, his fall was celebrated in Provence. When he escaped from [Elba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elba) on 1 March 1815, and landed at [Golfe-Juan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golfe-Juan), he detoured to avoid the cities of Provence, which were hostile to him, and therefore directed his small force directly to the northeast of it.[^cite_note-28]

### 19th century



Marseille in 1825

Provence enjoyed prosperity in the 19th century; the ports of Marseille and Toulon connected Provence with the expanding [French Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_colonial_empire) in North Africa and the Orient, especially after the opening of the [Suez Canal](29323.md) in 1869.

In April–July 1859, [Napoleon III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon_III) made a secret agreement with [Cavour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Count_Camillo_Benso_di_Cavour), Prime Minister of [Piedmont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piedmont), for France to assist in expelling Austria from the [Italian Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Peninsula) and bringing about a united Italy, in exchange for Piedmont ceding [Savoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savoy) and the [Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nice) region to France. He went to war with Austria in 1859 and won a victory at [Solferino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solferino), which resulted in Austria ceding [Lombardy](43807.md) to France. France immediately ceded Lombardy to [Piedmont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piedmont), and, in return, Napoleon received Savoy and Nice in 1860, and [Roquebrune-Cap-Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roquebrune-Cap-Martin) and [Menton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menton) in 1861.

The railroad connected Paris with Marseille (1848) and then with Toulon and Nice (1864). Nice, [Antibes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibes) and [Hyères](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hy%C3%A8res) became popular winter resorts for European royalty, including [Queen Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Victoria). Under Napoleon III, Marseille grew to a population of 250,000, including a very large Italian community. Toulon had a population of 80,000. The large cities like Marseille and Toulon saw the building of churches, opera houses, grand boulevards, and parks.

After the fall of Louis Napoleon following the defeat in the [Franco-Prussian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franco-Prussian_War), barricades went up in the streets of Marseille (23 March 1871) and the [Communards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communards), led by Gaston Cremieux and following the lead of the [Paris Commune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Commune), took control of the city. The Commune was crushed by the army and Cremieux was executed on 30 November 1871. Though Provence was generally conservative, it often elected reformist leaders; Prime Minister [Léon Gambetta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%A9on_Gambetta) was the son of a Marseille grocer, and future prime minister [Georges Clemenceau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Clemenceau) was elected deputy of Var in 1885.

The second half of the 19th century saw a revival of the [Provençal language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occitan_language) and culture, particularly traditional rural values. driven by a movement of writers and poets called the [Félibrige](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F%C3%A9librige), led by poet [Frédéric Mistral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fr%C3%A9d%C3%A9ric_Mistral). Mistral achieved literary success with his poem [Mirèio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mir%C3%A8io) (*Mireille* in French); he was awarded the Nobel Prize for literature in 1904.

### 20th century

Between World War I and World War II, Provence was bitterly divided between the more conservative rural areas and the more radical big cities. There were widespread strikes in Marseille in 1919, and riots in Toulon in 1935.

After the defeat of France by Germany in June 1940, France was divided into an occupied zone and unoccupied zone, with Provence in the unoccupied zone. Parts of eastern Provence were occupied by Italian soldiers. Collaboration and passive resistance gradually gave way to more active resistance, particularly after Nazi Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941 and the Communist Party became active in the resistance. [Jean Moulin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Moulin), the deputy of [Charles de Gaulle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_de_Gaulle), the leader of the Free France resistance movement, was parachuted into [Eygalières](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eygali%C3%A8res), in the [Bouches-du-Rhône](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bouches-du-Rh%C3%B4ne) on 2 January 1942 to unite the diverse resistance movements in all of France against the Germans.

In November 1942, following Allied landings in North Africa ([Operation Torch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Torch)), the Germans occupied all of Provence ([Operation Attila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Attila)) and then headed for Toulon ([Case Anton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_Anton)). The French fleet at Toulon sabotaged its own ships to keep them from falling into German hands.

The Germans began a systematic rounding-up of French Jews and refugees from Nice and Marseille. Many thousands were taken to concentration camps, and few survived. A large quarter around the port of Marseille was emptied of inhabitants and dynamited, so it would not serve as a base for the resistance. Nonetheless, the resistance grew stronger; the leader of the pro-German militia, the Milice, in Marseille was assassinated in April 1943.

On 15 August 1944, two months after the Allied landings in Normandy ([Operation Overlord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Overlord)), the [Seventh United States Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seventh_United_States_Army) under General [Alexander Patch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Patch), with a [Free French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_French) corps under General [Jean de Lattre de Tassigny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_de_Lattre_de_Tassigny), landed on the coast of Var between [St. Raphael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Rapha%C3%ABl,_Var) and [Cavalaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavalaire) ([Operation Dragoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Dragoon)). The American forces moved north toward [Manosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manosque), [Sisteron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sisteron) and [Gap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gap,_Hautes-Alpes), while the French First Armored Division under General Vigier liberated Brignoles, Salon, Arles, and Avignon. The Germans in Toulon resisted until 27 August, and Marseille was not liberated until 25 August.

After the end of the war, Provence faced an enormous task of reconstruction, particularly of the ports and railroads destroyed during the war. As part of this effort, the first modern concrete apartment block, the [Unité d'habitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit%C3%A9_d'habitation) of [Le Corbusier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Corbusier), was built in Marseille in 1947–52. In 1962, Provence absorbed a large number of French citizens who left Algeria after its independence. Since that time, large North African communities settled in and around the big cities, particularly Marseille and Toulon.

In the 1940s, Provence underwent a cultural renewal, with the founding of the [Avignon Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avignon_Festival) of theatre (1947), the reopening of the [Cannes Film Festival](101460.md) (begun in 1939), and many other major events. With the building of new highways, particularly the Paris Marseille autoroute which opened in 1970, Provence became destination for mass tourism from all over Europe. Many Europeans, particularly from Britain, bought summer houses in Provence. The arrival of the [TGV](57212.md) high-speed trains shortened the trip from Paris to Marseille to less than four hours.

In recent years, residents of Provence have struggled to reconcile economic development and population growth with their desire to preserve the region's unique landscape and culture.

## Geography



The Roman Province of [Gallia Narbonensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallia_Narbonensis) around 58 BC

The original Roman province was called Gallia Transalpina, then [Gallia Narbonensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallia_Narbonensis), or simply *Provincia Nostra* ('Our Province') or *Provincia*. It extended from the [Alps](981.md) to the [Pyrenees](24707.md) and north to the [Vaucluse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaucluse), with its capital in Narbo Martius (present-day [Narbonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narbonne)).

### Location and borders

In the 15th century, the County of Provence was bordered by the river [Var](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Var_\(river\)) on the east and the [Rhône](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rh%C3%B4ne) to the west, with the Mediterranean to the south, and a northern border that roughly followed the [Durance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durance). Bordering it on the northwest were two territories that had earlier formed part of Provence, i.e., the [Comtat Venaissin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comtat_Venaissin), centered on [Avignon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avignon) which was ruled by the pope from the 13th century until the [French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution), and, just north of this, the Principality of Orange, that through inheritance became a possession of [William the Silent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_the_Silent), who became the founder of the present ruling house of the Netherlands, the House of Orange-Nassau. The principality was independent between the 12th century and 1673 when it was occupied and then annexed by [Louis XIV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_XIV) during one of his wars with its prince, Dutch stadtholder William III, the future king of England. At the end of the 14th century, another piece of Provence along the Italian border, including [Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nice) and the lower Alps, was detached from Provence and attached to the lands of the duke of [Savoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savoy). The lower Alps were re-attached to France after the [Treaty of Utrecht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Utrecht) in 1713, but Nice did not return to France until 1860, during the reign of [Napoleon III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon_III).[^cite_note-29]

The administrative region of [Provence-Alpes-Cote d'Azur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provence-Alpes-Cote_d'Azur) was created in 1982. It included Provence, plus the territory of the Comtat Venaissin around Avignon, and the Principality of Orange, the eastern portion of the [Dauphiné](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dauphin%C3%A9), and the former [County of Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_of_Nice).

The area immediately to the north, in the Drome, is sometimes referred to as the [fr\:Drôme Provençale](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dr%C3%B4me%20proven%C3%A7ale), given its very similar features and landscapes.

### Rivers



The [Rhône](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rh%C3%B4ne) at Avignon

The Rhône river, on the western border of Provence, is one of the major rivers of France, and has been a highway of commerce and communications between inland France and the Mediterranean for centuries. It rises as the effluent of the [Rhône Glacier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rh%C3%B4ne_Glacier) in [Valais](198789.md), Switzerland, in the [Saint-Gotthard massif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Gotthard_massif), at an altitude of 1753 m. It is joined by the river [Saône](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sa%C3%B4ne) at Lyon. Along the Rhône Valley, it is joined on the right bank by the rivers [Eyrieux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eyrieux), Ardèche, [Cèze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C%C3%A8ze) and [Gardon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gardon) (or Gard), on the left Alps bank by rivers [Isère](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Is%C3%A8re), Drôme, [Ouvèze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouv%C3%A8ze) and Durance. At Arles, the Rhône divides itself in two arms, forming the [Camargue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camargue) delta, with all branches flowing into the Mediterranean Sea. One arm is called the "Grand Rhône"; the other one is the "Petit Rhône".



The [Gorge du Verdon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verdon_Gorge)

The Durance river, a tributary of the Rhône, has its source in the Alps near [Briançon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian%C3%A7on). It flows south-west through [Embrun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embrun,_Hautes-Alpes), Sisteron, Manosque, Cavaillon, and Avignon, where it meets the Rhône.

The [Verdon River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verdon_River) is a tributary of the Durance, rising at an altitude of 2,400 metres in the southwestern Alps near Barcelonette, and flowing southwest for 175 kilometres through the departments of Alpes-de-Haute-Provence and Var before it reaches the Durance at near [Vinon-sur-Verdon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vinon-sur-Verdon), south of Manosque. The Verdon is best known for its canyon, the [Verdon Gorge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verdon_Gorge). This limestone canyon, also called the 'Grand Canyon of Verdon', 20 kilometres in length and more than 300 metres deep, is a popular climbing and sight-seeing area.

The [Var River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Var_River) rises near the Col de la Cayolle (2,326 m; 7,631 ft) in the [Maritime Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maritime_Alps) and flows generally southeast for 120 kilometres (75 mi) into the Mediterranean between Nice and [Saint-Laurent-du-Var](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Laurent-du-Var). Before Nice was returned to France in 1860, the Var marked the eastern border of France along the Mediterranean. The Var is the unique case in France of a river giving a name to a department, but not flowing through that department (due to subsequent adjustments to the department's boundaries).

### Camargue

With an area of over 930 km<sup>2</sup> (360 sq mi), [Camargue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camargue) is western Europe's largest river delta (technically an island, as it is wholly surrounded by water). It is a vast plain comprising large [brine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brine) lagoons or *étangs*, cut off from the sea by sandbars and encircled by [reed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phragmites)-covered [marshes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsh) which are in turn surrounded by a large cultivated area. It is home to more than 400 species of birds, the brine ponds providing one of the few European habitats for the [greater flamingo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_flamingo).

### Mountains



[Alpilles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpilles) landscape near Le Destet

By considering the [Maritime Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maritime_Alps), along the border with Italy, as a part of the cultural Provence, they constitute the highest elevations of the region (the [Punta dell'Argentera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punta_dell'Argentera) has an elevation of 3,297 m). They form the border between the French *[département](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A9partement)* [Alpes-Maritimes](80989.md) and the Italian [province of Cuneo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Cuneo). [Mercantour National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercantour_National_Park) is located in the Maritime Alps. On the other hand, if the *département* [Hautes Alpes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hautes_Alpes) is also considered as part of the modern Provence, then the alpin [Écrins mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massif_des_%C3%89crins) represent the highest elevations of the region with the [Barre des Écrins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barre_des_%C3%89crins) culminating at 4102m.



View of Mont Ventoux from [Mirabel-aux-Baronnies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirabel-aux-Baronnies)



Vallon de Mollières, [Mercantour National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercantour_National_Park)

Outside of the Maritime Alps, [Mont Ventoux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mont_Ventoux) (Occitan: Ventor in classical norm or Ventour in Mistralian norm), at 1,909 metres (6,263 ft), is the highest peak in Provence. It is located some 20 km north-east of Carpentras, Vaucluse. On the north side, the mountain borders the Drôme département. It is nicknamed the "Giant of Provence", or "The Bald Mountain". Although geologically part of the [Alps](981.md), is often considered to be separate from them, due to the lack of mountains of a similar height nearby. It stands alone to the west of the [Luberon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luberon) range, and just to the east of the [Dentelles de Montmirail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dentelles_de_Montmirail), its foothills. The top of the mountain is bare limestone without vegetation or trees. The white limestone on the mountain's barren peak means it appears from a distance to be snow-capped all year round (its snow cover actually lasts from December to April).

The [Alpilles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpilles) are a chain of small mountains located about 20 kilometres (12 mi) south of Avignon. Although they are not particularly high – only some 387 metres (1,270 ft) at their highest point – the Alpilles stand out since they rise abruptly from the plain of the [Rhône](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rh%C3%B4ne) valley. The range is about 25 km long by about 8 to 10 km wide, running in an east–west direction between the Rhône and Durance rivers. The landscape of the Alpilles is one of arid [limestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limestone) peaks separated by dry valleys.



*[Mont Sainte-Victoire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mont_Sainte-Victoire_\(C%C3%A9zanne\))*, painted by Paul Cézanne

[Montagne Sainte-Victoire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montagne_Sainte-Victoire) is probably the best-known mountain in Provence, thanks to the painter Paul Cézanne, who could see it from his home, and painted it frequently. It is a [limestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limestone) mountain ridge which extends over 18 kilometres between the [départements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A9partements) of [Bouches-du-Rhône](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bouches-du-Rh%C3%B4ne) and [Var](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Var_\(d%C3%A9partement\)). Its highest point is the *Pic des mouches* at 1,011 m.

The [Massif des Maures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massif_des_Maures) (Moor Mountains) is a small chain of mountains that lies along the coast of the Mediterranean in the department of Var, between [Hyères](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hy%C3%A8res) et [Fréjus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fr%C3%A9jus). Its highest point is the signal de la Sauvette, 780 metres high. The name is a souvenir of the [Moors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moors) (*Maures* in Old French), [Arabs](2185.md) and [Berbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berbers) from North Africa, who settled on the coast of Provence in the 9th and 10th centuries. The Massif des Maures extends about sixty kilometres along the coast, and reaches inland about thirty kilometres. On the north it is bordered by a depression which is followed by the *[routes nationales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Route_nationale)* 97 and 7, and the railroad line between Toulon and Nice. On the south it ends abruptly at the Mediterranean, forming a broken and abrupt coastline.

The peninsula of [Saint-Tropez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Tropez) is part of the Massif des Maures, along with the [Giens Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giens_Peninsula) and the islands offshore of [Hyères](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%8Eles_d'Hy%C3%A8res); [Porquerolles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porquerolles), Port-Cros, and [île du Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%8Ele_du_Levant). Cape Sicié, west of Toulon, as well as the Massif de Tanneron, belong geologically to the Massif des Maures.

### The Calanques



Calanque En-Vau

The [Calanques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calanques) form a dramatic feature of the Provence coast, a 20-km long series of narrow inlets in the cliffs of the coastline between Marseille on the west and [Cassis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassis) on the east. The best known examples of this formation can be found in the [Massif des Calanques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massif_des_Calanques). The highest peak in the massif is [Mont Puget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mont_Puget), 565 metres high.

The best known calanques of the Massif des Calanques include the [Calanque de Sormiou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calanque_de_Sormiou), the [Calanque de Morgiou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calanque_de_Morgiou), the Calanque d'En-Vau, the Calanque de Port-Pin and the [Calanque de Sugiton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calanque_de_Sugiton).

The calanques are remains of ancient river mouths formed mostly during [Tertiary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertiary_\(period\)). Later, during [Quaternary glaciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternary_glaciation), as glaciers swept by, they further deepened those valleys which would eventually (at the end of the last glaciation) be invaded by the sea and become calanques.

The [Cosquer cave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosquer_cave) is an underwater grotto in the Calanque de Morgiou, 37 metres (121 ft) underwater, that was inhabited during [Paleolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleolithic) era, when the sea level was much lower than today. Its walls are covered with paintings and engravings dating back to between 27,000 and 19,000 BC, depicting animals such as bison, ibex, and horses, as well as sea mammals such as seals, and at least one bird, the auk.

### Landscapes



The [Garrigue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garrigue), typical landscape of Provence

The [garrigue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garrigue) is the typical landscape of Provence; it is a type of low, soft-leaved [scrubland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scrubland) or [chaparral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaparral) found on limestone soils around the [Mediterranean Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_Basin), generally near the seacoast, where the climate is moderate, but where there are annual summer drought conditions.[^cite_note-30] [Juniper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juniper) and stunted [holm oaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quercus_ilex) are the typical trees; aromatic lime-tolerant shrubs such as [lavender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lavender), [sage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvia_officinalis), [rosemary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosemary), [wild thyme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wild_thyme) and *[Artemisia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemisia_\(plant\))* are common garrigue plants. The open landscape of the garrigue is punctuated by dense thickets of [Kermes oak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kermes_oak).

## Climate



Mistral blowing near Marseille. In the center is the [Château d'If](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C3%A2teau_d'If)

Most of Provence has a [Mediterranean climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_climate), characterised by hot, dry summers, mild winters, little snow, and abundant sunshine. Within Provence there are micro-climates and local variations, ranging from the Alpine climate inland from Nice to the continental climate in the northern [Vaucluse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaucluse). The [winds of Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winds_of_Provence) are an important feature of the climate, particularly the [mistral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mistral_\(wind\)), a cold, dry wind which, especially in the winter, blows down the Rhône Valley to the [Bouches-du-Rhône](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bouches-du-Rh%C3%B4ne) and the [Var](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Var_\(department\)) Departments, and often reaches over one hundred kilometres an hour.

### Bouches-du-Rhône

Marseille, in the [Bouches-du-Rhône](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bouches-du-Rh%C3%B4ne), has an average of 59 days of rain a year, though when it does rain the rain is often torrential; the average annual rainfall is 544.4 mm. It snows an average of 2.3 days a year, and the snow rarely remains long. Marseille has an average of 2835.5 hours of sunshine a year. The average minimum temperature in January is 2.3 °C., and the average maximum temperature in July is 29.3 °C. The mistral blows an average of one hundred days a year.[^cite_note-infoclimat-31]

### Var

[Toulon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toulon) and Var, which includes [St. Tropez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Tropez) and [Hyères](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hy%C3%A8res), have a climate slightly warmer, dryer and sunnier than Nice and the Alpes-Maritime, but also less sheltered from the wind. Toulon has an average of 2899.3 hours of sunshine a year, making it the sunniest city in metropolitan France,[^cite_note-32] The average maximum daily temperature in August is 29.1 °C., and the average daily minimum temperature in January is 5.8 °C. The average annual rainfall is 665 mm, with the most rain from October to November. Strong winds blow an average of 118 days a year in Toulon, compared with 76 days at Fréjus further east. The strongest Mistral wind recorded in Toulon was 130 kilometres an hour.[^cite_note-33]

### Alpes-Maritimes

[Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nice) and the [Alpes-Maritimes](80989.md) department are sheltered by the Alps, and are the most protected part of the Mediterranean coast. The winds in this department are usually gentle, blowing from the sea to the land, though sometimes the mistral blows strongly from the northwest, or, turned by the mountains, from the east. In 1956, a mistral wind from the northwest reached the speed of 180 kilometres an hour at Nice airport. Sometimes in summer the [sirocco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sirocco) brings high temperatures and reddish desert sand from Africa (see [Winds of Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winds_of_Provence)).

Rainfall is infrequent, 63 days a year, but can be torrential, particularly in September, when storms and rain are caused by the difference between the colder air inland and the warm Mediterranean water temperature (20–24 degrees C.). The average annual rainfall in Nice is 767 mm, more than in Paris, but concentrated in fewer days.

Snow is extremely rare, usually falling once every ten years. 1956 was a very exceptional year, when 20 centimetres of snow blanketed the coast. In January 1985 the coast between Cannes and Menton received 30 to 40 centimetres of snow. In the mountains, the snow is present from November to May.

Nice has an annual average of 2694 hours of sunshine. The average maximum daily temperature in Nice in August is 28 °C., and the average minimum daily temperature in January is 6 °C.[^cite_note-34]

### Alpes-de-Haute-Provence

The department of [Alpes-de-Haute-Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpes-de-Haute-Provence) has a Mediterranean climate in the lower valleys under one thousand metres in altitude and an [alpine climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpine_climate) in the high valleys, such as the valleys of the Blanche, the Haut Verdon and the [Ubaye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ubaye_Valley), which are over 2500 metres high. The alpine climate in the higher mountains is moderated by the warmer air from the Mediterranean.

Haute-Provence has unusually high summer temperatures for its altitude and latitude ([44 degrees north](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/44th_parallel_north)). The average summer temperature is 22 to 23 °C. at an altitude of 400 metres, and 18 to 19 °C. at the altitude of 1000 metres; and the winter average temperature is 4 to 5 °C. at 400 metres and 0 C. at 1000 metres. The lower valleys have 50 days of freezing temperatures a year, more in the higher valleys. Sometimes the temperatures in the high valleys can reach −30 °C. Because of this combination of high mountains and Mediterranean air, it is not unusual that the region frequently has some of the lowest winter temperatures and some of the hottest summer temperatures in France.

Rainfall in Haute-Provence is infrequent – 60 to 80 days a year – but can be torrential; 650 to 900 mm. a year in the foothills and plateaus of the southwest, and in the valley of the Ubaye; and 900 to 1500 mm. in the mountains. Most rainfall comes in the autumn, in brief and intense storms; from mid-June to mid-August, rain falls during brief but violent thunderstorms. Thunder can be heard 30 to 40 days a year.

Snow falls in the mountains from November to May, and in midwinter can be found down to altitude of 1000–1200 metres on the shady side of the mountains and 1300 to 1600 metres on the sunny side. Snowfalls are usually fairly light, and melt rapidly.

The [mistral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mistral_\(wind\)), a wind blowing from the north and the northwest, is a feature of the climate in the western part of the department, bringing clear and dry weather. The eastern part of the department is more protected from the mistral. A southerly wind known as the [marin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marin_\(wind\)) brings warm air, clouds and rain.

Haute-Provence is one of the sunniest regions of France, with an average of between 2,550 and 2,650 hours of sunshine annually in the north of the department, and 2,700 to 2,800 hours in the southwest. The clear nights and sunny days cause a sharp difference between nighttime and daytime temperatures. Because of the clear nights, the region is home of important observatories, such as the [Observatory of Haute-Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observatory_of_Haute-Provence) in Saint-Michel-Observatoire near [Forcalquier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forcalquier).[^cite_note-35]

### Vaucluse

[Vaucluse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaucluse) is the meeting point of three of the four different climatic zones of France; it has a [Mediterranean climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_climate) in the south, an [alpine climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpine_climate) in the northeast, around the mountains of Vaucluse and the massif of the [Baronnies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baronnies); and a [continental climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_climate) in the northwest. The close proximity of these three different climates tends to moderate all of them, and the Mediterranean climate usually prevails.

[Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange,_Vaucluse) in the Vaucluse has 2595 hours of sunshine a year. It rains an average of 80 days a year, for a total of 693.4 mm a year. The maximum average temperature in July is 29.6 °C., and the average minimum temperature in January is 1.3 °C. There are an average of 110 days of strong winds a year.[^cite_note-36]

## Culture

### Art



The 14th-century ceiling of the cloister of Fréjus Cathedral is decorated with paintings of animals, people and [mythical creatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mythical_creature)



*[Triptych](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triptych) of the Burning Bush*, by Nicolas Froment, in [Aix Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aix_Cathedral) (15th century)

Artists have been painting in Provence since prehistoric times; paintings of bisons, seals, auks and horses dating to between 27,000 and 19,000 BC were found in the [Cosquer Cave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosquer_Cave) near Marseille.[^cite_note-37]

The 14th-century wooden ceiling of the cloister of [Fréjus Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fr%C3%A9jus_Cathedral) has a series of paintings of biblical scenes, fantastic animals, and scenes from daily life, painted between 1350 and 1360. They include paintings of a fallen angel with the wings of a bat, a demon with the tail of a serpent, angels playing instruments, a tiger, an elephant, an ostrich, domestic and wild animals, a mermaid, a dragon, a centaur, a butcher, a knight, and a juggler.[^cite_note-38]

[Nicolas Froment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Froment) (1435–1486) was the most important painter of Provence during the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Renaissance), best known for his [triptych](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triptych) of the Burning Bush (c. 1476), commissioned by King [René I of Naples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ren%C3%A9_I_of_Naples). The painting shows a combination of Moses, the Burning Bush, and the Virgin Mary "who gave birth but remained a virgin", just as the bush of Moses "-burned with fire, and the bush was not consumed". This is the explication according to a plaque in the cathedral. A more likely reason for the juxtaposition is that in 1400 a shepherd, or shepherds, discovered a miraculous statue of the Virgin and Child inside another burning bush (thorn bush specifically), in the village of L'Epine in the present day department of La Marne. The site and statue were later visited by the "Bon Roi René". The wings of the triptych show King René with [Mary Magdalene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Magdalene), [St. Anthony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_the_Great) and [St. Maurice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Maurice) on one side, and Queen Jeanne de Laval, with [Saint Catherine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catherine_of_Alexandria), [John the Evangelist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_the_Evangelist), and [Saint Nicholas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Nicholas) on the other.[^cite_note-39]

[Louis Bréa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Br%C3%A9a) (1450–1523) was a 15th-century painter, born in Nice, whose work is found in churches from Genoa to Antibes. His *Retable of Saint-Nicholas* (1500) is found in [Monaco](19261.md), and his *Retable de Notre-Dame-de-Rosaire* (1515) is found in [Antibes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibes).

[Pierre Paul Puget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Paul_Puget) (1620–1694), born in Marseille, was a painter of portraits and religious scenes, but was better known for his sculptures, found in [Toulon Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toulon_Cathedral), outside the [Hôtel de Ville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C3%B4tel_de_Ville,_Toulon) of Toulon, and in the [Louvre](17546.md). There is a mountain named for him near Marseille, and a square in Toulon.

In the 19th and 20th centuries, many of the most famous painters in the world converged on Provence, drawn by the climate and the clarity of the light. The special quality of the light is partly a result of the [Mistral (wind)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mistral_\(wind\)), which removes dust from the atmosphere, greatly increasing visibility.

- [Adolphe Monticelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolphe_Monticelli) (1824–1886) was born in Marseille, moved to Paris in 1846 and returned to Marseille in 1870. His work influenced [Vincent van Gogh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincent_van_Gogh) who greatly admired him.

  [^cite_note-40]

- [Paul Cézanne](24472.md) (1839–1906) was born in Aix-en-Provence, and lived and worked there most of his life. The local landscapes, particularly [Montagne Sainte-Victoire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montagne_Sainte-Victoire), featured often in his work. - [Vincent van Gogh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincent_van_Gogh) (1853–1890) lived little more than two years in Provence, but his fame as a painter is largely a result of what he painted there. During World War II he lived in [Vence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vence), then returned to Cimiez, where he died and is buried.

- [Pierre Bonnard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Bonnard) (1867–1947) retired to and died at [Le Cannet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Cannet).

- [Georges Braque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Braque) (1882–1963) painted frequently at [L'Estaque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L'Estaque) between 1907 and 1910.

- [Henri-Edmond Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri-Edmond_Cross) (1856–1910) discovered the Côte d'Azur in 1883 and painted at [Monaco](19261.md) and [Hyères](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hy%C3%A8res).

### Literature

Main articles: [Occitan language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occitan_language) and [Occitan literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occitan_literature)



[Raimbaut de Vaqueiras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raimbaut_de_Vaqueiras), from a collection of [troubadour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troubadour) songs, *BNF Richelieu Manuscrits Français 854*, *Bibliothèque Nationale Française*, Paris

Historically, the language spoken in Provence was [Provençal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proven%C3%A7al_dialect), a dialect of the [Occitan language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occitan_language), also known as langue d'oc, and closely related to [Catalan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalan_language). There are several regional variations: [vivaro-alpin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vivaro-alpin), spoken in the Alps, and the Provençal variations of south, including the maritime, the rhoadanien (in the Rhône Valley) and the [Niçois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ni%C3%A7ois) (in Nice). Niçois is the archaic form of Provençal closest to the original language of the [troubadours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troubadour), and is sometimes to said to be literary language of its own.[^cite_note-41]

Provençal was widely spoken in Provence until the beginning of the 20th century, when the French government launched an intensive and largely successful effort to replace regional languages with French. Today, Provençal is taught in schools and universities in the region, but is spoken regularly by a small number of people, probably less than 500,000, mostly elderly.

Writers and poets in the Occitan language



"Folquet de Marselha" in a 13th-century [chansonnier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chansonnier). Depicted in his episcopal robes.

The golden age of [Provençal literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proven%C3%A7al_literature), more correctly called [Occitan literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occitan_literature), was the 11th century and the 12th century, when the troubadours broke away from classical [Latin literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_literature) and composed romances and love songs in their own vernacular language. Among the most famous troubadours was [Folquet de Marselha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folquet_de_Marselha), whose love songs became famous all over Europe, and who was praised by [Dante](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dante) in his *[Divine Comedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_Comedy)*. In his later years, Folquet gave up poetry to become the Abbot of [Thoronet Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thoronet_Abbey), and then [Bishop of Toulouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Toulouse), where he fiercely persecuted the [Cathars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathars).

In the middle of the 19th century, there was a literary movement to revive the language, called the [Félibrige](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F%C3%A9librige), led by the poet [Frédéric Mistral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fr%C3%A9d%C3%A9ric_Mistral) (1830–1914), who shared the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1904.

### Cinema

Provence has a special place in the history of the motion picture – one of the first projected motion pictures, *[L'Arrivée d'un train en gare de La Ciotat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L'Arriv%C3%A9e_d'un_train_en_gare_de_La_Ciotat)* (*The Arrival of a Train into La Ciotat Station*), a fifty-second silent film, was made by [Auguste and Louis Lumière](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auguste_and_Louis_Lumi%C3%A8re) at the train station of the coastal town of [La Ciotat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Ciotat). It was shown to an audience in Paris on 28 December 1895, causing a sensation.[^cite_note-44]

Before its commercial premiere in Paris, the film was shown to invited audiences in several French cities, including La Ciotat. It was shown at the Eden Theater in September 1895, making that theatre one of the first motion picture theatres, and the only of the first theatres still showing movies in 2009.[^cite_note-45]

Three other of the earliest Lumiere films, *[Partie de cartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partie_de_cartes)*, *[l'Arroseur arrosé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L'Arroseur_arros%C3%A9)* (the first known filmed comedy), and *[Repas de bébé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repas_de_b%C3%A9b%C3%A9)*, were also filmed in La Ciotat in 1895, at the Villa du Clos des Plages, the summer residence of the Lumière Brothers.

### Cuisine

Main article: [Cuisine of Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuisine_of_Provence)

The cuisine of Provence is the result of the warm, dry Mediterranean climate; the rugged landscape, good for grazing sheep and goats but, outside of the [Rhône Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rh%C3%B4ne_Valley), poor soil for large-scale agriculture; and the abundant seafood on the coast.

The basic ingredients are olives and olive oil, garlic, sardines, rockfish, [sea urchins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_urchin) and octopus, lamb and goat, [chickpeas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chickpea), and local fruits, such as grapes, peaches, apricots, strawberries, cherries, and the famous melons of [Cavaillon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavaillon).

The fish frequently found on menus in Provence are the *[rouget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_mullet)*, a small red fish usually eaten grilled, and the *[loup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_seabass)* (known elsewhere in France as the *bar*), often grilled with fennel over the wood of grapevines.

- *[Aïoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A%C3%AFoli)* is a thick emulsion sauce made from olive oil flavoured with crushed garlic. It often accompanies a *[bourride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourride)*, a fish soup, or is served with potatoes and cod (fr. *Morue*). There are many recipes.

- *[Bouillabaisse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bouillabaisse)* is the classic seafood dish of Marseille. The traditional version is made with three fish: [scorpionfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scorpionfish), [sea robin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_robin), and [European conger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_conger), plus an assortment of other fish and shellfish, such as [John Dory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dory), monkfish, sea urchins, crabs and [sea spiders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_spiders) included for flavour. The seasoning is as important as the fish, including salt, [pepper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chili_pepper), onion, tomato, saffron, fennel, sage, thyme, bay laurel, sometimes orange peel, and a cup of white wine or cognac. In Marseille the fish and the broth are served separately—the broth is served over thick slices of bread with *[rouille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rouille)* (see below).

  [^cite_note-46]

- *[Brandade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brandade) de morue* is a thick purée of salt cod, olive oil, milk, and garlic, usually spread on toast.

  [^cite_note-47]

- *[Daube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daube) provençale* is a stew made with cubed beef braised in wine, vegetables, garlic, and *[herbes de provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbes_de_provence)*. Variations also call for olives, prunes, and flavouring with duck fat, vinegar, brandy, lavender, nutmeg, cinnamon, cloves, juniper berries, or orange peel. For best flavour, it is cooked in several stages, and cooled for a day between each stage to allow the flavours to meld together. In the [Camargue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camargue) area of France, bulls killed in the bullfighting festivals are sometimes used for *daube*.

- *[Fougasse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fougasse_\(bread\))* is the traditional bread of Provence, round and flat with holes cut out by the baker. Modern versions are baked with olives or other fillings inside. It is roughly equivalent to *[focaccia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Focaccia)*.

- *[Pissaladière](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pissaladi%C3%A8re)* is another speciality of Nice. Though it resembles a pizza, it is made with bread dough and the traditional variety never has a tomato topping. It is usually sold in bakeries, and is topped with a bed of onions, lightly browned, and a kind of paste, called *[pissalat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pissalat)*, made from sardines and anchovies, and the small black [olives of Nice](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olive%20de%20Nice), of the [Cailletier or Taggiasca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cailletier) variety, also found in Liguria.

- *[Ratatouille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratatouille)* is a traditional dish of stewed vegetables, which originated in Nice.

  [^cite_note-48]

- *[Panisse](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panisse)* is a dish made of chickpea flour, which is boiled into a paste, cooled and set into a mould, and then fried. It is not dissimilar to socca.

#### Wines

Main article: [Provence wine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provence_wine)

The [wines of Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provence_wine) were probably introduced into Provence around 600 BC by the Greek [Phoceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoceans) who founded Marseille and Nice. After the Roman occupation, in 120 BC the [Roman Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Senate) forbade the growing of vines and olives in Provence, to protect the profitable trade in exporting Italian wines, but in the late Roman empire retired soldiers from [Roman Legions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Legions) settled in Provence and were allowed to grow grapes.[^cite_note-49]

The Romans complained about the competition from and poor quality of the wines of Provence. In the 1st century AD the Roman poet [Martial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martial) condemned the wines of Marseille as "terrible poisons, and never sold at a good price."[^cite_note-50]



Wine estate near [Vaison-la-Romaine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaison-la-Romaine)

As recently as the 1970s the wines of Provence had the reputation of being rather ordinary: In 1971 wine critic [Hugh Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Johnson_\(wine\)) wrote: "The whites are dry and can lack the acidity to be refreshing; the reds are straightforward, strong and a trifle dull; it is usually the [rosés](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ros%C3%A9), often orange-tinted, which have most appeal." He added, "Cassis and Bandol distinguish themselves for their white and red wines respectively. Cassis (no relation of the blackcurrant syrup) is livelier than the run of Provençal white wine, and Bandol leads the red in much the same way."[^cite_note-51]

Since that time, cultivation of poorer varieties has been reduced and new technologies and methods have improved the quality considerably.

The wines of Provence are grown under demanding conditions; hot weather and abundant sunshine (Toulon, near Bandol, has the most sunshine of any city in France) which ripens the grapes quickly; little rain, and the mistral.

The great majority of the wines produced in Provence are rosés. The most characteristic grape is [mourvèdre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mourv%C3%A8dre), used most famously in the red wines of Bandol. Cassis is the only area in Provence known for its white wines.

There are three regional classifications ([Appellation d'origine contrôlée](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appellation_d'origine_contr%C3%B4l%C3%A9e) (AOC)) in Provence:

- *AOC Côtes de Provence* dates to 1997, though these wines were recognised in the 17th and 18th centuries, notably by [Madame de Sévigné](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madame_de_S%C3%A9vign%C3%A9), who reported the habits and preferred wines of the Court of [Louis XIV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_XIV). The title Côtes de Provence was already in use in 1848, but production was nearly destroyed by *[phylloxera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phylloxera)* later in that century, and took decades to recover. The appellation today covers 84 communes in Var and [Bouches-du-Rhône](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bouches-du-Rh%C3%B4ne), and one in [Alpes-Maritimes](80989.md). The principal grapes used in the red wines are [grenache](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grenache), mourvèdre, [cinsault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinsault), [tibouren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibouren), and [syrah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrah). For the white wines, [clairette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clairette_blanche), [vermentino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vermentino), [sémillon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%A9millon), and [ugni blanc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ugni_blanc). The appellation covers 20,300 hectares (50,000 acres). 80 percent of the production is rosé wine, fifteen percent is red wine, and 5 percent white wine.

- *AOC Coteaux d'Aix-en-Provence* was classified in 1985. The wines of Aix were originally planted by veterans of the Roman legions in the 1st century BC, and were promoted in the 15th century by [René I of Naples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ren%C3%A9_I_of_Naples), the last ruler of Provence. Most vineyards were destroyed by *phylloxera* in the 19th century, and very slowly were reconstituted. The principal grapes for red wines and rosés are grenache, mourvèdre, cinsault, syrah, [counoise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counoise), [carignan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carignan), and [cabernet sauvignon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabernet_sauvignon). White wines are made mainly with [bourboulenc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourboulenc), clairette, [grenache blanc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grenache_blanc), and vermentino. There are 4,000 hectares (9,900 acres) in production. 70 percent of the wines are rosés, 25 percent red wines, and 5 percent white wines.

- *AOC Coteaux varois en Provence* is a recent AOC in Provence. The name Coteaux Varois was first used in 1945, and became an AOC in 1993. The name was changed to Couteaux Varois en Provence in 2005. The red wines principally use grenache, cinsaut, mourvèdre, and syrah grapes. White wines use clairette, grenache blanc, rolle blanc, sémillon, and ugni blanc. There are 2,200 hectares (5,400 acres) in this AOL. It produces 80 percent rosés, 17 percent red wines, and 3 percent white wines.

In addition, there are five local classifications (*Les appellations locales*):

- [Bandol AOC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandol_AOC), grown in the department of Var on the coast west of Toulon, mostly around the villages of [La Cadiere d'Azur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Cadiere_d'Azur) and [Castellet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castellet). Wines of this appellation must have at least fifty percent mourvèdre grapes, though most have considerably more. Other grapes used are grenache, cinsault, syrah, and carignan.

- [AOC Cassis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AOC_Cassis), made near the coastal town of [Cassis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassis), between Toulon and Marseille, was the first wine in Provence to be classified as an AOC in 1936, and is best known for its white wines. Wines from Cassis are described in French literature as early as the 12th century. The grapes most commonly used are [marsanne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsanne), clairette, [ugni blanc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ugni_blanc), [sauvignon blanc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sauvignon_blanc), and [Bourboulenc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourboulenc). Rosé wines use grenache, carignan, and mourvèdre.

- AOC [Bellet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bellet); at the time of the French Revolution, the little town of Saint Roman de Bellet (now part of Nice) was the center of an important wine region. Production was nearly destroyed by *phylloxera* and by the two wars, and only in 1946 was the region again producing fully. It was classified as an AOC in 1941. Today the region is one of the smallest in France; just 47 hectares. The grapes are grown on terraces along the left bank of the Var River, east of the town. The major grapes grown for red wines and rosés are [braquet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braquet), [Folle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folle), and cinsault, blended sometimes with grenache. For white wines, the major grapes grown are [rolle blanc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolle_blanc?action=edit\&redlink=1), [roussane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roussane), [spagnol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spagnol?action=edit\&redlink=1), and [mayorquin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayorquin?action=edit\&redlink=1); the secondary grapes are clairette, [bourboulenc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourboulenc), [chardonnay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chardonnay), [pignerol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pignerol), and [muscat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muscat_\(grape_and_wine\)).

- [Palette AOC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palette_AOC); the little village of [Palete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Tholonet), four kilometres east of Aix-en-Provence, has long been famous for the production of a [vin cuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vin_cuit), or [fortified wine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortified_wine), used in the traditional Provence Christmas dessert, the [Thirteen desserts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirteen_desserts), and the Christmas cake called *pompo à l'oli*, or the olive-oil pump. This production was nearly abandoned, but is now being recreated. The main grapes for red wine are grenache, mourvèdre, and [cinsaut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinsaut); for the white wines clairette.

- AOC [Les Baux de Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_Baux_de_Provence); was established as an AOC for red and rosé wines in 1995.

South of Avignon, it occupies the north and south slopes of the [Alpilles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpilles), up to an altitude of 400 metres (1,300 ft), and extends about thirty kilometres from east to west. The principal grapes for the red wines are grenache, mourvèdre, and syrah. For the rosés, the main grapes are syrah and cinsault.

#### Pastis



A glass of diluted [pastis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastis)



Cochonnet next to the boule

[Pastis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastis) is the traditional liqueur of Provence, flavoured with [anise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anise) and typically containing 40–45% alcohol by volume. When [absinthe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absinthe) was banned in France in 1915, the major absinthe producers (then [Pernod Fils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pernod_Fils) and [Ricard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Ricard), who have since merged as [Pernod Ricard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pernod_Ricard)) reformulated their drink without the banned [wormwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absinthe_wormwood) and with more aniseed flavour, coming from [star anise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_anise), sugar and a lower alcohol content, creating pastis. It is usually drunk diluted with water, which it turns a cloudy color. It is especially popular in and around Marseille.

### Sports

[Pétanque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C3%A9tanque), a form of [boules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boules), is a popular sport played in towns and villages all over Provence.

A more athletic version of the sport called *jeu provençal* was popular throughout Provence in the 19th century – this version is featured in the novels and memoires of Marcel Pagnol; players ran three steps before throwing the ball, and it resembled at times a form of ballet. The modern version of the game was created in 1907 at the town of [La Ciotat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Ciotat) by a former champion of *jeu provençal&#x20;*&#x6E;amed Jules Hugues, who was unable to play because of his rheumatism. He devised a new set of rules where the field was much smaller, and players did not run before throwing the ball, but remained inside a small circle with their feet together. This gave the game its name, *lei peds tancats*, in the [Provençal dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proven%C3%A7al_dialect) of [Occitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occitan_language), 'feet together'. The first tournament was played in La Ciotat in 1910. The first steel boules were introduced in 1927.

The object is to throw a ball (boule) as close as possible to a smaller ball, called the cochonnet, (this kind of throw is called to *faire le point* or *pointer*); or to knock away a boules of the opponent that is close to the cochonnet (this is called to *tirer*). Players compete one-on-one (*tête-à-tête*), in teams of two (*doublettes*) or teams of three (*triplettes*). The object is to accumulate thirteen points. The point belongs to the ball the closest to the cochonnet. A player pitches balls until he can regain the point (*reprenne le point*) by having his ball closest to the cochonnet. Each ball from a single team, if there are no other balls from the other team closer to the cochonnet, counts as a point. The points are counted when all of the balls have been tossed by both teams.[^cite_note-52]
