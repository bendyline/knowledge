# United States district court

This article is about the district courts of the U.S. federal judicial system. For the district courts of various U.S. states' judicial systems, see [Courts of the United States § Courts by state of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courts_of_the_United_States#Courts_by_state_of_the_United_States).

The **United States district courts** are the [trial courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trial_court) of the [U.S. federal judiciary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_federal_judiciary). There is one district court for each of the 94 [federal judicial districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_federal_judicial_district). Each district covers one [U.S. state](18618239.md) or a portion of a state. There is at least one [federal courthouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_United_States_federal_courthouses) in each district, and many districts have more than one. District court decisions are appealed to the [U.S. court of appeals](70228.md) for the circuit in which they reside, except for certain specialized cases that are appealed to the [U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Court_of_Appeals_for_the_Federal_Circuit) or directly to the [U.S. Supreme Court](31737.md).

District courts are courts of [law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law), [equity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_equity), and [admiralty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Admiralty_court), and can hear both [civil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_law_\(common_law\)) and [criminal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_law) cases. But unlike [U.S. state courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_court_\(United_States\)), federal district courts are courts of [limited jurisdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limited_jurisdiction), and can only hear cases that involve disputes between [residents of different states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diversity_jurisdiction), [questions of federal law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_question_jurisdiction), or federal crimes.

## Legal basis

Unlike the U.S. Supreme Court, which was expressly established by [Article III of the Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_Three_of_the_United_States_Constitution), the district courts were established by Congress pursuant to authority delegated by Article III[^cite_note-1] through the enacting of a federal statute, the [Judiciary Act of 1789](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judiciary_Act_of_1789). There is no constitutional requirement that district courts exist at all.[^cite_note-Pfander_Page_83-2]

During the drafting and ratification of the [Constitution](31644.md), some opponents of a strong federal judiciary argued that the federal courts ought to be limited to the Supreme Court, which would hear appeals only from state courts. In other words, the state courts would be treated as [federal tribunals](2103888.md) under Article I of the Constitution for the purpose of hearing disputes under federal law, but their judges would not become officers of the federal government.[^cite_note-Pfander_Page_83-2] [Edward Carrington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Carrington) advocated this position in a letter to [James Madison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Madison), and it was also discussed by [Alexander Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Hamilton) in [Federalist No. 81](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federalist_No._81).[^cite_note-Pfander_Page_83-2] However, this view did not prevail, and the first Congress created the district court system that is still in place today.[^cite_note-Pfander_Page_83-2] Pursuant to the Constitution, nonetheless, [state courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_court_\(United_States\)) retain the power of [concurrent jurisdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concurrent_jurisdiction) in most federal matters.[^cite_note-Oakley_Page_64-3]

When the Act was first passed, there were thirteen districts created among the eleven states which had ratified the Constitution by that point. When North Carolina and Rhode Island voted to ratify, a district was created for each of them, bringing the number of districts to fifteen.

The [territories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territories_of_the_United_States) ([insular areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insular_area)) of [Guam](11974.md), the [Northern Mariana Islands](21402.md), and the [United States Virgin Islands](32135.md) each have one territorial court; these courts are called "district courts" and exercise the same jurisdiction as district courts,[^cite_note-USGOV-4][^cite_note-FJC-5] but differ from district courts in that territorial courts are [Article IV courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_territorial_court), with judges who serve ten-year terms rather than the [lifetime tenure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lifetime_tenure) of judges of [Article III courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_I_and_Article_III_tribunals), such as the district court judges.[^cite_note-FJC-5]

[American Samoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Samoa) having neither a district court nor a federal territorial court, its federal cases are sent either to the [District of Columbia](1786384.md) or [Hawaii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_District_of_Hawaii).[^cite_note-GAO_AS-6] The [Philippines](23440.md), previously part of the United States, were never part of the U.S. federal court system.[^cite_note-Go_Page_7-7]

## Geography

There are 89 districts in the 50 states, with a total of 94 districts including territories.[^cite_note-8] There is at least one judicial district for each [state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_state), the [District of Columbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/District_of_Columbia), and [Puerto Rico](23041.md).

Each state has between one and four districts. For states with multiple districts, they are named geographically. States with two districts all give them either Northern–Southern or Western–Eastern designations. Most states with three districts add a Middle District, with two exceptions: Illinois has a Central District instead of a Middle District, and Oklahoma has Northern, Western, and Eastern Districts. Of the three states with four districts, New York and Texas use all four directional designations, while California has a Central District and no Western District.

## Other federal trial courts

There are other federal trial courts that have nationwide [jurisdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisdiction) over certain types of cases, but the district court also has [concurrent jurisdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concurrent_jurisdiction) over many of those cases, and the district court is the only one with jurisdiction over civilian criminal cases.

The [United States Court of International Trade](1220886.md) addresses cases involving international trade and customs issues. The [United States Court of Federal Claims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Court_of_Federal_Claims) has [exclusive jurisdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exclusive_jurisdiction) over most claims for money damages against the United States, including disputes over federal contracts, unlawful [takings](144089.md) of private property by the federal government, and suits for injury on federal property or by a federal employee. The [United States Tax Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Tax_Court) has jurisdiction over contested pre-assessment determinations of [taxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxation_in_the_United_States).

## Judges

Main article: [United States federal judge](834368.md)

A judge of a United States district court is officially titled a "United States District Judge". Other [federal judges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_judge), including [circuit judges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_court_of_appeals) and [Supreme Court justices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_justices_of_the_Supreme_Court_of_the_United_States), can also sit in a district court upon assignment by the chief judge of the circuit or by the [Chief Justice of the United States](31739.md). The number of judges in each district court (and the structure of the judicial system generally) is set by [Congress](31756.md) in the [United States Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Code). The [president](24113.md) appoints the federal judges for terms of good behavior (subject to the [advice and consent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advice_and_consent) of the [Senate](24909346.md)), so the nominees often share at least some of his or her convictions. In states represented by a senator of the president's party, the senator (or the more senior of them if both senators are of the president's party) has substantial input into the nominating process, and through a tradition known as [senatorial courtesy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senatorial_courtesy) can exercise an unofficial veto over a nominee unacceptable to the senator.

Federal [magistrate judges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_magistrate_judge) are appointed by each district court pursuant to statute. They are appointed for an eight-year term and may be reappointed for additional eight-year terms. A magistrate judge may be removed "for incompetency, misconduct, neglect of duty, or physical or mental disability".[^cite_note-9] A magistrate judgeship may be a stepping stone to a district judgeship nomination.

District judges usually concentrate on managing their court's overall caseload, supervising trials, and writing opinions in response to important motions like the motion for [summary judgment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summary_judgment). Since the 1960s, routine tasks like resolving discovery disputes can, in the district judge's discretion, be referred to [magistrate judges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_magistrate_judge). Magistrate judges can also be requested to prepare reports and recommendations on contested matters for the district judge's consideration or, with the consent of all parties, to assume complete jurisdiction over a case including conducting the trial.

With the exception of the [territorial courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_territorial_court) ([Guam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/District_Court_of_Guam), the [Northern Mariana Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/District_Court_for_the_Northern_Mariana_Islands), and the [Virgin Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/District_Court_of_the_Virgin_Islands)), federal district judges are [Article III judges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_I_and_Article_III_tribunals#Article_III_courts) appointed for life, and can be removed involuntarily only when they violate the standard of "good behavior". The sole method of involuntary removal of a judge is through [impeachment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_impeachment_in_the_United_States) by the [United States House of Representatives](19468510.md) followed by a trial in the [United States Senate](24909346.md) and a conviction by a two-thirds vote. Otherwise, a judge, even if convicted of a [felony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felony) criminal offense by a jury, is entitled to hold office until retirement or death. In the history of the United States, twelve judges have been impeached by the House, and seven have been removed following conviction in the Senate. (For a table that includes the twelve impeached judges, see [Impeachment in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_federal_impeachments).)

A judge who has reached the age of 65 (or has become disabled) may retire or elect to go on [senior status](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senior_status) and keep working. Such senior judges are not counted in the quota of active judges for the district and do only whatever work they are assigned by the chief judge of the district, but they keep their offices (called "chambers") and staff, and many of them work full-time.

As of 2010, there were 678 authorized district court judgeships.[^cite_note-10]

A federal judge is addressed in writing as "[The Honorable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Honorable) John/Jane Doe" or "Hon. John/Jane Doe" and in speech as "Judge" or "Judge Doe" or, when presiding in court, "Your Honor".

## Clerks

Each district court appoints a clerk, who is responsible for overseeing filings made with the court, maintaining the court's records, processing fees, fines, and restitution, and managing the non-judicial work of the court, including information technology, budget, procurement, human resources, and financial. Clerks may appoint deputies, clerical assistants, and employees to carry out the work of the court. The clerk of each district court must reside in the district for which the clerk is appointed, except that the clerk of the District of Columbia and the clerk of the Southern District of New York may reside within twenty miles of their respective districts.

The Judiciary Act of 1789 authorized the Supreme Court and the judge of each U.S. District Court to appoint a clerk to assist with the administration of federal judicial business in those courts. The clerk for each district court was to also serve as clerk of the corresponding circuit court. The Judiciary Act required each clerk to issue the writs summoning jurors and "to record the decrees, judgments and determinations of the court of which he is clerk."

The Judicial Code (28 U.S.C. § 751) provides that the clerk is appointed, and may be removed, by the court. The clerk's duties are prescribed by the statute, by the court's customs and practices, and by policy established by the [Judicial Conference of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judicial_Conference_of_the_United_States). The clerk is appointed by order of the court [en banc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/En_banc) to serve the entire court. The role of the clerk and deputies or assistants should not be confused with the judges' [law clerks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_clerk), who assist the judges by conducting research and preparing drafts of opinions.

To be eligible to serve as a clerk, a person must have a minimum of 10 years of progressively responsible administrative experience in public service or business that provides a thorough understanding of organizational, procedural, and human aspects of managing an organization, and at least 3 of the 10 years must have been in a position of substantial management responsibility. An attorney may substitute the active practice of law on a year-for-year basis for the management or administrative experience requirement. Clerks do not have to be licensed attorneys, but some courts specify that a law degree is a preference for employment.

Unlike some state courts, the power of federal courts to hear cases and controversies is strictly limited. Federal courts may not decide every case that happens to come before them. In order for a district court to entertain a lawsuit, Congress must first grant the court subject matter jurisdiction over the type of dispute in question.

The district courts exercise original jurisdiction over—that is, they are empowered to conduct trials in—the following types of cases:

- Civil actions arising under the Constitution, laws, and treaties of the United States;

  [^cite_note-11]

- Certain civil actions between citizens of different states or citizens of a state and a foreign state;

  [^cite_note-12]

- Civil actions within the admiralty or maritime jurisdiction of the United States;

  [^cite_note-13]

- Criminal prosecutions brought by the United States;

  [^cite_note-14]

- Civil actions in which the United States is a party;

  [^cite_note-15]

  &#x20;and

- Many other types of cases and controversies

  [^cite_note-16]

For most of these cases, the jurisdiction of the federal district courts is [concurrent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concurrent_jurisdiction) with that of the [state courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_court_\(United_States\)). In other words, a plaintiff can choose to bring these cases in either a federal district court or a state court. Congress has established a procedure whereby a party, typically the defendant, can "remove" a case from state court to federal court, provided that the federal court also has original jurisdiction over the matter (meaning that the case could have been filed in federal court initially).[^cite_note-17] If the party that initially filed the case in state court believes that removal was improper, that party can ask the district court to "remand" the case to the state court system. For certain matters, such as patent and copyright infringement disputes and prosecutions for federal crimes, the jurisdiction of the district courts is exclusive of that of the state courts, meaning that only federal courts can hear those cases.[^cite_note-18]

In addition to their original jurisdiction, the district courts have appellate jurisdiction over a very limited class of judgments, orders, and decrees.[^cite_note-19]

## Attorneys



United States District Court Attorney Admissions Reciprocity Map (2015)

In order to represent a party in a case in a district court, a person must be an [attorney at law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attorney_at_law_\(United_States\)) and generally must be admitted to the bar of that particular court. The United States usually does not have a separate [bar examination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bar_examination) for federal practice (except with respect to patent practice before the [United States Patent and Trademark Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Patent_and_Trademark_Office)). [Admission to the bar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Admission_to_the_bar_in_the_United_States) of a district court is generally available to any attorney who is admitted to practice law in the state where the district court sits.[^cite_note-20]

56 districts (around 60% of all district courts) require an attorney to be admitted to practice in the state where the district court sits. The other 39 districts (around 40% of all district courts) extend admission to certain lawyers admitted in other states, although conditions vary from court to court. For example, the district courts in [New York City](645042.md) ([Southern District of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_Southern_District_of_New_York) and [Eastern District of New York](882402.md)) extend admission to attorneys admitted to the bar in Connecticut or Vermont and to the district court in that state, but otherwise require attorneys to be admitted to the New York bar. Only 13 districts extend admission to attorneys admitted to any U.S. state bar.[^cite_note-\:0-21][^cite_note-22]

The [Northern District of Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_Northern_District_of_Texas) extends attorney admission to members of any state bar, but in most cases, an attorney who does not reside or maintain a principal office in the district is required to designate "local counsel" with a residence or office within 50 miles of the courthouse.[^cite_note-23]

The attorney generally submits an application with a fee and takes the oath of admission. Local practice varies as to whether the oath is given in writing or in open court before a judge of the district. A "sponsor" admitted to the court's bar is often required.&#x20;

Several district courts require attorneys seeking admission to their bars to take an additional bar examination on federal law, including the [Southern District of Ohio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_Southern_District_of_Ohio),[^cite_note-24] the [Northern District of Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_Northern_District_of_Florida),[^cite_note-25] and the [District of Puerto Rico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_District_of_Puerto_Rico).[^cite_note-26]

*[Pro hac vice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pro_hac_vice)* admission is also available in most federal district courts on a case-by-case basis. Most district courts require *pro hac vice* attorneys to associate with an attorney admitted to practice before the court.[^cite_note-\:0-21]

## Appeals

Generally, a final ruling by a district court in either a civil or a criminal case can be appealed to the [United States court of appeals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_court_of_appeals) in the [federal judicial circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_federal_judicial_circuits) in which the district court is located, except that some district court rulings involving patents and certain other specialized matters must be appealed instead to the [United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Court_of_Appeals_for_the_Federal_Circuit), and in a very few cases the appeal may be taken directly to the [United States Supreme Court](31737.md).

## Largest and busiest district courts



The [First Street Courthouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Courthouse_\(First_Street,_Los_Angeles\)) in downtown [Los Angeles, California](18110.md), home to most of the district judges of the Western Division of the [Central District of California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_Central_District_of_California).

The [Central District of California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_Central_District_of_California) is the largest federal district by population;[^cite_note-27] it includes all five counties that make up [Greater Los Angeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Los_Angeles). By contrast, [New York City](645042.md) and the surrounding [metropolitan area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_metropolitan_area) are divided between the [Southern District of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_Southern_District_of_New_York) (which includes [Manhattan](45470.md), [The Bronx](3338.md) and [Westchester County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westchester_County,_New_York)) and the [Eastern District of New York](882402.md) (which includes [Brooklyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooklyn), [Queens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queens), [Staten Island](127062.md), [Nassau County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nassau_County,_New_York) and [Suffolk County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suffolk_County,_New_York)). New York suburbs in [Connecticut](6466.md) and [New Jersey](21648.md) are covered by the [District of Connecticut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_District_of_Connecticut) and [District of New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_District_of_New_Jersey), respectively.

The Southern District of New York and the Central District of California are the largest federal districts by number of judges, with 28 judges each.[^cite_note-28]

In 2007, the busiest district courts in terms of criminal federal felony filings were the [District of New Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_District_of_New_Mexico), [Western District of Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_Western_District_of_Texas), [Southern District of Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_Southern_District_of_Texas), and the [District of Arizona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_District_of_Arizona). These four districts all share the [border with Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico_%E2%80%93_United_States_border).[^cite_note-29] A crackdown on illegal immigration resulted in 75 percent of the criminal cases filed in the 94 district courts in 2007 being filed in these four districts and the other district that borders Mexico, the [Southern District of California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_District_of_California).[^cite_note-30] The busiest patent litigation court is the [United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_Eastern_District_of_Texas), with the most patent lawsuits filed there nearly every year.[^cite_note-31]

### Other abolished district courts

On rare occasions, an extinct district court was extinguished by merging it with other district courts. In every case except one, this has restored a district court that had been subdivided:

- Between 1794 and 1797, the [United States District Court for the District of North Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_District_of_North_Carolina) was divided into the United States district courts for the districts of [Edenton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_District_of_Edenton), [New Bern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_District_of_New_Bern), and [Wilmington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_District_of_Wilmington).

- Between 1801 and 1802, the [United States District Court for the District of New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_District_of_New_Jersey) was divided into the United States district courts for the districts of [East Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_District_of_East_Jersey) and [West Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_District_of_West_Jersey).

- When California was admitted as a state in 1850, it was initially divided into two districts, the Northern and the Southern.

  [^cite_note-calif-32]

  &#x20;The Southern District of California was abolished on July 27, 1866, and the State made to constitute one district, the statute providing that the Judge of the Northern District exercise the powers of the United States District Court for the District of California, and that all records of the Southern District Court be delivered to the Clerk of the Northern District Court.

  [^cite_note-calif-32]

  &#x20;Twenty years later, on August 5, 1886, Congress re-created the Southern District of California.

  [^cite_note-calif-32]

- Between 1911 and 1961, the [United States District Court for the District of South Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_District_of_South_Carolina) was divided into the United States district courts for the [Eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_Eastern_District_of_South_Carolina) and [Western](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_Western_District_of_South_Carolina) districts of South Carolina.

- The [United States District Court for the Eastern District of Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_Eastern_District_of_Illinois) was eliminated and a new [United States District Court for the Central District of Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_Central_District_of_Illinois) was created in its place on October 2, 1978.

There are a few additional extinct district courts that fall into neither of the above two patterns.

- From 1801 to 1802, the District of Columbia and pieces of Maryland and Virginia formed the [United States District Court for the District of Potomac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_District_of_Potomac), which was the first United States district court to cross state lines. During the same period, the [United States District Court for the District of Norfolk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_District_of_Norfolk) was carved out of another piece of Virginia. The United States district courts for the districts of Maryland and Virginia remained during this brief period.

- From 1801 to 1802, and again from 1802 to 1872, the state of North Carolina was subdivided into the United States district courts for the districts of [Albemarle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_District_of_Albemarle), [Cape Fear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_District_of_Cape_Fear), and [Pamptico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_District_of_Pamptico). These courts were extinguished when the state was reorganized into the United States district courts for the Eastern and Western districts of North Carolina.

- [United States District Court for the District of Orleans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_District_of_Orleans). This court was renamed the [United States District Court for the District of Louisiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_District_of_Louisiana) when the [Territory of Orleans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territory_of_Orleans) became the State of Louisiana.

- [United States District Court for the Canal Zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_Canal_Zone). This court was abolished, effective March 31, 1982, as part of the process of returning the [Canal Zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panama_Canal_Zone) to [Panama](22997.md). Cases then pending in the Canal Zone court were transferred to the [United States District Court for the Eastern District of Louisiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_Eastern_District_of_Louisiana) in [New Orleans](53842.md).

- [United States Court for China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Court_for_China). This court functioned as a district court between 1906 and 1943. It had jurisdiction over American citizens in China.
