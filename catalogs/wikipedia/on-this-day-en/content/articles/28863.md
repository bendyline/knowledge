The **Sea of Marmara**,[^cite_note-1] also known as the **Sea of Marmora**, the **Marmara Sea**, or **Propontis** (/prəˈpɒntɪs/; ancient Greek: Προποντίς /pro.ponˈdis/), is a small [inland sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inland_sea) entirely within the borders of [Turkey](11125639.md). It links the [Black Sea](3386.md) and the [Aegean Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegean_Sea) via the [Bosporus](3705.md) and [Dardanelles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dardanelles) straits, separating Turkey's [European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Thrace) and [Asian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia) sides. It has an area of 11,350 km<sup>2</sup> (4,380 sq mi), and its dimensions are roughly 280 km × 80 km (174 mi × 50 mi), considered as the smallest sea in the world.[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3] Its greatest depth is 1,370 m (4,490 ft).

## Name

The Sea of Marmara is named after the largest island on its south side, called [Marmara Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marmara_Island) because it is rich in [marble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marble) ([Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) μάρμᾰρον, *mármaron* "marble").[^cite_note-4]

In [classical antiquity](252905.md), it was known as the **Propontis**, from the Greek *pro* "in front of" + *pontos* "sea", specifically the Black Sea, since the Ancient Greeks sailed through it to reach the Black Sea.[^cite_note-5]

## Mythology

In [Greek mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mythology), a storm on the Propontis brought the [Argonauts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argonauts) back to an island they had left, precipitating a battle in which either [Jason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jason) or [Heracles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heracles) killed [King Cyzicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyzicus_\(Greek_mythology\)), who had mistaken them for his [Pelasgian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelasgia) enemies.[^cite_note-6]

## Geography and hydrology

The [International Hydrographic Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Hydrographic_Organization) defines the limits of the Sea of Marmara as:[^cite_note-7]

> *On the West*. The [Dardanelles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dardanelles) limit of the [Aegean Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegean_Sea) \[A line joining Kum Kale (26°11'E) and [Cape Helles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Helles)].
>
> *On the Northeast*. A line joining Cape Rumili with Cape Anatoli (41°13′N). The sea's south coast is heavily indented and includes the [Gulf of İzmit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_%C4%B0zmit) ([Turkish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_language): *İzmit Körfezi*), the [Gulf of Gemlik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Gemlik) ([Turkish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_language): *Gemlik Körfezi*), the [Gulf of Bandırma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Band%C4%B1rma) ([Turkish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_language): *Bandırma Körfezi*), and the [Gulf of Erdek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Erdek) ([Turkish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_language): *Erdek Körfezi*).

The surface [salinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salinity) of the Marmara averages about 22 parts per thousand, which is slightly more than that of the Black Sea, but only about two-thirds that of most [oceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean). The water is much more saline at the bottom of the sea, averaging a salinity around 38 parts per thousand, similar to that of the [Mediterranean Sea](19006.md). This high-density saline water does not migrate to the surface, as is also the case with the Black Sea. Water from the [Susurluk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simav_River), [Biga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biga_%C3%87ay%C4%B1) (Granicus), and [Gönen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B6nen) Rivers also reduces the salinity of the sea, though with less effect than on the Black Sea. With little land in [Thrace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrace) draining southward, almost all of these rivers flow from [Anatolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia).

### Islands

Two main groups of islands are in the Sea of Marmara. To the north lie the [Princes' Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princes'_Islands), an archipelago made up of the inhabited islands of [Kınaliada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C4%B1nal%C4%B1ada), [Burgazada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burgazada), [Heybeliada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heybeliada), [Büyükada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%BCy%C3%BCkada), and [Sedef Adası](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedef_Island), and several uninhabited islands, including [Sivriada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sivriada), [Yassıada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yass%C4%B1ada), [Kaşıkadası](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ka%C5%9F%C4%B1kadas%C4%B1,_%C4%B0stanbul) and [Tavşanadası](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tav%C5%9Fan_Adas%C4%B1). The inhabited islands are readily accessible by ferry from both the European and Asian shores of [İstanbul](3391396.md), and the entire archipelago forms part of the conurbation.

To the south lie the [Marmara Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marmara_Archipelago), an archipelago made up of the eponymous Marmara Island and three other inhabited islands – [Avşa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Av%C5%9Fa), [Paşalimanı](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pa%C5%9Faliman%C4%B1), and [Ekinlik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ekinlik_Island), as well as 17 largely uninhabited islands, including the prison island of [Imralı](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%B0mral%C4%B1), whose most famous prisoner, since 1999, has been the PKK leader [Abdullah Öcalan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdullah_%C3%96calan). These islands lie within [Balıkesir Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bal%C4%B1kesir_Province) and are most readily accessible from [Tekirdağ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tekirda%C4%9F) in [Thrace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trakya) or [Erdek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erdek) on the southern shore of the Sea of Marmara. In high summer, additional ferries travel to Avşa and Marmara Islands from the centre of İstanbul to facilitate a growing tourist trade.

Also, a few individual islands are elsewhere in the Sea of Marmara, such as Koç Adası, off [Tuzla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuzla,_Istanbul), which is privately owned by the [Koç family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko%C3%A7_family) of industrialists.

## Environmental challenges

The [North Anatolian Fault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Anatolian_Fault) runs under the sea and has triggered several major [earthquakes](10106.md), such as the [Izmit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1999_%C4%B0zmit_earthquake) and [Düzce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1999_D%C3%BCzce_earthquake) in August and November 1999, respectively. The August 1999 earthquake is commonly referred to as the [Marmara Earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marmara_Earthquake), since its epicentre lay under the sea and most of the places worst affected by the quake and ensuing [tsunami](31161.md) lay along its shores.[^cite_note-8]

In a storm on 29 December 1999, the Russian [oil tanker](15923603.md) [*Volgoneft-248*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volgoneft-248) broke in two in the Sea of Marmara, spilling more than 1,500 tonnes of oil into the water.[^cite_note-9]

The main cities in Turkey, especially Istanbul, the largest city, are around the Marmara Sea, a small inland sea. Despite its limited size, the basin is home to about one-third of the country's population. Rapid population growth and uncontrolled construction in this area have put heavy pressure on the sea. For many years, waste was dumped into the Marmara Sea with little or no treatment, surpassing the sea's ability to handle it. As a result, many species in the sea have disappeared, and fishing was banned in the "East Bay" of the Marmara Sea for years due to severe pollution. One of the most serious environmental problems in the Marmara Sea in recent years has been mucilage, a thick, sticky substance that appears for up to six months. The mucilage outbreak began in January and lasted until June in 2021. It caused habitat loss and economic issues, and disrupted vital ecosystem services, such as fishing.[^cite_note-10]
