**Contemporary architecture** is the [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture) of the 21st century. No single style is dominant.[^cite_note-1] Contemporary architects work in several different styles, from [postmodernism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postmodern_architecture), [high-tech architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-tech_architecture) and new references and interpretations of traditional architecture like [New Classical architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Classical_architecture)[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3] to highly conceptual forms and designs, resembling [sculpture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sculpture) on an enormous scale. Some of these styles and approaches make use of very advanced technology and modern building materials, such as [tube structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tube_\(structure\)) which allow construction of buildings that are taller, lighter and stronger than those in the 20th century, while others prioritize the use of [natural and ecological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_building) materials like stone, wood and [lime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lime_mortar).[^cite_note-4] One technology that is common to all forms of contemporary architecture is the use of new techniques of [computer-aided design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer-aided_design), which allow buildings to be designed and modeled on computers in three dimensions, and constructed with more precision and speed.

Contemporary buildings and styles vary greatly. Some feature [concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concrete) structures wrapped in [glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glass) or [aluminium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium) screens, very asymmetric [facades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facade), and [cantilevered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantilever) sections which hang over the street. Skyscrapers twist, or break into crystal-like facets. Facades are designed to shimmer or change color at different times of day.

Whereas the major monuments of [modern architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_architecture) in the 20th century were mostly concentrated in the Americas and Western Europe, contemporary architecture is global; important new buildings have been built in China, Russia, India and particularly in [Arab states of the Persian Gulf](3088555.md); the [Burj Khalifa](794957.md) in [Dubai](211583.md) was the tallest building in the world in 2019, and the [Shanghai Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanghai_Tower) in China was the second-tallest.

Additionally, in the late 20th century, [New Classical Architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Classical_architecture), a traditionalist response to [modernist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_architecture) architecture, emerged, continuing into the 21st century.[^cite_note-5] The 21st century saw the emergence of multiple organizations dedicated to the promotion of [traditional architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_architecture). Examples include the [International Network for Traditional Building, Architecture & Urbanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/INTBAU) (INTBAU),[^cite_note-6] the [Institute of Classical Architecture & Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Institute_of_Classical_Architecture_and_Art) (ICAA),[^cite_note-7] the [Driehaus Architecture Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Driehaus_Architecture_Prize).[^cite_note-8] Contemporary traditional architects include [Michael Graves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Graves), [Léon Krier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%A9on_Krier), [Yasmeen Lari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yasmeen_Lari), [Robert Stern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_A._M._Stern) and [Abdel-Wahed El-Wakil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdel-Wahed_El-Wakil).

Recently, in the realm of contemporary architecture, a philosophy known as "New Contextualism" has emerged, primarily coined and propagated by Bangladeshi architect and academic Mohammad Habib Reza.[^cite_note-9] This approach advocates for creating built environments that are profoundly informed by both historical precedents and future predictions, while embracing a holistic understanding of context.[^cite_note-10] Unlike universalist or purely modernist perspectives, New Contextualism emphasizes the deep integration of a design within its specific setting, considering not only the immediate site but also broader universal values, regional characteristics, and the socio-cultural fabric of a place.[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12] It stresses the importance of equity, social justice, and the revitalization of vernacular building traditions to achieve sustainable and inclusive designs. The philosophy encourages the use of data analytics and scenario planning to anticipate future needs and challenges, aiming for timeless yet adaptable architectural solutions.[^cite_note-13][^cite_note-14]

Most of the landmarks of contemporary architecture are the works of a small group of architects who work on an international scale. Many were designed by architects already famous in the late 20th century, including [Mario Botta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mario_Botta), [Frank Gehry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Gehry), [Jean Nouvel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Nouvel), [Norman Foster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Foster), [Ieoh Ming Pei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ieoh_Ming_Pei), [Dariush Borbor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dariush_Borbor) and [Renzo Piano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renzo_Piano), while others are the work of a new generation born during or after World War II, including [Zaha Hadid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaha_Hadid), [Santiago Calatrava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santiago_Calatrava), [Daniel Libeskind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Libeskind), [Jacques Herzog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Herzog), [Pierre de Meuron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_de_Meuron), [Rem Koolhaas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rem_Koolhaas), and [Shigeru Ban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shigeru_Ban). Other projects are the work of collectives of several architects, such as [UNStudio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNStudio) and [SANAA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SANAA), or large multinational agencies such as [Skidmore, Owings & Merrill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skidmore,_Owings_&_Merrill), with thirty associate architects and large teams of engineers and designers, and [Gensler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gensler), with 5,000 employees in 16 countries.

Some of the most striking and innovative works of contemporary architecture are art museums, which are often examples of sculptural architecture, and are the signature works of major architects. The Quadracci Pavilion of the [Milwaukee Art Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milwaukee_Art_Museum) in Milwaukee, Wisconsin, was designed by Spanish architect [Santiago Calatrava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santiago_Calatrava). Its structure includes a movable, wing-like *[brise soleil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brise_soleil)* that opens up for a wingspan of 217 feet (66 m) during the day, folding over the tall, arched structure at night or during bad weather.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETaschen2016108–111-15]

The [Walker Art Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walker_Art_Center) in Minneapolis (2005), was designed by the Swiss architects [Herzog and de Meuron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herzog_and_de_Meuron), who designed the [Tate Modern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tate_Modern) museum in London, and who won the [Pritzker Architecture Prize](53422.md), the most prestigious award in architecture, in 2001. It updates and provides a contrast to the austere earlier [Modernist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_architecture) structure designed by [Edward Larrabee Barnes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Larrabee_Barnes) by adding a five-story tower clad in panels of delicately sculpted gray aluminum, which change color with the changing light, connecting by a wide glass gallery leading to the older building. It also harmonizes with two stone churches opposite.[^cite_note-16]

The Polish-born American architect [Daniel Libeskind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Libeskind) (born 1946) is one of the most prolific of contemporary museum architects; He was an academic before he began designing buildings and was one of the early proponents of the architectural theory of [Deconstructivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deconstructivism_\(architecture\)). The exterior of his [Imperial War Museum North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_War_Museum_North) in [Manchester, England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester,_England) (2002), has an exterior which resembles, depending upon the light and time of day, huge and broken pieces of earth or armor plates, and is said to symbolize the destruction of war. In 2006 Libeskind finished the Hamilton Building of the [Denver Art Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denver_Art_Museum) in Denver Colorado, composed of twenty sloping planes, none of them parallel or perpendicular, covered with 230,000 square feet of [titanium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titanium) panels. Inside, the walls of the galleries are all different, sloping and asymmetric.[^cite_note-17] Libeskind completed another striking museum, the [Royal Ontario Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Ontario_Museum) in [Toronto](64646.md), Ontario, Canada (2007), also known as "The Crystal," a building whose form, resembles a shattered crystal.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETaschen2016392-18] Libeskind's museums have been both admired and attacked by critics. While admiring many features of the Denver Art Museum, *The New York Times*' architecture critic Nicolai Ouroussoff wrote that "In a building of canted walls and asymmetrical rooms—tortured geometries generated purely by formal considerations — it is virtually impossible to enjoy the art."[^cite_note-19]

The [De Young Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Young_Museum) in San Francisco was designed by the Swiss architects [Herzog & de Meuron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herzog_&_de_Meuron). It opened in 2005, replacing an older structure that was badly damaged in an earthquake in 1989. The new museum was designed to blend with the park's natural landscape and resist strong earthquakes. The building can move up to three feet (91 centimeters) on [ball-bearing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ball-bearing) sliding plates and viscous fluid dampers that absorb [kinetic energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_energy).

The [Zentrum Paul Klee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zentrum_Paul_Klee) by [Renzo Piano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renzo_Piano) is an art museum near Bern, Switzerland, located next to an autoroute in the Swiss countryside. The museum blends into the landscape by taking three rolling hills made of steel and glass. One building houses the gallery (which is almost entirely underground to preserve the fragile drawings of Klee from the effects of sunlight). At the same time, the other two "hills" contain an education center and administrative offices.[^cite_note-20]

The [Centre Pompidou-Metz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre_Pompidou-Metz), in [Metz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metz), France, (2010), a branch of the [Centre Pompidou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre_Pompidou) museum of modern art in Paris, was designed by [Shigeru Ban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shigeru_Ban), a Japanese architect who won the [Pritzker Prize for Architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pritzker_Prize_for_Architecture) in 2014. The roof is the most dramatic feature of the building; it is a 90 m (300 ft) wide hexagon with a surface area of 8,000 m<sup>2</sup> (86,000 sq ft), composed of sixteen kilometers of [glued laminated timber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glued_laminated_timber), that intersect to form hexagonal wooden units resembling the cane-work pattern of a Chinese hat. The roof's geometry is irregular, featuring curves and counter-curves over the entire building, particularly the three exhibition galleries. The entire wooden structure is covered with a white fiberglass membrane, and a coating of [teflon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytetrafluoroethylene) protects from direct sunlight and allows light to pass through.

The [Louis Vuitton Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Vuitton_Foundation) by [Frank Gehry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Gehry) (2014) is the gallery of contemporary art located adjacent to the [Bois de Boulogne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bois_de_Boulogne) in Paris was opened in October 2014. Gehry described his architecture as inspired by the glass [Grand Palais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Palais) of the 1900 Paris Exposition and by the enormous glass greenhouses of the [Jardin des Serres d'Auteuil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jardin_des_Serres_d'Auteuil) near the park, built by [Jean-Camille Formigé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Camille_Formig%C3%A9) in 1894–95.[^cite_note-21] Gehry had to work within strict height and volume restrictions, which required any part of the building over two stories to be made of glass. The building is low because of the height limits, sited in an artificial lake with water cascading beneath the building. The interior gallery structures are covered in a white fiber-reinforced concrete called Ductal.[^cite_note-22] Similar in concept to Gehry's [Walt Disney Concert Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walt_Disney_Concert_Hall), the building is wrapped in curving glass panels resembling sails inflated by the wind. The glass "Sails" are made of 3,584 laminated glass panels, each one a different shape, specially curved for its place in the design.[^cite_note-23] Inside the sails is a cluster of two-story towers containing 11 galleries of different sizes, with flower garden terraces, and rooftop spaces for displays.[^cite_note-24]

The new [Whitney Museum of American Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitney_Museum_of_American_Art) in New York City by [Renzo Piano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renzo_Piano) (2015) took a very different approach from the sculptural museums of Frank Gehry. The Whitney has an industrial-looking facade and blends into the neighborhood. Michael Kimmelman, the architecture critic of *[The New York Times](30680.md)* called the building a "mishmash of styles" but noted its similarity to Piano's [Centre Pompidou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre_Pompidou) in Paris, in the way that it mixed with the public spaces around it. "Unlike so much big-name architecture," Kimmelman wrote, "it's not some weirdly shaped trophy building into which all the practical stuff of a working museum must be fitted."[^cite_note-25]

The [San Francisco Museum of Modern Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_Museum_of_Modern_Art) is actually two buildings by different architects integrated on one site; an earlier (1995) five-story postmodernist structure by the Swiss architect [Mario Botta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mario_Botta), to which has been joined a much larger ten-story white gallery by the Norwegian-based firm of [Snøhetta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sn%C3%B8hetta_\(company\)) (2016). The expanded building includes a green [living wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_wall) of native plants in San Francisco; a free ground-floor gallery with 25-foot (7.6 m) tall glass walls that will place art on view to passersby and glass skylights that flood the upper floors of offices (though not the galleries) with light. The facades clad are with lightweight panels made of [Fibre-Reinforced Plastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibre-Reinforced_Plastic). The critical reaction to the building was mixed. Roberta Smith of *The New York Times* said the building set a new standard for museums and wrote: "The new building's rippling, sloping facade, rife with subtle curves and bulges, establishes a brilliant alternative to the straight-edged boxes of traditional modernism and the rebellion against them initiated by Frank Gehry, with his computer-inspired acrobatics."[^cite_note-26] On the other hand, the critic of *The Guardian* of London compared the facade of the building to "a gigantic meringue with a hint of Ikea."[^cite_note-27]

As an architectural and historical contrast to the museums mentioned in this section, there is the earlier contemporary art and architectural work, the Artillery Sheds and 100 untitled works in mill aluminium (1979–1986) at the [Chinati Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinati_Foundation) in Marfa, Texas, by artist, and later architect, [Donald Judd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Judd), which is uniquely a museum of 'permanent installation' without temporary exhibition spaces.[^cite_note-28] Judd was a outspoken critic of architectural approaches to the contemporary museums in his lifetime.[^cite_note-29][^cite_note-30]

Three concert halls were awarded with the [European Union Prize for Contemporary Architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union_Prize_for_Contemporary_Architecture): the [Oslo Opera House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oslo_Opera_House) by [Snøhetta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sn%C3%B8hetta_\(company\)) (2008), the [Harpa concert hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harpa_\(concert_hall\)) in [Reykjavík](25798.md) by [Henning Larsen Architects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henning_Larsen_Architects) and [Olafur Eliasson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olafur_Eliasson) (2011) as well as the [Szczecin Philharmonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Szczecin_Philharmonic) by [Barozzi Veiga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barozzi_Veiga?action=edit\&redlink=1) (2014).

The [Philharmonie de Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philharmonie_de_Paris) by French architect [Jean Nouvel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Nouvel) opened in 2015. The concert hall is at [La Villette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parc_de_la_Villette), in a park at the edge of Paris devoted to museums, a music school and other cultural institutions, where its unusual shape blends with the late 20th-century [modern architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_architecture). The exterior of the building takes the form of glittering irregular cliff cut by horizontal fins which reveal a ramp leading upwards The exterior is clad in thousands of small pieces of aluminum in three different colors, from white to gray to black. A path leads up the ramp to the top of building to a terrace with a dramatic view of the peripheric highway around the city. view of the neighborhood. The hall, like the Disney Hall in Los Angeles, has Vineyard style seating, with the audience surrounding the main stage. The seating can be re-arranged in different styles depending upon the type of music performed.[^cite_note-32] When it opened, the architectural critic of the London [Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian) compared it to a space ship that had crash-landed on the outskirts of the city.[^cite_note-33]

The [Elbphilharmonie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elbphilharmonie) concert hall in [Hamburg](13467.md), Germany, by [Herzog & de Meuron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herzog_&_de_Meuron), which was inaugurated in January 2017, is the tallest inhabited building in the city, with a height of 110 meters (360 feet). The glass concert hall, which has 2100 seats in Vineyard style, is perched atop a former warehouse. One side of the concert hall building contains a hotel, while the structure on the other side above the concert hall contains forty five apartments. The [skyscraper](29485.md) (usually defined as a building over 40 stories high)[^cite_note-35] first appeared in Chicago in the 1890s, and was largely an American style in the mid 20th century, but in the 21st century skyscrapers were found in almost every large city on every continent. A new construction technology, the [framed tube structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tube_\(structure\)), was first developed in the United States in 1963 by structural engineer [Fazlur Rahman Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fazlur_Rahman_Khan) of [Skimore, Owings and Merrill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skidmore,_Owings_&_Merrill), which permitted the construction of super-tall buildings, which needed fewer interior walls, had more window space, and could better resist lateral forces, such as strong winds.[^cite_note-36]

The [Burj Khalifa](794957.md) in [Dubai](211583.md), [United Arab Emirates](69328.md) is the [tallest structure in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_and_structures), standing at 829.8 m (2,722 ft).[^cite_note-CTBUHdb-37] Construction of the Burj Khalifa began in 2004, with the exterior completed 5 years later in 2009. The primary structure is reinforced concrete. Burj Khalifa was designed by [Adrian Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrian_Smith_\(architect\)), then of [Skidmore, Owings & Merrill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skidmore,_Owings_&_Merrill) (SOM). He also was lead architect on the [Jin Mao Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jin_Mao_Tower), [Pearl River Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_River_Tower), and [Trump International Hotel & Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trump_International_Hotel_and_Tower_\(Chicago\)).

[Adrian Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrian_Smith_\(architect\)) and his own firm are the architects for the building which, in 2020, meant to replace the Burj-Khalifa as the tallest building in the world. The [Jeddah Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeddah_Tower) in [Jeddah](277718.md), Saudi Arabia, is planned to be 1,008 meters, or (3,307 ft) tall, which will make it the tallest building in the world, and the first building to be more than one kilometer in height. Construction began in 2013, and the project is scheduled to be completed in 2020.[^cite_note-constructionweekaugust13crown-38]

After the destruction of the twin towers of the [World Trade Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Trade_Center_\(1973%E2%80%932001\)) in the [September 11 terrorist attacks](5058690.md), a new trade center was designed, with the main tower designed by [David Childs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Childs) of SOM. [One World Trade Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_World_Trade_Center), opened in 2015, is 1,776 feet (541 m) tall, making it the tallest building in the western hemisphere.

In London, one of the most notable contemporary landmarks is [30 St Mary Axe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/30_St_Mary_Axe), popularly known as "The Gherkin", designed by [Norman Foster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Foster) (2004). It replaced the [London Millennium Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Millennium_Tower) – a much taller project that Foster earlier had proposed for the same site, which would have been the tallest building in Europe, but was so tall that it interfered with the flight pattern for [Heathrow Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heathrow_Airport). The steel framework of the Gherkin is integrated into the glass facade.[^cite_note-39]

The tallest building in Poland and in the [European Union](9317.md) (as of 2023) is [Varso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varso) in [Warsaw](32908.md), designed by [Norman Foster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Foster). Completed in 2022, it is 310-meter (1,020-foot) tall.

The tallest building in Moscow is the [Federation Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federation_Tower), designed by the Russian architect [Sergei Tchoban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergei_Tchoban) with Peter Schweger. Completed in 2017, with a height 373 meters, it surpassed [Mercury City Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_City_Tower), another skyscraper in Moscow, when it was built as the tallest building in Europe.

The tallest building in China as of 2015 is the [Shanghai Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanghai_Tower) by the U.S. architectural and design firm of [Gensler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gensler). It is 632-meter (2,073-foot) tall, with 127 floors, making it in 2016 the second-tallest building in the world. It also features the fastest elevators, which reach a speed of 20.5 meters per second (67 feet per second; 74 kilometers per hour).[^cite_note-Inhabitat-40][^cite_note-CNNElevators-41]

Most skyscrapers are designed to express modernity; the most notable exception is the [Abraj Al Bait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraj_Al_Bait), a complex of seven skyscraper hotels build by the government of [Saudi Arabia](349303.md) to house pilgrims coming the holy shrine of [Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecca). The centerpiece of the group is the Makkah Palace Clock Tower Hotel, with a gothic revival tower; it was the fourth-tallest building in the world in 2016, 581.1 meters (1,906 feet) high.

A tendency in contemporary residential architecture, particularly in the rebuilding of older neighborhoods in large cities, is the luxury condominium tower, with very expensive apartments for sale designed by "starchitects", that is, internationally famous architects. These buildings frequently have little relationship with the architecture of their neighborhood, but stand like signature works of their architects.

[Daniel Libeskind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Libeskind) (born 1946), was born in Poland and studied, taught and practiced architecture in the United States. In 2016 he was professor of architecture at [UCLA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UCLA) in Los Angeles, He is known as much for his writings as his architecture; he was a founder of the movement called [Deconstructivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deconstructivism_\(architecture\)). Best known for his museums, he also constructed a notable complex of residential apartment buildings in [Singapore](27318.md) (2011) and [The Ascent at Roebling's Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ascent_at_Roebling's_Bridge) a 22-story apartment building in [Covington, Kentucky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covington,_Kentucky) (2008). The name of the latter is taken from the [Roebling Suspension Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roebling_Suspension_Bridge) nearby on the [Ohio River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio_River), but the structure of the building of luxury condominiums is extremely contemporary, sloping upward like the bridge cables to a peak, with a sharp edge and leaning slightly outward as the building rises.

One cheerful feature of contemporary residential architecture is color; [Bernard Tschumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Tschumi) uses colored ceramic tiles on facades as well as unusual forms to make his buildings stand out. One example is the [Blue Condominium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Condominium) in [New York City](645042.md) (2007).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDe_Bure2015208-42]

Another contemporary tendency is the conversion of industrial buildings into mixed residential communities. An example is the [Gasometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gasometer,_Vienna) in Vienna, a group of four massive brick gas production towers constructed at the end of the 19th century. They have been transformed into a mixed residential, office and commercial complex, completed between 1999 and 2001. Some residences are located inside the towers, and others are in new buildings attached to them. The upper floors are devoted to housing units the middle floors to offices, and the ground floors to entertainment and shopping malls. with sky bridges connecting the shopping mall levels. Each tower was built by a prominent architect the participants were [Jean Nouvel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Nouvel), [Coop Himmelblau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coop_Himmelblau), Manfred Wehldorn and [Wilhelm Holzbauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Holzbauer). The historic exterior walls of the towers were preserved.[^cite_note-43]

The [Isbjerget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isbjerget), Danish for "iceberg", in [Aarhus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aarhus), Denmark (2013), is a group of four buildings with 210 apartments, both rented and owned, for residents with a range of incomes, located on the waterfront of a former industrial port in Denmark. The complex was designed by the Danish firms [CEBRA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CEBRA) and [JDS Architects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JDS_Architects), French architect Louis Paillard and the Dutch firm SeARCH, and was financed by the Danish pension fund. The buildings are designed so that all the units, even those in the back, have a view of sea. The design and color of the buildings is inspired by [icebergs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icebergs). The buildings are clad in white [terrazzo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrazzo) and have balconies made of blue glass.

"coated with waterproof polyurethane and flame retardants" with two-inch gaps between them so that light can filter inside.[^cite_note-McGuigan2013-52]

The Swiss architects [Jacques Herzog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Herzog) and [Pierre de Meuron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_de_Meuron) designed the [Allianz Arena](1363716.md) in Munich, Germany, completed in 2005. It seats 75,000 spectators. The structure is wrapped in 2,874 [ETFE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETFE)-foil air panels that are kept inflated with dry air; each panel can be independently illuminated red, white, or blue. When illuminated, the stadium is visible from the Austrian Alps, fifty miles (80 kilometers) away.[^cite_note-53]

Among the most prestigious projects and best-known projects in contemporary architecture are the stadiums for the [Olympic Games](22576.md), whose architects are chosen by highly publicized international competitions. The [Beijing National Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beijing_National_Stadium), built for the 2008 Games and popularly known as the Bird's Nest because of its intricate exterior framework, was designed by the Swiss firm of [Herzog & de Meuron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herzog_&_de_Meuron), with Chinese architect [Li Xinggang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Li_Xinggang). It was designed to seat 91,000 spectators, and when constructed had a retractable roof, since removed. Like many contemporary buildings, it is actually two structures; a concrete bowl in which the spectators sit, surrounded at distance of fifty feet by a glass and steel framework. The exterior "Bird's nest" design was inspired by the pattern of Chinese ceramics. The stadium when completed was the largest enclosed space in the world, and was also the largest steel structure, with 26 kilometers of unwrapped steel.[^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55]

The [National Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Stadium_\(Kaohsiung\)) in [Kaohsiung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaohsiung), [Taiwan](25734.md) by Japanese architect [Toyo Ito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toyo_Ito) (2009), is the form of a dragon. Its other distinctive feature is the array of solar panels that cover almost all of the exterior, providing most of the energy needed by the complex.

Government buildings, once almost universally serious and sober looking, usually in variations of the school of [neoclassical architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoclassical_architecture), began to appear in more sculptural and even whimsical forms. One of the most dramatic examples was the [London City Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_Hall,_Southwark) by [Norman Foster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Foster) (2002), the headquarters of the [Greater London Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_London_Authority). The unusual egg-like building design was intended to reduce the amount of exposed wall and to save energy, though the results have not entirely met expectations.[^cite_note-56] One unusual feature is a helical stairway that spirals from the lobby up to the top of the building.

Some new government buildings, such as the [Parliament House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_House_\(Malta\)), Valletta, Malta by [Renzo Piano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renzo_Piano) (2015) created controversy because of the contrast between their style and the historic architecture around them.[^cite_note-57]

Most new government buildings attempt to express solidity and seriousness; an exception is the [Port Authority (*Havenhuis*)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Authority_Building_\(Antwerp\)) in [Antwerp](32149462.md), Belgium by [Zaha Hadid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaha_Hadid) (2016), where a ship-like structure of glass and steel on a white concrete perch seems to have landed atop the old port building constructed in 1922. The faceted glass structure also resembles a diamond, a symbol of Antwerp's role as the major market of diamonds in Europe. It was one of the last works of Hadid, who died in 2016.

The shopping malls are the elephants of commercial architecture, massive structures which combine retail stores, food outlets, and entertainment under one roof. The largest in area (though not in retail space, since much of the mall is devoted to entertainment and public space) is the [Dubai Mall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dubai_Mall) in the [United Arab Emirates](69328.md), designed by [DP Architects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DP_Architects) of Singapore and opened in (2008). Then, outside lie both the highest fountain and the tallest building in the world.

In competition with shopping malls are downtown department stores and shops of individual designer brands. These buildings are traditionally designed to attract attention and to express the modernity of the products they sell. A notable example is the [Selfridge's Department Store](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selfridges_Building,_Birmingham) in Birmingham, England, a department store designed by the firm of [Future Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Future_Systems), founded in 1979 by [Jan Kaplický](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Kaplick%C3%BD) (1937–2009). Designer brand shops try make their logo visible and to set themselves apart from department stores. One notable example is the [Louis Vuitton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Vuitton) store in the [Ginza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ginza) district of Tokyo, with a new facade designed by Japanese studio of Jun Aoki and Associates with a patterned and perforated shell based on the brand's logo.[^cite_note-61]

Spain for an international exposition there in 2008. The bridge, which also served as an exposition hall, is constructed of concrete reinforced with an external layer of [fiberglass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiberglass) in different shades of gray. Since the event closed, the bridge has been used to host expositions and shows.

Some smaller new bridges also offer simple but very innovative designs. The [Gateshead Millennium Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gateshead_Millennium_Bridge) in [Newcastle upon Tyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newcastle_upon_Tyne), England, (2004) designed by [Michel Virlogeux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Virlogeux), to enable pedestrians and cyclists to cross the [Tyne River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyne_River), tilts to one side to permit boats to pass beneath.

A growing tendency in the 21st century is [eco-architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eco-architecture), also termed [sustainable architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_architecture); buildings with features which conserve heat and energy, and sometimes produce their own energy through solar cells and windmills, and use solar heat to generate [solar hot water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_hot_water). They also may be built with their own [wastewater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wastewater) treatment and sometimes [rainwater harvesting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainwater_harvesting) Some buildings integrate gardens [green walls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_walls) and [green roofs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_roofs) into their structures. Other features of eco-architecture include the use of wood and recycled materials. There are several [green building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_building) certification programs, the best-known of which is the [Leadership in Energy and Environmental Design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leadership_in_Energy_and_Environmental_Design), or LEED rating, which measure the environmental impact of buildings.

Many urban skyscrapers such as [30 Saint Mary Axe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/30_Saint_Mary_Axe) in London use a double skin of glass to conserve energy. The double skin and curved shape of the building creates differences in air pressure which help keep the building cooler in summer and warmer in winter, reducing the need for air conditioning.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHopkins2014210-66]

[BedZED](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BedZED), designed by British architect [Bill Dunster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Dunster), is an entire community of eighty-two homes in [Hackbridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hackbridge), near London, built according to eco-architecture principles. Houses face south to take advantage of sunlight and have triple-glazed windows for insulation, a significant portion of the energy comes from solar panels, rainwater is collected and reused, and automobiles are discouraged. BedZED successfully reduced electricity usage by 45 percent and hot water usage by 81 percent of the borough average in 2010, though a successful system for producing heat by burning wood chips proved elusive and difficult.[^cite_note-BDR_revisit_2010-67]

The [CaixaForum Madrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CaixaForum_Madrid) is a museum and cultural center in Paseo del Prado 36, Madrid, by the Swiss architects [Herzog & de Meuron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herzog_&_de_Meuron), built between 2001 and 2007, is an example of both green architecture and recycling. The main structure is an abandoned brick electric power station, with new floors constructed on top. The new floors are encased in oxidized cast iron, which has a rusty red color as the brick of the old power station below it. The building next to it features a [green wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_wall) designed by French botanist [Patrick Blanc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Blanc). The red of the top floors contrast with the plants on the wall, while the green wall harmonizes with the botanical garden next door to the cultural center.[^cite_note-68]

Unusual materials are sometimes recycled for use in eco-architecture; they include denim from old blue jeans for insulation, and panels made from paper flakes, baked earth, [flax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flax), [sisal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sisal), or [coconut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coconut), and particularly fast-growing [bamboo](39029.md). Lumber and stone from demolished buildings are often reclaimed and reused for flooring, while hardware, windows and other details from older buildings are reused.
