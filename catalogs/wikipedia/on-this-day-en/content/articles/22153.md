# Nuclear power

"Atomic power" redirects here. For the film, see [Atomic Power (film)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_Power_\(film\)).

For countries with nuclear weapons, see [List of states with nuclear weapons](400821.md).



The [Leibstadt Nuclear Power Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leibstadt_Nuclear_Power_Plant) in Switzerland



Per capita nuclear power production

**Nuclear power** is the use of [nuclear reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_reaction) to produce [electricity](9550.md). Nuclear power can be obtained from [nuclear fission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fission), [nuclear decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_decay) and [nuclear fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fusion) reactions. Presently, the vast majority of electricity from nuclear power is produced by nuclear *fission* of [uranium](31743.md) and [plutonium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutonium) in [nuclear power plants](207347.md). Nuclear *decay* processes are used in niche applications such as [radioisotope thermoelectric generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioisotope_thermoelectric_generator) in some space probes such as *[Voyager 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyager_2)*.[^cite_note-1] Reactors producing controlled [*fusion* power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fusion_power) have been operated since 1958 but have yet to generate net power and are not expected to be commercially available in the near future.[^cite_note-2]

The first [nuclear power plant](207347.md) was built in the 1950s. The global installed nuclear capacity grew to 100 GW in the late 1970s, and then expanded during the 1980s, reaching 300 GW by 1990. The 1979 [Three Mile Island accident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Mile_Island_accident) in the [United States](3434750.md) and the 1986 [Chernobyl disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernobyl_disaster) in the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) resulted in increased regulation and public opposition to nuclear power plants. Nuclear power plants supplied 2,602 [terawatt hours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terawatt_hour) (TWh) of electricity in 2023, equivalent to about 9% of [global electricity generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_electricity_generation),[^cite_note-PerformanceReport-3] and were the second largest [low-carbon power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-carbon_power) source after [hydroelectricity](381399.md). As of November 2025, there are [416 civilian fission reactors in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_nuclear_reactors), with overall capacity of 376 GW,[^cite_note-\:3-4] 63 under construction and 87 planned, with a combined capacity of 66 GW and 84 GW, respectively.[^cite_note-WNA-5] The United States has the largest fleet of nuclear reactors, generating almost 800 TWh per year with an average [capacity factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capacity_factor) of 92%. The average global capacity factor is 89%.[^cite_note-\:3-4] Most new reactors under construction are [generation III reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generation_III_reactor) in Asia.

Nuclear power is a safe and sustainable energy source that reduces carbon emissions. Nuclear power generation results in one of the lowest levels of fatalities per unit of energy generated compared to other energy sources. One study estimated that each nuclear plant built could have saved 800,000 life years due to averted [air pollution](10934212.md) from fossil fueled power plants.[^cite_note-6] Coal, petroleum, [natural gas](22131.md) and hydroelectricity have each caused more fatalities per unit of energy due to air pollution and [accidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_accidents). Nuclear power plants also emit no [greenhouse gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_gases) and result in less life-cycle carbon emissions than common sources of [renewable energy](25784.md). The radiological hazards associated with nuclear power are the primary motivations of the [anti-nuclear movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-nuclear_movement), which contends that nuclear power poses threats to people and the environment, citing the potential for [accidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_and_radiation_accidents_and_incidents) like the [Fukushima nuclear disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukushima_nuclear_disaster) in Japan on March 11, 2011, and is too expensive to deploy when compared to alternative [sustainable energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_energy) sources.

## History

Main articles: [History of nuclear power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_nuclear_power) and [Timeline of nuclear power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_nuclear_power)

### Origins



The first light bulbs ever lit by electricity generated by nuclear power at [EBR-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EBR-1) at [Argonne National Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argonne_National_Laboratory)-West (now the [Idaho National Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idaho_National_Laboratory)), December 20, 1951.

[^cite_note-7]

The process of [nuclear fission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fission) was discovered in 1938 after over four decades of work on the science of [radioactivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactivity) and the elaboration of new [nuclear physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_physics) that described the components of [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom). Soon after the discovery of the fission process, it was realized that neutrons released by a fissioning nucleus could, under the right conditions, induce fissions in nearby nuclei, thus initiating a [self-sustaining chain reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_chain_reaction).[^cite_note-Inside_the_Atomic_Patent_Office-8] Once this was experimentally confirmed in 1939, scientists in many countries petitioned their governments for support for nuclear fission research, just on the cusp of [World War II](32927.md), in order to develop a [nuclear weapon](21785.md).[^cite_note-9]

In the United States, these research efforts led to the creation of the first human-made [nuclear reactor](22151.md), the [Chicago Pile-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Pile-1) under the [Stagg Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stagg_Field) stadium at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago), which achieved [criticality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criticality_\(status\)) on December 2, 1942. The reactor's development was part of the [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project), the [Allied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II) effort to create [atomic bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_bombs) during World War II. It led to the building of larger single-purpose [production reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Production_reactor) for the production of [weapons-grade plutonium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weapons-grade_plutonium) for use in the first nuclear weapons. The United States tested the first nuclear weapon in July 1945, the [Trinity test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_test), and the [atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_bombings_of_Hiroshima_and_Nagasaki) happened one month later.[^cite_note-10]



The launching ceremony of [USS *Nautilus*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Nautilus_\(SSN-571\)) January 1954. In 1958 it would become the first vessel to reach the [North Pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Pole).

[^cite_note-11]



The [Calder Hall nuclear power station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calder_Hall_nuclear_power_station) in the United Kingdom, the world's first commercial nuclear power station

Despite the military nature of the first nuclear devices, there was strong optimism in the 1940s and 1950s that nuclear power could provide cheap and endless energy.[^cite_note-12] Electricity was generated for the first time by a nuclear reactor on December 20, 1951, at the [EBR-I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EBR-I) experimental station near [Arco, Idaho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arco,_Idaho), which initially produced about 100 kW.[^cite_note-13][^cite_note-14] In 1953, American President [Dwight Eisenhower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwight_Eisenhower) gave his "[Atoms for Peace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atoms_for_Peace)" speech at the [United Nations](31769.md), emphasizing the need to develop "peaceful" uses of nuclear power quickly. This was followed by the [Atomic Energy Act of 1954](4280305.md) which allowed rapid declassification of U.S. reactor technology and encouraged development by the private sector.[^cite_note-usnrc_history-15]

### First power generation

The first organization to develop practical nuclear power was the [U.S. Navy](20518076.md), with the [S1W reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S1W_reactor) for the purpose of propelling [submarines](28825.md) and [aircraft carriers](2219.md). The first nuclear-powered submarine, [USS *Nautilus*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Nautilus_\(SSN-571\)), was put to sea in January 1954.[^cite_note-iaeapdf-16][^cite_note-17] The S1W reactor was a [pressurized water reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressurized_water_reactor). This design was chosen because it was simpler, more compact, and easier to operate compared to alternative designs, thus more suitable to be used in submarines. This decision would result in the PWR being the reactor of choice also for power generation, thus having a lasting impact on the civilian electricity market in the years to come.[^cite_note-18]

On June 27, 1954, the [Obninsk Nuclear Power Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obninsk_Nuclear_Power_Plant) in the [USSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USSR) became the world's first nuclear power plant to generate electricity for a [power grid](20344155.md), producing around 5 megawatts of electric power.[^cite_note-IAEANews-19] The world's first commercial nuclear power station, [Calder Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calder_Hall) at Windscale, England was connected to the national power grid on 27 August 1956. In common with a number of other [generation I reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generation_I_reactor), the plant had the dual purpose of producing [electricity](9550.md) and [plutonium-239](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutonium-239), the latter for the nascent [nuclear weapons program in Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_and_the_United_Kingdom).[^cite_note-20]

### Expansion and first opposition

The total global installed nuclear capacity initially rose relatively quickly, rising from less than 1 [gigawatt](21347693.md) (GW) in 1960 to 100 GW in the late 1970s.[^cite_note-iaeapdf-16] During the 1970s and 1980s rising economic costs (related to extended construction times largely due to regulatory changes and pressure-group litigation)[^cite_note-Bernard_L._Cohen_1990-21] and falling fossil fuel prices made nuclear power plants then under construction less attractive. In the 1980s in the U.S. and 1990s in Europe [electricity liberalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electricity_liberalization) made investment in new nuclear power plants (which have high initial capital costs but low operating costs) less desirable than [natural gas plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas_plant) (which had little political opposition, were faster to build, and had low fuel prices).[^cite_note-iaeapdf-16]

The [1973 oil crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_oil_crisis) had a significant effect on countries, such as [France](5843419.md) and [Japan](15573.md), which had relied more heavily on oil for electric generation to invest in nuclear power.[^cite_note-22] France would construct 25 nuclear power plants over the next 15 years,[^cite_note-palfreman-23][^cite_note-de_preneuf-24] and as of 2019, 71% of French electricity was generated by nuclear power, the highest percentage for any nation in the world.[^cite_note-\:0-25]

Some local opposition to nuclear power emerged in the United States in the early 1960s.[^cite_note-well-26] In the late 1960s, some members of the scientific community began to express pointed concerns.[^cite_note-wolfgang-27] These [anti-nuclear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-nuclear) concerns related to [nuclear accidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_accidents), [nuclear proliferation](22158.md), [nuclear terrorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_terrorism) and [radioactive waste disposal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-level_radioactive_waste_management).[^cite_note-bm-28] In the early 1970s, there were large protests about a proposed nuclear power plant in [Wyhl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wyhl), Germany. The project was cancelled in 1975. The anti-nuclear success at Wyhl inspired opposition to nuclear power in other parts of Europe and North America.[^cite_note-pub-29][^cite_note-got-30]

By the mid-1970s [anti-nuclear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-nuclear) activism gained a wider appeal and influence, and nuclear power started to become an issue of major public protest.[^cite_note-jimfalk-31][^cite_note-eleven-32] In some countries, the [nuclear power debate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_power_debate) "reached an intensity unprecedented in the history of technology controversies".[^cite_note-marcuse.org-33] The increased public hostility to nuclear power led to a longer license procurement process, more regulations and increased requirements for safety equipment, which made new construction much more expensive.[^cite_note-phyast.pitt.edu-34][^cite_note-35] In the United States, over [120 reactor proposals were ultimately cancelled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cancelled_nuclear_reactors_in_the_United_States)[^cite_note-36] and the construction of new reactors ground to a halt.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-37] The 1979 [accident at Three Mile Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Mile_Island_accident) played a major part in the reduction in the number of new plant constructions in many countries.[^cite_note-wolfgang-27]

### Chernobyl and renaissance



The town of [Pripyat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pripyat_\(city\)) abandoned since 1986, with the Chernobyl plant and the [Chernobyl New Safe Confinement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernobyl_New_Safe_Confinement) arch in the distance



[Olkiluoto 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olkiluoto_3) under construction in 2009. It was the first [EPR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EPR_\(nuclear_reactor\)), a modernized PWR design, to start construction.&#x20;

During the 1980s one new nuclear reactor started up every 17 days on average.[^cite_note-38] By the end of the decade, global installed nuclear capacity reached 300 GW. Since the late 1980s, new capacity additions slowed significantly, with the installed nuclear capacity reaching 365 GW in 2005.[^cite_note-39]

The 1986 [Chernobyl disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernobyl_disaster) in the [USSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USSR), involving an [RBMK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RBMK) reactor, altered the development of nuclear power and led to a greater focus on meeting international safety and regulatory standards.[^cite_note-40] It is considered the worst nuclear disaster in history both in total casualties, with 56 direct deaths, and financially, with the cleanup and the cost estimated at 18 billion [Rbls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_rouble) (US\$68 billion in 2019, adjusted for inflation).[^cite_note-OECD02-Ch2-41][^cite_note-GorbachevBoC-42] The international organization to promote safety awareness and the professional development of [operators in nuclear facilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reactor_operator), the [World Association of Nuclear Operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Association_of_Nuclear_Operators) (WANO), was created as a direct outcome of the 1986 Chernobyl accident. The Chernobyl disaster played a major part in the reduction in the number of new plant constructions in the following years.[^cite_note-wolfgang-27] Influenced by these events, Italy voted against nuclear power in a 1987 referendum,[^cite_note-43] becoming the first major economy to completely phase out nuclear power in 1990.[^cite_note-44]

In the early 2000s, nuclear energy was expecting a [nuclear renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_renaissance), an increase in the construction of new reactors, due to concerns about [carbon dioxide emissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide_emissions).[^cite_note-\:1-45] During this period, newer [generation III reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generation_III_reactor), such as the [EPR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EPR_\(nuclear_reactor\)) began construction.[^cite_note-46]

### Fukushima accident

Prospects of a nuclear renaissance were delayed by another nuclear accident.[^cite_note-\:1-45][^cite_note-carbonbrief_2016-47] The 2011 [Fukushima Daiichi nuclear accident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukushima_Daiichi_nuclear_accident) was caused by the [Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C5%8Dhoku_earthquake_and_tsunami), one of the largest earthquakes ever recorded. The [Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Plant](11986472.md) suffered three core meltdowns due to failure of the emergency cooling system for lack of electricity supply. This resulted in the most serious civilian nuclear accident since the 1986 Chernobyl disaster.[^cite_note-48]

The accident prompted a re-examination of [nuclear safety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_safety) and [nuclear energy policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_energy_policy) in many countries.[^cite_note-sciamer2011-49] Germany approved plans to close all its reactors by 2022, and many other countries reviewed their nuclear power programs.[^cite_note-50][^cite_note-news.xinhuanet.com-51] Following the disaster, Japan shut down all of its nuclear power reactors, some of them permanently, and in 2015 began a gradual process to restart the remaining 40 reactors, following safety checks and based on revised criteria for operations and public approval.[^cite_note-52]

In 2022, the Japanese government, under the leadership of Prime Minister [Fumio Kishida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fumio_Kishida), declared that 10 more nuclear power plants were to be reopened since the 2011 disaster.[^cite_note-53] Kishida is also pushing for research and construction of new safer nuclear plants to safeguard Japanese consumers from the fluctuating price of the fossil fuel market and reduce Japan's greenhouse gas emissions.[^cite_note-auto-54] Kishida intends to have Japan become a significant exporter of nuclear energy and technology to developing countries around the world.[^cite_note-auto-54]

### Current prospects

By 2015, the IAEA's outlook for nuclear energy had become more promising, recognizing the importance of low-carbon generation for mitigating [climate change](5042951.md).[^cite_note-55] As of 2015, the global trend was for new nuclear power stations coming online to be balanced by the number of old plants being retired.[^cite_note-56] In 2016, the [U.S. Energy Information Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Energy_Information_Administration) projected for its "base case" that world nuclear power generation would increase from 2,344 [terawatt hours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terawatt_hour) (TWh) in 2012 to 4,500 TWh in 2040. Most of the predicted increase was expected to be in Asia.[^cite_note-57] As of 2018, there were over 150 nuclear reactors planned including 50 under construction.[^cite_note-58] In January 2019, China had 45 reactors in operation, 13 under construction, and planned to build 43 more, which would make it the world's largest generator of nuclear electricity.[^cite_note-china19-59] As of 2021, 17 reactors were reported to be under construction. Its share of electricity from nuclear power was 5% in 2019.[^cite_note-dwfrance-60]

In October 2021, the Japanese cabinet approved the new Plan for Electricity Generation to 2030 prepared by the Agency for Natural Resources and Energy (ANRE) and an advisory committee, following public consultation. The nuclear target for 2030 requires the restart of another ten reactors. Prime Minister [Fumio Kishida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fumio_Kishida) in July 2022 announced that the country should consider building advanced reactors and extending operating licences beyond 60 years.[^cite_note-61]

As of 2022, with world oil and gas prices on the rise, while Germany is restarting its coal plants to deal with loss of Russian gas that it needs to supplement its *[Energiewende](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energiewende)*,[^cite_note-62] many other countries have announced ambitious plans to reinvigorate ageing nuclear generating capacity with new investments. French President [Emmanuel Macron](43671127.md) announced his intention to build six new reactors in coming decades, placing nuclear at the heart of France's drive for [carbon neutrality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_neutrality) by 2050.[^cite_note-63] Meanwhile, in the United States, the [Department of Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Energy), in collaboration with commercial entities, [TerraPower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TerraPower) and [X-energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-energy), is planning on building two different advanced nuclear reactors by 2027, with further plans for nuclear implementation in its long term green energy and energy security goals.[^cite_note-64]

During the 2020s, nuclear industry interest in [small modular reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_modular_reactor) (SMR) has grown enormously, on the expectation that smaller reactors will permit faster deployment with lower financing costs during the build, and lower cost from higher-volume factory manufacturing of the smaller major components, more than compensating for the economies of scale of traditional large reactors. In 2026, [Natixis Corporate and Investment Banking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BPCE_Group) reported that the SMR sector was still far from commercial reality and was entering a phase where engineering ambition must confront regulatory complexity, financing realities and industrial execution.[^cite_note-home-20260602-65]

Nuclear power plants are [thermal power stations](2549543.md) that generate electricity by using the [thermal energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_energy) released from [nuclear fission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fission). A fission nuclear power plant is generally composed of: a [nuclear reactor](22151.md), in which the nuclear reactions generating heat take place; a cooling system, which removes the heat from inside the reactor; a [steam turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_turbine), which converts the heat into [mechanical energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_energy); an [electric generator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_generator), which converts the mechanical energy into electrical energy.[^cite_note-WNAnuclearreactorbasics-67]

When a [neutron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron) hits the nucleus of a [uranium-235](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-235) or [plutonium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutonium) atom, it can split the nucleus into two smaller nuclei, which is a nuclear fission reaction. The reaction releases energy and neutrons. The released neutrons can hit other uranium or plutonium nuclei, causing new fission reactions, which release more energy and more neutrons. This is called a [chain reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_chain_reaction). In most commercial reactors, the reaction rate is contained by [control rods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_rod) that absorb excess neutrons. The controllability of nuclear reactors depends on the fact that a small fraction of neutrons resulting from fission are [delayed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delayed_neutron). The time delay between the fission and the release of the neutrons slows changes in reaction rates and gives time for moving the control rods to adjust the reaction rate.[^cite_note-WNAnuclearreactorbasics-67][^cite_note-68]

## Fuel cycle



The nuclear fuel cycle begins when uranium is mined, enriched, and manufactured into nuclear fuel (1), which is delivered to a [nuclear power plant](207347.md). After use, the spent fuel is delivered to a reprocessing plant (2) or to a final repository (3). In [nuclear reprocessing](197845.md), 95% of spent fuel can potentially be recycled to be returned to use in a power plant (4).

Main articles: [Nuclear fuel cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fuel_cycle) and [Integrated Nuclear Fuel Cycle Information System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_Nuclear_Fuel_Cycle_Information_System)

The [nuclear fuel cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fuel_cycle) starts with [uranium mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_mining). The [uranium ore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_ore) is then converted into a compact [ore concentrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ore_concentrate), known as [yellowcake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellowcake) (U<sub>3</sub>O<sub>8</sub>), to facilitate transport.[^cite_note-nrc_fuel-69] Fission reactors generally need [uranium-235](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-235), a [fissile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fissile) [isotope of uranium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotope_of_uranium). The concentration of uranium-235 in natural uranium is low (about 0.7%). Some reactors can use this natural uranium as fuel, depending on their [neutron economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_economy). These reactors generally have graphite or [heavy water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_water) moderators. For light water reactors, the most common type of reactor, this concentration is too low, and it must be increased by a process called [uranium enrichment](37555.md).[^cite_note-nrc_fuel-69] In civilian light water reactors, uranium is typically enriched to 3.5–5% uranium-235.[^cite_note-wna_fuel-70] The uranium is then generally converted into [uranium oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_oxide) (UO<sub>2</sub>), a ceramic, that is then compressively [sintered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sintered) into fuel pellets, a stack of which forms [fuel rods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_rod) of the proper composition and geometry for the particular reactor.[^cite_note-wna_fuel-70]

After some time in the reactor, the fuel have reduced fissile material and increased fission products, until its use becomes impractical.[^cite_note-wna_fuel-70] At this point, the spent fuel will be moved to a [spent fuel pool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spent_fuel_pool) which provides cooling for the thermal heat and shielding for ionizing radiation. After several months or years, the spent fuel is radioactively and thermally cool enough to be moved to dry storage casks or reprocessed.[^cite_note-wna_fuel-70]

### Uranium resources

Main articles: [Uranium market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_market), [Uranium mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_mining), and [Energy development § Nuclear](533487.md#Nuclear)



Proportions of the isotopes [uranium-238](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-238) (blue) and uranium-235 (red) found in natural uranium and in [enriched uranium](37555.md) for different applications. Light water reactors use 3–5% enriched uranium, while [CANDU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CANDU) reactors work with natural uranium.

[Uranium](31743.md) is a fairly common [element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_element) in the Earth's crust: it is approximately as common as [tin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tin) or [germanium](12242.md), and is about 40 times more common than [silver](27119.md).[^cite_note-71] Uranium is present in trace concentrations in most rocks, dirt, and ocean water, but is generally economically extracted only where it is present in relatively high concentrations. As of 2011 the world's known resources of uranium, economically recoverable at the arbitrary price ceiling of US\$130/kg, were enough to last for between 70 and 100 years in current reactors.[^cite_note-72]

Light water reactors (which account for almost all operational reactors) make relatively inefficient use of nuclear fuel, mostly using only the very rare uranium-235 isotope.[^cite_note-wna-wmitnfc-73] Limited uranium-235 supply may inhibit substantial expansion with the current nuclear technology.[^cite_note-sol1-74] [Nuclear reprocessing](197845.md) can make this waste reusable, and newer reactors also achieve a more efficient use of the available resources than older ones.[^cite_note-wna-wmitnfc-73] More advanced nuclear reactor technologies, such as [fast reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_reactor), can use much more of the natural uranium, use current nuclear waste as fuel, as well as creating new fuel out of non-fissile material (see [breeder reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breeder_reactor)). With a pure fast reactor fuel cycle with a burn up of all the uranium and [actinides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actinide) (which presently make up the most hazardous substances in nuclear waste), there is an estimated 160,000 years worth of uranium in total conventional resources and phosphate ore at the price of 60–100 US\$/kg.[^cite_note-75] These advanced fuel cycles and nuclear reprocessing are currently not widely used because the price of uranium is very low compared to the cost of nuclear plants, so it's more economically viable to mine new uranium rather than reprocess it. Nuclear reprocessing also carries higher risk of nuclear proliferation, as it separates material that can be used to manufacture nuclear weapons.[^cite_note-future1-76][^cite_note-civlib-77][^cite_note-detect-78]

Unconventional uranium resources also exist. Uranium is naturally present in seawater at a concentration of about 3 [micrograms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microgram) per liter,[^cite_note-books.google.ie-79] with 4.4 billion tons of uranium considered present in seawater at any time.[^cite_note-gepr.org-80] In 2014 it was suggested that it would be economically competitive to produce nuclear fuel from seawater if the process was implemented at large scale.[^cite_note-81] Over geological timescales, uranium extracted on an industrial scale from seawater would be replenished by both river erosion of rocks and the natural process of uranium [dissolved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaching_\(metallurgy\)) from the surface area of the ocean floor, both of which maintain the [solubility equilibria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solubility_equilibrium) of seawater concentration at a stable level.[^cite_note-gepr.org-80] Some commentators have argued that this strengthens the case for [nuclear power to be considered a renewable energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_power_as_sustainable_energy).[^cite_note-82]

### Waste

Main article: [Nuclear waste](37257.md)



Typical composition of [uranium dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_dioxide) fuel before and after approximately three years in the [once-through nuclear fuel cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Once-through_nuclear_fuel_cycle) of a [LWR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LWR)

[^cite_note-jaif-83]

The normal operation of nuclear power plants and facilities produce [radioactive waste](37257.md), or nuclear waste. This type of waste is also produced during plant decommissioning. There are two broad categories of nuclear waste: low-level waste and high-level waste.[^cite_note-nrc_waste-84] The first has low radioactivity and includes contaminated items such as clothing, which poses limited threat. High-level waste is mainly the spent fuel from nuclear reactors, which is very radioactive and must be cooled and then safely disposed of or reprocessed.[^cite_note-nrc_waste-84]

#### High-level waste

Main articles: [High-level waste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-level_waste) and [Spent nuclear fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spent_nuclear_fuel)



Activity of spent UOx fuel in comparison to the activity of natural [uranium ore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_ore) over time

[^cite_note-m.phys.org-85]

[^cite_note-jaif-83]



[Dry cask storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dry_cask_storage) vessels storing spent nuclear fuel assemblies

The most important waste stream from nuclear power reactors is [spent nuclear fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spent_nuclear_fuel), which is considered [high-level waste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-level_waste) (HLW). For [light water reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_water_reactor) (LWRs), spent fuel is typically composed of 95% uranium, 4% [fission products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fission_product), and about 1% [transuranic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transuranic) [actinides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actinides) (mostly [plutonium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutonium), [neptunium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neptunium) and [americium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americium)).[^cite_note-86] The fission products are responsible for the bulk of the short-term radioactivity, whereas the plutonium and other transuranics are responsible for the bulk of the long-term radioactivity.[^cite_note-87]

High-level waste must be stored isolated from the [biosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biosphere) with sufficient shielding so as to limit radiation exposure. After being removed from the reactors, used fuel bundles are stored for six to ten years in [spent fuel pools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spent_fuel_pool), which provide cooling and shielding against radiation. After that, the fuel is cool enough that it can be safely transferred to [dry cask storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dry_cask_storage).[^cite_note-88] The radioactivity decreases exponentially with time, such that it will have decreased by 99.5% after 100 years.[^cite_note-89] The more intensely radioactive short-lived [fission products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fission_products) (SLFPs) decay into stable elements in approximately 300 years, and after about 100,000 years, the spent fuel becomes less radioactive than natural uranium ore.[^cite_note-jaif-83][^cite_note-90]

Commonly suggested methods to isolate long-lived fission product (LLFP) waste from the biosphere include separation and [transmutation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_transmutation),[^cite_note-jaif-83] [synroc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synroc) treatments, or deep geological storage.[^cite_note-91][^cite_note-92][^cite_note-93][^cite_note-94]

[Thermal-neutron reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal-neutron_reactor), which presently constitute the majority of the world fleet, cannot burn up the [reactor grade plutonium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reactor_grade_plutonium) that is generated during the reactor operation. This limits the life of nuclear fuel to a few years. In some countries, such as the United States, spent fuel is classified in its entirety as a nuclear waste.[^cite_note-95] In other countries, such as France, it is largely reprocessed to produce a partially recycled fuel, known as mixed oxide fuel or [MOX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOX). For spent fuel that does not undergo reprocessing, the most concerning isotopes are the medium-lived [transuranic elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transuranic_element), which are led by reactor-grade plutonium (with a [half-life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-life) 24,000 years).[^cite_note-96] Some proposed reactor designs, such as the [integral fast reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_fast_reactor) and [molten salt reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molten_salt_reactor), can use as fuel the plutonium and other actinides in spent fuel from light water reactors, thanks to their [fast fission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_fission) spectrum. This offers a potentially more attractive alternative to deep geological disposal.[^cite_note-97][^cite_note-98][^cite_note-99]

The [thorium fuel cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorium_fuel_cycle) results in similar fission products, though creates a much smaller proportion of transuranic elements from [neutron capture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_capture) events within a reactor. Spent thorium fuel, although more difficult to handle than spent uranium fuel, may present somewhat lower proliferation risks.[^cite_note-100]

#### Low-level waste

Main article: [Low-level waste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-level_waste)

The nuclear industry also produces a large volume of [low-level waste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-level_waste), with low radioactivity, in the form of contaminated items like clothing, hand tools, water purifier resins, and (upon decommissioning) the materials of which the reactor itself is built. Low-level waste can be stored on-site until radiation levels are low enough to be disposed of as ordinary waste, or it can be sent to a low-level waste disposal site.[^cite_note-101]

#### Waste relative to other types

See also: [Radioactive waste § Naturally occurring radioactive material](37257.md#Naturally_occurring_radioactive_material)

In countries with nuclear power, radioactive wastes account for less than 1% of total industrial toxic wastes, much of which remains hazardous for long periods.[^cite_note-wna-wmitnfc-73] Overall, nuclear power produces far less waste material by volume than fossil-fuel based power plants.[^cite_note-102] Coal-burning plants, in particular, produce large amounts of toxic and mildly radioactive ash resulting from the concentration of [naturally occurring radioactive materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturally_occurring_radioactive_material) in coal.[^cite_note-103] A 2008 report from [Oak Ridge National Laboratory](38147.md) concluded that coal power actually results in more radioactivity being released into the environment than nuclear power operation, and that the population [effective dose equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_dose_equivalent) from radiation from coal plants is 100 times that from the operation of nuclear plants.[^cite_note-colmain-104] Although coal ash is much less radioactive than spent nuclear fuel by weight, coal ash is produced in much higher quantities per unit of energy generated. It is also released directly into the environment as [fly ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fly_ash), whereas nuclear plants use shielding to protect the environment from radioactive materials.[^cite_note-cejournal-105]

Nuclear waste volume is small compared to the energy produced. For example, at [Yankee Rowe Nuclear Power Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yankee_Rowe_Nuclear_Power_Station), which generated 44 billion [kilowatt hours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilowatt_hours) of electricity when in service, its complete spent fuel inventory is contained within sixteen casks.[^cite_note-106] It is estimated that to produce a lifetime supply of energy for a person at a western [standard of living](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_of_living) (approximately 3 [GWh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GWh)) would require on the order of the volume of a [soda can](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soda_can) of [low enriched uranium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_enriched_uranium), resulting in a similar volume of spent fuel generated.[^cite_note-Generation_Atomic-107][^cite_note-npr.org-108][^cite_note-109]

### Reprocessing

Main article: [Nuclear reprocessing](197845.md)

See also: [Plutonium Management and Disposition Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutonium_Management_and_Disposition_Agreement)

Most [thermal-neutron reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal-neutron_reactor) run on a [once-through nuclear fuel cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Once-through_nuclear_fuel_cycle), mainly due to the low price of fresh uranium. However, many reactors are also fueled with recycled fissionable materials that remain in spent nuclear fuel. The most common fissionable material that is recycled is the [reactor-grade plutonium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reactor-grade_plutonium) (RGPu) that is extracted from spent fuel. It is mixed with uranium oxide and fabricated into mixed-oxide or [MOX fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOX_fuel). Because thermal LWRs remain the most common reactor worldwide, this type of recycling is the most common. It is considered to increase the sustainability of the nuclear fuel cycle, reduce the attractiveness of spent fuel to theft, and lower the volume of high level nuclear waste.[^cite_note-120] Spent MOX fuel cannot generally be recycled for use in thermal-neutron reactors. This issue does not affect [fast-neutron reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast-neutron_reactor), which are therefore preferred in order to achieve the full energy potential of the original uranium.[^cite_note-berrytoll-121][^cite_note-IEEE_Spectrum-122]

The main constituent of spent fuel from LWRs is slightly [enriched uranium](37555.md). This can be recycled into [reprocessed uranium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reprocessed_uranium) (RepU), which can be used in a fast reactor, used directly as fuel in [CANDU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CANDU) reactors, or re-enriched for another cycle through an LWR. Re-enriching of reprocessed uranium is common in France and Russia.[^cite_note-WNA3-123] Reprocessed uranium is also safer in terms of nuclear proliferation potential.[^cite_note-124][^cite_note-125][^cite_note-126]

Reprocessing has the potential to recover up to 95% of the uranium and plutonium fuel in spent nuclear fuel, as well as reduce long-term radioactivity within the remaining waste. However, reprocessing has been politically controversial because of the potential for [nuclear proliferation](22158.md) and varied perceptions of increasing the vulnerability to [nuclear terrorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_terrorism).[^cite_note-berrytoll-121][^cite_note-bas2011-127] Reprocessing also leads to higher fuel cost compared to the once-through fuel cycle.[^cite_note-berrytoll-121][^cite_note-bas2011-127] While reprocessing can reduce the volume of high-level waste by 80%, it does not reduce the [fission products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fission_product) that are the primary causes of residual heat generation and radioactivity for the first century outside the reactor. Thus, reprocessed waste still requires an almost identical treatment to spent nuclear fuel at least for the first hundred years, after which the radioactivity of reprocessed waste may decline more rapidly.[^cite_note-WNA3-123]

Reprocessing of civilian fuel from power reactors is currently done in France, the United Kingdom, Russia, Japan, and India. In the United States, spent nuclear fuel is currently not reprocessed.[^cite_note-WNA3-123] The [La Hague reprocessing facility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Hague_reprocessing_facility) in France has operated commercially since 1976 and is responsible for half the world's reprocessing as of 2010.[^cite_note-128] It produces MOX fuel from spent fuel derived from several countries. More than 32,000 tonnes of spent fuel had been reprocessed as of 2015, with the majority from France, 17% from Germany, and 9% from Japan.[^cite_note-129]

### Breeding



[Nuclear fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fuel) assemblies being inspected before entering a [pressurized water reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressurized_water_reactor) in the United States

Main articles: [Breeder reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breeder_reactor) and [Nuclear power proposed as renewable energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_power_proposed_as_renewable_energy)

Breeding is the process of converting non-fissile material into fissile material that can be used as nuclear fuel. The non-fissile material that can be used for this process is called [fertile material](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertile_material), and constitute the vast majority of current nuclear waste. This breeding process occurs naturally in [breeder reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breeder_reactor). As opposed to light water thermal-neutron reactors, which use uranium-235 (0.7% of all natural uranium), fast-neutron breeder reactors use uranium-238 (99.3% of all natural uranium) or thorium. A number of fuel cycles and breeder reactor combinations are considered to be sustainable or renewable sources of energy.[^cite_note-130][^cite_note-Brundtland-131] In 2006 it was estimated that with seawater extraction, there was likely five billion years' worth of uranium resources for use in breeder reactors.[^cite_note-stanford-cohen-132]

Breeder technology has been used in several reactors, but as of 2006, the high cost of reprocessing fuel safely requires uranium prices of more than US\$200/kg before becoming justified economically.[^cite_note-wna-anpr-133] Breeder reactors are however being developed for their potential to burn all of the actinides (the most active and dangerous components) in the present inventory of nuclear waste, while also producing power and creating additional quantities of fuel for more reactors via the breeding process.[^cite_note-134][^cite_note-135] As of 2017, there are two breeders producing commercial power, [BN-600 reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BN-600_reactor) and the [BN-800 reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BN-800_reactor), both in Russia.[^cite_note-WNAfast-136] The [Phénix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ph%C3%A9nix) breeder reactor in France was powered down in 2009 after 36 years of operation.[^cite_note-WNAfast-136] Both China and India are building breeder reactors. The Indian 500 MWe [Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prototype_Fast_Breeder_Reactor) is in the commissioning phase,[^cite_note-137] with plans to build more.[^cite_note-138]

Another alternative to fast-neutron breeders are thermal-neutron breeder reactors that use uranium-233 bred from [thorium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorium) as fission fuel in the [thorium fuel cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorium_fuel_cycle).[^cite_note-wna-thorium-139] Thorium is about 3.5 times more common than uranium in the Earth's crust, and has different geographic characteristics.[^cite_note-wna-thorium-139] [India's three-stage nuclear power programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India's_three-stage_nuclear_power_programme) features the use of a thorium fuel cycle in the third stage, as it has abundant thorium reserves but little uranium.[^cite_note-wna-thorium-139]

## Decommissioning

Main article: [Nuclear decommissioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_decommissioning)

Nuclear decommissioning is the process of dismantling a [nuclear facility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_facility) to the point that it no longer requires measures for radiation protection,[^cite_note-140] returning the facility and its parts to a safe enough level to be entrusted for other uses.[^cite_note-iaea_decommissioning-141] Due to the presence of radioactive materials, nuclear decommissioning presents technical and economic challenges.[^cite_note-142] The costs of decommissioning are generally spread over the lifetime of a facility and saved in a decommissioning fund.[^cite_note-143]

Civilian nuclear power supplied 2,602 [terawatt hours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terawatt_hour) (TWh) of electricity in 2023, equivalent to about 9% of [global electricity generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_electricity_generation),[^cite_note-PerformanceReport-3] and was the second largest [low-carbon power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-carbon_power) source after [hydroelectricity](381399.md).[^cite_note-IEA2019-145] Nuclear power's contribution to global energy production was about 4% in 2023. This is a little more than wind power, which provided 3.5% of global energy in 2023.[^cite_note-146] Nuclear power's share of global electricity production has fallen from 16.5% in 1997, in large part because the economics of nuclear power have become more difficult.[^cite_note-ft-20180903-147]

As of November 2024, there are [415 civilian fission reactors in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_nuclear_reactors), with a combined electrical capacity of 374 [gigawatt](21347693.md) (GW).[^cite_note-\:3-4] There are also 66 nuclear power reactors under construction and 87 reactors planned, with a combined capacity of 72 GW and 84 GW, respectively.[^cite_note-WNA-5] The United States has the largest fleet of nuclear reactors, generating over 800 TWh per year with an average [capacity factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capacity_factor) of 92%.[^cite_note-\:2-148] Most reactors under construction are [generation III reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generation_III_reactor) in Asia.[^cite_note-149]

Regional differences in the use of nuclear power are large. The United States produces the most nuclear energy in the world, with nuclear power providing 19% of the electricity it consumes, while France produces the highest percentage of its electrical energy from nuclear reactors—65% in 2023.[^cite_note-\:0-25] In the [European Union](9317.md), nuclear power provides 22% of the electricity as of 2022.[^cite_note-WNA-EU-150] Nuclear power is the single largest low-carbon electricity source in the United States,[^cite_note-issues-151] and accounts for about half of the European Union's low-carbon electricity.[^cite_note-WNA-EU-150] [Nuclear energy policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_energy_policy) differs among European Union countries, and some, such as Austria, [Estonia](28222445.md), Ireland and [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_Italy), have no active nuclear power stations.[^cite_note-152]

In addition, there were approximately 140 naval vessels using [nuclear propulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_propulsion) in operation, powered by about 180 reactors.[^cite_note-153][^cite_note-154] These include military and some civilian ships, such as [nuclear-powered icebreakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear-powered_icebreaker).[^cite_note-155]

International research is continuing into additional uses of process heat such as [hydrogen production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_production) (in support of a [hydrogen economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_economy)), for [desalinating](156787.md) sea water, and for use in [district heating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/District_heating) systems.[^cite_note-156]

The organization recognises that the [median](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Median) cost of the most exported and produced nuclear energy facility in the 2010s the South Korean [APR1400](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/APR1400), remained "constant", including in export.

[^cite_note-157]

Main articles: [Economics of nuclear power plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics_of_nuclear_power_plants), [List of companies in the nuclear sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_companies_in_the_nuclear_sector), and [cost of electricity by source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost_of_electricity_by_source)

The economics of new nuclear power plants is a controversial subject. Nuclear power plants typically have high capital costs for building the plant. For this reason, comparison with other power generation methods is strongly dependent on assumptions about construction timescales and capital financing for nuclear plants. Because of this strong dependency, the final cost of electricity from nuclear power is strongly dependent on the [cost of capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost_of_capital).[^cite_note-158]

Analysis of the economics of nuclear power must also take into account who bears the risks of future uncertainties. As of 2010, all operating nuclear power plants have been developed by state-owned or [regulated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulated_market) [electric utility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_utility) monopolies.[^cite_note-ft-20100912-159] Many countries have since liberalized the [electricity market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electricity_market) where these risks, and the risk of cheaper competitors emerging before capital costs are recovered, are borne by plant suppliers and operators rather than consumers, which leads to a significantly different evaluation of the economics of new nuclear power plants.[^cite_note-MIT-2003-160]

The [levelized cost of electricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levelized_cost_of_electricity) (LCOE) from a new nuclear power plant is estimated to be 69 USD/MWh, according to an analysis by the [International Energy Agency](285292.md) and the [OECD](33853117.md) [Nuclear Energy Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_Energy_Agency). This represents the median cost estimate for an nth-of-a-kind nuclear power plant to be completed in 2025, at a [discount rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discounting) of 7%. Nuclear power was found to be the least-cost option among [dispatchable technologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dispatchable_generation).[^cite_note-IEA_LCOE_2020-161] [Variable renewables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variable_renewable_energy) can generate cheaper electricity: the median cost of onshore wind power was estimated to be 50 USD/MWh, and utility-scale solar power 56 USD/MWh.[^cite_note-IEA_LCOE_2020-161] However, these sources are not directly comparable to nuclear power as they are not [dispatchable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dispatchable_generation). Measures to [mitigate global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitigate_global_warming), such as a [carbon tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_tax) or [carbon emissions trading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_emissions_trading), may favor the economics of nuclear power.[^cite_note-162][^cite_note-163] At the assumed CO<sub>2</sub> emission cost of 30 USD/ton, power from coal (88 USD/MWh) and gas (71 USD/MWh) is more expensive than low-carbon technologies. Electricity from long-term operation of nuclear power plants by lifetime extension was found to be the least-cost option, at 32 USD/MWh.[^cite_note-IEA_LCOE_2020-161]

The high cost of construction is one of the biggest challenges for nuclear power plants. Nuclear power cost trends show large disparity by nation, design, build rate and the establishment of familiarity in expertise. The only two nations for which data is available that saw cost decreases in the 2000s were India and South Korea.[^cite_note-Lovering2016-164] New [small modular reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_modular_reactors), such as those developed by [NuScale Power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NuScale_Power), are aimed at reducing the investment costs for new construction by making the reactors smaller and modular, so that they can be built in a factory.

Certain designs had considerable early positive economics, such as the [CANDU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CANDU), which realized a much higher [capacity factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capacity_factor) and reliability when compared to generation II light water reactors up to the 1990s.[^cite_note-165] Due to the on-line refueling reactor design, [PHWRs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PHWR) (of which the CANDU design is a part) continue to hold many world record positions for longest continual electricity generation, often over 800 days.[^cite_note-166] The specific record as of 2019 is held by a PHWR at [Kaiga Atomic Power Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaiga_Atomic_Power_Station), generating electricity continuously for 962 days.[^cite_note-167]

Nuclear power plants, though capable of some grid-[load following](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Load_following), are typically run as much as possible to keep the cost of the generated electrical energy as low as possible, supplying mostly [base-load](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base-load) electricity.[^cite_note-168]

In some cases, Governments were found to force "consumers to pay upfront for potential cost overruns"[^cite_note-mil1-169] or subsidize uneconomic nuclear energy[^cite_note-170] or be required to do so.[^cite_note-francere-171] Nuclear operators are liable to pay for the waste management in the European Union.[^cite_note-euwastecosts-172] In the U.S., the Congress reportedly decided 40 years ago that the nation, and not private companies, would be responsible for storing radioactive waste with taxpayers paying for the costs.[^cite_note-173] The World Nuclear Waste Report 2019 found that "even in countries in which the polluter-pays-principle is a legal requirement, it is applied incompletely" and notes the case of the German [Asse II deep geological disposal facility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asse_II_mine), where the retrieval of large amounts of waste has to be paid for by taxpayers.[^cite_note-174] Similarly, other forms of energy, including fossil fuels and renewables, have a portion of their costs covered by governments.[^cite_note-175]

## Use in space



The [multi-mission radioisotope thermoelectric generator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-mission_radioisotope_thermoelectric_generator) (MMRTG), used in several space missions such as the [*Curiosity* Mars rover](36645032.md)&#x20;

Main article: [Nuclear power in space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_space)

The most common use of nuclear power in space is the use of [radioisotope thermoelectric generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioisotope_thermoelectric_generator), which use [radioactive decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_decay) to generate power. These power generators are relatively small scale (few kW), and they are mostly used to power [space missions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_mission) and experiments for long periods where solar power is not available in sufficient quantity, such as in the *[Voyager 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyager_2)* space probe.[^cite_note-WNA_space-176] A few space vehicles have been launched using [nuclear reactors](22151.md): 34 reactors belong to the Soviet [RORSAT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RORSAT) series and one was the American [SNAP-10A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SNAP-10A).[^cite_note-WNA_space-176]

Both [fission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fission) and fusion appear promising for [space propulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_propulsion) applications, generating higher mission velocities with less [reaction mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_mass).[^cite_note-WNA_space-176][^cite_note-177]

## Safety

See also: [Nuclear safety and security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_safety_and_security) and [Nuclear reactor safety system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_reactor_safety_system)



Death rates per unit of electricity production for different energy sources

Nuclear power plants have three unique characteristics that affect their safety, as compared to other power plants. Firstly, intensely [radioactive materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_material) are present in a nuclear reactor. Their release to the environment could be hazardous. Secondly, the [fission products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fission_product), which make up most of the intensely radioactive substances in the reactor, continue to generate a significant amount of [decay heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decay_heat) even after the fission [chain reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_chain_reaction) has stopped. If the heat cannot be removed from the reactor, the fuel rods may overheat and release radioactive materials. Thirdly, a [criticality accident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criticality_accident) (a rapid increase of the reactor power) is possible in certain reactor designs if the chain reaction cannot be controlled. These three characteristics have to be taken into account when designing nuclear reactors.[^cite_note-IAEAsafety-178]

All modern reactors are designed so that an uncontrolled increase of the reactor power is prevented by natural feedback mechanisms, a concept known as negative [void coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Void_coefficient) of reactivity. If the temperature or the amount of steam in the reactor increases, the fission rate inherently decreases. The chain reaction can also be manually stopped by inserting [control rods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_rod) into the reactor core. [Emergency core cooling systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergency_core_cooling_system) (ECCS) can remove the decay heat from the reactor if normal cooling systems fail.[^cite_note-179] If the ECCS fails, multiple physical barriers limit the release of radioactive materials to the environment even in the case of an accident. The last physical barrier is the large [containment building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Containment_building).[^cite_note-IAEAsafety-178]

With a death rate of 0.03 per [TWh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TWh), nuclear power is the second safest energy source per unit of energy generated, after solar power, in terms of mortality when the historical track-record is considered.[^cite_note-180] Energy produced by coal, petroleum, natural gas and [hydropower](14073.md) has caused more deaths per unit of energy generated due to [air pollution](10934212.md) and [energy accidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_accidents). This is found when comparing the immediate deaths from other energy sources to both the immediate and the latent, or predicted, indirect cancer deaths from nuclear energy accidents.[^cite_note-without_the_hot_air-181][^cite_note-theage2006-182] When the direct and indirect fatalities (including fatalities resulting from the mining and air pollution) from nuclear power and fossil fuels are compared,[^cite_note-MarkandyaWilkinson2007-183] the use of nuclear power has been calculated to have prevented about 1.84 million deaths from air pollution between 1971 and 2009, by reducing the proportion of energy that would otherwise have been generated by fossil fuels.[^cite_note-autogenerated1-184][^cite_note-Kharecha_Pushker_A_2013_4889–4895-185] Following the 2011 Fukushima nuclear disaster, it has been estimated that if Japan had never adopted nuclear power, accidents and pollution from coal or gas plants would have caused more lost years of life.[^cite_note-186]

Serious impacts of nuclear accidents are often not directly attributable to radiation exposure, but rather social and psychological effects. Evacuation and long-term displacement of affected populations created problems for many people, especially the elderly and hospital patients.[^cite_note-187] Forced evacuation from a nuclear accident may lead to social isolation, anxiety, depression, psychosomatic medical problems, reckless behavior, and suicide. A comprehensive 2005 study on the aftermath of the Chernobyl disaster concluded that the mental health impact is the largest public health problem caused by the accident.[^cite_note-riv12-188] [Frank N. von Hippel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_N._von_Hippel), an American scientist, commented that a disproportionate fear of ionizing radiation ([radiophobia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiophobia)) could have long-term psychological effects on the population of contaminated areas following the Fukushima disaster.[^cite_note-Frank_N._von_Hippel_27–36-189]

### Accidents



Following the 2011 [Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukushima_Daiichi_nuclear_disaster), the world's worst [nuclear accident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_accident) since 1986, 50,000 households were displaced after [radiation](25856.md) leaked into the air, soil and sea.

[^cite_note-190]

&#x20;Radiation checks led to bans of some shipments of vegetables and fish.

[^cite_note-191]



Reactor [decay heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decay_heat) as a fraction of full power after the reactor shutdown, using two different correlations. To remove the decay heat, reactors need cooling after the shutdown of the fission reactions. A loss of the ability to remove decay heat caused the [Fukushima accident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukushima_accident).

See also: [Energy accidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_accidents), [Nuclear and radiation accidents and incidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_and_radiation_accidents_and_incidents), and [Lists of nuclear disasters and radioactive incidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lists_of_nuclear_disasters_and_radioactive_incidents)

Some serious [nuclear and radiation accidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_and_radiation_accidents_by_death_toll) have occurred. The severity of nuclear accidents is generally classified using the [International Nuclear Event Scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Nuclear_Event_Scale) (INES) introduced by the [International Atomic Energy Agency](14984.md) (IAEA). The scale ranks anomalous events or accidents on a scale from 0 (a deviation from normal operation that poses no safety risk) to 7 (a major accident with widespread effects). There have been three accidents of level 5 or higher in the civilian nuclear power industry, two of which, the [Chernobyl accident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernobyl_accident) and the [Fukushima accident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukushima_accident), are ranked at level 7.

The first major nuclear accidents were the [Kyshtym disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyshtym_disaster) in the Soviet Union and the [Windscale fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windscale_fire) in the United Kingdom, both in 1957. The first major accident at a nuclear reactor in the USA occurred in 1961 at the [SL-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SL-1), a [U.S. Army](32087.md) experimental nuclear power reactor at the [Idaho National Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idaho_National_Laboratory). An uncontrolled chain reaction resulted in a [steam explosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_explosion) which killed the three crew members and caused a [meltdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_meltdown).[^cite_note-ido19313-192][^cite_note-193] Another serious accident happened in 1968, when one of the two [liquid-metal-cooled reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid-metal-cooled_reactor) on board the [Soviet submarine *K-27*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_submarine_K-27) underwent a [fuel element failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_element_failure), with the emission of gaseous [fission products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fission_product) into the surrounding air, resulting in 9 crew fatalities and 83 injuries.[^cite_note-johnston2007-194]

The Fukushima Daiichi nuclear accident was caused by the [2011 Tohoku earthquake and tsunami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Tohoku_earthquake_and_tsunami). The accident has not caused any radiation-related deaths but resulted in radioactive contamination of surrounding areas. The difficult [cleanup operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukushima_disaster_cleanup) is expected to cost tens of billions of dollars over 40 or more years.[^cite_note-Richard_Schiffman-195][^cite_note-Martin_Fackler-196] The [Three Mile Island accident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Mile_Island_accident) in 1979 was a smaller scale accident, rated at INES level 5. There were no direct or indirect deaths caused by the accident.[^cite_note-timenuke-197]

The impact of nuclear accidents is controversial. According to [Benjamin K. Sovacool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_K._Sovacool), fission [energy accidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_accidents) ranked first among energy sources in terms of their total economic cost, accounting for 41% of all property damage attributed to energy accidents.[^cite_note-bksenpol-198] Another analysis found that coal, oil, [liquid petroleum gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_petroleum_gas) and hydroelectric accidents (primarily due to the [Banqiao Dam disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banqiao_Dam_disaster)) have resulted in greater economic impacts than nuclear power accidents.[^cite_note-199] The study compares latent cancer deaths attributable to nuclear power with immediate deaths from other energy sources per unit of energy generated, and does not include fossil fuel related cancer and other indirect deaths created by the use of fossil fuel consumption in its "severe accident" (an accident with more than five fatalities) classification. The Chernobyl accident in 1986 caused approximately 50 deaths from direct and indirect effects, and some temporary serious injuries from [acute radiation syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_radiation_syndrome).[^cite_note-WHO2012-200] The future predicted mortality from increases in cancer rates is estimated at 4000 in the decades to come.[^cite_note-201][^cite_note-UNSCEAR_2008_D-202][^cite_note-UNSCEAR_GA-203]

Nuclear power works under an [insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insurance) framework that limits or structures accident liabilities in accordance with national and international conventions.[^cite_note-204] It is often argued that this potential shortfall in liability represents an external cost not included in the cost of nuclear electricity. This cost is small, amounting to about 0.1% of the [levelized cost of electricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levelized_cost_of_electricity), according to a study by the [Congressional Budget Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congressional_Budget_Office) in the United States.[^cite_note-205] These beyond-regular insurance costs for worst-case scenarios are not unique to nuclear power. [Hydroelectric power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroelectric_power) plants are similarly not fully insured against a catastrophic event such as [dam failures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dam_failure). For example, the failure of the [Banqiao Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banqiao_Dam) caused the death of an estimated 30,000 to 200,000 people, and 11 million people lost their homes. As private insurers base dam insurance premiums on limited scenarios, major disaster insurance in this sector is likewise provided by the state.[^cite_note-206]

### Attacks and sabotage

Main articles: [Vulnerability of nuclear plants to attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulnerability_of_nuclear_plants_to_attack), [Nuclear terrorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_terrorism), and [Nuclear safety in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_safety_in_the_United_States)

Terrorists could target [nuclear power plants](207347.md) in an attempt to release [radioactive contamination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_contamination) into the community. The United States 9/11 Commission has said that nuclear power plants were potential targets originally considered for the [September 11, 2001 attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/September_11,_2001_attacks). An attack on a reactor's [spent fuel pool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spent_fuel_pool) could also be serious, as these pools are less protected than the reactor core. The release of radioactivity could lead to thousands of near-term deaths and greater numbers of long-term fatalities.[^cite_note-fas12-207]

In the United States, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission carries out "Force on Force" (FOF) exercises at all nuclear power plant sites at least once every three years.[^cite_note-fas12-207] In the United States, plants are surrounded by a double row of tall fences which are electronically monitored. The plant grounds are patrolled by a sizeable force of armed guards.[^cite_note-208]

Insider sabotage is also a threat because insiders can observe and work around security measures.[^cite_note-209] In 1971, a fire intentionally started by a maintenance worker caused 5–10 million dollars' worth of damage to New York's [Indian Point Energy Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Point_Energy_Center).[^cite_note-210][^cite_note-211]

## Proliferation

Further information: [Nuclear proliferation](22158.md)

See also: [Plutonium Management and Disposition Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutonium_Management_and_Disposition_Agreement)

Nuclear proliferation is the spread of [nuclear weapons](21785.md), fissionable material, and weapons-related nuclear technology to states that do not already possess nuclear weapons. Many technologies and materials associated with the creation of a nuclear power program have a dual-use capability, in that they can also be used to make nuclear weapons. For this reason, nuclear power presents proliferation risks.

Nuclear power program can become a route leading to a nuclear weapon. An example of this is the concern over [Iran's nuclear program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran's_nuclear_program).[^cite_note-dfall2009-212] The re-purposing of civilian nuclear industries for military purposes would be a breach of the [Non-Proliferation Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Proliferation_Treaty), to which 190 countries adhere. As of April 2012, there are [thirty-one countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_power_by_country) that have civil nuclear power plants,[^cite_note-213] of which nine have nuclear weapons. The vast majority of these [nuclear weapons states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_state) have produced weapons before commercial nuclear power stations.

A fundamental goal for global security is to minimize the nuclear proliferation risks associated with the expansion of nuclear power.[^cite_note-dfall2009-212] The [Global Nuclear Energy Partnership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Nuclear_Energy_Partnership) was an international effort to create a distribution network in which developing countries in need of energy would receive [nuclear fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fuel) at a discounted rate, in exchange for that nation agreeing to forgo their own indigenous development of a uranium enrichment program. The France-based [Eurodif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurodif)/*European Gaseous Diffusion Uranium Enrichment Consortium* is a program that successfully implemented this concept, with [Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_Spain) and other countries without enrichment facilities buying a share of the fuel produced at the French-controlled enrichment facility, but without a transfer of technology.[^cite_note-214] Iran was an early participant from 1974 and remains a shareholder of Eurodif via [Sofidif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sofidif).

A 2009 United Nations report said that:

> the revival of interest in nuclear power could result in the worldwide dissemination of uranium enrichment and spent fuel reprocessing technologies, which present obvious risks of proliferation as these technologies can produce fissile materials that are directly usable in nuclear weapons.[^cite_note-bks2011-215]

On the other hand, power reactors can also reduce nuclear weapon arsenals when military-grade nuclear materials are reprocessed to be used as fuel in nuclear power plants. The [Megatons to Megawatts Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megatons_to_Megawatts_Program) is considered the single most successful [non-proliferation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-proliferation) program to date.[^cite_note-thebulletin.org-216] Up to 2005, the program had processed \$8 billion of high enriched, weapons-grade uranium into [low enriched uranium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_enriched_uranium) suitable as nuclear fuel for commercial fission reactors by diluting it with [natural uranium](2836053.md). This corresponds to the elimination of 10,000 nuclear weapons.[^cite_note-217] For approximately two decades, this material generated nearly 10 percent of all the electricity consumed in the United States, or about half of all U.S. nuclear electricity, with a total of around 7,000 [TWh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TWh) of electricity produced.[^cite_note-ReferenceB-218] In total it is estimated to have cost \$17 billion, a "bargain for US ratepayers", with Russia profiting \$12 billion from the deal.[^cite_note-ReferenceB-218] Much needed profit for the Russian nuclear oversight industry, which after the collapse of the [Soviet economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_economy), had difficulties paying for the maintenance and security of the Russian Federation's highly enriched uranium and warheads.[^cite_note-A_Farewell_to_Arms,_2014-219] The Megatons to Megawatts Program was hailed as a major success by anti-nuclear weapon advocates as it has largely been the driving force behind the sharp reduction in the number of nuclear weapons worldwide since the cold war ended.[^cite_note-thebulletin.org-216] However, without an increase in nuclear reactors and greater demand for fissile fuel, the cost of dismantling and down blending has dissuaded Russia from continuing their disarmament. As of 2013, Russia appears to not be interested in extending the program.[^cite_note-220]

## Geo-political risk

As of 2023, the Russian state nuclear company Rosatom is the largest player in the international nuclear power market, building nuclear plants around the world.[^cite_note-Szulecki_2023-221] Whereas Russian oil and gas were subject to international sanctions after the [Russian full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022](70149799.md), Rosatom was not targeted by sanctions.[^cite_note-Szulecki_2023-221] However, some countries, especially in Europe, scaled back or cancelled planned nuclear power plants that were to be built by Rosatom.[^cite_note-Szulecki_2023-221]

## Environmental impact

Main article: [Environmental impact of nuclear power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_of_nuclear_power)



The [Ikata Nuclear Power Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ikata_Nuclear_Power_Plant), a [pressurized water reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressurized_water_reactor) that cools by using a secondary coolant [heat exchanger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_exchanger) with a large body of water, an alternative cooling approach to large [cooling towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooling_towers)

Being a low-carbon energy source with relatively little land-use requirements, nuclear energy can have a positive environmental impact. Its largest potential negative impacts on the environment may arise from its transgenerational risks for nuclear weapons proliferation that may increase risks of their use in the future, risks for problems associated with the management of the radioactive waste such as groundwater contamination, risks for accidents and for risks for various forms of attacks on waste storage sites or reprocessing- and power-plants.[^cite_note-repr-222][^cite_note-wi1-223][^cite_note-worldnuclearwastereport-224][^cite_note-risks-225][^cite_note-plane1-226][^cite_note-10.3390/ijerph13070700-227][^cite_note-228] However, there have been a small number of disasters at nuclear power plants with substantial environmental impacts, mostly involving reactors with older designs.

Life-cycle greenhouse gas emissions of electricity supply technologies, median values calculated by [IPCC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPCC)

[^cite_note-IPCC_2014_Annex_III-229]

Nuclear power is one of the leading [low carbon power generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_carbon_power_generation) methods of producing [electricity](9550.md), and in terms of [total life-cycle greenhouse gas emissions per unit of energy generated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life-cycle_greenhouse-gas_emissions_of_energy_sources), has emission values comparable to or lower than [renewable energy](25784.md).[^cite_note-Nrel.gov-230][^cite_note-231] A 2014 analysis of the [carbon footprint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_footprint) literature by the [Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change](15030.md) (IPCC) reported that the embodied [total life-cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_cycle_assessment) [emission intensity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emission_intensity) of nuclear power has a median value of 12 g CO<sub>2</sub>[eq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide_equivalent)/[kWh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KWh), which is the lowest among all commercial [baseload](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baseload) energy sources.[^cite_note-IPCC_2014_Annex_III-229][^cite_note-report.mitigation2014.org-232] This is contrasted with [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal) and [natural gas](22131.md) at 820 and 490 g CO<sub>2</sub> eq/kWh.[^cite_note-IPCC_2014_Annex_III-229][^cite_note-report.mitigation2014.org-232] As of 2021, nuclear reactors worldwide have helped avoid the emission of 72 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide since 1970, compared to coal-fired electricity generation, according to a report.[^cite_note-Kharecha_Pushker_A_2013_4889–4895-185][^cite_note-233]

### Radiation

The average dose from natural [background radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Background_radiation) is 2.4 [millisievert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millisievert) per year (mSv/a) globally. It varies between 1 mSv/a and 13 mSv/a, depending mostly on the geology of the location. According to the United Nations ([UNSCEAR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNSCEAR)), regular nuclear power plant operations, including the nuclear fuel cycle, increases this amount by 0.0002 mSv/a of public exposure as a global average. The average dose from operating nuclear power plants to the local populations around them is less than 0.0001 mSv/a.[^cite_note-UNSCEAR_GA-203] For comparison, the average dose to those living within 50 miles (80 km) of a [coal power plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_power_plant) is over three times this dose, at 0.0003 mSv/a.[^cite_note-234]

Chernobyl resulted in the most affected surrounding populations and male recovery personnel receiving an average initial 50 to 100 mSv over a few hours to weeks, while the remaining global legacy of the worst nuclear power plant accident in average exposure is 0.002 mSv/a and is continuously dropping at the decaying rate, from the initial high of 0.04 mSv per person averaged over the entire populace of the Northern Hemisphere in the year of the accident in 1986.[^cite_note-UNSCEAR_GA-203]

## Debate

Main article: [Nuclear power debate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_power_debate)

See also: [Nuclear energy policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_energy_policy), [Pro-nuclear movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pro-nuclear_movement), and [Anti-nuclear movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-nuclear_movement)



Survey respondents in the European Union and US considered investment in nuclear energy to be a higher priority than those in China, though all three favored renewable energy much more strongly than nuclear overall.

[^cite_note-EIBsurvey_2023-235]

The nuclear power debate concerns the controversy which has surrounded the deployment and use of nuclear fission reactors to generate electricity from nuclear fuel for civilian purposes.[^cite_note-eleven-32][^cite_note-jstor.org-236][^cite_note-marcuse.org-33]

Proponents of nuclear energy regard it as a [sustainable energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_energy) source that reduces [carbon emissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_emissions) and increases [energy security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_security) by decreasing dependence on other energy sources that are often dependent on imports.[^cite_note-bloomberg.com-237][^cite_note-238][^cite_note-239] For example, proponents note that annually, nuclear-generated electricity reduces 470 million metric tons of carbon dioxide emissions that would otherwise come from fossil fuels.[^cite_note-240] Additionally, the amount of comparatively low waste that nuclear energy does create is safely disposed of by the large scale nuclear energy production facilities or it is repurposed/recycled for other energy uses.[^cite_note-241]

Proponents also claim that the present quantity of nuclear waste is small and can be reduced through the latest technology of newer reactors and that the operational safety record of fission-electricity in terms of deaths is so far "unparalleled".[^cite_note-Bernard_L._Cohen_1990-21] Kharecha and [Hansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Hansen) estimated that "global nuclear power has prevented an average of 1.84 million air pollution-related deaths and 64 gigatonnes of CO<sub>2</sub>-equivalent (GtCO<sub>2</sub>-eq) greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions that would have resulted from fossil fuel burning" and, if continued, it could prevent up to 7 million deaths and 240 GtCO<sub>2</sub>-eq emissions by 2050.[^cite_note-Kharecha_Pushker_A_2013_4889–4895-185]

Proponents also bring to attention the opportunity cost of using other forms of electricity. For example, the [United States Environmental Protection Agency](58666.md) estimates that coal kills 30,000 people a year as a result of its environmental impact,[^cite_note-242] while 60 people died in the Chernobyl disaster.[^cite_note-243] A real world example of impact provided by proponents is the 650,000 ton increase in carbon emissions in the two months following the closure of the [Vermont Yankee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vermont_Yankee) nuclear plant.[^cite_note-244]

A 2015 survey of [AAAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Association_for_the_Advancement_of_Science) members found that 65% support building more nuclear power plants, which rose to 79% among physicists.[^cite_note-245]

Opponents believe that nuclear power poses many threats to people's health and environment such as the risk of nuclear weapons proliferation,[^cite_note-246][^cite_note-Sturgis-247] long-term safe waste management and terrorism in the future.[^cite_note-gierec-248][^cite_note-protest-249] They also contend that nuclear power plants are complex systems where many things can and have gone wrong.[^cite_note-bksenpol-198][^cite_note-250]

Critics find that one of the largest drawbacks to building new nuclear fission power plants are the high costs when compared to alternatives of sustainable energy sources.[^cite_note-cnnchina-251][^cite_note-10.1016/j.erss.2014.04.015-252][^cite_note-10.1016/j.enpol.2016.03.012-253][^cite_note-10.1177/2399654418777765-254] Proponents note that focusing on the [levelized cost of energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levelized_cost_of_energy) (LCOE), however, ignores the value premium associated with 24/7 dispatchable electricity and the cost of storage and backup systems necessary to integrate variable energy sources into a reliable electrical grid.[^cite_note-255] "Nuclear thus remains the dispatchable low-carbon technology with the lowest expected costs in 2025. Only large hydro reservoirs can provide a similar contribution at comparable costs but remain highly dependent on the natural endowments of individual countries".[^cite_note-256] Regulatory requirements and licensing delays have been cited as one of the main drivers of nuclear cost increases; modern compliance costs average \$60 million per plant annually, which exceeds profit in some facilities.[^cite_note-257][^cite_note-258]



Anti-nuclear protest near [nuclear waste disposal centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_geological_repository) at [Gorleben](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gorleben) in northern Germany

Overall, many opponents find that nuclear energy cannot meaningfully contribute to [climate change mitigation](2119179.md). In general, they find it to be too dangerous, too expensive, to take too long for deployment, as much as to be an obstacle to achieving a transition towards sustainability and carbon-neutrality.[^cite_note-10.5281/zenodo.5573718-259][^cite_note-260][^cite_note-slowexpensive-261][^cite_note-262] These opponents find nuclear to be effectively a distraction in the competition for resources (i.e., human, financial, time, infrastructure and expertise) for the deployment and development of alternative, sustainable, [energy system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_system) technologies.[^cite_note-gates2-263][^cite_note-cd1-264][^cite_note-mil1-169][^cite_note-10.5281/zenodo.5573718-259][^cite_note-265]

Nevertheless, there is ongoing research and debate over costs of new nuclear, especially in regions where seasonal energy storage is difficult to provide and which aim to [phase out fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel_phase-out) in favor of [low carbon power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_carbon_power) faster than the global average.[^cite_note-266] Some find that financial transition costs for a 100% renewables-based European energy system that has completely phased out nuclear energy could be more costly by 2050 based on current technologies (i.e., not considering potential advances in e.g., [green hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_hydrogen), transmission and flexibility capacities, ways to reduce energy needs, geothermal energy and fusion energy) when the grid only extends across Europe.[^cite_note-267] Arguments of economics and safety are used by both sides of the debate.

### Comparison with renewable energy

See also: [Renewable energy debate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renewable_energy_debate)



Electricity from low-carbon sources by source, World

Slowing [global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming) requires a transition to a [low-carbon economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-carbon_economy), mainly by burning far less [fossil fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel). This has generated considerable interest and dispute in determining the best path forward to rapidly replace fossil-based fuels in the global [energy mix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_mix),[^cite_note-268][^cite_note-269] with intense academic debate.[^cite_note-270][^cite_note-GTM-NewFront-271] Sometimes the IEA says that countries without nuclear should develop it as well as their renewable power.[^cite_note-272]

Nuclear reactors produce a much smaller volume of waste compared to renewable energy sources, although nuclear waste is much more toxic, expensive to manage and longer-lived compared to waste from renewable technologies.[^cite_note-273][^cite_note-worldnuclearwastereport-224] Nuclear waste can be dangerous if leaked to the environment, and need to be stored safely for thousands or even hundreds of thousand of years.[^cite_note-274][^cite_note-275][^cite_note-276]

Nuclear plants are also far more complex to decommission compared to renewable energy plants. A nuclear plant needs to be disassembled and removed and much of the disassembled nuclear plant needs to be stored as low-level nuclear waste for a few decades.[^cite_note-277]

Nuclear power may also pose the risk of [nuclear proliferation](22158.md). Separated [plutonium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutonium) and [enriched uranium](37555.md) could be used for [nuclear weapons](21785.md), which pose a substantial global risk to human civilization and the environment.[^cite_note-wi1-223][^cite_note-worldnuclearwastereport-224][^cite_note-risks-225][^cite_note-plane1-226]

#### Speed of transition and investment needed

Analysis in 2015 by professor [Barry W. Brook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Brook_\(scientist\)) and colleagues found that nuclear energy could displace or remove fossil fuels from the electric grid completely within 10 years. This finding was based on the historically modest and proven rate at which nuclear energy was added in France and Sweden during their building programs in the 1980s.[^cite_note-journals.plos.org-278][^cite_note-279] In a similar analysis, Brook had earlier determined that 50% of all [global energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_energy_consumption), including transportation [synthetic fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_fuels) etc., could be generated within approximately 30 years if the global nuclear fission build rate was identical to historical proven installation rates calculated in [GW](21347693.md) per year per unit of global [GDP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GDP) (GW/year/\$).[^cite_note-brook_could_2012-280] This is in contrast to the conceptual studies for [100% renewable energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/100%25_renewable_energy) systems, which would require an order of magnitude more costly global investment per year, which has no historical precedent.[^cite_note-loftus_critical_2015-281] These renewable scenarios would also need far greater land devoted to onshore wind and onshore solar projects.[^cite_note-brook_could_2012-280][^cite_note-loftus_critical_2015-281] Brook notes that the "principal limitations on nuclear fission are not technical, economic or fuel-related, but are instead linked to complex issues of societal acceptance, fiscal and political inertia, and inadequate critical evaluation of the real-world constraints facing \[the other] low-carbon alternatives".[^cite_note-brook_could_2012-280]

#### Land use

The median land area used by US nuclear power stations per 1 GW installed capacity is 1.3 [square miles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_mile) (3.4 [km<sup>2</sup>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_kilometre)).[^cite_note-NEI_news_2015-282][^cite_note-Energy_gov_Fast_Facts-283] To generate the same amount of electricity annually (taking into account [capacity factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capacity_factor)) from [solar PV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_PV) would require about 60 square miles (160 km<sup>2</sup>), and from a wind farm about 310 square miles (800 km<sup>2</sup>).[^cite_note-NEI_news_2015-282][^cite_note-Energy_gov_Fast_Facts-283] Not included in this, is land required for the associated transmission lines, water supply, rail lines, mining and processing of nuclear fuel, and for waste disposal.[^cite_note-284]

## Research

### Advanced fission reactor designs

Main article: [Generation IV reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generation_IV_reactor)

Current fission reactors in operation around the world are [second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generation_II_reactor) or [third generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generation_III_reactor) systems, with most of the first-generation systems having been already retired. Research into advanced [generation IV reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generation_IV_reactor) types was officially started by the Generation IV International Forum (GIF) based on eight technology goals, including to improve economics, safety, proliferation resistance, natural resource use and the ability to consume existing nuclear waste in the production of electricity. Most of these reactors differ significantly from current operating light water reactors, and are expected to be available for commercial construction after 2030.[^cite_note-285]

### Hybrid fusion-fission

Main article: [Nuclear fusion–fission hybrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fusion%E2%80%93fission_hybrid)

Hybrid nuclear power is a proposed means of generating power by the use of a combination of nuclear fusion and fission processes. The concept dates to the 1950s and was briefly advocated by [Hans Bethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Bethe) during the 1970s, but largely remained unexplored until a revival of interest in 2009, due to delays in the realization of pure fusion. When a sustained nuclear fusion power plant is built, it has the potential to be capable of extracting all the fission energy that remains in spent fission fuel, reducing the volume of nuclear waste by orders of magnitude, and more importantly, eliminating all actinides present in the spent fuel, substances which cause security concerns.[^cite_note-hybrid-286]

### Fusion



The US has been counting on private industry to lead in fusion power, while more recently China's government has made fusion a national priority.

[^cite_note-NYTimes_20251213-287]

&#x20;In 2025, \$2.1 billion was poured into a single Chinese state-owned fusion company, an amount two and a half times the U.S. Energy Department's annual fusion budget.

[^cite_note-NYTimes_20251213-287]



Schematic of the [ITER](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITER) [tokamak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokamak) under construction in France

Main articles: [Nuclear fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fusion) and [Fusion power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fusion_power)

[Nuclear fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fusion_power) reactions have the potential to be safer and generate less radioactive waste than fission.[^cite_note-288][^cite_note-WorldEnergyCouncil-289] These reactions appear potentially viable, though technically quite difficult and have yet to be created on a scale that could be used in a functional power plant. Fusion power has been under theoretical and experimental investigation since the 1950s. [Nuclear fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fusion) research is underway but fusion energy is not likely to be commercially widespread before 2050.[^cite_note-fusion2-290][^cite_note-fusion3-291]

Several experimental nuclear fusion reactors and facilities exist. The largest and most ambitious international nuclear fusion project currently in progress is [ITER](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITER), a large [tokamak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokamak) under construction in France. ITER is planned to pave the way for commercial fusion power by demonstrating self-sustained nuclear fusion reactions with positive energy gain. Construction of the ITER facility began in 2007, but the project has run into many delays and budget overruns. The facility is now not expected to begin operations until the year 2034.[^cite_note-292] A follow on commercial nuclear fusion power station, [DEMO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DEMOnstration_Power_Station), has been proposed.[^cite_note-ITERorg-293][^cite_note-EFDA_Activities-294] There are also suggestions for a power plant based upon a different fusion approach, that of an [inertial fusion power plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_fusion_power_plant).

Fusion-powered electricity generation was initially believed to be readily achievable, as fission-electric power had been. However, the extreme requirements for continuous reactions and [plasma containment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_containment) led to projections being extended by several decades. In 2020, more than 80 years after [the first attempts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_nuclear_fusion#1930s), commercialization of fusion power production was thought to be unlikely before 2050.[^cite_note-ITERorg-293][^cite_note-fusion2-290][^cite_note-fusiongua-295][^cite_note-fusion3-291][^cite_note-fusion4-296]

To enhance and accelerate the development of fusion energy, the [United States Department of Energy](62866.md) (DOE) granted \$46 million to eight firms, including [Commonwealth Fusion Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_Fusion_Systems) and [Tokamak Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokamak_Energy) Inc, in 2023. This ambitious initiative aims to introduce pilot-scale fusion within a decade.[^cite_note-297]
