# Radionuclide

Not to be confused with [radionucleotide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radionucleotide_\(disambiguation\)).



Chart of known [nuclides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclides) as of 2013. The vast majority are radionuclides. (Note: some observationally stable nuclides, such as tungsten's, are marked as radionuclides, others are not.)

A **radionuclide** (**radioactive nuclide**, **radioisotope**, **radioactive isotope**, or **unstable isotope**[^cite_note-1]) is a [nuclide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclide) that is unstable and known to undergo [radioactive decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_decay) into a different nuclide, which may be another radionuclide (see [decay chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decay_chain)) or be stable.&#x20;

[Radioactive decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_decay) is a random [process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Process) at the level of single [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom): it is impossible to [predict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prediction) when one particular atom will decay.[^cite_note-not-predict-2][^cite_note-3] For a collection of atoms of a single nuclide, their average decay rate can be measured, and its [half-life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-life) (*t*<sub>1/2</sub>) calculated. Half-lives vary by 55 orders of magnitude and have no known limits.

All [chemical elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_element) have radionuclides - even the lightest, [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen), has a well-known radionuclide, [tritium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tritium) (though [helium](13256.md), [lithium](17561.md), and [boron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boron) have none with half-life over a second). Elements heavier than [lead](17747.md) ([*Z*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_number) > 82), and the elements [technetium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technetium) and [promethium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Promethium), have only radionuclides and do not exist in stable forms, though [bismuth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bismuth) can be treated as stable with the half-life of [its natural isotope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bismuth-209) being over a trillion times longer than the current [age of the universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_the_universe).

[Radiation](25856.md) emitted by radionuclides is almost always [ionizing radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionizing_radiation) and exposure above a certain level can cause damage to living organisms.[^cite_note-4]

## Production and effects

Artificial production methods of radionuclides include [neutron sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_source) such as [nuclear reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_reactors), as well as [particle accelerators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_accelerators) such as [cyclotrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclotrons).

The radiation from radionuclides generally has a harmful effect on [organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organism) including [humans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humans), although low levels of exposure occur naturally. The degree of harm depends on the [nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature) and extent of the radiation ([alpha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_decay), [beta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_decay), [gamma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_ray), or [neutron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_radiation)), the amount and nature of exposure (close contact, [inhalation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inhalation) or [ingestion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ingestion)), and the element's biochemical properties ([toxicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toxicity)). Increased risk of [cancer](105219.md) is unavoidable, and worse cases induce [cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation-induced_cancer), [chronic radiation syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronic_radiation_syndrome) or [acute radiation syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_radiation_syndrome). Radionuclides can be used as [weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weaponized) by the [fallout effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fallout) of [nuclear weapons](21785.md) and by [radiological weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiological_weapons).

Radionuclides are used in [nuclear medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_medicine) for both diagnosis and treatment. The term “[theranostics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theranostics)” (or “theragnostics”) refers to the combination of diagnosis and therapy of a disease within a single medical procedure or technique. An imaging tracer made with radionuclides is a [radioactive tracer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_tracer). [Radionuclide therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radionuclide_therapy) is a form of [radiotherapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiotherapy). A [pharmaceutical drug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmaceutical_drug) made with radionuclides is called a [radiopharmaceutical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiopharmaceutical).

## Overview

Radionuclides occur naturally and are artificially produced in [nuclear reactors](22151.md), [cyclotrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclotron), [particle accelerators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_accelerator) or [radionuclide generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radionuclide_generator). There are 735 known radionuclides with half-lives longer than an hour (see [list of nuclides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_nuclides)); 35 of those are [primordial radionuclides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primordial_radionuclide) whose presence on Earth has persisted from its formation, and another 62 are detectable in nature, continuously produced either as [daughter products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decay_product) of primordial radionuclides or by [cosmic radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_ray). More than 2400 radionuclides have half-lives less than 60 [minutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minutes). Most of those are only produced artificially, and have very short half-lives. For comparison, there are 251 [stable nuclides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stable_nuclide).&#x20;

### Natural

On Earth, naturally occurring radionuclides fall into three categories: primordial radionuclides, secondary radionuclides, and [cosmogenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmogenic) radionuclides.&#x20;

- Radionuclides are produced in [stellar nucleosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_nucleosynthesis) and [supernova explosions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernova_explosions) along with stable nuclides. Most decay quickly, but some can be observed astronomically and can play a part in understanding astrophysical processes. Primordial radionuclides, such as [uranium](31743.md) and [thorium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorium), still exist because their [half-lives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-life) are so long (>100 million years) that the Earth's initial content has not yet completely decayed. Some radionuclides have half-lives so long (many times the age of the universe) that decay has only recently been detected, and for most practical purposes they can be considered stable, most notably [bismuth-209](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bismuth-209): detection of this decay meant that [bismuth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bismuth) was no longer considered stable. It is possible that decay may be observed in other nuclides now considered stable, adding to the list of primordial radionuclides.

- Secondary radionuclides are radiogenic isotopes derived from the decay of primordial radionuclides. They have shorter half-lives than primordial radionuclides. They arise in the [decay chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decay_chain) of the primordial isotopes [thorium-232](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorium-232), [uranium-238](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-238), and [uranium-235](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-235) - such as the natural isotopes of [polonium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polonium) and [radium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radium) - some are also produced by natural [fission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_fission) and other [nucleogenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleogenic) processes.

- [Cosmogenic isotopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmogenic_isotopes), such as [carbon-14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon-14), are present because they are continually being formed on Earth, typically in the atmosphere, due to the action of [cosmic rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_ray).

Many of these radionuclides exist only in trace amounts in nature, including all cosmogenic nuclides. Secondary radionuclides in a [decay chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decay_chain) will occur in proportion to their half-lives, so short-lived ones will be very rare. For example, polonium can be found in [uranium](31743.md) ores at a concentration about 1 part 10<sup>10</sup> of uranium (0.1 mg per [metric ton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_ton)) by calculating the ratio of half-lives of [polonium-210](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polonium-210) to [uranium-238](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-238), its ultimate parent.

### Nuclear fission

Radionuclides are produced as an unavoidable result of [nuclear fission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fission) and [nuclear explosions](21785.md). The process of nuclear fission creates a wide range of [fission products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fission_products), most of which are radionuclides. Further radionuclides are created from irradiation of the nuclear fuel (creating a range of [actinides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actinides)) and of the surrounding structures, yielding [activation products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activation_products). This complex mixture of radionuclides with different chemistries and radioactivity makes handling [nuclear waste](37257.md) and dealing with [nuclear fallout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fallout) particularly problematic.

### Synthetic



[Americium-241](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americium-241) emitting [alpha particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_particle) inserted into a [cloud chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud_chamber)

[Synthetic radionuclides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_radionuclide) are created in [nuclear reactors](22151.md) or by particle accelerators (not necesssarily on purpose) or as decay products of such:[^cite_note-5]

- As well as being extracted from nuclear waste, radioisotopes can be produced deliberately with nuclear reactors, exploiting the high flux of [neutrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron) present. These neutrons activate elements placed within the reactor. A typical product from a nuclear reactor is [iridium-192](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iridium-192), from activation of [iridium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iridium) targets. The elements that have a large propensity to take up neutrons in the reactor are said to have a high [neutron cross-section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_cross-section), but even at low cross-sections this process is generally economical.

- Particle accelerators such as [cyclotrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclotron) accelerate particles to bombard a target to produce radionuclides. Cyclotrons accelerate (most often) protons at a target to produce positron-emitting radionuclides, e.g. [fluorine-18](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorine-18).

- [Radionuclide generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radionuclide_generator), standard for many medical isotopes, contain a parent radionuclide that decays to produce a shorter-lived radioactive daughter. A typical example is the [technetium-99m generator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technetium-99m_generator), which employs [molybdenum-99](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molybdenum-99) produced in a reactor.

## Uses

Radionuclides are used in two major ways: either for their radiation alone ([irradiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irradiation), [nuclear batteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_battery)) or for the combination of chemical properties and their radiation (tracers, biopharmaceuticals). For scientific study they may be used for their chemical properties alone when there is no stable form of that element.

- In [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology), radionuclides (most often of [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon)) can serve as [radioactive tracers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_tracer) because they are chemically very similar to the nonradioactive nuclides, so most chemical, biological, and ecological processes treat them in a nearly identical way. One can then examine the result with a radiation detector, such as a [Geiger counter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geiger_counter), to determine where the provided atoms were incorporated. For example, one might culture plants in an environment in which the [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) contained radioactive carbon; then the parts of the plant that incorporate atmospheric carbon would be radioactive. Radionuclides can be used to monitor processes such as [DNA replication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA_replication) or [amino acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amino_acid) transport.

- In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics) and [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology) radionuclide [X-ray fluorescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_fluorescence) (conventional X-ray sources may also be used) is used to determine [chemical composition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_composition) of the [compound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_compound). [Radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray) from a radionuclide source hits the sample and excites characteristic X-rays in the sample. This radiation is registered and the chemical composition of the sample can be determined from the analysis of the measured spectrum. By measuring the energy of the characteristic radiation lines, it is possible to determine the [proton number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_number) of the [chemical element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_element) that emits the radiation, and by measuring the number of emitted [photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon), it is possible to determine the [concentration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentration) of individual chemical elements.

- In [nuclear medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_medicine), radioisotopes are used for diagnosis, treatment, and research. Radioactive chemical tracers emitting gamma rays or positrons can provide diagnostic information about internal anatomy and the functioning of specific organs, including the [human brain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_brain).

- In [industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_sector), and in [mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining), radiation from radionuclides may be used to examine welds, to detect leaks, to study the rate of wear, erosion and corrosion of metals, and for on-stream analysis of a wide range of minerals and fuels.

- In [spacecraft](37910.md), radionuclides are used to provide power and heat, notably through [radioisotope thermoelectric generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioisotope_thermoelectric_generator) (RTGs) and [radioisotope heater units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioisotope_heater_unit) (RHUs).

- In [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics), radionuclides help discover new physics ([physics beyond the Standard Model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics_beyond_the_Standard_Model)) by measuring the energy and momentum of their beta decay products (for example, [neutrinoless double beta decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrinoless_double_beta_decay) and the search for [weakly interacting massive particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weakly_interacting_massive_particle)).

  [^cite_note-9]

- In [ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecology), radionuclides are used to trace and analyze pollutants, to study the movement of surface water, and to measure water runoffs from rain and snow, as well as the flow rates of streams and rivers.

- In [geology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology), [archaeology](18951655.md), and [paleontology](23084.md), natural radionuclides are used to measure ages of rocks, minerals, and fossil materials. This is called [radiometric dating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiometric_dating).

### Household [smoke detectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoke_detector)



Americium-241 container in a smoke detector



[Americium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americium-241)-241 capsule as found in smoke detector. The circle of darker metal in the center is americium-241; the surrounding casing is aluminium.

Radionuclides are present in many homes as they are used inside the most common household [smoke detectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoke_detector). The radionuclide used is [americium-241](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americium-241), which is created by bombarding plutonium with neutrons in a nuclear reactor. It decays by emitting [alpha particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_particle) and [gamma radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_radiation) to become [neptunium-237](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neptunium-237). Smoke detectors use a very small quantity of <sup>241</sup>Am (about 0.29 micrograms per smoke detector) in the form of [americium dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americium_dioxide). <sup>241</sup>Am is used as it emits alpha particles which ionize the air in the detector's [ionization chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionization_chamber). A small electric voltage is applied to the ionized air which gives rise to a small electric current. In the presence of smoke, some of the ions are neutralized, thereby decreasing the current, which activates the detector's alarm.[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11]

## Impacts on organisms

Radionuclides that find their way into the environment may cause harmful effects as [radioactive contamination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_contamination). They can also cause damage if they are excessively used during treatment or in other ways exposed to living beings, by [radiation poisoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_poisoning). Potential health damage from exposure to radionuclides depends on a number of factors, and "can damage the functions of healthy tissue/organs. Radiation exposure can produce effects ranging from skin redness and hair loss, to [radiation burns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_burn) and [acute radiation syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_radiation_syndrome). Prolonged exposure can lead to cells being damaged and in turn lead to cancer. Signs of cancerous cells might not show up until years, or even decades, after exposure."[^cite_note-Fact_sheet_N°371-12]

## Summary table for classes of nuclides, stable and radioactive

Following is a summary table for the [list of 986 nuclides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_nuclides) with half-lives greater than one hour. A total of 251 nuclides have never been observed to decay, and are classically considered stable. Of these, 90 are believed to be absolutely stable except to [proton decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton_decay) (which has never been observed), while the rest are "[observationally stable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observationally_stable)" and theoretically can undergo radioactive decay with extremely long half-lives.

The remaining tabulated radionuclides have half-lives longer than 1 hour, and are well-characterized (see [list of nuclides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_nuclides) for a complete tabulation). They include 31 nuclides with measured half-lives longer than the estimated age of the universe (13.8 billion years[^cite_note-13]), and another four nuclides with half-lives long enough (> 100 million years) that they are radioactive [primordial nuclides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primordial_nuclide), and may be detected on Earth, having survived from their presence in interstellar dust since before the formation of the [Solar System](26903.md), about 4.6 billion years ago. Another 60+ short-lived nuclides can be detected naturally as daughters of longer-lived nuclides or cosmic-ray products. The remaining known nuclides are known solely from artificial [nuclear transmutation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_transmutation).

Numbers may change slightly in the future as some nuclides now classified as stable are observed to be radioactive with very long half-lives.

This is a summary table[^cite_note-14] for the 986 nuclides with half-lives longer than one hour (including those that are stable), given in [list of nuclides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_nuclides).
