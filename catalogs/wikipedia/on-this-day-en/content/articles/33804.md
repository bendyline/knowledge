**Wellington**[^cite_note-20] is [the capital city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_of_New_Zealand) of [New Zealand](4913064.md). It is located at the southwestern tip of the [North Island](62046.md), between [Cook Strait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cook_Strait) and the [Remutaka Range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remutaka_Range). Wellington is the [third-largest city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cities_in_New_Zealand) in New Zealand (second largest in the North Island),[^cite_note-23] and is the administrative centre of the [Wellington Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Region). It is the [world's southernmost capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_national_capitals_by_latitude) of a [sovereign state](23604120.md).[^cite_note-World_Record2-24] Wellington features a temperate maritime climate, and is the world's windiest city by average [wind speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_speed).[^cite_note-25]

[Māori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ori_people) oral tradition tells that [Kupe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kupe) discovered and explored the region in about the 10th century. The area was initially settled by [Māori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ori_people) [iwi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iwi) such as [Rangitāne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rangit%C4%81ne) and [Muaūpoko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mua%C5%ABpoko). The disruptions of the [Musket Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musket_Wars) led to them being overwhelmed by northern iwi such as [Te Āti Awa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Te_%C4%80ti_Awa) in the early 19th century.[^cite_note-\:2-26]

Wellington's current form was originally designed by Captain [William Mein Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Mein_Smith), the first Surveyor General for [Edward Wakefield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Wakefield_\(New_Zealand_politician\))'s [New Zealand Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Company), in 1840.[^cite_note-Wellington's_plan-27] Smith's plan included a series of interconnected [grid plans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grid_plan), expanding along valleys and lower hill slopes,[^cite_note-Wellington's_plan_2-28] but without actually taking the terrain into account.[^cite_note-Wellington's_plan-27] The Wellington [urban area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_areas_of_New_Zealand), which only includes urbanised areas within Wellington City, has a population of 209,800 as of June 2025.[^cite_note-NZ_population_data_2018-16] The wider Wellington [metropolitan area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_area), including the cities of [Lower Hutt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Hutt), [Porirua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porirua) and [Upper Hutt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Hutt), has a population of 433,900 as of June 2025.[^cite_note-NZ_population_data_2018-16] The city has served as [New Zealand's capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_of_New_Zealand) since 1865, a status that is not defined in legislation, but established by convention; the [New Zealand Government](8899161.md) and [Parliament](340639.md), the [Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_New_Zealand) and most of the public services are based in the city.[^cite_note-Levine-29]

Wellington's economy is primarily [service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Service_sector)-based, with an emphasis on government, finance, business services, and the film industry. It is the centre of New Zealand's [film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinema_of_New_Zealand) and special effects industries, and increasingly a hub for information technology and innovation,[^cite_note-30] with two public research universities. Wellington is one of New Zealand's chief seaports and serves both domestic and international shipping. The city is chiefly served by [Wellington Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Airport) in [Rongotai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rongotai), the country's [third-busiest airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_the_busiest_airports_in_New_Zealand). Wellington's transport network includes [train and bus lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metlink_Wellington), which reach as far as the [Kāpiti Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C4%81piti_Coast_District) and [the Wairarapa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wairarapa), and ferries connect the city to the [South Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Island).

Often referred to as New Zealand's cultural capital, the culture of Wellington is diverse and often youth-driven.[^cite_note-31][^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33] One of the [world's most liveable cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World's_most_liveable_cities), the 2021 [Global Livability Ranking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Liveability_Ranking) tied Wellington with [Tokyo](30057.md) as fourth in the world.[^cite_note-34] From 2017 to 2018, [Deutsche Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsche_Bank) ranked it first in the world for both liveability and non-pollution.[^cite_note-mercer.com-35][^cite_note-36] Cultural precincts such as [Cuba Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuba_Street) and [Newtown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newtown,_New_Zealand) are renowned for creative innovation, "[op shops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charity_shop)", historic character, and [food](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_cuisine). Wellington is a leading [financial centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_centre) in the [Asia-Pacific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia-Pacific) region, being ranked 46th in the world by the [Global Financial Centres Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Financial_Centres_Index) for 2024. The [global city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_city) has grown from a bustling Māori settlement, to a colonial outpost, and from there to an [Australasian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australasia) capital that has experienced a "remarkable creative resurgence".[^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40]

## Toponymy

Wellington takes its name from [Arthur Wellesley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Wellesley,_1st_Duke_of_Wellington), the first [Duke of Wellington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duke_of_Wellington_\(title\)) and victor of the [Battle of Waterloo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Waterloo) (1815): his title comes from the town of [Wellington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington,_Somerset) in the [English county](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counties_of_England) of [Somerset](51763.md). It was named in November 1840 by the original settlers of the [New Zealand Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Company) on the suggestion of the directors of the same, in recognition of the Duke's strong support for the company's principles of colonisation and his "strenuous and successful defence against its enemies of the measure for colonising [South Australia](26716.md)". One of the founders of the settlement, [Edward Jerningham Wakefield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Jerningham_Wakefield), reported that the settlers "took up the views of the directors with great cordiality and the new name was at once adopted".[^cite_note-Edward-41]

In the [Māori language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ori_language), Wellington has three names:

- *[Te Whanganui-a-Tara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Te_Whanganui-a-Tara)*, meaning "the great harbour of Tara", refers to [Wellington Harbour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Harbour).

The legendary Māori explorer [Kupe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kupe), a chief from [Hawaiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaiki) (the homeland of Polynesian explorers, of unconfirmed geographical location, not to be confused with [Hawaii](13270.md)), was said to have stayed in the harbour prior to 1000 CE.[^cite_note-WCC_Māori_hist-43] Here, it is said he had a notable impact on the area, with local mythology stating he named the two islands in the harbour after his daughters, [Matiu (Somes Island)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matiu_/_Somes_Island), and [Mākaro (Ward Island)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81karo_/_Ward_Island).[^cite_note-50]

In [New Zealand Sign Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Sign_Language), the name is signed by raising the index, middle, and ring fingers of one hand, palm forward, to form a "W", and shaking it slightly from side to side twice.[^cite_note-51]

The city's location close to the mouth of the narrow Cook Strait leaves it vulnerable to strong gales, leading to the [nickname](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickname) of "Windy Wellington".[^cite_note-windy-52]

## History

### Māori settlement



Wellington – statue of [Kupe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kupe) Raiatea with his wife Te Aparangi

In [Māori mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ori_mythology), the legendary Polynesian explorer Kupe, a chief from [Hawaiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaiki) (the homeland of Polynesian explorers, of unconfirmed geographical location, not to be confused with [Hawaii](13270.md)), was said to have stayed in the harbour from c. 925.[^cite_note-WCC_Māori_hist-43][^cite_note-\:1-53] A later Māori explorer, Whatonga, named the harbour *[Te Whanganui-a-Tara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Te_Whanganui-a-Tara)* after his son Tara.[^cite_note-54] Before the 1820s, most of the inhabitants of the Wellington region identified as being Whatonga's descendants.[^cite_note-55]

At about 1820, the people living there were Ngāti Ira and other groups who claimed descent from the explorer Whatonga, including [Rangitāne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rangit%C4%81ne) and [Muaūpoko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mua%C5%ABpoko).[^cite_note-\:2-26] However, these groups were eventually forced out of *Te Whanganui-a-Tara* by a series of migrations by other [iwi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iwi) (Māori tribes) from the north.[^cite_note-\:2-26] The migrating groups were [Ngāti Toa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ng%C4%81ti_Toa), which came from [Kāwhia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kawhia_Harbour), Ngāti Rangatahi, from near [Taumarunui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taumarunui), and [Te Ātiawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Te_%C4%80ti_Awa), [Ngāti Tama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ng%C4%81ti_Tama), [Ngāti Mutunga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ng%C4%81ti_Mutunga), Taranaki and [Ngāti Ruanui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ng%C4%81ti_Ruanui) from [Taranaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taranaki). Ngāti Mutunga later moved on to the [Chatham Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chatham_Islands). The [Waitangi Tribunal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waitangi_Tribunal) has found that at the time of the signing of the [Treaty of Waitangi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Waitangi) in 1840, Te Ātiawa, Taranaki, Ngāti Ruanui, Ngāti Tama, and Ngāti Toa held [mana whenua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mana_whenua) interests in the area, through conquest and occupation.[^cite_note-\:2-26]

### Early European settlement



Lambton Harbour, Port Nicholson, c. 1840

Steps towards [European settlement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_settlers_in_New_Zealand) in the area began in 1839, when Colonel [William Wakefield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Wakefield) arrived to purchase land for the [New Zealand Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Company) to sell to prospective [British](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire) settlers.[^cite_note-\:2-26] Before this time, the Māori inhabitants had had contact with Pākehā whalers and traders.[^cite_note-56]

&#x20;Early Map of Wellington – Wellington County District

&#x20;This early map of Wellington County District shows several important historical sites, including Māori [pā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C4%81), pathways, [wāhi tapu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marae), and pre-1840 battle sites, as well as battle sites from the New Zealand Land Wars.

European settlement began with the arrival of an advance party of the New Zealand Company on the ship *[Tory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Company_ships#Tory)* on 20 September 1839, followed by 150 settlers on the *[Aurora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Company_ships#Aurora)* on 22 January 1840. Thus, the Wellington settlement preceded the signing of the [Treaty of Waitangi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Waitangi) (on 6 February 1840). The 1840 settlers constructed their first homes at [Petone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petone) (which they called Britannia for a time) on the flat area at the mouth of the [Hutt River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutt_River_\(New_Zealand\)). Within months that area proved swampy and flood-prone, and most of the newcomers transplanted their settlement across Wellington Harbour to [Thorndon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorndon,_New_Zealand) in present-day Wellington.[^cite_note-57] The town's layout was originally designed by Captain [William Mein Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Mein_Smith), the first Surveyor General for the New Zealand Company, in 1840.[^cite_note-Wellington's_plan-27] Smith's plan included a series of interconnected [grid plans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grid_plan), expanding along valleys and lower hill slopes,[^cite_note-Wellington's_plan_2-28] but without actually taking the terrain into account.[^cite_note-Wellington's_plan-27] Early growth was quick, with 700 buildings constructed by 1843. By 1865, the city had only grown to 1,200 buildings, the majority in [Te Aro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Te_Aro), with [Newtown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newtown,_New_Zealand) largely undeveloped.[^cite_note-58]

### National capital

See also: [Capital of New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_of_New_Zealand)



[Thorndon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorndon,_New_Zealand), the centre of government in Wellington, c. 1929. The original [Government House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_House,_Wellington) (now the site of the [Beehive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beehive_\(New_Zealand\))), [Parliament Buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Parliament_Buildings) and [Turnbull House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turnbull_House) are in the background.

The capital was moved to Wellington in 1865.[^cite_note-Levine-29]

Wellington became the capital city in place of [Auckland](18660332.md), which [William Hobson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Hobson) had made the capital in 1841. The [New Zealand Parliament](340639.md) had first met in Wellington on 7 July 1862, temporarily; in November 1863, the [Prime Minister of New Zealand](106321.md), [Alfred Domett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Domett), placed a resolution before Parliament in Auckland that "... it has become necessary that the [seat of government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seat_of_government) ... should be transferred to some suitable locality in [Cook Strait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cook_Strait) \[region]." There had been some concerns that the more populous [South Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Island) (where the goldfields were located) would choose to form a separate colony in the [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire). Several commissioners (delegates) invited from Australia, chosen for their neutral status, declared that the city was a suitable location because of its central location in New Zealand and its good [harbour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Harbour); it was believed that the whole [Royal Navy](26061.md) fleet could fit into the harbour.[^cite_note-59] Wellington's status as the capital is a result of [constitutional convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_convention_\(political_custom\)) rather than statute.[^cite_note-Levine-29]

Wellington is New Zealand's [political](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics_of_New_Zealand) centre, housing the nation's major government institutions. The New Zealand Parliament relocated to the new capital city, having spent the first ten years of its existence in Auckland.[^cite_note-60] A session of parliament officially met in the capital for the first time on 26 July 1865. At that time, the population of Wellington was just 4,900.[^cite_note-61]

The [Government Buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Government_Buildings,_Wellington) were constructed at [Lambton Quay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambton_Quay) in 1876. The site housed the original [government departments in New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_sector_organisations_in_New_Zealand). The public service rapidly expanded beyond the capacity of the building, with the first department leaving shortly after it was opened; by 1975 only the Education Department remained, and by 1990 the building was empty. The capital city is also the location of the highest court, the [Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_New_Zealand), and the historic former High Court building (opened 1881) has been, which enlarged and restored for its use. The Governor-General's residence, [Government House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_House,_Wellington) (the current building completed in 1910) is situated in [Newtown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newtown,_New_Zealand), opposite the [Basin Reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basin_Reserve). [Premier House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premier_House) (built in 1843 for Wellington's first mayor, [George Hunter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Hunter_\(mayor\))), the official residence of the [prime minister](106321.md), is in [Thorndon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorndon,_New_Zealand) on Tinakori Road.

In 1903 Wellington annexed the [Melrose Borough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melrose_Borough),[^cite_note-62] in 1919 it annexed the [Borough of Onslow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borough_of_Onslow) and [Karori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karori) and [Miramar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miramar,_New_Zealand) boroughs in 1920. The [Johnsonville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnsonville,_New_Zealand) Town District was annexed in 1953.[^cite_note-63]

Over six months in 1939 and 1940, Wellington hosted the [New Zealand Centennial Exhibition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Centennial_Exhibition), celebrating a century since the signing of the [Treaty of Waitangi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Waitangi). Held on 55 acres of land at Rongotai, it featured three exhibition courts, grand Art Deco-style edifices and a hugely popular three-acre amusement park. Wellington attracted more than 2.5 million visitors at a time when New Zealand's population was 1.6 million.[^cite_note-64]

## Geography



Satellite view of the Wellington area

Wellington is at the south-western tip of the [North Island](62046.md) on [Cook Strait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cook_Strait), separating the North and South Islands. On a clear day, the snowcapped [Kaikōura Ranges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaik%C5%8Dura_Ranges) are visible to the south across the strait. To the north stretch the golden beaches of the [Kāpiti Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C4%81piti_Coast). On the east, the [Remutaka Range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remutaka_Range) divides Wellington from the broad plains of the [Wairarapa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wairarapa), a [wine region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_wine-producing_regions) of national notability.

With a [latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latitude) of 41° 17' South, Wellington is the [southernmost capital city in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_southernmost_items).[^cite_note-World_Record-65]



Wellington City Centre and harbour from [Mount Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Victoria_\(Wellington_hill\))

Wellington is more densely populated than most other cities in New Zealand due to the restricted amount of land that is available between its harbour and the surrounding hills. It has very few open areas in which to expand, and this has brought about the development of the suburban towns. Because of its location in the [Roaring Forties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roaring_Forties) and its exposure to the winds blowing through [Cook Strait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cook_Strait), Wellington is the world's windiest city, with an average wind speed of 27 km/h (17 mph).[^cite_note-66]



View of the [Wellington Harbour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Harbour) from [Mount Cook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Cook,_Wellington)

Wellington's scenic natural harbour and green hillsides adorned with tiered suburbs of colonial villas are popular with tourists. The central business district (CBD) is close to Lambton Harbour, an arm of [Wellington Harbour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Harbour), which lies along an active [geological fault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fault_\(geology\)), clearly evident on its straight western shore. The land to the west of this rises abruptly, meaning that many suburbs sit high above the centre of the city. There is a network of bush walks and reserves maintained by the [Wellington City Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_City) and local volunteers. These include [Ōtari-Wilton's Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%8Ctari-Wilton's_Bush), dedicated to the protection and propagation of native plants. The Wellington region has 500 square kilometres (190 sq mi) of regional parks and forests. In the east is the [Miramar Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miramar_Peninsula), connected to the rest of the city by a low-lying isthmus at [Rongotai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rongotai), the site of [Wellington Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Airport). Industry has developed mainly in the [Hutt Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutt_Valley), where there are food-processing plants, engineering industries, vehicle assembly and oil refineries.[^cite_note-67]

The narrow entrance to the harbour is to the east of the Miramar Peninsula, and contains the dangerous shallows of [Barrett Reef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barrett_Reef), where many ships have been wrecked (notably the inter-island ferry [TEV *Wahine*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TEV_Wahine) in 1968).[^cite_note-68] The harbour has three islands: [Matiu/Somes Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matiu/Somes_Island), [Makaro/Ward Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makaro/Ward_Island) and [Mokopuna Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mokopuna_Island). Only Matiu/Somes Island is large enough for habitation. It has been used as a quarantine station for people and animals, and was an [internment camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internment) during both world wars. It is a conservation island, providing refuge for [endangered species](20110668.md), much like [Kapiti Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kapiti_Island) farther up the coast. There is access during daylight hours by ferry.

### Geology

Wellington suffered serious damage in a series of [earthquakes in 1848](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1848_Marlborough_earthquake)[^cite_note-69] and from another earthquake in 1855. The [1855 Wairarapa earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1855_Wairarapa_earthquake) occurred on the [Wairarapa Fault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wairarapa_Fault) to the north and east of Wellington. It was probably the most powerful earthquake in recorded New Zealand history,[^cite_note-70] with an estimated magnitude of at least 8.2 on the [Moment magnitude scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moment_magnitude_scale). It caused vertical movements of two to three metres over a large area, including raising land out of the harbour and turning it into a tidal swamp. Much of this land was subsequently [reclaimed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reclamation_of_Wellington_Harbour) and is now part of the central business district. For this reason, the street named [Lambton Quay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambton_Quay) is 100 to 200 metres (325 to 650 ft) from the harbour – plaques set into the footpath mark the shoreline in [1840](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_New_Zealand_history#1840s), indicating the extent of reclamation. The [1942 Wairarapa earthquakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1942_Wairarapa_earthquakes) caused considerable damage in Wellington.

The area has high seismic activity even by New Zealand standards, with a major fault, the [Wellington Fault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Fault), running through the centre of the city and several others nearby. Several hundred minor faults lines have been identified within the urban area. Inhabitants, particularly in high-rise buildings, typically notice several earthquakes every year. For many years after the 1855 earthquake, the majority of buildings were made entirely from wood. The 1996-restored [Government Buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Buildings_\(Wellington,_New_Zealand\))[^cite_note-71] near Parliament is the largest wooden building in the Southern Hemisphere. While masonry and [structural steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_steel) have subsequently been used in building construction, especially for office buildings, [timber framing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timber_framing) remains the primary structural component of almost all residential construction. Residents place their confidence in good [building regulations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Building_code), which became more stringent in the 20th century. Since the Canterbury earthquakes of [2010](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Canterbury_earthquake) and [2011](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Christchurch_earthquake), earthquake readiness has become even more of an issue, with buildings declared by [Wellington City Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_City_Council) to be earthquake-prone,[^cite_note-72][^cite_note-73] and the costs of meeting new standards.[^cite_note-74][^cite_note-75]

Every five years, a year-long [slow earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slow_earthquake) occurs beneath Wellington, stretching from Kāpiti to the [Marlborough Sounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marlborough_Sounds). It was first measured in 2003, and reappeared in 2008 and 2013.[^cite_note-76] They release as much energy as a magnitude 7 earthquake, but due to their slow nature, cause no damage.[^cite_note-77]

During July and August 2013 there were many earthquakes, mostly in Cook Strait near [Seddon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seddon,_New_Zealand). The sequence started on 21 July 2013 when the magnitude 6.5 [Seddon earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2013_Seddon_earthquake) hit the city, but no tsunami report was confirmed nor any major damage.[^cite_note-78] On 16 August 2013 the [Lake Grassmere earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2013_Lake_Grassmere_earthquake) struck, this time magnitude 6.6, but again no major damage occurred, though many buildings were evacuated.[^cite_note-79] On 20 January 2014 [a rolling 6.2 magnitude earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Eket%C4%81huna_earthquake) struck the lower North Island 15 km east of [Eketāhuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eket%C4%81huna) and was felt in Wellington, but little damage was reported initially, except at [Wellington Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Airport) where one of the two giant eagle sculptures commemorating [The Hobbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hobbit) became detached from the ceiling.[^cite_note-80][^cite_note-81]

At two minutes after midnight on Monday 14 November 2016, the 7.8 magnitude [Kaikōura earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Kaik%C5%8Dura_earthquake), which was centred between Culverden and Kaikōura in the South Island, caused the Wellington CBD, [Victoria University of Wellington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_University_of_Wellington), and the [Wellington suburban rail network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_transport_in_the_Wellington_Region#Trains) to be largely closed for the day to allow inspections. The earthquake damaged a considerable number of buildings, with 65% of the damage being in Wellington. Subsequently, a number of recent buildings were demolished rather than being rebuilt, often a decision made by the insurer. Two of the buildings demolished were about eleven years old – the seven-storey [NZDF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NZDF) headquarters[^cite_note-82][^cite_note-83] and [Statistics House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistics_House) at [CentrePort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CentrePort_Wellington) on the waterfront.[^cite_note-84] The docks were closed for several weeks after the earthquake.[^cite_note-85]

### Climate

&#x20;Averaging 2,055 hours of sunshine per year, the climate of Wellington is temperate [marine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanic_climate), ([Köppen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification): *Cfb*, [Trewartha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trewartha_climate_classification): *Cflk bordering Cfbk*), generally moderate all year round with warm summers and cool to mild winters, and rarely sees temperatures above 27 °C (81 °F) or below 4 °C (39 °F). The hottest recorded temperature in the city is 31.1 °C (88 °F), while −1.9 °C (29 °F) is the coldest.[^cite_note-90] The city is notorious for its southerly blasts in winter, which may make the temperature feel much colder. It is generally very windy all year round[^cite_note-91] with high rainfall; average annual rainfall is 1,250 mm (49 in), June and July being the wettest months. [Frosts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frost) are quite common in the hill suburbs and the [Hutt Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutt_Valley) between May and September. Snow is very rare at low altitudes, although snow fell on the city and many other parts of the Wellington region during separate [events](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_New_Zealand_snowstorms) on 25 July 2011 and 15 August 2011 as well as on 4 August 2026.[^cite_note-92][^cite_note-93] Snow at higher altitudes is more common, with light flurries recorded in higher suburbs every few years.[^cite_note-94] Hail is relatively rare throughout winter, but can make driving conditions dangerous.[^cite_note-95] From 19 to 20 April 2026, Wellington experienced torrential downpours and heavy flash floods, causing the government to declare a State of Emergency.[^cite_note-96][^cite_note-97] Wellington had relatively warm and dry spells in the summers of 2021, (notably 2024), and 2025,[^cite_note-\:4-98] causing excessive water demand from residents.[^cite_note-99] Climate change has affected Wellington, as over a 5-year period from 2020 to 2025, it became much drier[^cite_note-\:4-98] and warmer.[^cite_note-100][^cite_note-101] On 29 January 2019, the suburb of Kelburn (instruments near the old [Metservice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metservice) building in the [Wellington Botanic Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Botanic_Garden)) reached 30.3 °C (87 °F), the highest temperature at that site since records began in 1927.[^cite_note-102]

## Architecture

Further information: [List of tallest buildings in Wellington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_in_Wellington)



The old [Public Trust Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Trust_Building) in Lambton Quay is an example of Edwardian architecture in Wellington, built from a solid granite and brick veneer.

Wellington showcases a variety of architectural styles from the past 150 years – 19th-century wooden cottages, such as the [Italianate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italianate) [Katherine Mansfield Birthplace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katherine_Mansfield_Birthplace) in Thorndon; streamlined [Art Deco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_Deco) structures such as the old [Wellington Free Ambulance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Free_Ambulance) headquarters, the Central Fire Station, Fountain Court Apartments, the [City Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_Gallery_Wellington), and the [former Post and Telegraph Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Former_Post_and_Telegraph_Building); and the curves and vibrant colours of post-modern architecture in the CBD.

&#x20;The oldest building is the 1858 [Nairn Street Cottage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nairn_Street_Cottage) in [Mount Cook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Cook,_Wellington).[^cite_note-127] The tallest building is the [Majestic Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majestic_Centre) on [Willis Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willis_Street) at 116 metres high, the second-tallest being the [structural expressionist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_expressionist) [Aon Centre (Wellington)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aon_Centre_\(Wellington\)) at 103 metres.[^cite_note-128] [Futuna Chapel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Futuna_Chapel) in [Karori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karori) is an iconic building designed by Māori architect John Scott and is architecturally considered one of the most significant New Zealand buildings of the 20th century.[^cite_note-129]&#x20;



[Old St Paul's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_St_Paul's,_Wellington) was the Anglican [pro-cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pro-cathedral), and is one of the oldest structures in Wellington.

[Old St Paul's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_St_Paul's,_Wellington) is an example of 19th-century [Gothic Revival architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_Revival_architecture) adapted to colonial conditions and materials, as is [St Mary of the Angels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Mary_of_the_Angels,_Wellington). [Sacred Heart Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacred_Heart_Cathedral,_Wellington) is a [Palladian Revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palladian_architecture) [Basilica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica) with the [Portico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portico) of a [Roman or Greek temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Temple). The [Museum of Wellington City & Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Wellington_City_&_Sea) in the [Bond Store](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Harbour_Board_Head_Office_and_Bond_Store) is in the [Second French Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_French_Empire) style, and the [Wellington Harbour Board Wharf Office Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Harbour_Board_Wharf_Office_Building) is in a late English Classical style. There are several restored theatre buildings: the [St James Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._James_Theatre,_Wellington), the [Opera House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opera_House,_Wellington) and the [Embassy Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embassy_Theatre,_Wellington).

[Te Ngākau Civic Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civic_Square,_Wellington) is surrounded by the [Town Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Town_Hall) and council offices, the [Michael Fowler Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Fowler_Centre), the [Wellington Central Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Central_Library), the [City-to-Sea Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City-to-Sea_bridge,_Wellington), and the [City Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_Gallery_Wellington).

As it is the capital city, there are many notable government buildings. The Executive Wing of [New Zealand Parliament Buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Parliament_Buildings), on the corner of Lambton Quay and Molesworth Street, was constructed between 1969 and 1981 and is commonly referred to as [the Beehive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beehive_\(New_Zealand\)). Across the road is the largest wooden building in the [Southern Hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Hemisphere),[^cite_note-130] part of the [old Government Buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Buildings_\(Wellington,_NZ\)) which now houses part of [Victoria University of Wellington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_University_of_Wellington)'s Law Faculty.



[Old Government Buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Government_Buildings,_Wellington), Lambton Quay, the second-largest wooden building in the world and the largest in the Southern Hemisphere

A modernist building housing the [Museum of New Zealand Te Papa Tongarewa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_New_Zealand_Te_Papa_Tongarewa) lies on the waterfront, on Cable Street. It is strengthened using [base isolation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_isolation)[^cite_note-131] – essentially seating the entire building on supports made from lead, steel and rubber that slow down the effect of an earthquake.

&#x20;Other notable buildings include [Wellington Town Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Town_Hall), [Wellington railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_railway_station), [Dominion Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominion_Museum) (now [Massey University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massey_University)), [Aon Centre (Wellington)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aon_Centre_\(Wellington\)), [Wellington Regional Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Regional_Stadium), and [Wellington Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Airport) at [Rongotai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rongotai). Leading architects include [Frederick Thatcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Thatcher), [Frederick de Jersey Clere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_de_Jersey_Clere), [W. Gray Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._Gray_Young), [Bill Alington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Alington), [Ian Athfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_Athfield), [Roger Walker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Walker_\(architect\)).&#x20;



[Te Wharewaka o Pōneke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Te_Wharewaka_o_P%C5%8Dneke) on the Wellington waterfront

Wellington contains many iconic sculptures and structures, such as [the Bucket Fountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bucket_Fountain) in [Cuba Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuba_Street,_Wellington) and *Invisible City* by [Anton Parsons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anton_Parsons) on Lambton Quay. Kinetic sculptures have been commissioned, such as the [Zephyrometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zephyrometer).[^cite_note-132] This 26-metre orange spike built for movement by artist Phil Price has been described as "tall, soaring and elegantly simple", which "reflects the swaying of the yacht masts in the Evans Bay Marina behind it" and "moves like the needle on the dial of a nautical instrument, measuring the speed of the sea or wind or vessel."[^cite_note-133]

Wellington has many different architectural styles, such as classic [Painted Ladies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Painted_ladies) in [Mount Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Victoria_\(Wellington_suburb\)), [Newtown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newtown,_New_Zealand) and [Oriental Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriental_Bay), Wooden [Art Deco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_Deco) houses spread throughout (especially further north in the [Hutt Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutt_Valley)), the classic masonry buildings in Cuba Street, [state houses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_housing_in_New_Zealand) particularly in the Hutt and Wellington's southern suburbs, [railway houses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railways_Department's_Housing_Scheme) in [Ngaio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ngaio,_New_Zealand) and other railway-side suburbs, large modern buildings in the city centre (such as the distinctive skyscraper called the [Majestic Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majestic_Centre)) and grand Victorian buildings common in the inner city as well.

## Housing and real estate

See also: [Housing in New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Housing_in_New_Zealand)



Apartments at [Oriental Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriental_Bay)

The early buildings of Wellington were mostly made from timber due to ample supply. The [1848 Marlborough earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1848_Marlborough_earthquake) destroyed most brick buildings leading to most buildings using timber. Fires tore through the city early on due to the large amount of wooden buildings and the first municipal government tried to establish a building code preventing the use of inflammable materials. The council's actions were controversial and opposed by local architects and businessmen. The regulations were gradually weakened due to lack of enforcement and a desire to keep construction busy during the [Long Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Depression). Despite the suggestion from [Charles Tringham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Tringham) to include regulations around earthquake susceptibility there was not any adoption of regulations designed to reduce the risk of collapse form an earthquake.[^cite_note-raupo2deco-134]<sup>: 51–63 </sup>

### House prices

#### Historic

Wellington experienced a real estate boom in the early 2000s and the effects of the international property bust at the start of 2007. In 2005, the market was described as "robust".[^cite_note-135] By 2008, property values had declined by about 9.3% over a 12-month period, according to one estimate. More expensive properties declined more steeply, sometimes by as much as 20%.[^cite_note-136] "From 2004 to early 2007, rental yields were eroded and positive cash flow in property investments disappeared as house values climbed faster than rents. Then that trend reversed and yields slowly began improving", according to two *[The New Zealand Herald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Zealand_Herald)* reporters writing in May 2009.[^cite_note-Andrea_Milner_and_Jonathan_Milne-137] In the middle of 2009, house prices had dropped, interest rates were low, and buy-to-let property investment was again looking attractive, particularly in the Lambton precinct, according to these two reporters.[^cite_note-Andrea_Milner_and_Jonathan_Milne-137]

#### Current

Since 2009, house prices in Wellington have increased significantly. In May 2021, the Real Estate Institute of New Zealand (REINZ) reported the median house price was \$1,057,000 in Wellington City, \$930,000 in Porirua, \$873,500 in Lower Hutt and \$828,000 in Upper Hutt, compared to a national median house price of \$820,000.[^cite_note-138] The substantial increase in house prices has made it difficult for first home buyers to purchase a home in the city and is also credited with pushing up the house prices in neighbouring cities like Porirua.[^cite_note-\:0-139] Wellington house prices peaked in February 2022, and by December 2023 had fallen by 25%.[^cite_note-140]

Housing costs have been identified as making it difficult for those in professions such as nursing, for example – to afford to live in Wellington.[^cite_note-141][^cite_note-142] The median rent in Wellington has also increased significantly in recent years to \$600 per week, higher even than Auckland.[^cite_note-143]

### Housing quality

Despite the high cost of housing in the capital, the quality of housing in Wellington has been criticised as being poor. 18.4% of houses in Wellington City are sometimes or always mouldy and 24% are sometimes or always damp.[^cite_note-144] Both of these are higher than the New Zealand average.

### Demographics



A row of classic [weatherboard houses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weatherboard) in the [Mount Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Victoria_\(Wellington_suburb\)) neighbourhood, emblematic of the style of the area

A Wellington City Council survey conducted in March 2009 found the typical central city apartment dweller was a New Zealand native aged 24 to 35 with a professional job in the downtown area, with household income higher than surrounding areas.[^cite_note-Apartment_Dwellers-145] Three-quarters (73%) walked to work or university, 13% travelled by car, 6% by bus, 2% bicycled (although 31% own bicycles), and did not travel very far since 73% worked or studied in the central city.[^cite_note-Apartment_Dwellers-145] The large majority (88%) did not have children in their apartments; 39% were couples without children; 32% were single-person households; 15% were groups of people flatting together.[^cite_note-Apartment_Dwellers-145] Most (56%) owned their apartment; 42% rented.[^cite_note-Apartment_Dwellers-145] The report continued: "The four most important reasons for living in an apartment were given as lifestyle and city living (23%), close to work (20%), close to shops and cafes (11%) and low maintenance (11%) ... City noise and noise from neighbours were the main turnoffs for apartment dwellers (27%), followed by a lack of outdoor space (17%), living close to neighbours (9%) and apartment size and a lack of storage space (8%)."[^cite_note-Apartment_Dwellers-145][^cite_note-146]

Households are primarily one-family, making up 66.9% of households, followed by single-person households (24.7%); there were fewer multiperson households and even fewer households containing two or more families. These counts are from the 2013 census for the Wellington region (which includes the surrounding area in addition to the four cities).[^cite_note-147]

[Wellington Harbour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Harbour) ranks as one of New Zealand's chief seaports and serves both domestic and international shipping. The port handles approximately 10.5 million tonnes of cargo on an annual basis,[^cite_note-148] importing petroleum products, motor vehicles, minerals and exporting meats, wood products, dairy products, wool, and fruit. Many [cruise ships](314855.md) also use the port.



[Lambton Quay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambton_Quay) in the central business district

The Government sector has long been a mainstay of the economy, which has typically risen and fallen with it. Traditionally, its central location meant it was the location of many head offices of various sectors – particularly finance, technology and heavy industry – many of which have since relocated to Auckland following economic deregulation and privatisation.[^cite_note-149][^cite_note-150]

In recent years, tourism, arts and culture, film, and [ICT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_and_communications_technology) have played a bigger role in the economy. Wellington's median income is well above the average in New Zealand,[^cite_note-151] and the highest of all New Zealand cities.[^cite_note-152] It has a much higher proportion of people with tertiary qualifications than the national average.[^cite_note-153] Major companies with their headquarters in Wellington include:

### Tourism

See also: [Tourism in New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism_in_New_Zealand)



Elephant House at [Wellington Zoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Zoo)

[Tourism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism) is a major contributor to the city's economy, injecting approximately NZ\$1.3 billion into the region annually and accounting for 9% of total FTE employment.[^cite_note-156] The city is consistently named as New Zealanders' favourite destination in the quarterly FlyBuys Colmar Brunton Mood of the Traveller survey[^cite_note-157] and it was ranked fourth in [Lonely Planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lonely_Planet) Best in Travel 2011's Top 10 Cities to Visit in 2011.[^cite_note-Lonely_Planet-158]

New Zealanders make up the largest visitor market, with 3.6 million visits each year; New Zealand visitors spend on average NZ\$2.4 million a day.[^cite_note-159] There are approximately 540,000 international visitors each year, who spend 3.7 million nights and NZ\$436 million. The largest international visitor market is Australia, with over 210,000 visitors, spending approximately NZ\$334 million annually.[^cite_note-160] It has been argued that the construction of the [Te Papa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Te_Papa) museum helped transform Wellington into a tourist destination.[^cite_note-161]

Wellington tourism is promoted by Positively Wellington Tourism, an organisation to promote regional tourism,[^cite_note-\:3-162] set up as a council controlled organisation by Wellington City Council in 1997.[^cite_note-163] The organisation's council funding comes through the Downtown Levy commercial rate.[^cite_note-164] In the decade to 2010, the city saw growth of over 60% in commercial guest nights. It has been promoted through a variety of campaigns and taglines, starting with the iconic Absolutely Positively Wellington advertisements.[^cite_note-165] The long-term domestic marketing strategy was a finalist in the 2011 CAANZ Media Awards.[^cite_note-166]



[Wellington Cable Car](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Cable_Car)

Popular tourist attractions include [Wellington Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Wellington_City_&_Sea), [Wellington Zoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Zoo), [Zealandia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zealandia_\(wildlife_sanctuary\)) and [Wellington Cable Car](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Cable_Car). [Cruise tourism](314855.md) is experiencing a major boom in line with nationwide development. The 2010/11 season saw 125,000 passengers and crew visits on 60 liners. There were 80 vessels booked for visits in the 2011/12 season – estimated to inject more than NZ\$31 million into the economy and representing a 74% increase in the space of two years.[^cite_note-167]

Wellington is a popular conference tourism destination due to its compact nature, cultural attractions, award-winning restaurants and access to government agencies. In the year ending March 2011, there were 6,495 conference events involving nearly 800,000 delegate days; this injected approximately NZ\$100 million into the economy.[^cite_note-168]

## Arts and culture

### Culture



[Cuba Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuba_Street,_Wellington) is considered the microcosm of Wellington's culture, being "quirky" and packed with retail and art, such as the [Bucket Fountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bucket_Fountain) (pictured).

Owing to the work of Positively Wellington Tourism in marketing it as "the coolest little capital",[^cite_note-\:3-162] the city has been injected into the global zeitgeist as exactly that.[^cite_note-169] It has been traditionally acclaimed as New Zealand's "cultural and creative capital".[^cite_note-170][^cite_note-171][^cite_note-172] The city is known for its coffee scene, with now-globally common foods and drinks such as the [flat white](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_white) perfected here.[^cite_note-173][^cite_note-174] Wellington has a strong coffee culture – the city has more cafés per capita than [New York City](645042.md) – and was pioneered by [Italian](385155.md) and [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) immigrants to areas such as [Mount Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Victoria_\(Wellington_suburb\)), [Island Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Island_Bay,_New_Zealand) and [Miramar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miramar,_New_Zealand).[^cite_note-175] Nascent influence is derived from [Ethiopian](6204551.md) migrants. Wellington's ethnically diverse population also includes significant [Malaysian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysian_New_Zealanders),[^cite_note-176] [Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_New_Zealanders), [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_New_Zealanders), [Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_New_Zealanders), [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_New_Zealanders), [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_New_Zealanders),[^cite_note-177] [Indian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_New_Zealanders), [Samoan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samoan_New_Zealanders) and indigenous [Taranaki Whānui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taranaki_Wh%C4%81nui_ki_te_Upoko_o_te_Ika) communities. Wellington is noted for is contributions to art, cuisine[^cite_note-178] and international filmmaking (with [Avatar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avatar_\(2009_film\)) and [The Lord of the Rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings_\(film_series\)) being largely produced in the city) among many other factors listed below. The [World of Wearable Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_of_Wearable_Art) (WOW) is an annual event that brings lots of visitors to Wellington every year.[^cite_note-179]

#### Film

Filmmakers [Sir Peter Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Jackson), [Sir Richard Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Taylor_\(filmmaker\)) and a growing team of creative professionals have turned the eastern suburb of [Miramar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miramar,_New_Zealand) into a film-making, post-production and special effects infrastructure centre, giving rise to the moniker '[Wellywood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellywood)'.[^cite_note-182][^cite_note-183] Jackson's companies include [Wētā Workshop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W%C4%93t%C4%81_Workshop), [Wētā FX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W%C4%93t%C4%81_FX), Camperdown Studios, post-production house [Park Road Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Park_Road_Post), and Stone Street Studios near Wellington Airport.[^cite_note-newzealand1-184][^cite_note-tws0910xx-185] Films shot partly or wholly in Wellington include the [*Lord of The Rings* trilogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings_\(film_series\)), *[King Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Kong_\(2005_film\))* and *[Avatar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avatar_\(2009_film\))*. Jackson described Wellington: "Well, it's windy. But it's actually a lovely place, where you're pretty much surrounded by water and the bay. The city itself is quite small, but the surrounding areas are very reminiscent of the hills up in northern California, like [Marin County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marin_County,_California) near San Francisco and the Bay Area climate and some of the architecture. Kind of a cross between that and Hawaii."[^cite_note-186]

Notable Wellington directors include [Jane Campion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Campion), [Geoff Murphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoff_Murphy), [Robert Sarkies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Sarkies), and [Taika Waititi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taika_Waititi).[^cite_note-187][^cite_note-188]



[Embassy Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embassy_Theatre,_Wellington)

Wellington has a large number of independent cinemas, including the [Embassy Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embassy_Theatre,_Wellington), Penthouse, the Roxy and Light House, which participate in film festivals throughout the year. Wellington has one of the country's highest turn-outs for the annual [New Zealand International Film Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_International_Film_Festivals). There are a number of other film festivals hosted in Wellington, such as Doc Edge (documentary),[^cite_note-189] the Japanese Film Festival[^cite_note-190] and Show Me Shorts (short films).[^cite_note-191]

In 2019, Wellington was designated as a City of Film by [UNESCO](21786641.md).[^cite_note-192]

## Cuisine

Wellington is characterised by small dining establishments, and its [café culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffee_culture) is internationally recognised, being known for its large number of coffeehouses.[^cite_note-Reid2014-198][^cite_note-199] Wellington restaurants offer cuisines including from Europe, Asia and Polynesia; for dishes that have a distinctly [New Zealand style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuisine_of_New_Zealand), there are lamb, beef, pork and venison, salmon, crayfish (lobster), [Bluff oysters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluff_oyster), [pāua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C4%81ua) (abalone), [mussels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mussel), [scallops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scallop), [pipis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paphies_australis) and [tuatua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuatua) (both New Zealand shellfish); kūmara (sweet potato); [kiwifruit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiwifruit) and [tamarillo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamarillo); and [pavlova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pavlova_\(dessert\)), the national dessert.[^cite_note-200]

The first local government for the city was the Wellington Borough Council, which was established in 1842 but dissolved 18 months later. In 1863 the [Wellington Provincial Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Provincial_Council) formed the Wellington Town Board. The Wellington Town Board was overseen by commissioners that had the power to levy rates, manage water supply, organise road layout, and was responsible for fire prevention.[^cite_note-raupo2deco-134]

Wellington city is administered by the [territorial authority](243907.md) of [Wellington City Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_City_Council). Its present mayor is [Andrew Little](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Little_\(New_Zealand_politician\)), who was elected in 2025.[^cite_note-Electionz_-_2025_Elections-6]

### National

Wellington is covered by four general electorates: [Mana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mana_\(New_Zealand_electorate\)), [Ōhāriu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%8Ch%C4%81riu), [Rongotai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rongotai_\(New_Zealand_electorate\)), and [Wellington Central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Central_\(New_Zealand_electorate\)). It is also covered by two Māori electorates: [Te Tai Hauāuru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Te_Tai_Hau%C4%81uru), and [Te Tai Tonga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Te_Tai_Tonga). Each electorate returns one member to the [New Zealand House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_House_of_Representatives). Two general electorates are held by the [Labour Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Labour_Party) and two are held by the [Green Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Green_Party) and the two Maori electorates are held by [Te Pāti Māori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Te_P%C4%81ti_M%C4%81ori).

[Victoria University of Wellington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_University_of_Wellington) has four campuses and works with a three-trimester system (beginning March, July, and November).[^cite_note-202] It enrolled 21,380 students in 2008; of these, 16,609 were full-time students. Of all students, 56% were female and 44% male. While the student body was primarily New Zealanders of European descent, 1,713 were Māori, 1,024 were Pacific students, 2,765 were international students. 5,751 degrees, diplomas and certificates were awarded. The university has 1,930 full-time employees.[^cite_note-203]

[Massey University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massey_University) has a Wellington campus known as the "creative campus" and offers courses in communication and business, engineering and technology, health and well-being, and creative arts. Its school of design was established in 1886 and has research centres for studying public health, sleep, Māori health, small & medium enterprises, disasters, and tertiary teaching excellence.[^cite_note-Massey_University-204] It combined with Victoria University to create the [New Zealand School of Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_School_of_Music).[^cite_note-Massey_University-204]

The [University of Otago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Otago) has a Wellington branch, with its Wellington School of Medicine and Health.

[Whitireia New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitireia_New_Zealand) has large campuses in Porirua, Wellington and Kapiti; the [Wellington Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Institute_of_Technology) and New Zealand's National Drama school, [Toi Whakaari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toi_Whakaari). The Wellington area has numerous primary and secondary schools.

## Transport

See also: [Public transport in the Wellington Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_transport_in_the_Wellington_Region), [List of bus routes in the Wellington Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_bus_routes_in_the_Wellington_Region), and [Cycling in Wellington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycling_in_Wellington)



Commuting patterns in the Wellington region during 2006; darker red lines indicate greater traffic. Source: Statistics New Zealand.

[^cite_note-205]

Wellington is served by [State Highway 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_Highway_1_\(New_Zealand\)) in the west and [State Highway 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_Highway_2_\(New_Zealand\)) in the east, meeting at the [Ngauranga Interchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ngauranga_Interchange) north of the city centre, where SH 1 runs through the city to the airport. There are two other state highways in the wider region: [State Highway 58](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_Highway_58_\(New_Zealand\)) which provides a direct connection between the Hutt Valley and Porirua, and [State Highway 59](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_Highway_59_\(New_Zealand\)) which follows a coastal route between Linden and Mackays Crossing and was previously part of SH 1.[^cite_note-SH_59_renumbering-206][^cite_note-State_Highway_59_switch_confirmed_for_December-207] Road access into the capital is constrained by the mountainous terrain – between Wellington and the Kāpiti Coast, SH 1 passes through the steep and narrow Wainui Saddle, nearby SH 59 travels along the Centennial Highway, a narrow section of road between the Paekākāriki Escarpment and the [Tasman Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tasman_Sea), and between Wellington and Wairarapa SH 2 transverses the [Rimutaka Ranges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rimutaka_Range) on a similar narrow winding road. Wellington has two motorways: the [Johnsonville–Porirua Motorway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnsonville%E2%80%93Porirua_Motorway) (largely part of SH 1, with the northernmost section part of SH 59) and the [Wellington Urban Motorway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Urban_Motorway) (entirely part of SH 1), which in combination with a small non-motorway section in the Ngauranga Gorge connect Porirua with Wellington city. A third motorway in the wider region, the [Transmission Gully Motorway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmission_Gully_Motorway) forming part of the SH 1 route and officially opened on 30 March 2022, leaves the Johnsonville-Porirua Motorway at the boundary between Wellington and Porirua and provides the main route between Wellington and the wider North Island.[^cite_note-208]



[Wellington railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_railway_station)

Bus transport in Wellington is supplied by several different operators under the banner of Metlink. Buses serve almost every part of Wellington city, with most of them running along the "Golden Mile" from [Wellington railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_railway_station) to [Courtenay Place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courtenay_Place,_Wellington). Until October 2017, there were nine [trolleybus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trolleybus) routes, all other buses running on [diesel](81761.md). The [trolleybus network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trolleybuses_in_Wellington) was the last public system of its kind in the [Southern Hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Hemisphere).[^cite_note-209]

Wellington lies at the southern end of the [North Island Main Trunk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Island_Main_Trunk) railway (NIMT) and the [Wairarapa Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wairarapa_Line), converging on [Wellington railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_railway_station) at the northern end of central Wellington. Two long-distance services leave from Wellington: the [Capital Connection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_Connection), for commuters from [Palmerston North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palmerston_North), and the [Northern Explorer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Explorer) to [Auckland](18660332.md).



[Matangi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_FP_class_electric_multiple_unit) [electric multiple unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_multiple_unit)

Four [electrified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railway_electrification_system) [suburban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suburban_rail) lines radiate from Wellington railway station to the outer suburbs to the north of Wellington – the [Johnsonville Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnsonville_Line) through the hillside suburbs north of central Wellington; the [Kāpiti Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C4%81piti_Line) along the NIMT to Waikanae on the Kāpiti Coast via Porirua and Paraparaumu; the [Melling Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melling_Line) to Lower Hutt via Petone; and the [Hutt Valley Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutt_Valley_Line) along the Wairarapa Line via Waterloo and [Taitā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tait%C4%81,_New_Zealand) to Upper Hutt. A diesel-hauled carriage service, the [Wairarapa Connection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wairarapa_Connection), connects several times daily to Masterton in the Wairarapa via the 8.8-kilometre-long (5.5 mi) [Rimutaka Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rimutaka_Tunnel). Combined, these five services carry 11.64 million passengers per year.[^cite_note-MoT-ridership-210] [CentrePort Wellington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CentrePort_Wellington) is the operator of the port of Wellington, and provides infrastructure for shipping and cargo, including the commercial [wharves in Wellington Harbour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wharves_in_Wellington_Harbour). It also provides port services for the [Cook Strait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cook_Strait) [ferries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferries) to [Picton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picton,_New_Zealand) in the [South Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Island), operated by state-owned [Interislander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interislander) and private [Bluebridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strait_Shipping). Local ferries connect Wellington city centre with Eastbourne and Seatoun.[^cite_note-211]

[Wellington Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Airport) is 6 kilometres (3.7 mi) south-east of the city centre. It is serviced by flights from across New Zealand, Australia, and Fiji. Flights to other international destinations require a transfer at another airport, as aircraft range is limited by Wellington's short (2,081-metre or 6,827-foot) [runway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runway), which has become an issue in recent years regarding the Wellington region's economic performance.[^cite_note-212][^cite_note-213]

[Cycling in Wellington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycling_in_Wellington) has been increasing in popularity as a mode of transport. Mode share has increased from 2% in the early 2000s to over 4% in 2013.[^cite_note-214] During the 2020s, a network of cycling infrastructure has been deployed across the city.[^cite_note-215]

## Infrastructure

### Electric power

Wellington's first public electricity supply was established in 1904, alongside the introduction of electric trams, and was originally supplied at 105 volts 80 hertz. The conversion to the now-standard 230/400 volts 50 hertz began in 1925, the same year the city was connected to the [Mangahao hydroelectric scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mangahao_Power_Station). Between 1924 and 1968, the city's supply was supplemented by a coal-fired power station at Evans Bay.[^cite_note-216]

Today, Wellington city is supplied from four [Transpower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transpower_New_Zealand_Limited) substations: Takapu Road, Kaiwharawhara, Wilton, and Central Park (Mount Cook). [Wellington Electricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Electricity) owns and operates the local distribution network.

The city is home to two large wind farms, [West Wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_West_Wind) and [Mill Creek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mill_Creek_Wind_Farm), which combined contribute up to 213 MW of electricity to the city and the national grid.

While Wellington experiences regular strong winds, and only 63% of Wellington Electricity's network is underground, the city has a very reliable power supply. In the year to March 2018, Wellington Electricity disclosed the average customer spent just 55 minutes without power due to unplanned outages.[^cite_note-217]

### Natural gas

Wellington was one of the original nine towns and cities in New Zealand to be supplied with natural gas when the [Kapuni gas field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kapuni) in South Taranaki entered production in 1970, and a 260-kilometre-long (160 mi) high-pressure pipeline from the field to the city was completed. The high-pressure transmission pipelines supplying Wellington are now owned and operated by [First Gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Gas), with [Powerco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powerco) owning and operating the medium- and low-pressure distribution pipelines within the urban area.[^cite_note-218]

### The three waters

Main article: [Water supply and sanitation in the Wellington Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_supply_and_sanitation_in_the_Wellington_Region)

[Drinking water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drinking_water), [stormwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stormwater), and [wastewater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wastewater) infrastructure for the Wellington metropolitan area are provided by [Tiaki Wai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiaki_Wai), a Council Controlled Organisation owned by the five councils: Wellington City, Hutt, Upper Hutt and Porirua city councils, and the [Greater Wellington Regional Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Wellington_Regional_Council). Council water assets were previously managed by an [infrastructure asset management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrastructure_asset_management) company, [Wellington Water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Water) before being transferred to Tiaki Wai on 1 July 2026.[^cite_note-219]

Wellington's first piped water supply came from a spring in 1867.[^cite_note-220] [Greater Wellington Regional Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Region) now supplies Lower Hutt, Porirua, Upper Hutt and Wellington with up to 220 million litres a day.[^cite_note-221] The water comes from [Wainuiomata River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wainuiomata_River) (since 1884), [Hutt River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutt_River_\(New_Zealand\)) (1914), [Ōrongorongo River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%8Crongorongo_River) (1926) and the [Waiwhetū Aquifer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waiwhetu_Aquifer).[^cite_note-222]

There are four wastewater treatment stations serving the Wellington metropolitan area, located at:[^cite_note-223]

- [Moa Point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moa_Point) (serving Wellington city)

- [Seaview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seaview,_Lower_Hutt) (serving Lower Hutt and Upper Hutt)

- [Karori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karori) (serving the suburb)

- [Porirua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porirua) (serving northern Wellington suburbs, Tawa and Porirua city)

The Wellington metropolitan area faces challenges with ageing infrastructure for the three waters, and there have been some significant failures, particularly in wastewater systems. The water supply is vulnerable to severe disruption during a major earthquake, although a wide range of projects are planned to improve the resilience of the water supply and allow a limited water supply post-earthquake.[^cite_note-224][^cite_note-225]

In May 2021, the Wellington City Council approved a 10-year plan that included expenditure of \$2.7 billion on water pipe maintenance and upgrades in Wellington city, and an additional \$147 to \$208 million for plant upgrades at the Moa Point wastewater treatment plant.[^cite_note-226] In November 2023, Wellington Water noted that on-going investment of \$1 billion per annum was required to address water issues across the Greater Wellington region, but that this amount was beyond the funding capacity of councils.[^cite_note-227]

## Media

### Newspapers

For many years Wellington had two daily newspapers – *[The Evening Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Evening_Post_\(New_Zealand\))* in the afternoon and *[The Dominion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dominion_\(Wellington\))* in the morning. *The Evening Post* was founded in 1865 by Dublin-born printer, newspaper manager and leader-writer [Henry Blundell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Blundell_\(publisher\)),[^cite_note-228] while *The Dominion* was first published on 26 September 1907, the day New Zealand achieved [Dominion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominion) status.[^cite_note-Papers_Past_Dom-229] The two newspapers merged in 2002 to form *The Dominion Post*[^cite_note-230] and in April 2023 the merged newspaper was renamed *[The Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Post_\(New_Zealand_newspaper\))*.[^cite_note-231]

### Radio

Wellington is [served by 26 full-power radio stations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_radio_stations_in_Wellington): 17 on FM, four on AM, and five on both FM and AM.

The Wellington radio market is the third-largest in New Zealand, with 392,500 listeners aged 10 and over. The largest commercial stations by share as of May 2025 are [Newstalk ZB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newstalk_ZB) (16.3%), [The Breeze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Breeze_\(New_Zealand_radio_station\)) (13.6%), [ZM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZM_\(radio_station\)) and [The Rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Rock_\(radio_station\)) (8.3% each), and [Mai FM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mai_FM) (6.0%).[^cite_note-232]

### Television

Television broadcasts began in Wellington on 1 July 1961 with the launch of channel WNTV1, becoming the third New Zealand city (after Auckland and Christchurch) to receive regular television broadcasts. WNTV1's main studios were in Waring Taylor Street in central Wellington and broadcast from a transmitter atop Mount Victoria. In 1967, the Mount Victoria transmitter was replaced with a more powerful transmitter at [Mount Kaukau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Kaukau).[^cite_note-233] In November 1969, WNTV1 was networked with its counterpart stations in Auckland, Christchurch and Dunedin to form NZBC TV.

In 1975, the NZBC was broken up, with Wellington and Dunedin studios taking over NZBC TV as [Television One](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TVNZ_1) while Auckland and Christchurch studios launched [Television Two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TVNZ_2). At the same time, the Wellington studios moved to the new purpose-built [Avalon Television Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avalon,_New_Zealand) in Lower Hutt. In 1980, Televisions One and Two merged under a single company, [Television New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TVNZ) (TVNZ). The majority of television production moved to Auckland over the 1980s, culminating in the opening of TVNZ's new Auckland television centre in 1989.

Today, digital terrestrial television ([Freeview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeview_\(New_Zealand\))) is available in the city, transmitting from Mount Kaukau plus three infill transmitters at Baxters Knob, Fitzherbert, and Haywards.[^cite_note-234]

## Wellington metropolitan area

The wider [metropolitan area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_area) for Wellington encompasses areas administered by four [local authorities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_government_in_New_Zealand): [Wellington City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_City_Council) itself, on the peninsula between Cook Strait and [Wellington Harbour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Harbour); [Porirua City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porirua) on [Porirua Harbour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porirua_Harbour) to the north, notable for its large [Māori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ori_people) and [Pasifika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasifika_New_Zealanders) communities; and [Lower Hutt City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Hutt) and [Upper Hutt City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Hutt), largely suburban areas to the northeast, together known as the [Hutt Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hutt_Valley). Depending on the source, the Wellington metro area may include [Waikanae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waikanae), [Paraparaumu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraparaumu) and [Paekākāriki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paek%C4%81k%C4%81riki) on the Kāpiti Coast, and/or [Featherston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Featherston,_New_Zealand) and [Greytown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greytown,_New_Zealand) in the [Wairarapa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wairarapa).



The Wellington urban area (pink) is administered by four local authorities.



Population density in Wellington region (2008) based on census data

The urban areas of the four local authorities have a combined population of 427,600 residents as of June 2025.[^cite_note-NZ_population_data_2018-16]

The four cities comprising the Wellington metropolitan area have a total population of 433,900 (June 2025),[^cite_note-NZ_population_data_2018-16] with the urban area containing 98.5% of that population. The remaining areas are largely mountainous and sparsely farmed or parkland and are outside the urban area boundary. More than most cities, life is dominated by its central business district (CBD). Approximately 62,000 people work in the CBD, only 4,000 fewer than work in Auckland's CBD, despite that city having four times the population.

The [Waikanae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waikanae)-[Paraparaumu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraparaumu)-[Paekākāriki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paek%C4%81k%C4%81riki) combined urban area in the Kāpiti Coast district is sometimes included in the Wellington metro area due to its exurban nature and strong transport links with Wellington. If included as part of the Wellington metro, Waikanae-Paraparaumu-Paekākāriki would add 45,240 to the population (as of June 2025).[^cite_note-NZ_population_data_2018-16]

[Featherston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Featherston,_New_Zealand) and [Greytown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greytown,_New_Zealand) in the Wairarapa are rarely considered part of the Wellington metropolitan area, being physically separated from the rest of the metropolitan area by the [Remutaka Range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remutaka_Range). However, both have significant proportions of their employed population working in Wellington city and the Hutt Valley (36.1% and 17.1% in 2006 respectively)[^cite_note-240] and are considered part of the Wellington functional urban area by Statistics New Zealand.[^cite_note-241]

The four urban areas combined had a usual resident population of 401,850 at the [2018 New Zealand census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_New_Zealand_census), an increase of 26,307 people (7.0%) since the [2013 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2013_New_Zealand_census), and an increase of 42,726 people (11.9%) since the [2006 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_New_Zealand_census). There were 196,911 males and 204,936 females, giving a sex ratio of 0.961 males per female. Of the total population, 74,892 people (18.6%) were aged up to 15 years, 93,966 (23.4%) were 15 to 29, 185,052 (46.1%) were 30 to 64, and 47,952 (11.9%) were 65 or older.[^cite_note-\:02-242]
