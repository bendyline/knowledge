[Cuba](5042481.md) has a [developing](78449.md) [command economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Command_economy) dominated by [state-run enterprises](204768.md). The [Communist Party of Cuba](38776970.md) maintains high levels of public sector control and exerts significant influence over the Cuban economy. The island has a low cost of living, inexpensive [public transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_in_Cuba), as well as subsidized education, healthcare, and food. Cuba's economic growth has historically been weak due to high [labour emigration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Migrant_worker), import dependency, an [ongoing energy crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024%E2%80%932025_Cuba_blackouts), foreign trade sanctions, and limited [tourism in Cuba](4736325.md). Cuba is one of the [poorest countries in Latin America and the Caribbean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Latin_American_and_Caribbean_countries_by_GDP_\(PPP\)) with high inflation, food shortages, and extreme poverty. It is heavily indebted due to its large public sector and high [deficit spending](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deficit_spending), with a global [sovereign debt](695460.md) burden.[^cite_note-\:72-17]

In the 19th century, Cuba was one of the most prosperous pre-industrial Latin American countries with the export of tobacco, sugar, and coffee. At the [Cuban Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_Revolution) of 1953–1959, during the military dictatorship of [Fulgencio Batista](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fulgencio_Batista), Cuba was on a growth trajectory within Latin America. During the [Cold War](325329.md), the Cuban economy was heavily subsidized – 10% to 40% of Cuban GDP in various years – by the [Eastern Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Bloc), due to their geopolitical alignment with the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union).[^cite_note-Mesa-Logo2-18] Cuba endured severe economic downturn when the Soviet Union collapsed, with GDP declining 33% between 1990 and 1993. A protracted economic malaise known as the [Special Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Period) overcame Cuba from 1991 to 2001. The Cuban economy rebounded in 2003 with marginal liberalization and foreign support from [Venezuela](32374.md), [China](5405.md), and [Russia](25391.md).[^cite_note-Mesa-Logo2-18] The [United States](3434750.md) has maintained an [economic embargo against Cuba](1105954.md) since 1960 due to geopolitical tensions.&#x20;

## History



Historical GDP per capita development from 1829 to 2018

### Colonial and Republican period (1870–1959)

Further information: [Economy of Republic of Cuba (1902–1959)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Republic_of_Cuba_\(1902%E2%80%931959\))

Although Cuba belonged to the high-income countries of Latin America since the 1870s, [income inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Income_inequality) was high, accompanied by capital outflows to foreign investors.[^cite_note-19] In the early half of the 20th century sugar, tobacco, and coffee exports, along with tourism from the U.S. provided the country with rapid growth.[^cite_note-20]

Before the [Cuban Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_Revolution), in 1958, Cuba had a per-capita GDP of \$2,363, which placed it in the middle of Latin American countries at the time, according to the Maddison Project.[^cite_note-maddisonproj-21] PBS quotes a better ranking for Cuba of 5th in the Western Hemisphere during the early 20th century, and gives high ratings for life expectancy, literacy and per capita ownership of automobiles, telephones, and television sets.[^cite_note-AE-PBS-22] According to the UN, between 1950 and 1955, Cuba had a life expectancy of 59.4 years, which placed it in 56th place in the global ranking.[^cite_note-unlifeexp-23]

Its proximity to the [United States](3434750.md) made it a familiar holiday destination for wealthy Americans. Their visits for gambling, horse racing, and golfing[^cite_note-smithsonianmag.com-24] made tourism an important economic sector. Tourism magazine *Cabaret Quarterly* described [Havana](49719.md) as "a mistress of pleasure, the lush and opulent goddess of delights".[^cite_note-smithsonianmag.com-24] Cuban dictator [Fulgencio Batista](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fulgencio_Batista) had plans to line the [Malecon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malec%C3%B3n,_Havana), Havana's famous walkway by the water, with hotels and casinos to attract even more tourists. The country was highly dependent on its sugar production.[^cite_note-\:2025MesaLago-25]<sup>: 38 </sup>

In the late 1950s, Cuba's oil sector was controlled by three large international oil companies: [Standard Oil of New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Oil_of_New_Jersey) (Esso), [Texaco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texaco), and [Royal Dutch Shell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shell_plc).[^cite_note-\:022-26]<sup>: 39 </sup>

### Revolutionary era (1959–present)

The [Cuban Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_Revolution) under the leadership of [Fidel Castro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fidel_Castro) brought a sharp break with earlier economic, social and political policies in Cuba, introducing a planned state-run command economy, an alliance with the [Soviet Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Bloc), and a trade embargo by the United States that ended trade and tourism with that country. Cuba moved down the world income distribution after the revolution and as of 2012, per capita income appear to be below the peak of Cuba's pre-revolutionary levels.[^cite_note-ward-2012-1-27]

#### Early economic planning (1959–1967)

Main articles: [Agrarian reforms in Cuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agrarian_reforms_in_Cuba), [Four Year Plan (Cuba)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Year_Plan_\(Cuba\)), and [Great Debate (Cuba)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Debate_\(Cuba\))

On 3 March 1959, Fidel Castro seized control of the Cuban Telephone Company, which was a subsidiary of the [International Telephone and Telecommunications Corporation.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITT_Corporation) This was the first of many nationalizations made by the new government; the assets seized totaled US\$9 billion.[^cite_note-28]

After the 1959 Revolution, citizens were not required to pay a personal [income tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Income_tax).[^cite_note-\:0-29] The government also began to subsidize [healthcare](4726127.md) and education for all citizens; this action created strong national support for the new revolutionary government.

The [USSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) and Cuba reestablished their diplomatic relations in May 1960. When oil refineries like Shell, Texaco, and Esso refused to refine Soviet oil, Castro nationalized that industry as well, taking over the refineries on the island.[^cite_note-Cuba_and_the_U.S._Empire-30] Days later in response, the United States cut the Cuban sugar quota completely; Eisenhower was quoted saying "This action amounts to economic sanctions against Cuba. Now we must look ahead to other economic, diplomatic, and strategic moves."[^cite_note-Cuba_and_the_U.S._Empire-30] Cuba and the Soviet Union signed their first trade deal that year, in which Cuba traded sugar to the Soviet Union in exchange for fuel.[^cite_note-\:022-26]<sup>: 2 </sup> On 7 February 1962, Kennedy expanded the United States embargo to cover almost all U.S. imports.[^cite_note-\:122-31]

Using machinery and equipment provided by the Soviet Union and other socialist countries, from 1959 to 1963, the Cuban government attempted to implement [import substitution industrialization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Import_substitution_industrialization).[^cite_note-\:2025MesaLago-25]<sup>: 35 </sup> This approach was deemed to have failed by 1964 and Cuba resumed an export strategy focused on sugar.[^cite_note-\:2025MesaLago-25]<sup>: 38–39 </sup> The economy remained inefficient and over-specialized in a few commodities purchased by the [Eastern Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Bloc) countries.[^cite_note-32]

By the late 1960s, Cuba became dependent on Soviet economic, political, and military aid. It was also around this time that Castro began privately believing that Cuba could bypass the various stages of socialism and progress directly to [pure communism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_society).[^cite_note-33] General Secretary [Leonid Brezhnev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonid_Brezhnev) consolidated Cuba's dependence on the USSR when, in 1973, Castro caved to Brezhnev's pressure to become a full member of [Council for Mutual Economic Assistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comecon) (Comecon).[^cite_note-34] Comecon deemed Cuba one of its underdeveloped member countries and therefore Cuba could obtain oil in direct exchange for sugar at a rate that was highly favorable to Cuba.[^cite_note-\:022-26]<sup>: 41 </sup> Hard currency Cuba obtained from re-exporting oil facilitated Cuba's importation of goods from non-Comecon countries and facilitated its investments in social services.[^cite_note-\:022-26]<sup>: 57 </sup>

#### 1968–1990

Main articles: [Revolutionary Offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutionary_Offensive), [Institutionalization process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutionalization_process), and [Rectification process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectification_process)

In 1970 as part of the [Revolutionary Offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutionary_Offensive) economic campaign, Fidel Castro attempted to motivate the Cuban people to harvest 10 million tons of sugar, in Spanish known as *[La Zafra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zafra_\(agriculture\))*, to increase their exports and grow their economy.[^cite_note-\:022-26]<sup>: 37–38 </sup> Despite the help of most of the Cuban population, the country fell short and produced only 7.56 million tons.[^cite_note-35] In July 1970, after the harvest was over, Castro took responsibility for the failure, but later that same year, shifted the blame toward the Sugar Industry Minister saying "Those technocrats, geniuses, super-scientists assured me that they knew what to do to produce the ten million tons."[^cite_note-36]

During the Revolutionary period, Cuba was one of the few developing countries to provide [foreign aid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_aid) to other countries. Foreign aid began with the construction of six hospitals in Peru in the early 1970s.[^cite_note-Monitor2-37] Between 1970 and 1985, Cuba sustained high rates of growth: \[Cuba] was actually following the World Bank recipe from the 1970s: redistribution with growth".[^cite_note-38] Foreign aid expanded later in the 1970s to the point where some 8000 Cubans worked in overseas assignments. Cubans built housing, roads, airports, schools, and other facilities in [Angola](701.md), [Ethiopia](187749.md), [Laos](17752.md), [Guinea](12176.md), [Tanzania](30118.md), and other countries. By the end of 1985, 35,000 Cuban workers had helped build projects in some 20 Asian, African, and Latin American countries.[^cite_note-Monitor2-37]

For [Nicaragua](21362.md) in 1982, Cuba pledged to provide over \$130 million worth of agricultural and machinery equipment and some 4000 technicians, doctors, and teachers.[^cite_note-Monitor2-37] Over the course of the 1980s, Cuba provided approximately 90,000 tons of oil to Nicaragua per year to support the [Sandinista revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicaraguan_Revolution).[^cite_note-\:022-26]<sup>: 58 </sup> From 1986 through 1990, Fidel Castro began the Rectification Process in an effort to decrease market elements in the economy.[^cite_note-\:2025MesaLago-25]<sup>: 28 </sup>

In 1986, Cuba defaulted on its \$10.9 billion debt to the [Paris Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Club). In 1987, Cuba stopped making payments on that debt. In 2002, Cuba defaulted on \$750 million in Japanese loans.[^cite_note-39]

The 1980s was an era of low poverty. In the mid-80s, no more than 6% of Cubans were poor (poverty being defined as a state of not being able to satisfy basic needs with earned income). In comparison, the poverty rate throughout Latin America in that era averaged more than 40%, and in some countries more than 70%.[^cite_note-Espina-2025-revista-40]

#### Special Period (1991–1994)

Main article: [Special Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Period)



Cuban oil production and consumption remained depressed [from 1991 to 2000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Period) during an extended period of economic distress.&#x20;

In 1991 an extended period of [economic crisis](2878852.md) began in [Cuba](5042481.md) primarily due to the [dissolution of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union) and the [Comecon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comecon).[^cite_note-41] This era was referred to as the "Special Period in Peacetime",[^cite_note-\:022-26]<sup>: 84 </sup> lateusly in 1990 and 1991 and did not recover for five years. Cuba had been insulated from world sugar prices by Soviet price guarantees.[^cite_note-cordova-42]

[Malnutrition](258979.md) resulted in an outbreak of diseases.[^cite_note-cordova-42] Although the collapse of centrally planned economies in the Soviet Union and other countries of the Eastern bloc subjected Cuba to severe economic difficulties, which led to a drop in calories per day from 3052 in 1989 to 2600 in 2006, mortality rates were not strongly affected thanks to the priority given on maintaining a [social safety net](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_safety_net).[^cite_note-43] The Cuban economy began to improve again following a rapid improvement in trade and diplomatic relations between Cuba and Venezuela following the election of [Hugo Chávez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugo_Ch%C3%A1vez) in Venezuela in 1998, who became Cuba's most important trading partner and diplomatic ally.[^cite_note-cordova-42]

#### Reforms and recovery (1994–2011)

Main article: [Cuban economic reforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_economic_reforms)



Evolution of [GDP-per-capita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_\(nominal\)_per_capita) of Cuba and some other Caribbean countries, 1945–2010&#x20;

The government undertook several [economic reforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_economic_reforms) to stem excess [liquidity](56121.md), increase labor incentives, and alleviate serious shortages of food, [consumer goods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumer_good), and services. To alleviate the economic crisis, the government introduced a few [market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_economy)-oriented reforms, including opening to tourism, allowing [foreign investment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_direct_investment), legalizing the U.S. dollar, and authorizing self-employment for some 150 occupations. These measures resulted in modest economic growth. The [liberalized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_market) agricultural markets were introduced in October 1994, at which state and private farmers sell above-quota production at [free market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_market) prices, broadened legal consumption alternatives, and reduced [black market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_market) prices.

Government efforts to lower subsidies to unprofitable enterprises and to shrink the money supply caused the semi-official [exchange rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange_rate) for the [Cuban peso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_peso) to move from a peak of 120 to the dollar in the summer of 1994 to 21 to the dollar by year-end 1999. The drop in GDP halted in 1994 when Cuba reported 0.7% growth, followed by increases of 2.5% in 1995 and 7.8% in 1996. Growth slowed again in 1997 and 1998 to 2.5% and 1.2% respectively. One of the key reasons was the failure to notice that sugar production had become uneconomic. Reflecting on the Special Period, [First Secretary of the Communist Party of Cuba](38776884.md) [Fidel Castro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fidel_Castro) later admitted that many mistakes had been made, "The Soviet Union collapsed, oil cost \$40 a barrel, and sugar prices were at basement levels, so why did we not rationalize the industry?"[^cite_note-44] [Living conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Living_conditions) in 1999 remained well below the 1989 level.

Due to the continued growth of tourism, growth began in 1999 with a 6.2% increase in GDP.[^cite_note-45] Growth then picked up, with a growth in GDP of 11.8% in 2005 according to government figures.[^cite_note-46] In 2007 the Cuban economy grew by 7.5%, higher than the Latin American average. Accordingly, the cumulative growth in GDP since 2004 stood at 42.5%.[^cite_note-47][^cite_note-48]

Starting in 1996, the government imposed [income taxes on self-employed Cubans](42209204.md).[^cite_note-\:0-29] Cuba ranked third in the region in 1958 in GDP-per-capita, surpassed only by [Venezuela](32374.md) and [Uruguay](31843.md). It had descended to 9th, 11th, or 12th place in the region by 2007. Cuban social indicators suffered less.[^cite_note-mesa-49]

Every year the United Nations holds a vote asking countries to choose if the United States is justified in its economic embargo against Cuba and whether it should be lifted. 2016 was the first year that the United States abstained from the vote, rather than voting no, "since 1992 the US and Israel have constantly voted against the resolution – occasionally supported by the Marshall Islands, Palau, Uzbekistan, Albania and Romania".[^cite_note-50] In its 2020 report to the United Nations, Cuba stated that the total cost to Cuba from the United States embargo is \$144 billion since its inception.[^cite_note-51]

#### Post-Fidel Castro reforms (2011–2021)

> Either we change course or we sink.

— First Secretary of the Communist Party of Cuba\
[Raúl Castro](221749.md), December 2010

[^cite_note-52]

In 2011, "\[t]he new economic reforms were introduced, effectively creating a new economic system", which the [Brookings Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brookings_Institution) dubbed the "New Cuban Economy".[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-54] Since then, over 400,000 Cubans have signed up to become entrepreneurs. As of 2012 the government listed 181 official jobs no longer under their control—such as taxi driver, construction worker and shopkeeper. Workers must purchase licenses to work for some roles, such as a mule driver, palm-tree trimmer, or well digger.&#x20;

Around 2000, half the country's sugar mills closed. Before reforms, imports were double exports, doctors earned £15 per month, and families supplemented incomes with extra jobs. After reforms, more than 150,000 farmers could lease land from the government for surplus crop production. Before the reforms, the only real estate transactions involved homeowners swapping properties; reforms legalized the buying and selling of real estate and created a real estate boom in the country. In 2012 a Havana fast-food burger/pizza restaurant, La Pachanga, started in the owner's home; as of 2012 it served 1,000 meals on a Saturday at £3 each. Tourists can now ride factory steam locomotives through closed sugar mills.[^cite_note-BBCSimonReeve2012-55]

In 2008, Raúl Castro's administration hinted that the purchase of computers, DVD players, and microwaves would become legal. Monthly wages remain less than 20 U.S. dollars.[^cite_note-Weissert-56] Mobile phones, which had been restricted to Cubans working for foreign companies and government officials, were legalized in 2008.[^cite_note-Weissert-56] In 2010 Fidel Castro, in agreement with Raúl Castro's reformist sentiment, admitted that the Cuban model based on the old Soviet centralized planning model was no longer sustainable. The brothers encouraged the development of a cooperative variant of socialism – where the state plays a less active role in the economy – and the formation of worker-owned co-operatives and self-employment enterprises.[^cite_note-57]

To remedy Cuba's economic structural distortions and inefficiencies, the Sixth Congress approved an expansion of the internal market and access to global markets on 18 April 2011. A comprehensive list of changes is:[^cite_note-Domínguez2012-58][^cite_note-59]

On 20 December 2011, a new credit policy allowed Cuban banks to finance entrepreneurs and individuals wishing to make major purchases to make home improvements in addition to farmers. "Cuban banks have long provided loans to farm cooperatives, they have offered credit to new recipients of farmland in usufruct since 2008, and in 2011 they began making loans to individuals for business and other purposes".[^cite_note-60]

The system of rationed food distribution in Cuba was known as the [*Libreta de Abastecimiento*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationing_in_Cuba) ("supplies booklet"). As of 2012 ration books at bodegas still procured rice, oil, sugar, and matches above the government average wage of £15 monthly.[^cite_note-BBC2012SimonReeve-61] Raúl Castro signed Law 313 in September 2013 to set up a [special economic zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_economic_zone), the first in the country, in the port city of [Mariel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariel,_Cuba).[^cite_note-62] The zone is exempt from normal Cuban economic legislation.[^cite_note-\:022-26]<sup>: 159 </sup> On 22 October 2013, the government eventually announced its intention to end the dual-currency system.[^cite_note-63] The [convertible peso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_convertible_peso) (CUC) was no longer issued from 1 January 2021 and ceased circulation on 30 December 2021.

In February 2019, Cuban voters approved a new constitution granting the right to private property and greater access to free markets while also maintaining Cuba's status as a socialist state.[^cite_note-freerereconomy-64][^cite_note-somefreerereconomy-65] In June 2019, the 16th ExpoCaribe trade fair took place in Santiago.[^cite_note-66] Since 2014, the Cuban economy has seen a dramatic uptick in foreign investment.[^cite_note-foreigninvestors-67] In November 2019, Cuba's state newspaper, *[Granma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granma_\(newspaper\))*, published an article acknowledging that despite the deterioration in relations between the U.S. and Cuban governments, the Cuban government continued to make efforts to attract foreign investment in 2018.[^cite_note-foreigninvest-68] In December 2018, the official [Cuban News Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_News_Agency) reported that 525 foreign direct investment projects were reported in Cuba, a dramatic increase from the 246 projects reported in 2014.[^cite_note-69]

#### Modern Cuban economy (2021–present)



A Cuban café in Havana, 2023

Cuba's GDP dropped more than 10% from 2018 to 2024.[^cite_note-RTP-ECiC-6-8-25-70] The modern Cuban economy continues to face challenges related to an [ongoing energy crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024%E2%80%932025_Cuba_blackouts), foreign trade sanctions, and limited [tourism](4736325.md). The Cuban economy was negatively affected by the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_Cuba) from 2020 to 2022, with sudden drops in remittances and tourism. In February 2021, the Cuban government authorized private initiatives in more than 1,800 occupations.[^cite_note-71]

In 2020, the country's economy declined by 11%, the country's worst decline in nearly 30 years.[^cite_note-72] Cubans have faced shortages of basic goods as a result.[^cite_note-\:2-73] The Cuban economy received large-scale foreign investment from [Venezuela](32374.md), [China](5405.md) and [Russia](25391.md), while the [United States](3434750.md) sharply tightened their [embargo against Cuba](1105954.md) in 2025. Cuba has been in an [economic recession](25382.md) since 2020, with GDP contracting 1.1% and inflation at 24% in 2024.[^cite_note-74] The [halt of Venezuelan oil shipments](82348646.md), following the [United States' intervention in Venezuela](82021123.md) in January 2026, worsened Cuba's severe energy crisis.[^cite_note-75]

## Migration

Outbound migration from Cuba has had a negative impact on its economy. Albizu-Campos notes that revolutionary Cuba has had a number of mass emigrations — "such as El Mariel in 1980 (125,000), the Balseros Crisis in 1994 (34,500) and Boca de Camarioca in 1965 (about 5,000)"[^cite_note-RTP-ECiC-6-8-25-70] — but that departures from Cuba beginning in October 2021, particularly to the United States, far exceeding previous migrations,[^cite_note-RTP-ECiC-6-8-25-70] and has been called "devastating" for the Cuban economy.[^cite_note-NYT-depopulating-76] Causes include the economic sanctions of the Trump administration, COVID pandemic, and economic mismanagement,[^cite_note-RTP-ECiC-6-8-25-70] as well as the desire for greater opportunities abroad.[^cite_note-Delgado-cuban-migration-2024-77] Among the effects of the migration of working-age population on Cuba are less economic development, a strain on the country's pension system and increased caregiving burdens for the elderly and young who remain in Cuba.[^cite_note-Delgado-cuban-migration-2024-77][^cite_note-NYT-depopulating-76]

## Sectors

### Energy

Cuba burns 100,000 barrels of oil daily to supply electricity (15 TWh in 2023), with small contributions from other sources.[^cite_note-zar-78][^cite_note-79] About 25% of Cuba's electricity is generated on ships with floating power plants. As of 2023, eight [powerships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powership) from Turkey provided 770 MW from burning oil.[^cite_note-80]

As of 2011, 96% of electricity was produced from fossil fuels. Solar panels were introduced in some rural areas to reduce blackouts, brownouts, and the use of kerosene. Citizens were encouraged to swap inefficient lamps with newer models to reduce consumption. A power tariff reduced inefficient use.[^cite_note-81] In 2007, Cuba produced an estimated 16.89 billion kWh of electricity and consumed 13.93 billion kWh with no exports or imports, as the island is not connected to other areas.[^cite_note-The_World_Factbook-82]



Electricity consumption in Cuba from 1985 to 2011

The [Energy Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_Revolution) is a program begun by Cuba in 2005.[^cite_note-\:022-26]<sup>: 4 </sup> This program focused on developing the country's socioeconomic status and transitioning Cuba into an energy-efficient economy with diverse energy resources.[^cite_note-auto-83] Cuba's energy sector lacks the resources to produce optimal amounts of power. One of the issues the Energy Revolution program faces comes from Cuba's power production suffering from the absence of investment and the ongoing trade sanctions imposed by the United States.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-84] Likewise, the energy sector has received a multimillion-dollar investment distributed among a network of power resources. However, customers are experiencing [rolling blackouts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolling_blackout) of power from energy companies to preserve electricity during Cuba's economic crisis.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-84]

Furthermore, an outdated electricity grid (losing 16% in transmission)[^cite_note-feli-85] that's been damaged by hurricanes caused the energy crisis in 2004 and continued to be a major issue during the Energy Revolution.[^cite_note-auto-83] Cuba responded to this situation by providing a variety of different types of energy resources. 6000 small diesel generators, 416 fuel oil generators, 893 diesel generators, 9.4 million incandescent bulbs for energy-saving lamps, 1.33 million fans, 5.5 million electric pressure cookers, 3.4 million electric rice cookers, 0.2 million electric water pumps, 2.04 million domestic refrigerators and 0.1 million televisions were distributed among territories.[^cite_note-ReferenceB-86] The electrical grid was restored to only 90% until 2009.

The country frequently suffers rolling blackouts due to fuel shortages, and many plants are shut down due to a lack of fuel. In [October 2024, the entire country suffered a multiday electricity blackout](78148753.md) when the Antonio Guiteras power plant failed and efforts to restart the grid were not successful.[^cite_note-87][^cite_note-88]

#### Renewables

[Renewable energy](25784.md) has become a major priority as the government has promoted wind and solar power.[^cite_note-89] Under a March 2017 law, the Cuban government has begun to roll out solar panels to every home in Cuba.[^cite_note-\:02-90]<sup>: 95 </sup> The crucial challenge the Energy Revolution program will face is developing sustainable energy in Cuba while also accounting for the challenges presented by economic sanctions, the detrimental effects of hurricanes that hit the country, and the nature of Cuba as a developing nation.[^cite_note-ReferenceB-86]

The passage of Decree-Law 345 in 2019 permits Cubans to purchase [photovoltaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photovoltaics) solar panels for private use and to sell surplus energy to state company Unión Eléctrica.[^cite_note-\:022-26]<sup>: 159 </sup> In response to the tightening of United States sanctions on fuel imports and the ensuing energy crisis in 2024 and 2025, Cuba rapidly accelerated its transition to renewable energy.[^cite_note-91] In 2022, about 1.5% of electricity came from solar power.[^cite_note-ember-92] By 2025, renewable energy supplied approximately 10% of Cuba's electricity, up from 3.6% the previous year.[^cite_note-93] Between early 2025 and early 2026, the country connected 49 new solar parks to the national grid, adding over 1,000 megawatts (MW) of capacity.[^cite_note-94]

The expansion is heavily supported by financing and equipment from China. In December 2024, Havana and Beijing signed agreements to build seven solar parks with a combined capacity of 35 MW.[^cite_note-95] Under the National Energy Transition Strategy, the Cuban government aims to construct 92 solar parks by 2028, projecting an addition of 2,000 MW (2 gigawatts) of solar capacity, which is nearly equivalent to the country's entire fossil fuel generation capacity.[^cite_note-96][^cite_note-97] The country also set targets to increase renewables to 26% of its total energy supply by 2035.[^cite_note-98] Wind energy development includes the construction of the La Herradura 1 and La Herradura 2 wind farms in Las Tunas province, with capacities of 33 MW and 50 MW respectively, built with Chinese backing.[^cite_note-feli-85][^cite_note-99]

Despite the rapid deployment of solar infrastructure, challenges remain. A lack of utility-scale battery storage limits the effectiveness of solar power during peak evening demand hours, and the modernization of Cuba's ageing electrical grid, which loses an estimated 16% of electricity in transmission, requires significant capital investment that the state struggles to afford.[^cite_note-100] However the potential for renewables is significant. A 2026 academic study published in *Renewable and Sustainable Energy Transition* projected that Cuba could achieve 93% of its electricity from renewable sources by 2050 through a combination of 13,000 gigawatt-hours (GWh) of solar energy and 11,000 GWh of wind energy.[^cite_note-101]

[Pumpjacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pumpjack) in Cuba, 2005

In 2017, Cuba produced and consumed an estimated 1189 million m<sup>3</sup> of natural gas and has 70.79 billion m<sup>3</sup> of proved reserves. The nation did not export or import any natural gas.[^cite_note-The_World_Factbook-82]

### Industry

Industrial production accounted for almost 37% of Cuban GDP or US\$6.9 billion and employed 24% of the population, or 2,671,000 people, in 1996.[^cite_note-107] A rally in sugar prices in 2009 stimulated investment and development of sugar processing.

#### Biotechnology and pharmaceutics

In the 1980s, Cuban scientists developed a [vaccine](32653.md) against a strain of [bacterial meningitis B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacterial_Meningitis), which eliminated what had been a serious disease on the island. The Cuban vaccine is used throughout Latin America.[^cite_note-108] The [Center of molecular immunology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_molecular_immunology) (CIM) developed [nimotuzumab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nimotuzumab), a monoclonal antibody used to treat cancer. Nimotuzumab is an inhibitor of [epidermal growth factor receptor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epidermal_growth_factor_receptor) (EGFR), which is over-expressed in many cancers. Nimotuzumab is now being developed with international partners. In 2003 Cuba's biotechnology and pharmaceutical industry was gaining in importance.[^cite_note-109] Among the products sold internationally are [vaccines](32653.md) against various viral and bacterial pathogens.[^cite_note-110] Cuba has done pioneering work on the development of drugs for cancer treatment.[^cite_note-111]

During the [COVID-19 pandemic](62750956.md), Cuba developed two [COVID-19 vaccines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_vaccines). [Soberana 02](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soberana_02) is produced by the [Pasteur Institute of Iran](41907959.md) and the [Finlay Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finlay_Institute), a Cuban epidemiological research institute.[^cite_note-112] [Abdala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdala_\(vaccine\)) was developed by the [Center for Genetic Engineering and Biotechnology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_for_Genetic_Engineering_and_Biotechnology) in Cuba.[^cite_note-113] Cuba's biotechnology sector developed in response to the limitations on technology transfer, international financing, and international trade resulting from the United States embargo.[^cite_note-\:02-90]<sup>: 120 </sup> The Cuban biotechnology sector is entirely state-owned.[^cite_note-\:02-90]<sup>: 120 </sup>

#### Healthcare

Main article: [Healthcare in Cuba](4726127.md)

This section is an excerpt from [Healthcare in Cuba](4726127.md).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Healthcare_in_Cuba\&action=edit)]

The [Cuban](5042481.md) [government](73249.md) operates a [national health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_health_care) system and assumes fiscal and administrative responsibility for the [health care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_care) of all its citizens.[^cite_note-Healthcare_in_Cuba_Harvard-115] All [healthcare in Cuba](4726127.md) is free to Cuban residents.[^cite_note-Healthcare_in_Cuba_bbc190417-116] There are no private hospitals or clinics as all health services are government-run.

Like the rest of the Cuban economy, Cuban medical care suffered following the end of [Soviet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) [subsidies](146719.md) in 1991. The [United States embargo against Cuba](1105954.md) also has an effect.[^cite_note-Healthcare_in_Cuba_US_embargo-117] Difficulties include low salaries for doctors, poor facilities, poor provision of equipment, and the frequent absence of essential drugs.[^cite_note-Healthcare_in_Cuba_\:7-118][^cite_note-Healthcare_in_Cuba_\:8-119]

The Cuban healthcare system has emphasized the export of health professionals through international missions, aiding global health efforts. However, while these missions generate significant revenue and serve as a tool for political influence, domestically, Cuba faces challenges including medication shortages and disparities between medical services for locals and foreigners.[^cite_note-Healthcare_in_Cuba_\:4-120] Despite the income from these missions, only a small fraction of the national budget has been allocated to public health, underscoring contrasting priorities within the nation's healthcare strategy.[^cite_note-Healthcare_in_Cuba_\:4-120]

#### Housing

As of late 2023, *[Havana Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Havana_Times)* reported a total housing stock of 3.8 million properties in Cuba, with deficit of more than 855,000 units, and approximately 40% of the 3.8 million units in "fair or poor condition".[^cite_note-Snail-HT-26-6-25-121] Economist Ricardo Torres, reports that of the large number of Soviet-built public apartment blocks that provide housing, as of early 2026, approximately half will require reconstruction.[^cite_note-Is_Cuba_Next?_|JL_Anderson_|NYer|23_March_2026-122] New housing is being constructed in Cuba, at a rate much below the growth of population.[^cite_note-Mesa-Lago_Housing-123]

As of late 2019, about 85% of Cubans own their own homes and the land it sits on. These are Cubans who were renting housing from landlords whose buildings were expropriated by the state in 1960, and who have paid rent on that housing for twenty years.[^cite_note-Mesa-Lago_Housing-123] In October 2011 the 1988 General Housing Act was amended to allow the sale and purchase of homes by Cuban citizens and foreign residents.[^cite_note-Brizuela-HT-16-10-23-124] Residential rental properties in Cuba that are owned and managed by the state typically have heavily subsidized rent significantly lower than market rates in other countries to ensure affordability for renters. It has lead to complaints of deterioration of housing because maintenance and renovation due to insufficient funds.[^cite_note-MG-DCPR-2024-125]

#### Tourism

Main article: [Tourism in Cuba](4736325.md)



A white sand beach in [Varadero, Cuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varadero), 2003

In the mid-1990s, tourism surpassed sugar, the mainstay of the Cuban economy, as the primary source of foreign exchange. Havana devotes significant resources to building tourist facilities and renovating historic structures. ## Foreign investment



Cuban trade levels from 1985 to 2011 showing heavy import dependency.&#x20;

The [U.S. embargo against Cuba](1105954.md) serves as an international deterrent to foreign investment.[^cite_note-132] Within Cuba itself, foreign ownership in land is prohibited; foreign entities are only allowed to rent land.[^cite_note-\:2025MesaLago-25]<sup>: 31 </sup> Cuba began courting foreign investment in the Special Period during the 1990s. Foreign investors must form joint ventures with the Cuban government. Cuban officials said in early 1998 that 332 joint ventures had begun. Many of these are loans or contracts for management, supplies, or services normally not considered equity investments in Western economies. In 2017, the country reported a record \$2 billion in foreign investment.[^cite_note-foreigninvestors-67] It was reported that foreign investment in Cuba had increased from 2014 to 2019.[^cite_note-foreigninvestors-67] In September 2019, EU foreign policy chief [Federica Mogherini](42031342.md) stated during a three-day visit to Cuba that the European Union is committed to helping Cuba develop its economy.[^cite_note-133]

In 2023, Canada receives the largest share of Cuban exports (30.6%) – 70 to 80% of which go through Indiana Finance BV, a company owned by the Dutch [Van 't Wout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_'t_Wout) family, who have close personal ties with Fidel Castro.[^cite_note-\:5-134] This trend can be seen in other colonial Caribbean communities with direct political ties with the global economy.&#x20;

### Support from Venezuela

Further information: [Cuba–Venezuela relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuba%E2%80%93Venezuela_relations)



A proposed electricity route between [Venezuela](32374.md) and Cuba in 2008

[Venezuela](32374.md) is the largest trading partner to Cuba and is widely considered to be systemically important to the Cuban economy.[^cite_note-135][^cite_note-\:10-136] Since the 1990s, the two nations have [shared a close relationship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuba%E2%80%93Venezuela_relations) and have maintained strong economic collaboration.[^cite_note-137][^cite_note-138] Initial trading was centered on exchanging Venezuelan oil for visiting Cuban doctors in a form of [medical diplomacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_diplomacy).[^cite_note-139][^cite_note-140] Venezuelan investors are the only foreign investor allowed to hold 100% ownership in businesses, with all other foreign investors requiring joint investment with Cuba. According to Carmelo Mesa-Lago, a Cuban-born U.S. economist, in nominal terms, the Venezuelan subsidy is higher than the subsidy which the Soviet Union gave to Cuba.[^cite_note-\:10-136] In 2012, Venezuela accounted for 20.8% of Cuba's GDP, while Cuba only accounted for roughly 4% of Venezuela's.[^cite_note-141] The Venezuelan economy has been in [complete collapse since 2010](52966258.md), hampering its ability to support Cuba.[^cite_note-142][^cite_note-143] The [U.S. military intervention in Venezuela](82021123.md) in 2026 resulted in a [blockade of oil tankers](81814844.md) around Cuba, halting Venezuelan oil imports to the island.[^cite_note-144]

### Support from China

Further information: [China–Cuba relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China%E2%80%93Cuba_relations)

[China](5405.md) has been the second-largest trade partner to Cuba, with a 16.9% share of the Cuban export market.[^cite_note-\:5-134][^cite_note-145] The trade relationship between the two nations has been mixed, with the Chinese government routinely entering, and then promptly exiting, trade agreements with Cuba.[^cite_note-\:8-146] From 2017 to 2022, bilateral trade has contracted 33% due to a lack of foreign investment protection for Chinese capital.[^cite_note-\:8-146] Following the collapse of the Cuban sugar industry in 2024, China suspended their yearly 400,000-ton sugar order.[^cite_note-\:8-146] Cuba entered into the Chinese-led [Cross-Border Interbank Payment System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-Border_Interbank_Payment_System) (CIPS) in 2025, expanding their access to international payment processing.[^cite_note-\:8-146]

### Support from Russia

Further information: [Cuba–Russia relations](17446369.md)

[Russia](25391.md) has maintained a special relationship with Cuba since the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union), first trading Cuban sugar in exchange for Soviet energy.[^cite_note-\:022-26]<sup>: 2 </sup> In 2025, the Russian government pledged \$1 billion in economic aid to Cuba, spanning to 2030, focusing on infrastructure and oil.[^cite_note-\:9-147] Russia has made strategic investments into the Cuban energy and tourism sectors, with nearly 100,000-tons of Russian oil heading to Cuba in 2025.[^cite_note-\:9-147] During the [Russian invasion of Ukraine](70149799.md), the Cuban government sought access to Russia's [war economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_economy) by offering 20,000 Cuban mercenaries for remittance income.[^cite_note-148]

## Currencies

Main articles: [Dual economy of Cuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_economy_of_Cuba) and [Dollarization of Cuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dollarization_of_Cuba)

Cuba maintains the [Cuban peso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_peso) as its official currency.[^cite_note-\:23-149] The nation's currency is on a [floating-rate exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating_exchange_rate), the value of the peso fluctuates given demand and supply.[^cite_note-\:23-149] The Cuban government facilitates an official exchange rate of US\$1 for every 24 CUP.[^cite_note-\:23-149] Within the informal market, the exchange rate has sharply devalued, reflecting a semi-dollarized economic system alongside the U.S. dollar.[^cite_note-\:23-149]

## Public sector

See also: [CIMEX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CIMEX) and [GAESA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GAESA)

As of 2018, the public sector accounted for 90.8% of the Cuban economy and 67% of employment.[^cite_note-\:2025MesaLago-25]<sup>: 4 </sup>

Cuba used a planned economy with state-ownership over the large majority of the [means of production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Means_of_production).[^cite_note-\:2025MesaLago-25]<sup>: 18 </sup> The public sector thus contributes to the majority of economic activity, with the Cuban government encouraging the formation of [worker co-operatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worker_co-operative) and [self-employment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-employment) since the 1990s.[^cite_note-freerereconomy-64] It later legalized limited private property and free-market rights along with [foreign direct investment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_direct_investment) in 2018.[^cite_note-somefreerereconomy-65][^cite_note-foreigninvestors-67] Cuba describes these revisions to its economic model as the "updated" model.[^cite_note-\:2025MesaLago-25]<sup>: 18 </sup>

Public sector employment remains high compared to the private sector but has steadily decreased since the 1980s.[^cite_note-\:4-150][^cite_note-\:6-151] All public and most private investment activity requires government approval or oversight.[^cite_note-\:2-73][^cite_note-foreigninvest-68] Economic data published by the Cuban government is not always independently verified, limiting Cuba's inclusion on [economic rankings in Latin America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Latin_American_and_Caribbean_countries_by_GDP_\(PPP\)).

The state retains land in [usufruct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usufruct), a type of legal ownership.[^cite_note-\:2025MesaLago-25]<sup>: 31 </sup>

## Private sector

See also: [Cuentapropista](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuentapropista), [Paladar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paladar), and [Sociolismo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociolismo)

As of 2023, public-sector employment was 65%, and private-sector employment was 35%, compared to the 2000 ratio of 76% to 23% and the 1981 ratio of 91% to 8%.[^cite_note-\:2-73][^cite_note-\:4-150][^cite_note-\:6-151] Cuba differs from some other Communist economies such as [Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Vietnam#Economic_development_strategy) and [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_market_economy), where the private sector is large and thriving, in that in Cuba private enterprises are considered to be complementary to the large state enterprises that dominate its economy.[^cite_note-Oppmann-Frum-Atlantic-2026-complimentary-152]

Owners of small private restaurants (*[paladares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paladar)*) originally could seat no more than 12 people and can only employ family members.[^cite_note-O'Rourke2007-153] Set monthly fees must be paid regardless of income earned, and frequent inspections yield stiff fines when any of the many self-employment regulations are violated. As of 2012, more than 150,000 farmers had signed up to lease land from the government for bonus crops. Before, homeowners were only allowed to swap; once buying and selling were allowed, prices rose.[^cite_note-BBC2012SimonReeve-61]

In February 2021, the government said that it would allow the private sector to operate in most sectors of the economy, with only 124 activities reserved for the public sector,[^cite_note-\:2-73] such as national security, health, and educational services.[^cite_note-\:3-154] In August 2021, the Cuban government started allowing citizens to create small and medium-sized private companies, which are allowed to employ up to 100 people. As of 2023, 8,000 companies have been registered in Cuba.[^cite_note-\:4-150][^cite_note-155]

## Public finances

Information on Cuba's financial condition is limited due to inaccessibility of financial data published by the [Communist Party of Cuba](38776970.md). *[The Economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Economist)* estimated that Cuba maintained a [budget deficit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deficit_spending) of over 10% of GDP in 2024, one of highest deficit spending rates in the world.[^cite_note-\:7-156] The latest independently verified estimate of Cuba's foreign debt was published in 2020, with an approximate \$19.7 billion in foreign debt.[^cite_note-\:11-157] In 2017, Cuba's debt-to-GDP ratio was estimated as 47.7%.[^cite_note-CIAWFCU-12]

### Taxes and revenue

Main article: [Taxation in Cuba](42209204.md)

The Cuban government maintains a top individual income tax rate of 50% and a top corporate tax rate of 30%. An additional 4% tax is levied on wholly foreign-owned companies. During the [2009 global recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Recession), Cuba reported \$47 billion in tax revenue with \$50 billion in public spending.[^cite_note-The_World_Factbook-82]

### International debt repayment

Cuba has borrowed extensively since 1959 and been heavily indebted since the 1970s and 1980s with debts incurred under [Fidel Castro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fidel_Castro).[^cite_note-\:7-156] His brother, [Raúl Castro](221749.md), began a concerted effort to restructure and to ask for forgiveness of loans and debts with creditor countries following the 2009 global recession.[^cite_note-158][^cite_note-\:1-159] Since the 2010s, Cuba has defaulted on a significant amount of its sovereign debt, with large portion of their debt preemptively forgiven by many foreign creditors.[^cite_note-\:11-157][^cite_note-160]

In 2011, China forgave \$6 billion in debt owed to it by Cuba.[^cite_note-161]

In 2013, Mexico's Finance Minister [Luis Videgaray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luis_Videgaray) announced a loan issued by Mexico's foreign trade development bank [Bancomext](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bancomext) to Cuba more than 15 years prior was worth \$487 million. The governments agreed to "waive" 70% of it, approximately \$340.9 million. Cuba would repay the remaining \$146.1 million over ten years.[^cite_note-162]



Russian president [Vladimir Putin](32817.md) forgave 90% of Cuban debt owed to Russia in 2014

In 2014, before making a diplomatic visit to Cuba, Russian President [Vladimir Putin](32817.md) forgave over 90% of the debt owed to Russia by Cuba. The forgiveness totaled \$32 billion. A remaining \$3.2 billion would be paid over ten years.[^cite_note-\:1-159]

In 2015, Cuba entered into negotiations over its \$11.1 billion debt to 14 European nation-states of the [Paris Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Club). In December 2015, the parties announced an agreement – Paris Club nations agreed to forgive \$8.5 billion of the \$11.1 billion total debt, mostly by waiving interest, service charges, and penalties accrued over the more than two decades of non-payment. The regime viewed the agreement favorably to resolve the long-standing issues and build business confidence, increasing [direct foreign investment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_foreign_investment) and as a preliminary step to gaining access to credit lines in Europe.[^cite_note-163][^cite_note-164]

In 2018, during a diplomatic visit to Cuba, the General Secretary of the Communist Party of Vietnam [Nguyễn Phú Trọng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguy%E1%BB%85n_Ph%C3%BA_Tr%E1%BB%8Dng) wrote off Cuba's official debt to Vietnam. The forgiveness totaled \$143.7 million.[^cite_note-165][^cite_note-\:12-166]

In 2019, Cuba once again defaulted on its Paris Club debt. Of the estimated payment due in 2019 of \$80 million, Cuba made only a partial payment that left \$30 million owed for that year. Cuban Deputy Prime Minister Ricardo Cabrisas wrote a letter to [Odile Renaud-Basso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odile_Renaud-Basso), president of the Paris Club, noting that Cuba was aware that "circumstances dictated that we were not able to honour our commitments". He maintained that they had "the intention of settling" the payments in arrears by 31 May 2020.[^cite_note-\:13-167][^cite_note-\:14-168] In May 2020, with payments still not made, Deputy PM Cabrisas sent a letter to the fourteen Paris Club countries in the agreement requesting a "\[payment] moratorium for 2019, 2020 and 2021 and a return to paying in 2022".[^cite_note-\:15-169] Payments had still not resumed by August 2023, with a new payment calendar still being negotiated.[^cite_note-\:16-170][^cite_note-\:17-171]

### Wages

Public sector wages were increased in connection with the abolition of the Cuban convertible peso (CUC). In June 2019, the government announced an increase, especially for teachers and health personnel, by about 300%.[^cite_note-\:18-172]

On 1 January 2021, the government launched the "Tarea Ordenamiento" (Ordering Task), eliminating the use of the Cuban convertible peso (CUC) leaving only the Cuban peso (CUP) as currency. Wages and pensions were increased again, between 4 and 9 times, for all the public sectors. The dollar price was fixed by the Cuban central bank at 24 CUP, although Cubans could not buy dollars with pesos due to the drought of foreign currency created by the COVID-19 pandemic.[^cite_note-\:19-173]

The minimum wage for Cuba rose to 2,100 pesos at the same time as the Tarea Ordenamiento, and has remained there as of mid 2025. The average monthly salary, as of the end of 2024, was 5,839 pesos.[^cite_note-nothing-HT-30-7-2025-174]

### Income distribution

Overall [income distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Income_distribution) in Cuba compared favorably with that of other Latin American countries during the 19th century. "Available data must be viewed cautiously and assumed to portray merely a rough approximation of conditions at the time," according to author Susan Eckstein. There were profound [social inequalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_inequalities) between city and countryside and between whites and blacks, with trade and unemployment problems.[^cite_note-\:20-175]

Statistics cited by Carmelo Mesa-Lago and Jorge Pérez-López note that between 1989 and 1999 inequality on the island increased considerably: the [Gini coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gini_coefficient) rose from 0.250 to 0.407 and the ratio of earnings between the wealthiest 20% of Cubans and the least wealthy 20% grew from 3.8-to-1 to 13.5-to-1.[^cite_note-Mesa-Lago-2013-19-176]

The *[Miami Herald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Herald)* described levels of inequality in Cuba in 2016:

> ... about 27 percent of Cubans earn under \$50 per month; 34 percent earn the equivalent of \$50 to \$100 per month; and 20 percent earn \$101 to \$200. Twelve percent reported earning \$201 to \$500 a month; and almost 4 percent said their monthly earnings topped \$500, including 1.5 percent who said they earned more than \$1,000.[^cite_note-177]

### Welfare

Cuba's social welfare system elements of:&#x20;

1. universal free, state-financed system of public health-care;

   [^cite_note-Mesa-Lago_welfare_2023-178]

2. state-financed universal education system;

3. social insurance/pensions financed by state enterprises and the government;

4. universal subsidies on some food;

5. subsidized public housing.

Cuba has state-financed social assistance for groups that lack coverage.[^cite_note-Mesa-Lago_welfare_2023-178]

#### Social assistance

Social assistance is provided to groups deemed vulnerable including the disabled, mothers with children who have severe disabilities, and people who live alone with severe disabilities.[^cite_note-\:2025MesaLago-25]<sup>: 85 </sup> Social assistance includes, for example, monetary benefits, food, and personal services.[^cite_note-\:2025MesaLago-25]<sup>: 85 </sup> Because it is universal and free, the healthcare system already covers the poor.[^cite_note-\:2025MesaLago-25]<sup>: 86 </sup> Elderly people (age 65 for men, age 60 for women) in need, lacking a contributory pension, are provided with social assistance pensions.[^cite_note-\:2025MesaLago-25]<sup>: 85 </sup>

#### Pensions

Cuba has a public pension system.[^cite_note-\:2025MesaLago-25]<sup>: 73 </sup> From 1959 to 1963, the government unified all pension schemes into a single state-owned fund which was initially a [pay-as-you-go fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pay-as-you-go_pension_plan) but shortly thereafter changed to being funded through the government's general budget.[^cite_note-\:2025MesaLago-25]<sup>: 77 </sup> In 1989, Cuba had the most developed pension system in Latin America.[^cite_note-\:2025MesaLago-25]<sup>: 7 </sup> In 2009, Raúl Castro increased minimum pensions by \$2USD to \$9.50/month.[^cite_note-\:21-179]

#### Libreta

See also: [Rationing in Cuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationing_in_Cuba)

Cuba is also known for its system of food distribution – the *[libreta de abastecimiento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationing_in_Cuba)* ("supplies booklet"). Established in 1962 to ensure the equitable distribution of basic goods in the wake of a newly imposed American embargo, the system provides rations each person can buy for a fraction of their value. Originally intended to cover almost all food items, by mid-2024 it provided only a fraction of what it once did, its products often arriving "late, in poor quality or not at all".[^cite_note-Reuters_Slashes_17_September_2024-180][^cite_note-Pie-181]

### Human development rating

While income levels in Revolutionary Cuba are low, it has traditionally pointed to its high HDI ratings (HDI being a statistical composite of life expectancy, education and per capita income indicators) made possible by rationed food, free education, universal healthcare, etc. The [Human Development Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Development_Index) of Cuba ranked much higher than most Latin American nations in 2008.[^cite_note-comandate-182] Poverty levels reported by the government have traditionally been is one of the lowest in the developing world.[^cite_note-\:22-183]

In 2021, Cuba ranked 83rd out of 191 on the [Human Development Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Development_Index) in the high human development category.[^cite_note-2022_components-184] For 2023 Cuba's HDI rating was 0.762, slightly above the world rating of 0.756, but below its comparative rating in 2008 when it was among the highest in Latin America. It was now higher than some neighboring states such as Honduras, Suriname, Jamaica, but lower than Mexico, Brazil,Trinidad and Tobago and other countries.[^cite_note-UNHD-185]

Cuba's social welfare system is unique in Latin America due to the following characteristics: 1) a public health-care system that provides universal free services entirely financed by the state (there is no national health insurance); 2) an education system that is also public, universal, free and fully financed by the Treasury; 3) public (social insurance) contributory pensions mainly financed by state enterprises and the government with relatively small contributions from a minority of the insured; and 4) social assistance for vulnerable groups that lack social insurance coverage, also state-financed (there are no conditional cash transfer programs).
