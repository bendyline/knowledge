# Energy in the United States



US energy consumption in 2025

[^cite_note-EIA_1.3-1]



In 2024, wind and solar together overtook coal for the first time in the US. Natural gas remains the largest energy source in the US at 43%.

[^cite_note-2]

**Energy in the United States** is obtained from a variety of sources, although the majority came from [fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel) in 2025: 37% of the nation's [energy](533487.md) originated from [petroleum](23195.md), 36% from [natural gas](22131.md), and 9% from [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal). Electricity from [nuclear power](22153.md) supplied 9% and [renewable energy](25784.md) supplied 9%, which includes [biomass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomass_\(energy\)), [wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_power), [hydro](381399.md), [solar](13690575.md) and [geothermal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geothermal_energy).[^cite_note-EIA_Facts-3][^cite_note-EIA_1.3-1] Energy consumption in 2025 was mostly for industry (35%) and transportation (37%), with use in homes (15%) and commercial buildings (13%) making up the remainder.[^cite_note-EIA_2.1a-4][^cite_note-EIA_2.1b-5] The United States' portion of the [electrical grid in North America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_grid_in_North_America) had a [nameplate capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nameplate_capacity) of 1,281 [GW](21347693.md#Gigawatt)[^cite_note-EIA_Electric_4.3-6] and produced 4,429 [TWh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TWh)[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_7.2b-7] in 2025, using 32% of primary energy to do so.[^cite_note-8]

Natural gas overtook coal as the dominant source for electric generation in 2016. Coal was overtaken by nuclear for the first time in 2020 and by renewables in 2023.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_7.2b-7]

Energy figures are measured in [BTU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BTU), with 1 BTU equal to 1.055 [kJ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joule#Multiples) and 1 quadrillion BTU (1 [quad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quad_\(unit\))) equal to 1.055 EJ. Because BTU is a unit of [heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat), sources that generate electricity directly are multiplied by a conversion factor to equate them with sources that use a [heat engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_engine).[^cite_note-9]

The United States was the second-largest energy producer and consumer in 2024 after [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_policy_of_China).[^cite_note-EIA_World-10] The United States had a per capita energy consumption of 277 million BTU (292 GJ), ranking it tenth in the world behind [Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_in_Canada), [Iceland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_in_Iceland), and several [Arab nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_world).[^cite_note-EIA_World-10]

## History

Further information: [Environmental history of the United States § History of energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_history_of_the_United_States#History_of_energy)



Per capita energy use by source



The percentage of energy use by source



&#x20;Energy consumption by source

From its founding until the late 19th century, population and energy use in the United States both increased by about 3% per year,[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_D1-12] resulting in a relatively constant per capita energy use of 100 million BTU. [Wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood_fuel) made up the majority of this until near the end of the 1800s, meaning the average American burned eight tons of wood each year.[^cite_note-13]

The [Industrial Revolution in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution_in_the_United_States) saw an increased use of [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal). By the late 1800s, it had surpassed wood as the major source of energy, as it was cheaper for those living in cities. Lighting, heating and transportation could all be fueled by coal.[^cite_note-14] Between 1925 and 1975, coal use was more stagnant, although it dominated the growing electricity sector.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_7.2a-15] [Petroleum](23195.md) and [natural gas](22131.md) took its place, as car ownership doubled in the two decades [after the war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_history_of_the_United_States#Post-World_War_II_prosperity:_1945%E2%80%931973),[^cite_note-16] and as [the number of pipelines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas_pipeline_system_in_the_United_States) rose dramatically.[^cite_note-17]

During the first half of the 20th century, per capita energy use doubled to 200 million BTU, and reached 300 million BTU by the late 60s. After a peak of 360 million BTU in 1979, per capita consumption generally declined, and dipped below 300 million BTU in 2020.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_1.7-18] In comparison, the world average increased from 68 to 74 million BTU (72 to 78 GJ) per person between 1980 and 2020.[^cite_note-EIA_World-10]

Domestic oil production peaked in 1970 and did not recover for 40 years. However, [oil](4207510.md) imports allowed consumption to double. Oil represented the vast majority of net imports during this time.[^cite_note-EIA_Imports-19] Increased reliance on imported oil coincided with the [1973 oil crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_oil_crisis).

Total imports peaked in 2005, when they represented 30% of total consumption. A consistent decline occurred over the next 15 years, as oil production doubled and domestic use receded. This allowed the United States to be a net exporter of energy for the first time in 70 years. As of 2021, the US net exports 3.9% of energy production.[^cite_note-EIA_Imports-19]

Around 2010, many other trends began to reverse. By 2020, improvements in [fracking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fracking) had allowed [natural gas](22131.md) production to increase by more than half,[^cite_note-20] while coal decreased by half.

[Renewables](25784.md) in the 20th century consisted mainly of [hydro](381399.md) and wood. Hydro power had been relatively constant since 1970, while wood saw a bump in 1980. Between 2000 and 2020, wind, solar and biofuels increased by a factor of ten. By 2020, wind had surpassed hydro, and biofuels had surpassed wood.[^cite_note-EIA_Renewable-21]

## Primary energy production

[U.S. Energy Information Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Energy_Information_Administration).[^cite_note-23] View [source data](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Data\:US%20Primary%20Energy%20Production%20by%20Source.tab).

The United States is the world's second-largest producer of energy. It produces 16% of the world's energy, about three-fourths as much as China.[^cite_note-EIA_World-10] Since 2019, the country has been a net exporter of energy. In 2023, 102.8 quads were produced and net exports were 7.6% of production.[^cite_note-EIA_Imports-19]

[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_1.2-24]

### Fossil fuels

[Fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuels) have long produced most energy in the US, accounting for 84% of total production[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_1.2-24] and 60% of electric generation as of 2023.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_7.2a-15] Although costs for some fossil fuels are declining, renewables are becoming cheaper faster.[^cite_note-25] Despite this, use of fossil fuels has remained near 80% for the past 30 years.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_1.2-24]

The vast majority of [carbon emissions](9528025.md) in the US came from fossil fuels.[^cite_note-26] The largest sources for carbon pollution from energy were petroleum (46%), natural gas (35%) and coal (19%),[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_11.1-27] and of petroleum, motor gasoline (21%) and diesel (12%) were the largest contributors. As gas has been replacing coal, emissions from the two combined have declined from a peak in 2008, down 25% as of 2021. During the same time frame, petroleum declined by only 17%.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_11.1-27] Fossil fuels in the United States produced 8% more carbon dioxide in 2022 compared to 2020.[^cite_note-28]

In 2023, fossil fuels generated 2,509 TWh of electricity, accounting for 60 percent of all electricity generation.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_7.2a-15]

#### Coal

Main article: [Coal power in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_power_in_the_United_States)

See also: [List of coal-fired power stations in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_coal-fired_power_stations_in_the_United_States)

[Coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal) made up 11% of production in 2021,[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_1.2-24] 90% of which went to producing electricity.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_7.2a-15] Coal [electrified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrification) rapidly throughout the 50s, 60s and 70s and half of annual electricity was from coal until the 2010s. Coal peaked in 2006, when it represented a third of total energy production. During the 2010s it underwent a steady decline, mostly being replaced by natural gas. In contrast, net exports have remained near 2 quads in the same time period, but this now amounts to 17% of total production.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_1.4c-29]

The decline of coal has many factors, including the aforementioned rise of natural gas, the closing of old plants, and environmental regulations.[^cite_note-30] Coal supply has not become an issue, as the US has the largest coal reserve in the world, 40% larger than Russia.[^cite_note-EIA_World-10]

The first DC plant in the US was a coal plant in [New York City](645042.md), opened in 1882. The first AC plant was a coal plant in [Ehrenfeld, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ehrenfeld,_Pennsylvania), opened in 1902.[^cite_note-31] Among the largest plants still operating is the [James H. Miller Jr. Electric Generating Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_H._Miller_Jr._Electric_Generating_Plant) in [West Jefferson, Alabama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Jefferson,_Alabama)[^cite_note-32] and among the oldest is the James E. Rogers Energy Complex in [Mooresboro, North Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mooresboro,_North_Carolina).[^cite_note-33]

#### Natural gas

Main article: [Natural gas in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas_in_the_United_States)

See also: [List of natural gas-fired power stations in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_natural_gas-fired_power_stations_in_the_United_States)



US natural gas production, imports, and exports



Natural gas production by field

Dry [natural gas](22131.md) was 36% of production in 2021, making it the largest source of energy in the US.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_1.2-24] It is also the largest electricity source, making up 38% of generation.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_7.2a-15] Natural gas surpassed coal for production in 2011 and for generation in 2016. Between 2006 and 2022, the US has gone from net importing 4 quads of natural gas to exporting 4 quads.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_1.4c-29]

The United States has been the world's largest producer of natural gas since 2011, when it surpassed [Russia](25391.md). However, the US ranks 7th in proven reserves.[^cite_note-EIA_World-10] Differences in supply explain why gas is cheaper in the US than it is in Europe.[^cite_note-34]

Among the largest natural gas power plants in the US is [West County Energy Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_County_Energy_Center) in [Palm Beach County, Florida](73647.md).[^cite_note-35] [Philadelphia](50585.md) had the first natural gas distributor in the US.[^cite_note-36]

[Natural gas liquids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas_liquids) are liquid hydrocarbons obtained from natural gas fields. Production has tripled from 2000 to 2022, now making up 9% of fossil fuels.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_1.2-24] The US leads the world in NGL production, ahead of Saudi Arabia.[^cite_note-EIA_World-10]

During the 2023-24 winter heating season in the United States, natural gas inventories ended at 2,290 billion cubic feet (Bcf), 39% above the five-year average, due to mild weather resulting in reduced consumption. Withdrawals from storage were approximately 1,500 Bcf, lower than the usual 2,000 Bcf. By March 2024, the [Henry Hub](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Hub) spot price had decreased to \$1.50 per million British thermal units (MMBtu), significantly below the forecasted \$3.10/MMBtu, with expectations of prices staying below \$2.00/MMBtu until the second half of 2024, averaging \$2.20/MMBtu for the year[^cite_note-37]

#### Petroleum

Main article: [Petroleum in the United States](19086358.md)



US oil production, imports, and exports

[Crude oil](23195.md) made up 24% of production in 2021.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_1.2-24] Oil has more than doubled from a slump in the early 2000s, even surpassing its previous peak in 1970. Imports have declined during the same time period, but the US still net imports 20% of consumption.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_1.4c-29]

The US has been the largest producer of crude oil since 2018, ahead of Saudi Arabia.[^cite_note-EIA_World-10] Texas produces far more oil than any other state.[^cite_note-38]

U.S. oil and gas production was higher at the end of both the [Trump](4848272.md) and [Biden](145422.md) administrations compared to the beginning of their terms. Oil and gas constitute the majority of energy consumption in the United States. This production not only enhances energy security but also helps maintain lower prices. However, the combustion of oil and gas significantly contributes to climate change.[^cite_note-39]

### Nuclear



Cherenkov radiation

Main article: [Nuclear power in the United States](4131402.md)

[Nuclear power](22153.md) was 8.3% of total production[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_1.2-24] and 18.8% of electric generation[^cite_note-Electric_3.1.A-40] in 2021. Seven states made up half the total: [Illinois](14849.md) (12%), [Pennsylvania](23332.md) (10%), [South Carolina](27956.md) (7%), [Alabama](303.md) (6%), [North Carolina](21650.md) (6%), [Texas](29810.md) (5%) and [Tennessee](30395.md) (5%).

The US generated 772 TWh of nuclear power in 2022, surpassing [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_China) (395 TWh) and [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_France) (282 TWh). However, nuclear power constitutes a much smaller percentage of total power generation (18%) when compared to France (63%).[^cite_note-41]

Nuclear had significant growth from the 70s through the 90s, but has stagnated since.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_1.2-24] A cessation of new nuclear plant construction coincided with the [accident at Three Mile Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Mile_Island_accident).[^cite_note-42] Future growth may come from smaller reactors.[^cite_note-43]

Among the largest nuclear plants in the US are [Grand Gulf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Gulf_Nuclear_Station) in [Claiborne County, Mississippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claiborne_County,_Mississippi) and [Peach Bottom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peach_Bottom_Nuclear_Generating_Station) in [York County, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/York_County,_Pennsylvania).[^cite_note-44] The oldest reactor still in commercial operation is [Nine Mile Point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nine_Mile_Point) in [Scriba, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scriba,_New_York).[^cite_note-45]

### Renewables



Renewable energy sources, 2021.

[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_1.2-24]

Main article: [Renewable energy in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renewable_energy_in_the_United_States)

[Renewable energy](25784.md) in the United States accounted for 12.5% of the total production in 2021,[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_1.2-24] and 20.7% of electric generation.[^cite_note-Electric_3.1.A-40] The category has seen rapid growth, doubling in total output between 2000 and 2020. They have exceeded nuclear since 2011 and surpassed coal in 2020 for the first time since wood fuel fell out of use.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_1.2-24]

Renewables are expected to continue growing.[^cite_note-46] Because many of these are [variable renewable energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variable_renewable_energy), there are plans for considerable growth in [battery storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battery_storage_power_station).[^cite_note-47]

#### Biomass

See also: [Biofuel in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biofuel_in_the_United_States)



&#x20;An ethanol plant in [Nevada, Iowa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nevada,_Iowa)



&#x20;Wood burner in [Scotia, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotia,_California)&#x20;

[Biomass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomass_\(energy\)) made up 5% of total production and 38% of renewables in 2022.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_1.2-24] About 49% of this was [biofuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biofuels), 43% was [wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood_fuel), and 8% was [waste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Municipal_solid_waste#Energy_generation) and other biomass.

[Ethanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethanol) made up the majority of biofuels in 2022, while [biodiesel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiesel) and renewable diesel made up about 16%. For ethanol, about 40% of the available energy is lost or diverted to co-products during the manufacturing process,[^cite_note-48] and the equivalent of 20% is used to power and run the equipment.[^cite_note-49] About 45% of domestic corn output goes toward producing ethanol.[^cite_note-50] The US led the world in ethanol production in 2021, producing more than half of the total.[^cite_note-51] The US is a net exporter of biofuels.

Use of biofuels increased by a factor of 10 between 2000 and 2020, eventually exceeding wood.[^cite_note-EIA_Renewable-21] The desire for ethanol originated from the need to replace [methyl tert-butyl ether](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methyl_tert-butyl_ether), which was contaminating groundwater.[^cite_note-52] The [Energy Policy Act of 2005](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_Policy_Act_of_2005) mandated an increased use of ethanol.[^cite_note-53] Most [gasoline](23639.md) sold in the US contains up to 10% ethanol.[^cite_note-54]

Industry uses two-thirds of the wood fuel in the US, while the residential sector uses about a quarter. [Wood](38922691.md) and [paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulp_and_paper_industry) factories use wood waste on-site to reduce their energy costs. About 9% of homes used [firewood](481098.md) in 2020.[^cite_note-55]

Wood made up the majority of energy consumption until near the end of the 1800s, after which it declined in total use for several decades, to about half its peak.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_D1-12] The 80s saw the first significant increase in over a century, of about 65%, before declining again by the 2010s.[^cite_note-EIA_Renewable-21]

#### Geothermal



&#x20;[McGinness Hills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McGinness_Hills_Geothermal_Complex) in Nevada

Main article: [Geothermal energy in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geothermal_energy_in_the_United_States)

Two-thirds of the [geothermal energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geothermal_energy) in the US in 2021 was electric, with the remainder being direct use and heat pumps.[^cite_note-EIA_Renewable-21][^cite_note-56] [Geothermal power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geothermal_power) made up 2% of renewable generation,[^cite_note-Electric_3.1.A-40] 70% of which came from California.[^cite_note-State_Electric-57] [The Geysers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Geysers) is the largest complex of geothermal energy production in the world.[^cite_note-58] The US ranks first in geothermal capacity, ahead of [Indonesia](14579.md) and the [Philippines](23440.md).[^cite_note-IRENA-59]

#### Hydro



&#x20;[Grand Coulee Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Coulee_Dam) in Washington

Main article: [Hydroelectricity in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroelectricity_in_the_United_States)

[Hydro](381399.md) made up 6% of electric generation and 29% of renewable generation in 2021.[^cite_note-Electric_3.1.A-40] Three states made up over half of the total: [Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_\(State\)) (29%), [New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_\(State\)) (12%) and [Oregon](26811621.md) (11%).[^cite_note-State_Electric-57] Overall 246 TWh were generated across 1,449 conventional plants and 40 [pumped storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pumped-storage_hydroelectricity) plants.[^cite_note-EIA_Electric_4.1-60] As of 2021, the US was fourth in the world in total hydroelectric generation behind Canada and Brazil, each generating over 350 TWh. China produced the most, with an estimated 1,300 TWh.[^cite_note-IRENA-59]

Hydro has been used in the US since 1880 when it powered the Wolverine Chair factory in [Grand Rapids, Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Rapids,_Michigan).[^cite_note-Hydro_explained-61] Additionally electricity from hydropower has been sold in the U.S. since 1882 with a plant in [Appleton, Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appleton,_Wisconsin) being the first to sell it.[^cite_note-62]By 1950, 29% of total electricity production came from hydro, as it produced 96 TWh.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_7.2b-7] Hydro generation had tripled by the 70s, but has not consistently grown since. Hydro has had large fluctuations from year to year: for example in 2010, generation jumped from 253 to 311 TWh the next year, before dropping to 269 TWh the year after that. While hydro has maintained generation in the 200 to 350 TWh range for the past few decades, its share of the total has declined as other sources have risen. Since 2019, wind power has exceeded hydro as the largest renewable electricity source.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_7.2b-7]

At 132 years old, the plant in [Whiting, Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whiting,_Wisconsin) is the oldest power plant still running in the US.[^cite_note-Hydro_explained-61] The [Grand Coulee Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Coulee_Dam) is the largest plant [for hydro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_hydroelectric_power_stations_in_the_United_States) and [in general](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_power_stations_in_the_United_States) in the US, and the fifth-largest hydro plant [in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_hydroelectric_power_stations). Built in Washington in 1942, it continues to operate with a capacity of 6,765 MW.[^cite_note-Hydro_explained-61] The [Bath County Pumped Storage Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bath_County_Pumped_Storage_Station) is the second-largest such facility in the world, with a capacity of 3,003 MW.[^cite_note-63]

#### Solar



&#x20;[Concentrated solar thermal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentrated_solar_power) plant ([Ivanpah Solar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivanpah_Solar_Power_Facility))

Main article: [Solar power in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_power_in_the_United_States)

[Solar](13690575.md) made up 4% of electric generation and 19% of renewable generation in 2021. Of the 164 TWh generated, 70% was by utilities and an estimated 30% was small-scale such as rooftop solar.[^cite_note-Electric_3.1.A-40] Three states made up over half of total grid generation: [California](5407.md) (30%), [Texas](29810.md) (13%) and [North Carolina](21650.md) (9%).[^cite_note-State_Electric-57] In 2021, the US grid produced the second most solar power in the world, behind China's 328 GWh.[^cite_note-IRENA-59]

Since 2006, solar has been the fastest growing energy source in the US, expanding 50% per year on average.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_7.2a-15] The largest solar farms in the US include [Solar Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_Star) in [Rosamond, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosamond,_California), [Desert Sunlight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desert_Sunlight_Solar_Farm) in [Desert Center, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desert_Center,_California) and [Copper Mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_Mountain_Solar_Facility) in [Boulder City, Nevada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boulder_City,_Nevada).[^cite_note-EIA_Plants-64] The [Bureau of Land Management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_of_Land_Management) leases federal land to be used for solar farms.[^cite_note-65]

Almost all states employ [net metering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Net_metering) to compensate owners of small-scale solar, although [policies by state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Net_metering_in_the_United_States) vary considerably.[^cite_note-66]

#### Wind



&#x20;Wind turbines in [Maine](19977.md)

Main article: [Wind power in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_power_in_the_United_States)

[Wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_power) made up 9% of electric generation and 44% of renewable generation in 2021.[^cite_note-Electric_3.1.A-40] Four states made up over half of the total: [Texas](29810.md) (26%), [Iowa](26810748.md) (10%), [California](5407.md) (9%) and [Kansas](16716.md) (7%).[^cite_note-State_Electric-57] The US grid produces significantly more wind power than Germany or India, but less than half of China.[^cite_note-IRENA-59]

Since 1998, wind power has grown 23% per year on average in the US.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_7.2a-15] Prominent areas for wind turbines include [Alta Wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alta_Wind_Energy_Center) in [Kern County, California](80557.md), [Shepherds Flat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shepherds_Flat_Wind_Farm) in [Gilliam County, Oregon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilliam_County,_Oregon) and [Roscoe Wind Farm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roscoe_Wind_Farm) in [Nolan County, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nolan_County,_Texas).[^cite_note-EIA_Plants-64]

## Final energy consumption



Consumption by sector, 2021

&#x20;&#x20;

An example of each consumption sector: a refinery, a house, a car and an office

[Industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industry_\(economics\)) has long been the country's largest energy sector.[^cite_note-EIA_2.1a-4][^cite_note-EIA_2.1b-5] It used 33% of total energy in 2021, most of which was divided evenly between natural gas, electricity and petroleum. A survey from 2018 estimated that the largest energy users were the [chemical industry](58721.md) (30%), [petroleum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_refining_processes) and [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_preparation_plant) processing (18%), [mining](23756501.md) (9%) and [paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulp_and_paper_industry) (9%).[^cite_note-67]

The most energy-intensive industry was by far petroleum and coal, at over 30 billion BTU per employee. The paper industry was second at 6.5 billion BTU per employee. Each of these handles energy sources as part of their raw materials (fossil fuels and wood).[^cite_note-68] The same survey found that half of the electric use was to drive machines and about 10% each for heating, cooling and electro-chemical processes. Most of the remainder was for factory lighting and [HVAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HVAC). About half of the natural gas was for process heating, and most of the rest was for [boilers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boilers).[^cite_note-69]

[Transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transportation) used 28% of energy, almost all of which was petroleum and other fuels. Half of the combustible fuels that make up the transportation sector were [gasoline](23639.md), and half of the vehicle usage was for [cars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cars) and small trucks.[^cite_note-70] [Diesel](81761.md) and [heavier trucks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_truck_types) each made up about a quarter of their respective categories; [jet fuel](1205435.md) and [aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_travel) were about a tenth each. Biofuels such as ethanol and biodiesel made up 5%, while natural gas was 4%. Electricity from [mass transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_transit) was 0.2%; electricity for light passenger vehicles is counted in other sectors, but figures from the US Department of Energy estimate that 2.1 million electric vehicles used 6.1 TWh to travel 19 billion miles, indicating an average fuel efficiency of 3.1 miles per kWh.[^cite_note-71]

Over two-thirds of the energy used by homes, offices, and other commercial businesses is electric, including [electric losses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_conversion_efficiency).[^cite_note-72][^cite_note-73] Most of the energy used in homes was for space heating (34%) and [water heating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_heating) (19%), much more than the amount used for space cooling (16%) and [refrigeration](1036259.md) (7%).[^cite_note-74] Businesses use similar percentages for space cooling and refrigeration. They use less for space and water heating but more for [lighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lighting) and cooking.[^cite_note-75]

Most homes in the US are [single-family detached](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House),[^cite_note-76] which on average use almost triple the energy of [apartments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apartments) in larger buildings.[^cite_note-77] However, single family households have 50% more persons and triple the floor space. Usage per square foot of living space is roughly equal for most housing types except small apartment buildings and [mobile homes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_homes). Small apartments are more likely to be older than other housing types,[^cite_note-78] while mobile homes tend to have poor [insulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Building_insulation).[^cite_note-79]

### Regional variation

The state with the lowest per capita energy use is [Rhode Island](25410.md), at 161 million BTU per year, and the highest is [Louisiana](18130.md), at 908 million BTU per year. Energy use and prices often have an inverse relationship; [Hawaii](13270.md) uses some of the least energy per capita but pays the highest price on average, while Louisiana pays the least on average.[^cite_note-SEDS-80]

Residential prices follow a similar trend, but the differences between states are usually less drastic. The exception is Hawaii, which pays 84 cents per million BTU, more than double the next largest state of [Florida](18933066.md), which pays 33 cents.[^cite_note-81]

Household energy use varies by home type and by region. Although single-family detached homes are less common in the [Northeast](431669.md), the average house there uses 60% more energy than one in the [West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_United_States). Some of the regional differences can be explained by climate, as two-thirds of northeastern homes are in cold regions, while less than a third of western homes are.[^cite_note-82][^cite_note-83]

The land-use decisions of cities and towns also explain some of the regional differences in energy use. [Townhouses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Townhouses) are more energy efficient than single-family homes because less heat, for example, is used per person. Similarly, areas with more homes in a compact neighborhood encourage walking, biking and transit, thereby reducing transportation energy use. A 2011 U.S. EPA study found that multi-family homes in urban neighborhoods, with well-insulated buildings and fuel-efficient cars, use less than two-thirds of the energy used by conventionally built single-family houses in suburban areas (with standard cars).[^cite_note-84]

## Electricity

Main article: [Electricity sector of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electricity_sector_of_the_United_States)



United States power stations by type and nameplate capacity



Generation by source

[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_7.2a-15]



Electricity prices by sector in the United States

The United States is the world's second-largest producer and consumer of electricity. It generates 15% of the world's electricity supply, about half as much as China.[^cite_note-EIA_World_Electric-85]

The United States produced 3,988 TWh in 2021. Total generation has been flat since 2010. Net electricity imports were 39 TWh, or about 1% of sales. Historically, net imports have been between just under 0% to just over 1.5%.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_7.2b-7]

[Fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel) made up the majority of generation, with natural gas providing 38% and coal 23%. Petroleum provided less than 1%. Natural gas as a proportion of fossil fuels has been increasing since the 90's, with coal peaking around 2008 and natural gas becoming the top fossil fuel in 2016.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_7.2a-15]

Nuclear provided 20%, a level consistent since about 1990. Nuclear surpassed coal in 2020 for the first time, as coal dipped to its lowest proportion in over 70 years.

Among renewables, wind and solar continued to expand, with wind exceeding hydro since 2019. Renewable use has doubled from 2010 to 2020, reaching 21% of total generation.

### Electric consumption



Home electrification

[Electric grid consumption](6168349.md) in the US was 3,806 TWh in 2021. Since 2010, total consumption has remained within 2% of this figure. Per capita consumption was 11.5 MWh in 2021, down 8% from its peak in 2007.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_7.6-88][^cite_note-US_Stats-89]

Residential customers used 39% of total electricity.[^cite_note-EIA_Monthly_7.6-88] Each month, the average customer used 886 kWh and paid \$121 at an average rate of 13.7 cents/kWh.[^cite_note-90] The commercial sector used 35% and industrial used 26%. Transportation used less than half of one percent.

System loss within the grid includes use in the generation process and transmission losses, as well as unaccounted loads. For 2021, this amounted to 203 TWh, or 5.3% of grid generation. Electricity used directly at the commercial or industrial level added 139 TWh, so total consumption was 3,945 TWh.[^cite_note-91]

The northeastern US has long paid the highest electricity prices, while simultaneously using it the least per capita.[^cite_note-92] [California](5407.md) is an outlier in the west region; its neighbors tend to pay some of the lowest rates in the country, while California is second only to [Hawaii](13270.md) and [Alaska](624.md).
