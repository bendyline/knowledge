**Mars** is the fourth [planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet) from the [Sun](26751.md). It is also known as the "**Red Planet**",[^cite_note-nasa-facts-25] for [its orange-red](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_surface_color) appearance.[^cite_note-Rees2012-26][^cite_note-nasa_hematite-27] Mars is a desert-like [rocky planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocky_planet) with a tenuous [atmosphere](4548379.md) that is primarily [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) (CO<sub>2</sub>). At the average surface level the atmospheric pressure is a few thousandths of [Earth](9228.md)'s, atmospheric temperature ranges from −153 to 20 °C (−243 to 68 °F),[^cite_note-nasa-facts-25] and [cosmic radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_radiation) is high. Mars retains some water, [in the ground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater_on_Mars) as well as thinly in the atmosphere, forming [cirrus clouds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cirrus_cloud), [fog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fog), [frost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frost), larger polar regions of [permafrost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permafrost) and [ice caps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martian_polar_ice_caps) (with seasonal CO<sub>2</sub> snow), but no bodies of liquid surface water. Its surface gravity is roughly a third of Earth's or double that of the Moon. Its mean diameter, 6,779 km (4,212 mi),[^cite_note-28] is about [half the Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Solar_System_objects_by_size)'s, or twice the Moon's, and its surface area is around the size of all the dry land of Earth.[^cite_note-nasa-facts-25]

[Fine dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martian_regolith#Atmospheric_dust) is prevalent across the surface and the atmosphere, being picked up and spread at the low Martian gravity even by the weak wind of the tenuous atmosphere. The terrain of Mars roughly follows a north–south divide, the [Martian dichotomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martian_dichotomy), with the northern hemisphere mainly consisting of relatively flat, low lying [plains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plain), and the southern hemisphere of cratered [highlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highland). [Geologically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology_of_Mars), the planet is fairly active with [marsquakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsquake) trembling underneath the ground, but also hosts many enormous [volcanoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcano) that are extinct (the tallest is [Olympus Mons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympus_Mons), 21.9 km or 13.6 mi tall), as well as one of the largest [canyons](78478.md) in the Solar System ([Valles Marineris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valles_Marineris), 4,000 km or 2,500 mi long). Mars has [two natural satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moons_of_Mars) that are small and irregular in shape: [Phobos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phobos_\(moon\)) and [Deimos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deimos_\(moon\)). With a significant [axial tilt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axial_tilt) of 25 degrees, Mars experiences [seasons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Season), like Earth (which has an axial tilt of 23.5 degrees). A [Martian solar year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timekeeping_on_Mars) is equal to 1.88 Earth years (687 Earth days), a Martian solar day ([sol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_sol)) is equal to 24.6 hours.

Mars formed along with the other planets approximately 4.5 billion years ago. During the martian [Noachian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noachian) period (4.5 to 3.5 billion years ago), its surface was marked by [meteor impacts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_event), valley formation, erosion, the [possible presence of water oceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_ocean_theory) and the loss of its [magnetosphere](20479.md). The [Hesperian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hesperian) period (beginning 3.5 billion years ago and ending 3.3–2.9 billion years ago) was dominated by widespread [volcanic activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanism) and flooding that carved immense [outflow channels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outflow_channels). The [Amazonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazonian_\(Mars\)) period, which continues to the present, is the currently dominating and remaining [influence on geological processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeolian_processes). Because of Mars's geological history, the possibility of past or present [life on Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_on_Mars) remains an area of active scientific investigation, with some possible traces needing further examination.

Being [visible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apparent_magnitude) with the naked eye in Earth's sky as a red [wandering star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_planet), Mars has been [observed throughout history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Mars_observation), acquiring diverse associations in different cultures. In 1963 the first flight to Mars took place with [Mars 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_1), but communication was lost en route. The first successful flyby [exploration of Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exploration_of_Mars) was conducted in 1965 with [Mariner 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariner_4). In 1971 [Mariner 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariner_9) entered orbit around Mars, being the first spacecraft to orbit any body other than the Moon, Sun or Earth; following in the same year were the first uncontrolled impact ([Mars 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_2)) and first successful landing ([Mars 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_3)) on Mars. Probes have been active on Mars continuously since 1997. At times, more than ten [probes have simultaneously operated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_missions_to_Mars) in orbit or on the surface, more than at any other planet beyond Earth. Mars is an often proposed target for future [crewed exploration missions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_mission_to_Mars), though no such mission is currently planned.

## Natural history

Main article: [Geological history of Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geological_history_of_Mars)

### Formation

Scientists have theorized that during the [Solar System's formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formation_and_evolution_of_the_Solar_System), Mars was created as the result of a [random process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_process) of run-away [accretion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accretion_\(astrophysics\)) of material from the [protoplanetary disk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protoplanetary_disk) that orbited the Sun. Mars has many distinctive chemical features caused by its position in the Solar System. Elements with comparatively low boiling points, such as [chlorine](5667.md), [phosphorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphorus), and [sulfur](27127.md), are much more common on Mars than on Earth; these elements were probably pushed outward by the young Sun's energetic [solar wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_wind).[^cite_note-ssr96_1_4_197-29]

### Late Heavy Bombardment

After the formation of the planets, the inner Solar System may have been subjected to the so-called [Late Heavy Bombardment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Heavy_Bombardment). About 60% of the surface of Mars shows a record of impacts from that era,[^cite_note-zharkov93-30][^cite_note-icarus165_1-31][^cite_note-barlow88-32] whereas much of the remaining surface is probably underlain by immense impact basins caused by those events. However, more recent modeling has disputed the existence of the Late Heavy Bombardment.[^cite_note-33] There is evidence of an enormous impact basin in the Northern Hemisphere of Mars, spanning 10,600 by 8,500 kilometres (6,600 by 5,300 mi), or roughly four times the size of the Moon's [South Pole–Aitken basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Pole%E2%80%93Aitken_basin), which would be the largest impact basin yet discovered if confirmed.[^cite_note-northcratersn-34] It has been hypothesized that the basin was formed when Mars was struck by a [Pluto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pluto)-sized body about four billion years ago. The event, thought to be the cause of the [Martian hemispheric dichotomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martian_dichotomy), created the smooth [Borealis basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borealis_basin) that covers 40% of the planet.[^cite_note-sciam080627-35][^cite_note-nyt080626-36]

A 2023 study shows evidence, based on the [orbital inclination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_inclination) of [Deimos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deimos_\(moon\)) (a small [moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_satellite) of Mars), that Mars may once have had a [ring system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_system) 3.5 billion years to 4 billion years ago.[^cite_note-37] This ring system may have been formed from a moon, 20 times more [massive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) than [Phobos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phobos_\(moon\)), orbiting Mars billions of years ago; and Phobos would be a remnant of that ring.[^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39]

### Geological periods



Martian time periods (millions of years ago)

Epochs:



The geological history of Mars can be split into many periods, but the following are the three primary periods:[^cite_note-jog91-40][^cite_note-ssr_96_1_4-41]

- [Noachian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noachian) period: Formation of the oldest extant surfaces of Mars, 4.5 to 3.5 billion years ago. Noachian age surfaces are scarred by many large impact craters. The [Tharsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tharsis) bulge, a volcanic upland, is thought to have formed during this period, with extensive flooding by liquid water late in the period. Named after [Noachis Terra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noachis_Terra).

  [^cite_note-\:0-42]

- [Hesperian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hesperian) period: 3.5 to between 3.3 and 2.9 billion years ago. The Hesperian period is marked by the formation of extensive lava plains. Named after [Hesperia Planum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hesperia_Planum).

  [^cite_note-\:0-42]

- [Amazonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazonian_\(Mars\)) period: between 3.3 and 2.9 billion years ago to the present. Amazonian regions have few [meteorite impact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_event) craters but are otherwise quite varied. [Olympus Mons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympus_Mons) formed during this period, with lava flows elsewhere on Mars. Named after [Amazonis Planitia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazonis_Planitia).

  [^cite_note-\:0-42]

### Recent geological activity

Geological activity is still taking place on Mars. The [Athabasca Valles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athabasca_Valles) is home to sheet-like lava flows created about 200 million years ago. Water flows in the [grabens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graben) called the [Cerberus Fossae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerberus_Fossae) occurred less than 20 million years ago, indicating equally recent volcanic intrusions.[^cite_note-ag44_4-43] The *[Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_Reconnaissance_Orbiter)* has captured images of avalanches.[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45]

## Physical characteristics

Main articles: [Geology of Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology_of_Mars) and [Composition of Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composition_of_Mars)



Mars depicted to scale alongside the [planetary-mass objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary-mass_object) of the [Inner Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_Solar_System). From left: [Mercury](19694.md), [Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus), [Earth](9228.md), the [Moon](19331.md), Mars and [Ceres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceres_\(dwarf_planet\)).

Mars is approximately half the diameter of Earth or twice that of the Moon, with a surface area only slightly less than the total area of Earth's dry land.[^cite_note-NasaFactSheet-3] Mars is less dense than Earth, having about 15% of Earth's volume and 11% of Earth's [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass), resulting in about 38% of [Earth's surface gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_of_Earth). Mars is the only presently known example of a [desert planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desert_planet), a rocky planet with a surface akin to that of Earth's [deserts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desert). The red-orange appearance of the Martian surface is caused by [iron(III) oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron\(III\)_oxide) ([nanophase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanophase) Fe<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>) and the [iron(III) oxide-hydroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron\(III\)_oxide-hydroxide) mineral [goethite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goethite).[^cite_note-rust-46] It can look like [butterscotch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butterscotch);[^cite_note-ismars-47] other common surface colors include golden, brown, tan, and greenish, depending on the [minerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineral) present.[^cite_note-ismars-47]

### Internal structure



Internal structure of Mars, assuming no inner core.

[^cite_note-Stähler-48]

[^cite_note-2023Natur.622..712S-49]

Mapped gravity field of Mars

Like Earth, Mars is [differentiated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_differentiation) into a dense metallic [core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_core) overlaid by less dense rocky layers.[^cite_note-Nimmo_2005-50][^cite_note-mars-in-depth-51] The outermost layer is the crust, which is on average about 42–56 kilometres (26–35 mi) thick,[^cite_note-2023GeoRL..5003482K-52] with a minimum thickness of 6 kilometres (3.7 mi) in [Isidis Planitia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isidis_Planitia), and a maximum thickness of 117 kilometres (73 mi) in the southern [Tharsis plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tharsis).[^cite_note-2022JGRE..12707298W-53] For comparison, Earth's crust averages 27.3 ± 4.8 km in thickness.[^cite_note-2013GGG....14.2003H-54] The most abundant elements in the Martian [crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crust_\(geology\)) are [silicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon), [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen), [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron), [magnesium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium), [aluminum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium), [calcium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium), and [potassium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium). Mars is confirmed to be seismically active;[^cite_note-2023GeoRL..5003619F-55] in 2019, it was reported that [InSight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/InSight) had detected and recorded over 450 [marsquakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsquake) and related events.[^cite_note-2020NatCo..11.1014G-56][^cite_note-2020NatGe..13..183B-57]

Beneath the crust is a silicate [mantle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantle_\(geology\)) responsible for many of the [tectonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tectonics_of_Mars) and [volcanic features](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanology_of_Mars) on the planet's surface. The upper Martian mantle is a [low-velocity zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-velocity_zone), where the velocity of seismic waves is lower than surrounding depth intervals. The mantle appears to be rigid down to the depth of about 250 km,[^cite_note-2023Natur.622..712S-49] giving Mars a very thick [lithosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithosphere) compared to Earth. Below this the mantle gradually becomes more ductile, and the seismic wave velocity starts to grow again.[^cite_note-Khan2021-58] The Martian mantle does not appear to have a thermally insulating layer analogous to Earth's [lower mantle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_mantle); instead, below 1050 km in depth, it becomes mineralogically similar to Earth's [transition zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_zone_\(Earth\)).[^cite_note-Stähler-48] At the bottom of the mantle lies a basal [liquid silicate layer](51138349.md) approximately 150–180 km thick.[^cite_note-2023Natur.622..712S-49][^cite_note-2023Natur.622..718K-59] The Martian mantle appears to be highly heterogenous, with dense fragments up to 4 km across, likely injected deep into the planet by colossal impacts \~4.5 billion years ago; high-frequency waves from eight marsquakes slowed as they passed these localized regions, and modeling indicates the heterogeneities are compositionally distinct debris preserved because Mars lacks plate tectonics and has a sluggishly convecting interior that prevents complete homogenization.[^cite_note-Charalambous2025-60][^cite_note-61]

Mars's [iron and nickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron%E2%80%93nickel_alloy) core is at least partially molten, and may have a solid inner core.[^cite_note-2023Natur.619..733L-62][^cite_note-ArsTechnicaOn2023Natur.619..733L-63][^cite_note-Bi2025-64] It is around half of Mars's radius, approximately 1650–1675 km, and is enriched in light elements such as [sulfur](27127.md), oxygen, [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon), and [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen).[^cite_note-10.1038/d41586-023-03151-x-65][^cite_note-10.1038/d41586-023-03271-4-66] The temperature of the core is estimated to be 2000–2400 K,[^cite_note-67] compared to 5400–6230 K for [Earth's solid inner core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_inner_core). Whether or not a solid inner core is present inside Mars remains a topic of active research; a 2023 study using data from the [InSight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/InSight) lander suggested the absence of a solid inner core,[^cite_note-2023Natur.619..733L-62] however a 2025 study based on the same data reported the detection of a solid inner core 613 kilometres (381 mi) ± 67 kilometres (42 mi) in radius.[^cite_note-Bi2025-64]

### Surface geology

Further information: [Martian regolith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martian_regolith)

Mars 360 animation of the [Martian surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martian_surface)

Mars is a [terrestrial planet](48510.md) with a surface that consists of minerals containing [silicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon) and oxygen, [metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal), and other elements that typically make up [rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_\(geology\)). The [Martian surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martian_surface) is primarily composed of [tholeiitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tholeiitic_magma_series) [basalt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basalt),[^cite_note-science324_5928_736-68] although parts are more [silica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silica)-rich than typical basalt and may be similar to [andesitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andesitic) rocks on Earth, or silica glass. Regions of low [albedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albedo) suggest concentrations of [plagioclase feldspar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plagioclase_feldspar), with northern low albedo regions displaying higher than normal concentrations of sheet silicates and high-silicon glass. Parts of the southern highlands include detectable amounts of high-calcium [pyroxenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyroxene). Localized concentrations of [hematite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hematite) and [olivine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olivine) have been found.[^cite_note-jgr107_E6-69] Much of the surface is deeply covered by finely grained [iron(III) oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron\(III\)_oxide) dust.[^cite_note-sci300a-70]

The *[Phoenix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenix_\(spacecraft\))* lander returned data showing Martian soil to be slightly alkaline and containing elements such as [magnesium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium), [sodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium), [potassium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium) and [chlorine](5667.md). These nutrients are found in soils on Earth, and are necessary for plant growth.[^cite_note-bbc080627-71] Experiments performed by the lander showed that the Martian soil has a [basic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_\(chemistry\)) [pH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH) of 7.7, and contains 0.6% [perchlorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perchlorate) by weight,[^cite_note-72][^cite_note-73] concentrations that are [toxic to humans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martian_soil#Toxicity).[^cite_note-toxicmars-74][^cite_note-toxicsoil1-75]

[Streaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_slope_streak) are common across Mars and new ones appear frequently on steep slopes of craters, troughs, and valleys. The streaks are dark at first and get lighter with age. The streaks can start in a tiny area, then spread out for hundreds of metres. They have been seen to follow the edges of boulders and other obstacles in their path. The commonly accepted hypotheses include that they are dark underlying layers of soil revealed after avalanches of bright dust or [dust devils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_devil).[^cite_note-jpl_dust_devil-76] Several other explanations have been put forward, including those that involve water or even the growth of organisms.[^cite_note-gpl29_23_41-77][^cite_note-oleb33_4_515-78]

[Environmental radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_radioactivity) levels on the surface are on average 0.64 [millisieverts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sievert) of radiation per day, and significantly less than the radiation of 1.84 millisieverts per day or 22 [millirads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rad_\(radiation_unit\)) per day during the flight to and from Mars.[^cite_note-Williams_2016-79][^cite_note-Wall_2013-80] For comparison the radiation levels in [low Earth orbit](47568.md), where Earth's [space stations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_station) orbit, are around 0.5 millisieverts of radiation per day.[^cite_note-NASA_Jet_Propulsion_Laboratory_(JPL)_2003-81] [Hellas Planitia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellas_Planitia) has the lowest surface radiation at about 0.342 millisieverts per day, featuring [lava tubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lava_tube) southwest of [Hadriacus Mons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadriacus_Mons) with potentially levels as low as 0.064 millisieverts per day,[^cite_note-Paris_Davies_Tognetti_Zahniser_2020-82] comparable to radiation levels during flights on Earth.

### Magnetic characteristics

Main article: [Magnetic field of Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field_of_Mars)

Although Mars has no evidence of a structured [global magnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field_of_Mars),[^cite_note-magnetosphere-83] observations show that parts of the planet's crust have been magnetized, suggesting that alternating polarity reversals of its dipole field have occurred in the past. This [paleomagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleomagnetism) of magnetically susceptible minerals is similar to the [alternating bands found on Earth's ocean floors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_tectonics#Magnetic_striping). One hypothesis, published in 1999 and re-examined in October 2005 (with the help of the *[Mars Global Surveyor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_Global_Surveyor)*), is that these bands suggest [plate tectonic activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_tectonics) on Mars four billion years ago, before the planetary [dynamo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamo_theory) ceased to function and the planet's magnetic field faded.[^cite_note-plates-84]

## Geography and features

Main article: [Geography of Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Mars)

Further information: [Areoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Areoid)

See also: [Category\:Surface features of Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category\:Surface_features_of_Mars)



Topographical map of Mars with features labeled and the [Martian dichotomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martian_dichotomy) visible (northern low lying and southern highland hemispheres)



[Mars surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martian_surface) map rendered using a color map and a [heightmap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heightmap) from [NASA](18426568.md)

Although better remembered for mapping the Moon, [Johann Heinrich von Mädler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Heinrich_von_M%C3%A4dler) and [Wilhelm Beer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Beer) were the first areographers. They began by establishing that most of Mars's surface features were permanent and by more precisely determining the planet's rotation period. In 1840, Mädler combined ten years of observations and drew the first map of Mars.[^cite_note-85]

Features on Mars are named from a variety of sources. [Albedo features](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_albedo_features_on_Mars) are named for classical mythology. Craters larger than roughly 50 km are named for deceased scientists and writers and others who have contributed to the study of Mars. Smaller craters are named for towns and villages of the world with populations of less than 100,000. Large valleys are named for the word "Mars" or "star" in various languages; smaller valleys are named for rivers.[^cite_note-usgs-86]

Large [albedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albedo) features retain many of the older names but are often updated to reflect new knowledge of the nature of the features. For example, *Nix Olympica* (the snows of Olympus) has become *[Olympus Mons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympus_Mons)* (Mount Olympus).[^cite_note-viking_1950_2000-87] The surface of Mars as seen from Earth is divided into two kinds of areas, with differing albedo. The paler plains covered with dust and sand rich in reddish iron oxides were once thought of as Martian "continents" and given names like [Arabia Terra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabia_Terra) (*land of Arabia*) or [Amazonis Planitia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazonis_Planitia) (*Amazonian plain*). The dark features were thought to be seas, hence their names [Mare Erythraeum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mare_Erythraeum), Mare Sirenum and [Aurorae Sinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurorae_Sinus). The largest dark feature seen from Earth is [Syrtis Major Planum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrtis_Major_Planum).[^cite_note-88] The permanent northern polar ice cap is named [Planum Boreum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planum_Boreum). The southern cap is called [Planum Australe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planum_Australe).[^cite_note-\:3-89]

Mars's equator is defined by its rotation, but the location of its [Prime Meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Meridian) was specified, as was Earth's (at [Greenwich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenwich)), by choice of an arbitrary point; Mädler and Beer selected a line for their first maps of Mars in 1830. After the spacecraft *[Mariner 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariner_9)* provided extensive imagery of Mars in 1972, a small crater (later called [Airy-0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airy-0)), located in the [Sinus Meridiani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinus_Meridiani) ("Middle Bay" or "Meridian Bay"), was chosen by [Merton E. Davies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merton_E._Davies), [Harold Masursky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Masursky), and [Gérard de Vaucouleurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%A9rard_de_Vaucouleurs) for the definition of 0.0° longitude to coincide with the original selection.[^cite_note-90][^cite_note-archinal_caplinger-91][^cite_note-92]

Because Mars has no oceans, and hence no "[sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level)", a zero-elevation surface had to be selected as a reference level; this is called the *[areoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Areoid)*[^cite_note-NASAMola2007-93] of Mars, analogous to the terrestrial [geoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoid).[^cite_note-ArdalanKarimi2009-94] Zero altitude was defined by the height at which there is 610.5 [Pa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal_\(unit\)) (6.105 [mbar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bar_\(unit\))) of atmospheric pressure.[^cite_note-pers66-95] This pressure corresponds to the [triple point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triple_point) of water, and it is about 0.6% of the sea level surface pressure on Earth (0.006 atm).[^cite_note-lunine99-96]

For mapping purposes, the [United States Geological Survey](23814944.md) divides the surface of Mars into [thirty cartographic quadrangles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_quadrangles_on_Mars), each named for a classical albedo feature it contains.[^cite_note-97] In April 2023, *[The New York Times](30680.md)* reported an updated global map of Mars based on images from the [Hope spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hope_Mars_Mission).[^cite_note-NYT-20230415-98] A related, but much more detailed, global Mars map was released by [NASA](18426568.md) on 16 April 2023.[^cite_note-STD-20230416-99]

### Volcanoes

Main article: [Volcanism on Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanism_on_Mars)



Picture of the tallest volcano on Mars, [Olympus Mons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympus_Mons). It is approximately 550 km (340 mi) across.

The vast upland region [Tharsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tharsis) contains several massive volcanoes, which include the [shield volcano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shield_volcano) [Olympus Mons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympus_Mons). The edifice is over 600 km (370 mi) wide.[^cite_note-100][^cite_note-Plescia04-101] Because the mountain is so large, with complex structure at its edges, giving a definite height to it is difficult. Its local relief, from the foot of the cliffs which form its northwest margin to its peak, is over 21 km (13 mi),[^cite_note-Plescia04-101] a little over twice the height of [Mauna Kea](267366.md) as measured from its base on the ocean floor. The total elevation change from the plains of [Amazonis Planitia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazonis_Planitia), over 1,000 km (620 mi) to the northwest, to the summit approaches 26 km (16 mi),[^cite_note-Comins-102] roughly three times the height of [Mount Everest](42179.md), which in comparison stands at just over 8.8 kilometres (5.5 mi). Consequently, Olympus Mons is either the [tallest or second-tallest mountain in the Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_mountains_in_the_Solar_System); the only known mountain which might be taller is the [Rheasilvia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rheasilvia) peak on the asteroid [Vesta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4_Vesta), at 20–25 km (12–16 mi).[^cite_note-Schenk2012-103]

### Impact topography

The [dichotomy of Martian topography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martian_dichotomy) is striking: northern plains flattened by lava flows contrast with the southern highlands, pitted and cratered by ancient impacts. It is possible that, four billion years ago, the Northern Hemisphere of Mars was struck by an object one-tenth to two-thirds the size of Earth's [Moon](19331.md). If this is the case, the Northern Hemisphere of Mars would be the site of an [impact crater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_crater) 10,600 by 8,500 kilometres (6,600 by 5,300 mi) in size, or roughly the area of Europe, Asia, and Australia combined, surpassing [Utopia Planitia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utopia_Planitia) and the Moon's [South Pole–Aitken basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Pole%E2%80%93Aitken_basin) as the largest impact crater in the Solar System.[^cite_note-104][^cite_note-\:6-105][^cite_note-106]

Mars is scarred by 43,000 impact craters with a diameter of 5 kilometres (3.1 mi) or greater.[^cite_note-wright03-107] The largest exposed crater is [Hellas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellas_Planitia), which is 2,300 kilometres (1,400 mi) wide and 7,000 metres (23,000 ft) deep, and is a light [albedo feature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albedo_feature) clearly visible from Earth.[^cite_note-108][^cite_note-109] There are other notable impact features, such as [Argyre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argyre_Planitia), which is around 1,800 kilometres (1,100 mi) in diameter,[^cite_note-110] and [Isidis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isidis_Planitia), which is around 1,500 kilometres (930 mi) in diameter.[^cite_note-111] Due to the smaller mass and size of Mars, the probability of an object colliding with the planet is about half that of Earth. Mars is located closer to the [asteroid belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid_belt), so it has an increased chance of being struck by materials from that source. Mars is more likely to be struck by short-period [comets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comet), *i.e.*, those that lie within the orbit of [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter).[^cite_note-emp9-112]

Martian craters can have a morphology that suggests the ground became wet after the meteor impact.[^cite_note-emp45-113]

### Tectonic sites



[Valles Marineris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valles_Marineris), taken by the *[Viking 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viking_1)* probe

The large canyon, [Valles Marineris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valles_Marineris) (Latin for [Mariner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariner_program) Valleys, also known as Agathodaemon in the old canal maps[^cite_note-114]), has a length of 4,000 kilometres (2,500 mi) and a depth of up to 7 kilometres (4.3 mi). The length of Valles Marineris is equivalent to the length of Europe and extends across one-fifth the circumference of Mars. By comparison, the [Grand Canyon](46989.md) on Earth is only 446 kilometres (277 mi) long and nearly 2 kilometres (1.2 mi) deep. Valles Marineris was formed due to the swelling of the Tharsis area, which caused the crust in the area of Valles Marineris to collapse. In 2012, it was proposed that Valles Marineris is not just a [graben](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graben), but a plate boundary where 150 kilometres (93 mi) of [transverse motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transform_fault) has occurred, making Mars a planet with possibly a two-[tectonic plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tectonic_plate) arrangement.[^cite_note-tectonic-115][^cite_note-Lin,_An-116]

### Holes and caves

Images from the [Thermal Emission Imaging System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_Emission_Imaging_System) (THEMIS) aboard NASA's [Mars Odyssey orbiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001_Mars_Odyssey) have revealed seven possible [cave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cave) entrances on the flanks of the volcano [Arsia Mons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arsia_Mons).[^cite_note-cushing_titus_wynn07-117] The caves, named after loved ones of their discoverers, are collectively known as the "seven sisters".[^cite_note-nau070328-118] Cave entrances measure from 100 to 252 metres (328 to 827 ft) wide and they are estimated to be at least 73 to 96 metres (240 to 315 ft) deep. Because light does not reach the floor of most of the caves, they may extend much deeper than these lower estimates and widen below the surface. "Dena" is the only exception; its floor is visible and was measured to be 130 metres (430 ft) deep. The interiors of these caverns may be protected from micrometeoroids, UV radiation, [solar flares](54648.md) and high energy particles that bombard the planet's surface.[^cite_note-bbc070317-119][^cite_note-120]

## Atmosphere

Main article: [Atmosphere of Mars](4548379.md)

Image of Mars

A broad view of Mars's atmosphere by *Hope* orbiter.

Mars lost its [magnetosphere](20479.md) 4 billion years ago,[^cite_note-swind-124] possibly because of numerous asteroid strikes,[^cite_note-125] so the [solar wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_wind) interacts directly with the Martian [ionosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionosphere), lowering the atmospheric density by [stripping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_stripping) away atoms from the outer layer.[^cite_note-\:7-126] Both *[Mars Global Surveyor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_Global_Surveyor)* and *[Mars Express](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_Express)* have detected ionized atmospheric particles trailing off into space behind Mars,[^cite_note-swind-124][^cite_note-swind2-127] and this atmospheric loss is being studied by the [MAVEN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MAVEN) orbiter. Compared to Earth, the [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_body_atmosphere) of Mars is quite rarefied. [Atmospheric pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_pressure) on the surface today ranges from a low of 30 [Pa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal_\(unit\)) (0.0044 [psi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound_per_square_inch)) on [Olympus Mons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympus_Mons) to over 1,155 Pa (0.1675 psi) in [Hellas Planitia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellas_Planitia), with a mean pressure at the surface level of 600 Pa (0.087 psi).[^cite_note-bolonkin09-128] The highest atmospheric density on Mars is equal to that found 35 kilometres (22 mi)[^cite_note-atkinson07-129] above Earth's surface. The resulting mean surface pressure is only 0.6% of Earth's 101.3 kPa (14.69 psi). The [scale height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_height) of the atmosphere is about 10.8 kilometres (6.7 mi),[^cite_note-carr06-130] which is higher than Earth's 6 kilometres (3.7 mi), because the surface [gravity of Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_of_Mars) is only about 38% of Earth's.[^cite_note-131]

The atmosphere of Mars consists of about 96% [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide), 1.93% [argon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argon) and 1.89% [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen) along with traces of [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen) and water.[^cite_note-NasaFactSheet-3][^cite_note-Abundance-132][^cite_note-\:7-126] The atmosphere is quite dusty, containing particulates about 1.5 [μm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%CE%9Cm) in diameter which give the [Martian sky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extraterrestrial_skies#Mars) a [tawny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tawny_\(color\)) color when seen from the surface.[^cite_note-dusty-133] It may take on a [pink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pink) hue due to [iron oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_oxide) particles suspended in it.[^cite_note-Rees2012-26]

&#x20;Despite repeated detections of [methane on Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane_on_Mars),[^cite_note-134] there is no scientific consensus as to its origin.[^cite_note-135] One suggestion is that methane exists on Mars and that its concentration fluctuates seasonally.[^cite_note-Sample2018-136][^cite_note-plumes-137][^cite_note-nature460-138] The existence of methane could be produced by non-biological process such as [serpentinization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serpentinite) involving water, carbon dioxide, and the mineral [olivine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olivine), which is known to be common on Mars, or by Martian life.[^cite_note-139][^cite_note-olivine-140]



[Escaping atmosphere](4548379.md) on Mars ([carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon), [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen), and [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen)) by [MAVEN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MAVEN) in [UV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraviolet%E2%80%93visible_spectroscopy)

[^cite_note-NASA-20141014-NJ-141]

Compared to Earth, its higher concentration of atmospheric CO<sub>2</sub> and lower surface pressure may be why sound is attenuated more on Mars, where natural sources are rare apart from the wind. Using acoustic recordings collected by the *Perseverance* rover, researchers concluded that the speed of sound there is approximately 240 m/s for frequencies below 240 Hz, and 250 m/s for those above.[^cite_note-142][^cite_note-143]

[Auroras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurora_on_Mars) have been detected on Mars.[^cite_note-144][^cite_note-145][^cite_note-NASA-20150318-146] Because Mars lacks a global magnetic field, the types and distribution of auroras there differ from those on Earth;[^cite_note-147] rather than being mostly restricted to polar regions as is the case on Earth, a Martian aurora can encompass the planet.[^cite_note-\:8-148] In September 2017, NASA reported [radiation](25856.md) levels on the surface of the planet Mars were temporarily [doubled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orders_of_magnitude_\(radiation\)), and were associated with an [aurora](49658.md) 25 times brighter than any observed earlier, due to a massive, and unexpected, [solar storm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronal_mass_ejection) in the middle of the month.[^cite_note-\:8-148][^cite_note-nasa20170929-149]

### Climate

Main article: [Climate of Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_of_Mars)



Mars without (on left) and with a global dust storm in July 2001 (on right), including different visible water ice cloud covers, as seen by the Hubble Space Telescope.



Rendering of the change of [CO<sub>2</sub> ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dry_ice) (not water ice) coverage on the north (left) and south (right) poles of Mars between northern and southern summer.

Mars has seasons, alternating between its northern and southern hemispheres, similar to on Earth. Additionally the orbit of Mars has, compared to Earth's, a large [eccentricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_eccentricity) and approaches [perihelion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apsis) when it is summer in its southern hemisphere and winter in its northern, and aphelion when it is winter in its southern hemisphere and summer in its northern. As a result, the seasons in its southern hemisphere are more extreme and the seasons in its northern are milder than would otherwise be the case. The summer temperatures in the south can be warmer than the equivalent summer temperatures in the north by up to 30 °C (54 °F).[^cite_note-goodman97-150]

Martian surface temperatures vary from lows of about −110 °C (−166 °F) to highs of up to 35 °C (95 °F) in equatorial summer.[^cite_note-hot-19] The wide range in temperatures is due to the thin atmosphere which cannot store much solar heat, the low atmospheric pressure (about 1% that of the [atmosphere of Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_Earth)), and the low [thermal inertia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_inertia) of Martian soil.[^cite_note-nasa_surface-151] The planet is 1.52 times as far from the Sun as Earth, resulting in just 43% of the amount of sunlight.[^cite_note-disc920901-152][^cite_note-153]

Mars has the largest [dust storms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_storm) in the Solar System, reaching speeds of over 160 km/h (100 mph). These can vary from a storm over a small area, to gigantic storms that cover the entire planet. They tend to occur when Mars is closest to the Sun, and have been shown to increase global temperature.[^cite_note-philips01-154] Seasons also produce [dry ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dry_ice) covering [polar ice caps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martian_polar_ice_caps).[^cite_note-a652-155]

## Hydrology

Main article: [Water on Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_on_Mars)

See also: [Groundwater on Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater_on_Mars)



Mars contains water, though mostly as dust covered polar layers of ice, as mapped in this image.

While Mars contains [water in larger amounts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_on_terrestrial_planets_of_the_Solar_System), most of it is dust covered water ice at the [Martian polar ice caps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martian_polar_ice_caps).[^cite_note-specials1-156][^cite_note-bbc040124-157][^cite_note-158][^cite_note-sci299-159][^cite_note-\:3-89] The volume of water ice in the south polar ice cap, if melted, would be enough to cover most of the surface of the planet with a depth of 11 metres (36 ft).[^cite_note-nasa070315-160]

Water in its liquid form cannot persist on the surface due to Mars's low atmospheric pressure, which is less than 1% that of Earth.[^cite_note-nasa.gov-161] Only at the lowest of elevations are the pressure and temperature high enough for liquid water to exist for short periods.[^cite_note-mars-in-depth-51][^cite_note-jgr110-162]

Although little water is present in the atmosphere, there is enough to produce clouds of water ice and different cases of [snow](28191.md) and frost, often mixed with snow of [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) [dry ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dry_ice).[^cite_note-k104-163]

### Past hydrosphere



Artist's impression of Mars four billion years ago

[Landforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geomorphology) visible on Mars strongly suggest that liquid water has existed on the planet's surface. Huge linear swathes of scoured ground, known as [outflow channels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outflow_channels), cut across the surface in about 25 places. These are thought to be a record of erosion caused by the catastrophic release of water from subsurface aquifers, though some of these structures have been hypothesized to result from the action of glaciers or lava.[^cite_note-Kerr2005-164][^cite_note-Jaeger2007-165] One of the larger examples, [Ma'adim Vallis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ma'adim_Vallis), is 700 kilometres (430 mi) long, much greater than the Grand Canyon, with a width of 20 kilometres (12 mi) and a depth of 2 kilometres (1.2 mi) in places. It is thought to have been carved by flowing water early in Mars's history.[^cite_note-lucchita_rosanova-166] The youngest of these channels is thought to have formed only a few million years ago.[^cite_note-nature434-167]

Elsewhere, particularly on the oldest areas of the Martian surface, finer-scale, dendritic [networks of valleys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valley_networks_\(Mars\)) are spread across significant proportions of the landscape. Features of these valleys and their distribution strongly imply that they were carved by [runoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_runoff) resulting from precipitation in early Mars history. Subsurface water flow and [groundwater sapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater_sapping) may play important subsidiary roles in some networks, but precipitation was probably the root cause of the incision in almost all cases.[^cite_note-CraddockHoward2002-168]

Along craters and canyon walls, there are thousands of features that appear similar to terrestrial [gullies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gullies). The gullies tend to be in the highlands of the Southern Hemisphere and face the Equator; all are poleward of 30° latitude. A number of authors have suggested that their formation process involves liquid water, probably from melting ice,[^cite_note-sci288-169][^cite_note-nasa061206-170] although others have argued for formation mechanisms involving carbon dioxide frost or the movement of dry dust.[^cite_note-bbc061206-171][^cite_note-nasa061206b-172] No partially degraded gullies have formed by weathering and no superimposed impact craters have been observed, indicating that these are young features, possibly still active.[^cite_note-nasa061206-170] Other geological features, such as [deltas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_delta) and [alluvial fans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alluvial_fan) preserved in craters, are further evidence for warmer, wetter conditions at an interval or intervals in earlier Mars history.[^cite_note-Lewis2006-173] Such conditions necessarily require the widespread presence of [crater lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_crater_lake) across a large proportion of the surface, for which there is independent mineralogical, sedimentological and geomorphological evidence.[^cite_note-Matsubara2011-174] Further evidence that liquid water once existed on the surface of Mars comes from the detection of specific minerals such as [hematite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hematite) and [goethite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goethite), both of which sometimes form in the presence of water.[^cite_note-nasa040303-175]

### History of observations and findings of water evidence

Main articles: [Water on Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_on_Mars) and [Chronology of discoveries of water on Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronology_of_discoveries_of_water_on_Mars)



As top surface layer water appears readily visible at some places on Mars, as in this polar [crater called Korolev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korolev_\(Martian_crater\)), seen here in [3D projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D_projection).

The chemical signature of water vapor on Mars was first unequivocally demonstrated in 1963 by spectroscopy using an Earth-based telescope.[^cite_note-176][^cite_note-177] In 2004, *Opportunity* detected the mineral [jarosite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jarosite). This forms only in the presence of acidic water, showing that water once existed on Mars.[^cite_note-nasa101001-178][^cite_note-179] The *Spirit* rover found concentrated deposits of [silica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silica) in 2007 that indicated wet conditions in the past, and in December 2011, the mineral [gypsum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gypsum), which also forms in the presence of water, was found on the surface by NASA's Mars rover Opportunity.[^cite_note-180][^cite_note-nasa-181][^cite_note-nationalgeographic-182] It is estimated that the amount of water in the upper mantle of Mars, represented by [hydroxyl ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroxyl_ion) contained within Martian minerals, is equal to or greater than that of Earth at 50–300 parts per million of water, which is enough to cover the entire planet to a depth of 200–1,000 metres (660–3,280 ft).[^cite_note-nationalgeographic1-183][^cite_note-184]

On 18 March 2013, [NASA](18426568.md) reported evidence from instruments on the [*Curiosity* rover](36645032.md) of [mineral hydration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineral_hydration), likely hydrated [calcium sulfate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium_sulfate), in several [rock samples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_\(geology\)) including the broken fragments of ["Tintina" rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tintina_\(rock\)) and ["Sutton Inlier" rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_rocks_on_Mars#Curiosity) as well as in [veins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vein_\(geology\)) and [nodules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nodule_\(geology\)) in other rocks like "Knorr" rock and "Wernicke" rock.[^cite_note-NASA-20130318-185][^cite_note-BBC-20130319-186] Analysis using the rover's [DAN instrument](36645032.md#Dynamic_Albedo_of_Neutrons_\(DAN\)) provided evidence of subsurface water, amounting to as much as 4% water content, down to a depth of 60 centimetres (24 in), during the rover's traverse from the *[Bradbury Landing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bradbury_Landing)* site to the *Yellowknife Bay* area in the *[Glenelg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glenelg,_Mars)* terrain.[^cite_note-NASA-20130318-185] In September 2015, NASA announced that they had found strong evidence of hydrated [brine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brine) flows in [recurring slope lineae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recurring_slope_lineae), based on spectrometer readings of the darkened areas of slopes.[^cite_note-187][^cite_note-188][^cite_note-Ojhaetal2015-189] These streaks flow downhill in Martian summer, when the temperature is above −23 °C, and freeze at lower temperatures.[^cite_note-190] These observations supported earlier hypotheses, based on timing of formation and their rate of growth, that these dark streaks resulted from water flowing just below the surface.[^cite_note-SeasonalFlows-191] However, later work suggested that the lineae may be dry, granular flows instead, with at most a limited role for water in initiating the process.[^cite_note-192] A definitive conclusion about the presence, extent, and role of liquid water on the Martian surface remains elusive.[^cite_note-193][^cite_note-194]

Researchers suspect much of the low northern plains of the planet were [covered with an ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_ocean_hypothesis) hundreds of meters deep, though this theory remains controversial.[^cite_note-Head1999-195] In March 2015, scientists stated that such an ocean might have been the size of Earth's [Arctic Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_Ocean). This finding was derived from the ratio of [protium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protium_\(isotope\)) to [deuterium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deuterium) in the modern Martian atmosphere compared to that ratio on Earth. The amount of Martian deuterium (D/H = 9.3 ± 1.7 10<sup>−4</sup>) is five to seven times the amount on Earth (D/H = 1.56 10<sup>−4</sup>), suggesting that ancient Mars had significantly higher levels of water. Results from the *Curiosity* rover had previously found a high ratio of deuterium in [Gale Crater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gale_Crater), though not significantly high enough to suggest the former presence of an ocean. Other scientists caution that these results have not been confirmed, and point out that Martian climate models have not yet shown that the planet was warm enough in the past to support bodies of liquid water.[^cite_note-NYT-20150305-196] Near the northern polar cap is the 81.4 kilometres (50.6 mi) wide [Korolev Crater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korolev_\(Martian_crater\)), which the [Mars Express](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_Express) orbiter found to be filled with approximately 2,200 cubic kilometres (530 cu mi) of water ice.[^cite_note-TG1218-197]

In November 2016, NASA reported finding a large amount of underground ice in the [Utopia Planitia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utopia_Planitia) region. The volume of water detected has been estimated to be equivalent to the volume of water in [Lake Superior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Superior) (which is 12,100 cubic kilometers[^cite_note-198]).[^cite_note-199][^cite_note-NASA-20161122-200] During observations from 2018 through 2021, the ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter spotted indications of water, probably subsurface ice, in the Valles Marineris canyon system.[^cite_note-201]

## Orbital motion

Main article: [Orbit of Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_of_Mars)

See also: [Timekeeping on Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timekeeping_on_Mars)

Mars circling the Sun further and slower than Earth

Orbit of Mars and other Inner Solar System planets

Mars's average distance from the Sun is roughly 230 million km (143 million mi), and its orbital period is 687 (Earth) days. The solar day (or [sol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_sol)) on Mars is only slightly longer than an Earth day: 24 hours, 39 minutes, and 35.244 seconds.[^cite_note-202] A Martian year is equal to 1.8809 Earth years, or 1 year, 320 days, and 18.2 hours.[^cite_note-NasaFactSheet-3] The [gravitational potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_potential) difference and thus the [delta-v needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta-v_budget) to transfer between Mars and Earth is the second lowest for Earth.[^cite_note-Petropoulos_Longuski_Bonfiglio_2000_pp._776–783-203][^cite_note-Taylor_2020-204]

The axial tilt of Mars is 25.19° relative to its [orbital plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_plane), which is similar to the [axial tilt of Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecliptic#Obliquity_of_the_ecliptic).[^cite_note-NasaFactSheet-3] As a result, Mars has seasons like Earth, though on Mars they are nearly twice as long because its orbital period is that much longer. In the present day, the orientation of the [north pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_pole) of Mars is close to the star [Deneb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deneb).[^cite_note-barlow08-24]

Mars has a relatively pronounced [orbital eccentricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_eccentricity) of about 0.09; of the seven other planets in the Solar System, only [Mercury](19694.md) has a larger orbital eccentricity. It is known that in the past, Mars has had a much more circular orbit. At one point, 1.35 million Earth years ago, Mars had an eccentricity of roughly 0.002, much less than that of Earth today.[^cite_note-mars_eccentricity-205] Mars's [cycle of eccentricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milankovitch_cycles) is 96,000 Earth years compared to Earth's cycle of 100,000 years.[^cite_note-Meeus2003-206]

Mars has its closest approach to Earth ([opposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opposition_\(astronomy\))) in a [synodic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synodic_period) of 779.94 days. It should not be confused with Mars [conjunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjunction_\(astronomy\)), where the Earth and Mars are at opposite sides of the Solar System and form a straight line crossing the Sun. The average time between the successive oppositions of Mars, its synodic period, is 780 days; but the number of days between successive oppositions can range from 764 to 812.[^cite_note-Meeus2003-206] The distance at close approach varies between about 54 and 103 million km (34 and 64 million mi) due to the planets' [elliptical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellipse) orbits, which causes comparable variation in [angular size](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_size).[^cite_note-Laskar2003-207] At their furthest Mars and Earth can be as far as 401 million km (249 million mi) apart.[^cite_note-m455-208] Mars comes into opposition from Earth every 2.1 years. The planets come into opposition near Mars's [perihelion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perihelion) in 2003, 2018 and 2035, with the 2020 and 2033 events being particularly close to perihelic opposition.[^cite_note-\:1-209][^cite_note-210][^cite_note-211]



Mars seen through a 16-inch amateur telescope, at 2020 opposition

The mean [apparent magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apparent_magnitude) of Mars is +0.71 with a standard deviation of 1.05.[^cite_note-Mallama_and_Hilton-22] Because the orbit of Mars is eccentric, the magnitude at [opposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opposition_\(planets\)) from the Sun can range from about −3.0 to −1.4.[^cite_note-MallamaSky-212] The minimum brightness is magnitude +1.86 when the planet is near [aphelion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apsis) and in [conjunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjunction_\(astronomy\)) with the Sun.[^cite_note-Mallama_and_Hilton-22] At its brightest, Mars (along with [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter)) is second only to Venus in apparent brightness.[^cite_note-Mallama_and_Hilton-22] Mars usually appears distinctly yellow, orange, or red. When farthest away from Earth, it is more than seven times farther away than when it is closest. Mars is usually close enough for particularly good viewing once or twice at 15-year or 17-year intervals.[^cite_note-213] Optical ground-based telescopes are typically limited to resolving features about 300 kilometres (190 mi) across when Earth and Mars are closest because of Earth's atmosphere.[^cite_note-usra-214]

As Mars approaches opposition, it begins a period of [retrograde motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apparent_retrograde_motion), which means it will appear to move backwards in a looping curve with respect to the background stars. This retrograde motion lasts for about 72 days, and Mars reaches its peak apparent brightness in the middle of this interval.[^cite_note-zeilik02-215]

## Moons

Main articles: [Moons of Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moons_of_Mars), [Phobos (moon)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phobos_\(moon\)), and [Deimos (moon)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deimos_\(moon\))



The moons of Mars [Deimos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deimos_\(moon\)) and [Phobos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phobos_\(moon\)) as seen from Mars, compared at [apparent size](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_size) to the in apparent and actual size much larger [Moon](19331.md) as seen from Earth. If they would be as far away from Mars as the Moon from Earth, they would appear as faint star-like features in the Martian sky.



Orbits of natural and artificial satellites around Mars at scale, with the furthest (Deimos) at 23,460 km (14,580 mi)

Mars has two relatively small (compared to Earth's) natural moons, [Phobos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phobos_\(moon\)) (about 22 km (14 mi) in diameter) and [Deimos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deimos_\(moon\)) (about 12 km (7.5 mi) in diameter), which orbit at 9,376 km (5,826 mi) and 23,460 km (14,580 mi) around the planet. The origin of both moons is unclear, although a popular theory states that they were asteroids captured into Martian orbit.[^cite_note-esa31031-216]

Both satellites were discovered in 1877 by [Asaph Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asaph_Hall) and were named after the characters [Phobos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phobos_\(mythology\)) (the deity of panic and fear) and [Deimos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deimos_\(mythology\)) (the deity of terror and dread), twins from [Greek mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mythology) who accompanied their father [Ares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ares), god of war, into battle.[^cite_note-217] [Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_\(mythology\)) was the Roman equivalent to Ares. In modern [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language), the planet retains its ancient name *Ares* (Aris: *Άρης*).[^cite_note-\:6-105]

From the surface of Mars, the motions of Phobos and Deimos appear different from that of the Earth's satellite, the [Moon](19331.md). Phobos rises in the west, sets in the east, and rises again in just 11 hours. Deimos, being only just outside [synchronous orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synchronous_orbit) – where the orbital period would match the planet's period of rotation – rises as expected in the east, but slowly. Because the orbit of Phobos is below a synchronous altitude, [tidal forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_force) from Mars are gradually lowering its orbit. In about 50 million years, it could either crash into Mars's surface or break up into a ring structure around the planet.[^cite_note-218]

The origin of the two satellites is not well understood. Their low albedo and [carbonaceous chondrite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonaceous_chondrite) composition have been regarded as similar to asteroids, supporting a capture theory. The unstable orbit of Phobos would seem to point toward a relatively recent capture. But both have [circular orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_orbit) near the equator, which is unusual for captured objects, and the required capture dynamics are complex. Accretion early in the history of Mars is plausible, but would not account for a composition resembling asteroids rather than Mars itself, if that is confirmed.[^cite_note-219] Mars may have yet-undiscovered moons, smaller than 50 to 100 metres (160 to 330 ft) in diameter, and a dust ring is predicted to exist between Phobos and Deimos.[^cite_note-adler-220]

A third possibility for their origin as satellites of Mars is the involvement of a third body or a type of impact disruption. More-recent lines of evidence for Phobos having a highly porous interior,[^cite_note-Andert-221] and suggesting a composition containing mainly [phyllosilicates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phyllosilicates) and other minerals known from Mars,[^cite_note-Giuranna-222] point toward an origin of Phobos from material ejected by an impact on Mars that reaccreted in Martian orbit, similar to the [prevailing theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giant_impact_hypothesis) for the origin of Earth's satellite. Although the [visible and near-infrared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_and_near-infrared) (VNIR) spectra of the moons of Mars resemble those of outer-belt asteroids, the [thermal infrared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_infrared) spectra of Phobos are reported to be inconsistent with [chondrites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chondrite) of any class.[^cite_note-Giuranna-222] It is also possible that Phobos and Deimos were fragments of an older moon, formed by debris from a large impact on Mars, and then destroyed by a more recent impact upon the satellite.[^cite_note-223]

More recently, a study conducted by a team of researchers from multiple countries suggests that a lost moon, at least fifteen times the size of Phobos, may have existed in the past. By analyzing rocks which point to tidal processes on the planet, it is possible that these tides may have been regulated by a past moon.[^cite_note-224]

## Human observations and exploration

Main articles: [Exploration of Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exploration_of_Mars) and [History of Mars observation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Mars_observation)

The history of observations of Mars is marked by oppositions of Mars when the planet is closest to Earth and hence is most easily visible, which occur every couple of years. Even more notable are the [perihelic oppositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perihelic_opposition) of Mars, which are distinguished because Mars is close to perihelion, making it even closer to Earth.[^cite_note-\:1-209]

### Ancient observations

The ancient [Sumerians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumer) named Mars [Nergal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nergal), the god of war and plague. During Sumerian times, Nergal was a minor deity of little significance, but, during later times, his main cult center was the city of [Nineveh](21699.md).[^cite_note-Rabkin2005-225] In Mesopotamian texts, Mars is referred to as the "star of judgement of the fate of the dead".[^cite_note-226] The existence of Mars as a wandering object in the night sky was also recorded by the ancient [Egyptian astronomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_astronomy) and, by 1534 BCE, they were familiar with the [retrograde motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apparent_retrograde_motion) of the planet.[^cite_note-paob85-227] By the period of the [Neo-Babylonian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Babylonian_Empire), the [Babylonian astronomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_astronomy) were making regular records of the positions of the planets and systematic observations of their behavior. For Mars, they knew that the planet made 37 [synodic periods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_period), or 42 circuits of the zodiac, every 79 years. They invented arithmetic methods for making minor corrections to the predicted positions of the planets.[^cite_note-north08-228][^cite_note-swerdlow98-229] In [Ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece), the planet was known as [Πυρόεις](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyroeis).[^cite_note-230] Commonly, the Greek name for the planet now referred to as Mars, was Ares. It was the Romans who named the planet Mars, for their god of war, often represented by the sword and shield of the planet's namesake.[^cite_note-231]

In the fourth century BCE, [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle) noted that Mars disappeared behind the Moon during an [occultation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occultation), indicating that the planet was farther away.[^cite_note-232] [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy), a Greek living in [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria),[^cite_note-harland-233] attempted to address the problem of the orbital motion of Mars. Ptolemy's model and his collective work on astronomy was presented in the multi-volume collection later called the *[Almagest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almagest)* (from the Arabic for "greatest"), which became the authoritative treatise on [Western astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_astronomy) for the next fourteen centuries.[^cite_note-234] Literature from ancient China confirms that Mars was known by [Chinese astronomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_astronomy) by no later than the fourth century BCE.[^cite_note-needham_ronan85-235] In the [East Asian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Asian) cultures, Mars is traditionally referred to as the "fire star" (火星) based on the [*Wuxing* system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wuxing_\(Chinese_philosophy\)).[^cite_note-236][^cite_note-237][^cite_note-238]

### Early modern observations



Diagram of the [geocentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocentric) trajectory of Mars through several periods of [apparent retrograde motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apparent_retrograde_motion) in [Johannes Kepler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Kepler)'s *Astronomia Nova* (1609)

In 1609 [Johannes Kepler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Kepler) published a 10-year study of Martian orbit,[^cite_note-v529-239] using the [diurnal parallax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diurnal_parallax) of Mars, measured by [Tycho Brahe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tycho_Brahe), to make a preliminary calculation of the relative distance to the planet.[^cite_note-taton03-240] From Brahe's observations of Mars, Kepler deduced that the planet orbited the Sun not in a circle, but in an [ellipse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellipse). Moreover, Kepler showed that Mars sped up as it approached the Sun and slowed down as it moved farther away, in a manner that later physicists would explain as a consequence of the [conservation of angular momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_angular_momentum).[^cite_note-TMU-241]<sup>: 433–437 </sup>



First drawn record of Martian features (Manuscript K), by [Christiaan Huygens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christiaan_Huygens), observed on 28 November 1659.

[^cite_note-v462-242]

&#x20;The feature is probably [Syrtis Major Planum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrtis_Major_Planum). Huygens calculated from the moving of the features the rotation of Mars.

In 1610 the first use of a telescope for astronomical observation, including Mars, was performed by Italian astronomer [Galileo Galilei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_Galilei).[^cite_note-jha15-243] With the telescope the diurnal parallax of Mars was again measured in an effort to determine the Sun-Earth distance. This was first performed by [Giovanni Domenico Cassini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Domenico_Cassini) in 1672. The early parallax measurements were hampered by the quality of the instruments.[^cite_note-hirschfeld01-244] The only [occultation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occultation) of Mars by Venus observed was that of 13 October 1590, seen by [Michael Maestlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Maestlin) at [Heidelberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heidelberg).[^cite_note-sat57-245]

### Martian "canals"

Main article: [Martian canals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martian_canals)



A 1962 map of Mars published by the U.S. [Aeronautical Chart and Information Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeronautical_Chart_and_Information_Center), showing canals snaking through the Martian landscape. At the time, the existence of canals was still highly controversial as no close-up pictures of Mars had been taken (until [Mariner 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariner_4)'s flyby in 1965).

By the 19th century, the resolution of telescopes reached a level sufficient for surface features to be identified. On 5 September 1877, a perihelic opposition to Mars occurred. The Italian astronomer [Giovanni Schiaparelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Schiaparelli) used a 22-centimetre (8.7 in) telescope in [Milan](36511.md) to help produce the first detailed map of Mars. These maps notably contained features he called *canali*, which, with the possible exception of the natural canyon [Valles Marineris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valles_Marineris), were later shown to be an [optical illusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_illusion). These *canali* were supposedly long, straight lines on the surface of Mars, to which he gave names of famous rivers on Earth. His term, which means "channels" or "grooves", was popularly mistranslated in English as "canals".[^cite_note-246][^cite_note-sagan80-247]

Influenced by the observations, the orientalist [Percival Lowell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percival_Lowell) founded [an observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lowell_Observatory) which had 30- and 45-centimetre (12- and 18-in) telescopes. The observatory was used for the exploration of Mars during the last good opportunity in 1894, and the following less favorable oppositions. He published several books on Mars and life on the planet, which had a great influence on the public.[^cite_note-basalla06-248][^cite_note-NYT-20151001-249] The *canali* were independently observed by other astronomers, such as [Henri Joseph Perrotin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Joseph_Perrotin) and [Louis Thollon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Thollon) in Nice, using one of the largest telescopes of that time.[^cite_note-maria_lane05-250][^cite_note-ba3-251]

The seasonal changes (consisting of the diminishing of the polar caps and the dark areas formed during Martian summers) in combination with the canals led to speculation about life on Mars, and it was a long-held belief that Mars contained vast seas and vegetation. As bigger telescopes were used, fewer long, straight *canali* were observed. During observations in 1909 by [Antoniadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoniadi) with an 84-centimetre (33 in) telescope, irregular patterns were observed, but no *canali* were seen.[^cite_note-zahnle01-252]

### First exploration



The very first two images from, and of, another planet (Mars), by [Mariner 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariner_4),

[^cite_note-u445-253]

&#x20;the second spacecraft to reach Mars and the first to transmit data (15 July 1965). The data disproved Mars being hospitable and having canals.

The first spacecraft from Earth to visit Mars was [Mars 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_1) of the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_space_program), which flew by in 1963, but contact was lost en route. [NASA](18426568.md)'s *[Mariner 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariner_4)* followed and became the first spacecraft to successfully transmit from Mars; launched on 28 November 1964, it made its closest approach to the planet on 15 July 1965. *Mariner 4* detected the weak Martian radiation belt, measured at about 0.1% that of Earth, and captured the first images of another planet from deep space.[^cite_note-254]

Once [spacecraft](37910.md) visited the planet during the 1960s and 1970s, many previous concepts of Mars were radically broken. After the results of the *Viking* life-detection experiments, the hypothesis of a dead planet was generally accepted.[^cite_note-ward_brownlee00-255] The data from *Mariner 9* and *Viking* allowed better maps of Mars to be made.

### Renewed exploration

Until 1997 and after Viking 1 shut down in 1982, Mars was only visited by three unsuccessful probes, two flying past without contact ([Phobos 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phobos_1), 1988; [Mars Observer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_Observer), 1993), and one ([Phobos 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phobos_2) 1989) malfunctioning in orbit before reaching its destination [Phobos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phobos_\(moon\)).

In 1997 [Mars Pathfinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_Pathfinder) became the first successful rover mission beyond the Moon and started together with *[Mars Global Surveyor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_Global_Surveyor)* (operated until late 2006) an uninterrupted active robotic presence at Mars that has lasted until today. It produced complete, extremely detailed maps of the Martian topography, magnetic field and surface minerals.[^cite_note-Distant_worlds:_milestones_in_planetary_exploration-256]

Starting with these missions a range of new improved crewless [spacecraft](37910.md), including [orbiters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbiter), [landers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_lander), and [rovers](421366.md), have been sent to Mars, with successful missions by the NASA (United States), [Jaxa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaxa) ([Japan](15573.md)), [ESA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ESA), [United Kingdom](31717.md), [ISRO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISRO) ([India](14533.md)), [Roscosmos](406573.md) (Russia), the [United Arab Emirates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohammed_bin_Rashid_Space_Centre), and [CNSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CNSA) ([China](5405.md)) to study the planet's surface, climate, and geology,[^cite_note-257] uncovering the different elements of the history and dynamic of the hydrosphere of Mars and possible traces of ancient life.

### Current missions

As of 2023, Mars is host to ten functioning [spacecraft](37910.md).

Eight are in orbi&#x74;*: [2001 Mars Odyssey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001_Mars_Odyssey)*, *[Mars Express](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_Express)*, *[Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_Reconnaissance_Orbiter)*, *[MAVEN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MAVEN)*, *[ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ExoMars_Trace_Gas_Orbiter)*, the *[Hope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emirates_Mars_Mission)* orbiter, and the *[Tianwen-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tianwen-1)* orbiter.[^cite_note-258][^cite_note-259]

Another two are on the surface: the [Mars Science Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_Science_Laboratory) *[Curiosity](36645032.md)* rover and the *[Perseverance](63297153.md)* rover.[^cite_note-nyt-china-2021-260]

Collected maps are available online at websites including [Google Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Mars). NASA provides two online tools: Mars Trek, which provides visualizations of the planet using data from 50 years of exploration, and [Experience Curiosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experience_Curiosity), which simulates traveling on Mars in 3-D with *Curiosity*.[^cite_note-261][^cite_note-262]

## Habitability and habitation

Main article: [Life on Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_on_Mars)



Layering, nodules, reaction fronts and organic detections marked in an image from ancient fossilized mud at [Cheyava Falls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheyava_Falls), being the most promising finds potentially showing traces of remains of ancient Martian life.

During the late 19th century, it was widely accepted in the astronomical community that Mars had life-supporting qualities, including the presence of oxygen and water.[^cite_note-CampbellWW-275] However, in 1894 [W. W. Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Wallace_Campbell) at [Lick Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lick_Observatory) observed the planet and found that "if water vapor or oxygen occur in the atmosphere of Mars it is in quantities too small to have been detected by spectroscopes then available".[^cite_note-CampbellWW-275] That observation contradicted many of the measurements of the time and was not widely accepted.[^cite_note-CampbellWW-275] Campbell and [V. M. Slipher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vesto_Slipher) repeated the study in 1909 using better instruments, but with the same results. It was not until the findings were confirmed by [W. S. Adams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Sydney_Adams) in 1925 that the myth of the Earth-like habitability of Mars was finally broken.[^cite_note-CampbellWW-275] However, even in the 1960s, articles were published on Martian biology, putting aside explanations other than life for the seasonal changes on Mars.[^cite_note-science136_3510-276]

The current understanding of [planetary habitability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_habitability) – the ability of a world to develop environmental conditions favorable to the emergence of life – favors planets that have liquid water on their surface. Most often this requires the orbit of a planet to lie within the [habitable zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumstellar_habitable_zone), which for the Sun is estimated to extend from within the orbit of Earth to about that of Mars.[^cite_note-Kopparapu2013b-277] During perihelion, Mars dips inside this region, but Mars's thin (low-pressure) atmosphere prevents liquid water from existing over large regions for extended periods. The past flow of liquid water demonstrates the planet's potential for habitability. Recent evidence has suggested that any water on the Martian surface may have been too salty and acidic to support regular terrestrial life.[^cite_note-saltlife-278]

The environmental conditions on Mars are a challenge to sustaining organic life: the planet has little [heat transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_transfer) across its surface, it has poor insulation against [bombardment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_wind#Atmospheres) by the [solar wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_wind) due to the absence of a magnetosphere and has insufficient atmospheric pressure to retain water in a liquid form (water instead [sublimes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sublimation_\(phase_transition\)) to a gaseous state). Mars is nearly, or perhaps totally, geologically dead; the end of volcanic activity has apparently stopped the recycling of chemicals and minerals between the surface and interior of the planet.[^cite_note-hannsson97-279]

Evidence suggests that the planet was once significantly more habitable than it is today, but whether living [organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organism) ever existed there remains unknown. The [*Viking* probes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viking_probes) of the mid-1970s carried experiments designed to detect microorganisms in Martian soil at their respective landing sites and had positive results, including a temporary increase in CO<sub>2</sub> production on exposure to water and nutrients. This sign of life was later disputed by scientists, resulting in a continuing debate, with NASA scientist [Gilbert Levin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert_Levin) asserting that *Viking* may have found life.[^cite_note-nytobit-280] A 2014 analysis of Martian meteorite EETA79001 found [chlorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorate), [perchlorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perchlorate), and [nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrate) ions in sufficiently high concentrations to suggest that they are widespread on Mars. UV and X-ray radiation would turn chlorate and perchlorate ions into other, highly [reactive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox) [oxychlorines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxyanion), indicating that any organic molecules would have to be buried under the surface to survive.[^cite_note-281]

Small quantities of [methane](18582230.md) and [formaldehyde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formaldehyde) detected by Mars orbiters are both claimed to be possible evidence for life, as these [chemical compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_compound) would quickly break down in the Martian atmosphere.[^cite_note-icarus172-282][^cite_note-form-283] Alternatively, these compounds may instead be replenished by volcanic or other geological means, such as [serpentinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serpentinite).[^cite_note-olivine-140] [Impact glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impactite), formed by the impact of meteors, which on Earth can preserve signs of life, has also been found on the surface of the impact craters on Mars.[^cite_note-brown20140418-284][^cite_note-Schultz2014-285] Likewise, the glass in impact craters on Mars could have preserved signs of life, if life existed at the site.[^cite_note-nasapr20150608-286][^cite_note-brown20150608-287][^cite_note-sciam20150612-288]

The [Cheyava Falls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheyava_Falls) rock discovered on Mars in June 2024 has been designated by NASA as a "potential [biosignature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biosignature)" and was core sampled by the [Perseverance](63297153.md) rover for possible return to Earth and further examination. Although highly intriguing, no definitive final determination on a biological or abiotic origin of this rock can be made with the data currently available.[^cite_note-289]

### Human mission proposals



A NASA [ISRU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISRU) system concept for autonomous robotic excavation and processing of Mars soil to extract water for use in exploration missions.

Several plans for a [human mission to Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_mission_to_Mars) have been proposed, but none have come to fruition. The NASA Authorization Act of 2017 directed NASA to study the feasibility of a crewed Mars mission in the early 2030s; the resulting report concluded that this would be unfeasible.[^cite_note-290][^cite_note-291] In addition, in 2021, China was planning to send a crewed Mars mission in 2033.[^cite_note-292] [Privately held companies](752732.md) such as [SpaceX](832774.md) have also [proposed plans to send humans to Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Mars_Colonization_Program), with the eventual goal to [settle on the planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonization_of_Mars).[^cite_note-293] As of 2024, SpaceX has proceeded with the development of the [Starship](59118602.md) launch vehicle with the goal of Mars colonization. In plans shared with the company in April 2024, Elon Musk envisions the beginning of a Mars colony within the next twenty years. This would be enabled by the planned [mass manufacturing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_manufacturing) of Starship and initially sustained by resupply from Earth, and [in situ resource utilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_situ_resource_utilization) on Mars, until the Mars colony reaches full self sustainability.[^cite_note-294] Any future human mission to Mars will likely take place within [the optimal Mars launch window](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exploration_of_Mars#Launch_windows), which occurs every 26 months. The moon Phobos has been proposed as an anchor point for a [space elevator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_elevator).[^cite_note-295] Besides national space agencies and space companies, groups such as the [Mars Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_Society)[^cite_note-296] and [The Planetary Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Planetary_Society)[^cite_note-297] advocate for human missions to Mars.

## In culture

Main articles: [Mars in culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_in_culture) and [Mars in fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_in_fiction)

See also: [Planets in astrology § Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planets_in_astrology#Mars)



*[The War of the Worlds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_War_of_the_Worlds)* by [H. G. Wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._G._Wells), 1897, depicts an [invasion of Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alien_invasion) by fictional Martians.

Mars is named after the [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome) [god of war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_\(mythology\)) ([Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mythology) [Ares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ares)), but was also associated with the demi-god [Heracles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heracles) (Roman [Hercules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hercules)) by ancient Greek astronomers, as detailed by [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle).[^cite_note-DRGA_Planetae-298] This association between Mars and war dates back at least to [Babylonian astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_astronomy), in which the planet was named for the god [Nergal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nergal), deity of war and destruction.[^cite_note-299][^cite_note-300] It persisted into modern times, as exemplified by [Gustav Holst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Holst)'s orchestral suite *[The Planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Planets)*, whose famous first movement labels Mars "The Bringer of War".[^cite_note-301] [The planet's symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet_symbols), a circle with a spear pointing out to the upper right, is also used as a symbol for the male gender.[^cite_note-302] The symbol dates from at least the 11th century, though a possible predecessor has been found in the Greek [Oxyrhynchus Papyri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxyrhynchus_Papyri).[^cite_note-jones-1999-303]

The first movement of Gustav Holst's The Planets - Mars, The Bringer of War (TMO Orchestra, Incheon, 2023)

The idea that Mars was populated by intelligent [Martians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martian) became widespread in the late 19th century. [Schiaparelli's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Schiaparelli) "canali" observations combined with [Percival Lowell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percival_Lowell)'s books on the subject put forward the standard notion of a planet that was a drying, cooling, dying world with ancient civilizations constructing irrigation works.[^cite_note-304] Many other observations and proclamations by notable personalities added to what has been termed "Mars Fever".[^cite_note-fergus04-305] In the present day, high-resolution mapping of the surface of Mars has revealed no artifacts of habitation, but pseudoscientific speculation about intelligent life on Mars still continues. Reminiscent of the *canali* observations, these speculations are based on small scale features perceived in the spacecraft images, such as "pyramids" and the "[Face on Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cydonia_\(Mars\))".[^cite_note-306] In his book *[Cosmos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmos_\(Sagan_book\))*, planetary astronomer [Carl Sagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Sagan) wrote: "Mars has become a kind of mythic arena onto which we have projected our Earthly hopes and fears."[^cite_note-sagan80-247]

The depiction of Mars in fiction has been stimulated by its dramatic red color and by nineteenth-century scientific speculations that its surface conditions might support not just life but intelligent life.[^cite_note-lightman97-307] This gave way to many [science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction) stories involving these concepts, such as [H. G. Wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._G._Wells)'s *[The War of the Worlds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_War_of_the_Worlds)*, in which Martians seek to escape their dying planet by invading Earth; [Ray Bradbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Bradbury)'s *[The Martian Chronicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Martian_Chronicles)*, in which human explorers accidentally destroy a Martian civilization; as well as [Edgar Rice Burroughs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Rice_Burroughs)'s series *[Barsoom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barsoom)*, [C. S. Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._S._Lewis)'s novel *[Out of the Silent Planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Out_of_the_Silent_Planet)* (1938),[^cite_note-sanford09-308] and a number of [Robert A. Heinlein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_A._Heinlein) stories before the mid-sixties.[^cite_note-buker02-309] Since then, depictions of Martians have also extended to animation. A comic figure of an intelligent Martian, [Marvin the Martian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marvin_the_Martian), appeared in *[Haredevil Hare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haredevil_Hare)* (1948) as a character in the [Looney Tunes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Looney_Tunes) animated cartoons of [Warner Brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warner_Brothers), and has continued as part of popular culture to the present.[^cite_note-rabkin05-310] After the [*Mariner*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariner_program) and [*Viking*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viking_program) spacecraft had returned pictures of Mars as a lifeless and canal-less world, these ideas about Mars were abandoned; for many science-fiction authors, the new discoveries initially seemed like a constraint, but eventually the post-*Viking* knowledge of Mars became itself a source of inspiration for works like [Kim Stanley Robinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kim_Stanley_Robinson)'s [*Mars* trilogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_trilogy).[^cite_note-311]
