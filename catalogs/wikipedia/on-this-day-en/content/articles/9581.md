The **European Parliament** (**EP**) is one of the two [legislative bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislature) of the [European Union](9317.md) (EU) and one of its [seven institutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutions_of_the_European_Union). Together with the [Council of the European Union](5866.md) (known as the Council and informally as the Council of Ministers), it adopts European legislation, following a proposal by the [European Commission](9974.md). The Parliament is composed of 720 [members](258909.md) (MEPs), after the June 2024 European elections, from a previous 705 MEPs. It represents the second-largest democratic electorate in the world (after the [Parliament of India](410238.md)), with an electorate of around 375 million eligible [voters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting) in 2024.[^cite_note-13][^cite_note-14][^cite_note-18_new_MEPs_take_their_seats-15]

Since 1979, the Parliament has been directly elected every five years by the [citizens of the European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizenship_of_the_European_Union) through [universal suffrage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_suffrage). [Voter turnout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voter_turnout) in parliamentary elections decreased each time after [1979](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1979_European_Parliament_election) until [2019](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_European_Parliament_election), when voter turnout increased by eight percentage points, and rose above 50% for the first time since [1994](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1994_European_Parliament_election).[^cite_note-16] The [voting age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_age) is 18 in all [EU member states](10890716.md) except for [Malta](19137.md), [Belgium](3343.md), [Austria](26964606.md) and [Germany](11867.md), where it is 16, and [Greece](12108.md), where it is 17.[^cite_note-17]

The European Parliament has legislative power in that the adoption of EU legislation normally requires its approval, and that of the Council, in what amounts to a bicameral legislature. However, it does not formally possess the [right of initiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_of_initiative_\(legislative\)) (i.e. the right to formally initiate the legislative procedure) in the way that most [national parliaments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_parliaments_of_the_European_Union) of the member states do, as the right of initiative is a prerogative of the European Commission.[^cite_note-Parliament's_powers-18][^cite_note-Pot_initiative-19] Nonetheless, the Parliament and the Council each have the right to request the Commission to initiate the legislative procedure and put forward a proposal.[^cite_note-20]

The Parliament is, in protocol terms, the "first institution" of the European Union (mentioned first in [its treaties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaties_of_the_European_Union) and having ceremonial precedence over the other EU institutions),[^cite_note-Protocol-21] and shares equal legislative and [budgetary powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budget_of_the_European_Union) with the Council (except on a few issues where [special legislative procedures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislature_of_the_European_Union#Special_legislative_procedures) apply). It likewise has equal control over the EU budget. Ultimately, the European Commission, which serves as the executive branch of the EU, is accountable to Parliament. In particular, Parliament can decide whether or not to approve the [European Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Council)'s nominee for [President of the Commission](24468.md), and is further tasked with approving (or rejecting) the appointment of the Commission as a whole. It can subsequently force the current Commission to resign by adopting a [motion of censure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_of_censure).[^cite_note-Parliament's_powers-18]

The [president of the European Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_European_Parliament) is the body's [speaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speaker_\(politics\)) and presides over the multi-party chamber. The five largest political groups are the [European People's Party Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_People's_Party_Group) (EPP), the [Progressive Alliance of Socialists and Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_Alliance_of_Socialists_and_Democrats) (S\&D), [Patriots for Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriots_for_Europe) (PfE), the [European Conservatives and Reformists Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Conservatives_and_Reformists_Group) (ECR), and [Renew Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renew_Europe) (Renew). The last EU-wide election was [held in 2024](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_European_Parliament_election).

The Parliament's headquarters are officially in [Strasbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strasbourg), France,[^cite_note-treaty-22] and has [its administrative offices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretariat_of_the_European_Parliament) in [Luxembourg City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luxembourg_City). [Plenary sessions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plenary_session) are normally held in Strasbourg for four days a month, but sometimes there are additional sessions in Brussels,[^cite_note-about-eu-23] while the Parliament's committee meetings are held primarily in Brussels, Belgium.[^cite_note-about-eu-23][^cite_note-Consolidated_Treaty-24] In practice, the Parliament works three weeks per month in Brussels and one week (four days) in Strasbourg.

## History

Further information: [History of the European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_European_Union)



The European Parliament's flag until 1983

The Parliament, like the other EU institutions, was not designed in its current form when it first met on 10 September 1952. One of the oldest common institutions, it began as the **Common Assembly** of the [European Coal and Steel Community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Coal_and_Steel_Community) (ECSC). It was a consultative assembly of 78 appointed parliamentarians [drawn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_mandate) from the national parliaments of member states, having no legislative powers.[^cite_note-ENA_History-25][^cite_note-26] The change since its foundation was highlighted by Professor [David Farrell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Farrell_\(political_scientist\)) of the [University of Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Manchester): "For much of its life, the European Parliament could have been justly labelled a 'multi-lingual talking shop' \[but] is now one of the most powerful legislatures in the world"."[^cite_note-Farrell_Powerful-27] This was done notably through successive treaty changes and other agreements.[^cite_note-28]

Its development since its foundation shows how the [European Union](9317.md)'s structures have evolved without a clear 'master plan'. [Tom Reid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._R._Reid) of *[The Washington Post](102226.md)* has said of the union that "nobody would have deliberately designed a government as complex and as redundant as the EU".[^cite_note-29] Even the Parliament's [three working locations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Location_of_European_Union_institutions#European_Parliament) are a result of various disagreements and partial agreements among the governments of the Member States. Although most [MEPs](258909.md) would prefer to be based just in Brussels, where it conducts the bulk of its work, at the 1992 Edinburgh [summit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Council), France engineered an agreement whereby the European Parliament's official seat is in Strasbourg, but most of its work is done in Brussels.[^cite_note-ENA_History-25][^cite_note-30]

### Consultative assembly



Session of the Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe in the former [House of Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Europe) in Strasbourg, France in January 1967. [Willy Brandt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willy_Brandt), [West German minister for Foreign Affairs](1329748.md), is speaking.

The body was not mentioned in the original [Schuman Declaration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schuman_Declaration). It was assumed or hoped that difficulties with the British would be resolved to allow the [Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_Assembly_of_the_Council_of_Europe) to perform legislative tasks. A separate Assembly was introduced during negotiations on the Treaty as an institution to counterbalance and monitor the [executive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_\(government\)) while providing democratic legitimacy.[^cite_note-ENA_History-25] The wording of the [ECSC Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Paris_\(1951\)) demonstrated leaders' desire for more than a normal consultative assembly by allowing for direct election and using the term "representatives of the people". Its early importance was highlighted when the Assembly was given the task of drawing up the draft treaty to establish a [European Political Community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Political_Community_\(1952\)). By this document, the Ad Hoc Assembly was established on 13 September 1952[^cite_note-AEIpolitical_union_draft_treaty_1-31] with extra members, but after the failure of the negotiated and proposed [European Defence Community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Defence_Community) (French parliament veto), the project was dropped.[^cite_note-ENA_composition-32]

Instead, the [European Economic Community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Economic_Community) and [Euratom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euratom) were established in 1958 by the [Treaties of Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Rome). The Common Assembly was shared by all three communities (which had separate executives) and it renamed itself the **European Parliamentary Assembly**.[^cite_note-ENA_History-25] The first meeting was held on 19 March 1958 having been set up in Luxembourg City, it elected Schuman as its president and on 13 May it rearranged itself to sit according to political ideology rather than nationality.[^cite_note-1958_history-33] This is seen as the birth of the modern European Parliament, with Parliament's 50 years celebrations being held in March 2008 rather than 2002.[^cite_note-50_years-34]

The three communities [merged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merger_Treaty) their remaining organs as the [European Communities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Communities) in 1967, and the body's name was changed to the current "European Parliament" in 1962.[^cite_note-ENA_History-25] In 1970 the Parliament was granted power over areas of the [Communities' budget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budget_of_the_European_Union), which were expanded to the whole budget in 1975.[^cite_note-ENA_Budget-35] Under the Rome Treaties, the Parliament should have become elected. However, the Council was required to agree a uniform voting system beforehand, which it failed to do. The Parliament threatened to take the Council to the [European Court of Justice](87187.md); this led to a compromise whereby the Council would agree to elections, but with each Member State using its own electoral system, leaving the issue of a uniform voting systems to be decided at a later date.[^cite_note-Hoskyns-36]

For its sessions the assembly, and later the parliament, until 1999 convened in the same premises as the [Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_Assembly_of_the_Council_of_Europe): the House of Europe[^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38] until 1977, and the [Palace of Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_Europe) until 1999.

### Elected Parliament



A plenary session in the [Palace of Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_Europe) in April 1985, in Strasbourg, France. It was the EP's hemicycle until 1999 when a new building was constructed in Strasbourg, France.

In 1979, its members were [directly elected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elections_in_the_European_Union) for the [first time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1979_European_Parliament_election). This sets it apart from similar institutions such as those of the [Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_Assembly_of_the_Council_of_Europe) or [Pan-African Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-African_Parliament) which are appointed.[^cite_note-ENA_History-25][^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40] After that first election, the parliament held its first session on 17 July 1979, electing [Simone Veil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simone_Veil) MEP as its president.[^cite_note-41] Veil was also the first female president of the Parliament since it was formed as the Common Assembly.

As an elected body, the Parliament began to draft proposals addressing the functioning of the EU. For example, in 1984, inspired by its previous work on the Political Community, it drafted the "draft Treaty establishing the European Union" (also known as the 'Spinelli Plan' after its rapporteur [Altiero Spinelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altiero_Spinelli) MEP). Although it was not adopted as such by the Member States, many of its ideas were later taken up in subsequent amending treaties.[^cite_note-ENA_TEU-42] Furthermore, the Parliament began holding votes on proposed [Commission Presidents](24468.md) from the 1980s, before it was given any formal right to veto their appointment.[^cite_note-Commission_Appointment-43]

Since it became an elected body, the membership of the European Parliament has expanded when new nations have joined (the membership was also adjusted upwards in 1994 after [German reunification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_reunification)). Following this, the [Treaty of Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Nice) imposed a cap on the number of members to be elected: 732, later raised to 751 by the [Treaty of Lisbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Lisbon).[^cite_note-ENA_History-25]

Like the other institutions, the Parliament's [seat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seat_\(legal_entity\)) was not yet fixed. The provisional arrangements placed Parliament in [Strasbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strasbourg), while the Commission and Council had their seats in [Brussels](3708.md). In 1985 the Parliament, wishing to be closer to these institutions, built a second chamber in Brussels and moved some of its work there despite protests from some states. A final agreement was eventually reached by the [European Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Council) in 1992. It stated the Parliament would retain its formal seat in Strasbourg, where twelve sessions a year would be held, but with all other parliamentary activity in Brussels. This two-seat arrangement was contested by the Parliament, but was later enshrined in the [Treaty of Amsterdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Amsterdam). To this day the [institution's locations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Location_of_European_Union_institutions) are a source of contention.[^cite_note-ENA_seats-44]

The Parliament gained more powers from successive revisions of the EU treaties, notably through the extension of the [ordinary legislative procedure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_legislative_procedure) (originally called the codecision procedure),[^cite_note-ENA_legis-45] and the right to approve international agreements through the consent procedure.

In 1999, the Parliament forced the resignation of the [Santer Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santer_Commission).[^cite_note-46] The Parliament had refused to approve the [Community budget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budget_of_the_European_Union) over allegations of fraud and mis-management in the commission. The two main parties took on a government-opposition dynamic for the first time during the crisis which ended in the Commission resigning en masse, the first of any forced resignation, in the face of an impending censure from the Parliament.[^cite_note-Ringe-47]

### Commission accountability to the Parliament

The Parliament had always had the right to dismiss the European Commission in a vote of censure, but it initially had no role in its appointment. In the 1992 [Treaty of Maastricht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Maastricht), the Member States gave the Parliament the right to approve or reject an incoming Commission. In the 1997 [Treaty of Amsterdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Amsterdam), they gave it the right to approve or reject an incoming [Commission President](24468.md), subsequently (in the [Treaty of Lisbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Lisbon)) describing Parliament's vote as the "election" of the President, and requiring the European Council to make its proposal to Parliament in light of the results of the European elections.[^cite_note-Constitution_info-48]

In 2004, following the largest trans-national election in history, the European Council proposed as Commission President a candidate, [José Manuel Barroso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Manuel_Barroso), from the largest political party (the EPP). The Parliament approved him by 431 votes to 251. However, when it came to the vote on the Commission as a whole, MEPs raised doubts about some of the nominees following their performance in the public hearings of them conducted by Parliament's committees. Most notably, the [Civil Liberties committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_on_Civil_Liberties,_Justice_and_Home_Affairs) rejected [Rocco Buttiglione](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocco_Buttiglione) for the post of [Commissioner for Justice, Freedom and Security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Commissioner_for_Justice,_Freedom_and_Security) over his views on homosexuality. That was the first time the Parliament had ever opposed an incoming Commissioner and, despite Barroso's initial insistence upon Buttiglione, the Parliament forced Buttiglione to be withdrawn. A number of other Commissioners also had to be withdrawn or reassigned before Parliament voted to allow the [Barroso Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barroso_Commission) to take office.[^cite_note-IHT_Butt-49][^cite_note-50]



Parliament's overhaul of the [Bolkestein directive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolkestein_directive) signalled a major growth in status for Parliament.

The Parliament also became more assertive in amending legislative proposals put forward by the European Commission. A notable example was on the [Bolkestein directive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolkestein_directive) in 2006, when the Parliament voted by a large majority for over 400 amendments that changed the fundamental principle of the law. The *[Financial Times](136566.md)* described it in the following terms:[^cite_note-FT_CIO-51]

> That is where the European parliament has suddenly come into its own. It marks another shift in power between the three central EU institutions. Last week's vote suggests that the directly elected MEPs, in spite of their multitude of ideological, national and historical allegiances, have started to coalesce as a serious and effective EU institution, just as enlargement has greatly complicated negotiations inside both the Council and Commission.

— "How the European parliament got serious", *Financial Times* (23 February 2006)

In 2007, for the first time, Justice Commissioner [Franco Frattini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franco_Frattini) included Parliament in talks on the second [Schengen Information System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schengen_Information_System) even though, in this field at the time, MEPs only needed to be consulted on parts of the package. After that experiment, Frattini indicated he would like to include Parliament in all justice and criminal matters, informally pre-empting the new powers they were due to gain in 2009 as part of the [Treaty of Lisbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Lisbon).[^cite_note-Frattini_2009-52]

### Recent history

Further information: [Barroso Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barroso_Commission)

Between 2007 and 2009, a [special working group on parliamentary reform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_working_group_on_parliamentary_reform) implemented a series of changes to modernise the institution such as more speaking time for rapporteurs, increased committee co-operation and other efficiency reforms.[^cite_note-REFO12-53][^cite_note-REFO3-54]

The Lisbon Treaty came into force on 1 December 2009, granting Parliament powers over the entire [EU budget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budget_of_the_European_Union), making Parliament's [legislative powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_legislative_procedure) equal to the Council's in nearly all areas and describing Parliament's vote on an incoming [Commission President](24468.md) as an "election", with the European Council having to make its proposal to Parliament in light of the results of the European elections.[^cite_note-Constitution_info-48]

Barroso gained the support of the European Council for a second term and secured majority support from the Parliament in September 2009. Parliament voted 382 votes in favour and 219 votes against (117 abstentions) with support of the [European People's Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_People's_Party), [European Conservatives and Reformists Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Conservatives_and_Reformists_Party) and the [Alliance of Liberals and Democrats for Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alliance_of_Liberals_and_Democrats_for_Europe).[^cite_note-B2V-55] The liberals gave support after Barroso gave them a number of concessions; the liberals previously joined the socialists' call for a delayed vote (the EPP had wanted to approve Barroso in July of that year).[^cite_note-B2C-56]

Once Barroso put forward the candidates for his next Commission, another case of MEPs opposing a particular nominee arose. Bulgarian nominee [Rumiana Jeleva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rumiana_Jeleva) was forced to step down by Parliament due to concerns over her experience and financial interests. She only had the support of the EPP which began to retaliate on left wing candidates before Jeleva gave in and was replaced (setting back the final vote further).[^cite_note-RJ-57]

Before the final vote on the Commission, Parliament demanded a number of concessions as part of a future working agreement under the new Lisbon Treaty. The deal includes that Parliament's president will attend high level Commission meetings. Parliament will have an observer seat in the EU's Commission-led international negotiations and have a right to information on agreements. Parliament did not secure an explicit vote over the appointment of [delegation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_diplomatic_missions_of_the_European_Union) heads and [special representatives](2899127.md) for foreign policy, but it was agreed that they will appear before parliament after they have been appointed by the [High Representative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Representative). Parliament wanted a pledge from the Commission that it would automatically put forward legislation when parliament requests. Barroso considered this an infringement on the Commission's powers but did agree to respond within three months. Most requests are already responded to positively.[^cite_note-B2A-58]

During the setting up of the [European External Action Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_External_Action_Service) (EEAS), Parliament used its control over the EU budget to influence the shape of the EEAS. MEPs had aimed at getting greater oversight over the EEAS by linking it to the Commission and having political deputies to the [High Representative](750942.md). MEPs did not manage to get everything they demanded. However, they got broader financial control over the new body.[^cite_note-EEAS-59][^cite_note-60]

In December 2017, [Politico](9172097.md) denounced the insufficient racial diversity among Members of the European Parliament.[^cite_note-61] The subsequent news coverage contributed to create the Brussels So White movement to campaign to rectify this situation.[^cite_note-62]

On gender balance, some 37 percent of MEPs were women in the 2014–19 Parliament and 40 percent in the 2019–24 Parliament, a greater proposition than in most national Parliaments in Member States [^cite_note-63] In January 2019, MEPs supported proposals to boost opportunities for women and tackle sexual harassment in the European Parliament.[^cite_note-64]

In 2022, four people were arrested because of corruption. This came to be known as the [Qatar corruption scandal at the European Parliament](72479300.md).[^cite_note-65]

In October 2023, the Parliament adopted a resolution to condemn "Hamas' despicable terrorist attacks against Israel".[^cite_note-66]



Moment of silence for victims of Hamas' terrorist attack on Israel, October 2023

Since then, Europe's initial and almost unconditional support for Israel started to dwindle due to the disproportionate [attacks on the civilian population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israeli_war_crimes_in_the_Gaza_war) of [Gaza](12047.md) and the [Occupied West Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupied_West_Bank), with tens of thousands of deaths reported. In July 2025, the EU was weighing partly suspending parts of the [EU-Israel Association Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel%E2%80%93European_Union_relations) over reports the country is breaching its human rights obligations in Gaza.[^cite_note-67] A majority for a suspension was not found, but the EU’s 27 foreign ministers agreed to "keep a close watch" on Israel’s compliance with a recent agreement to improve humanitarian aid access into Gaza. They also delayed agreeing on a list of 10 options to respond to Israel's action in Gaza during a meeting of the Foreign Affairs Council.[^cite_note-68]

The Parliament and Council have been compared to the two [chambers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chambers_of_parliament) of a [bicameral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicameral) legislature.[^cite_note-69] However, there are some differences from [national legislatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_parliaments_of_the_European_Union); for example, neither the Parliament nor the Council have the power of [legislative initiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislative_initiative) (except for the fact that the Council has the power in some [intergovernmental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intergovernmentalism#A_theory_of_regional_integration) matters). In [Community matters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Community), this is a power uniquely reserved for the [European Commission](9974.md) (the executive). Therefore, while Parliament can amend and reject legislation, to make a proposal for legislation, it needs the Commission to draft a [bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_\(proposed_law\)) before anything can become law.[^cite_note-70] The value of a right of initiative has anyway been questioned by noting that in the national legislatures of the member states 85% of initiatives introduced without executive support fail to become law.[^cite_note-Kreppel-71] Yet it has been argued by former Parliament president [Hans-Gert Pöttering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans-Gert_P%C3%B6ttering) that as the Parliament does have the right to ask the Commission to draft such legislation, and as the Commission is following Parliament's proposals more and more Parliament does have a *de facto* right of legislative initiative.[^cite_note-Pot_initiative-19]

The Parliament also has a great deal of indirect influence, through [non-binding resolutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union_law#Legislation) and [committee hearings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committees_of_the_European_Parliament), as a "pan-European [soapbox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soapbox)" with the ear of [thousands of Brussels-based journalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brussels_and_the_European_Union#Lobbyists_and_journalists). There is also an indirect effect on [foreign policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Foreign_and_Security_Policy); the Parliament must approve all development grants, including those overseas. For example, the support for [post-war Iraq](5043324.md) reconstruction, or incentives for the cessation of [Iranian nuclear development](721807.md), must be supported by the Parliament. Parliamentary support was also required for the [transatlantic](698.md) passenger data-sharing deal with the United States.[^cite_note-Rockwell-72] Finally, Parliament holds a non-binding vote on new EU treaties and treaties amending the EU treaties, but cannot veto them. However, when Parliament threatened to vote down the Nice Treaty, the [Belgian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgian_Federal_Parliament) and [Italian Parliaments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_Italy) said that if it did so, they would veto the treaty on the European Parliament's behalf.[^cite_note-73]

### Legislative procedure

With each new treaty, the powers of the Parliament, in terms of its role in the [Union's legislative procedures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_legislative_procedure), have expanded. The procedure which has slowly become dominant is the "[ordinary legislative procedure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_legislative_procedure)" (previously named "[codecision procedure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union_legislative_procedure)"), which provides an equal footing between Parliament and Council. In particular, under the procedure, the Commission presents a proposal to Parliament and the Council which can only become law if both agree on a text, which they do (or not) through successive readings up to a maximum of three. In its first reading, Parliament may send amendments to the Council which can either adopt the text with those amendments or send back a "common position". That position may either be approved by Parliament, or it may reject the text by an [absolute majority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majority), causing it to fail, or it may adopt further amendments, also by an absolute majority. If the Council does not approve these, then a "[Conciliation Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trilogue_meeting)" is formed. The committee is composed of the Council members plus an equal number of MEPs who seek to agree a compromise. Once a position is agreed, it has to be approved by Parliament, by a simple majority.[^cite_note-Parliament's_powers-18][^cite_note-decision_making-74] This is also aided by Parliament's mandate as the only directly democratic institution, which has given it leeway to have greater control over legislation than other institutions, for example over its changes to the [Bolkestein directive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolkestein_directive) in 2006.[^cite_note-FT_CIO-51]

In practice, most legislation is adopted at the first reading stage after the Parliament and the Council, having set out their initial positions, then negotiate a compromise text.[^cite_note-75] These negotiations take place in so-called ["trilogue" meetings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trilogue_meeting), in which the Commission is also present.

In a few areas, *special legislative procedures* apply. These include justice and home affairs, budget and taxation, and certain aspects of other policy areas, such as the fiscal aspects of environmental policy. In these areas, the Council or Parliament decide law alone after consulting the other (or with its consent).[^cite_note-lisbon_explain-76]

There are different types of [European Union law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union_law#Legislation).[^cite_note-Parliament's_powers-18] The strongest act is a [regulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulation_\(European_Union\)), an [act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Act_of_Parliament) or [law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statutory_law) which is directly applicable in its entirety. Then there are [directives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directive_\(European_Union\)) which bind member states to certain goals which they must achieve. They do this through their own laws and hence have room to manoeuvre in deciding upon them. A [decision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union_decision) is an instrument which is applicable to a particular person or group. Institutions may also issue [recommendations and opinions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union_recommendation) which are merely non-binding, declarations.[^cite_note-77]

### Budget

The Parliament and the Council are also the Union's budgetary authority since the [Budgetary Treaties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budgetary_treaties_of_the_European_Communities) of the 1970s and the [Lisbon Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisbon_Treaty). The [EU budget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budget_of_the_European_Union) is subject to a form of the ordinary legislative procedure with a single reading giving Parliament power over the entire budget (before 2009, its influence was limited to certain areas) on an equal footing to the Council. If there is a disagreement between them, it is taken to a conciliation committee as it is for legislative proposals. If the joint conciliation text is not approved by the Council, the Parliament may adopt the budget definitively, but only by a three-fifths majority.[^cite_note-lisbon_explain-76]

The Parliament is also responsible for discharging the implementation of previous budgets based on the annual report of the [European Court of Auditors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Court_of_Auditors). It has refused to grant discharge only twice, in 1984 and in 1998. On the latter occasion it led to the resignation of the [Santer Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santer_Commission); highlighting how the discharge power gives Parliament a great deal of power over the Commission.[^cite_note-Hoskyns-36][^cite_note-Ringe-47][^cite_note-budget_background-78] Parliament also makes extensive use of its budgetary, and other powers, elsewhere; for example in the setting up of the [European External Action Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_External_Action_Service), Parliament had a de facto veto over its design as it has to approve the budgetary and staff changes.[^cite_note-EEAS-59]

### Control of the executive



European Parliament in 2025

The [President of the European Commission](24468.md) is proposed by the European Council on the basis of the European elections to Parliament.[^cite_note-Constitution_info-48] That proposal has to be approved by the Parliament (by a majority of members of the Parliament) who thereby "elect" the President according to the treaties. Following the approval of the Commission President, the members of the Commission are proposed by the President in accord with the member states. Each Commissioner comes before a relevant parliamentary committee hearing covering the proposed portfolio. They are then, as a body, approved or rejected by the Parliament.[^cite_note-autogenerated2-79][^cite_note-Commission_Oversight-80]

In practice, the Parliament has never voted against a President or his Commission, but the threat to do so has produced concessions to Parliament on the Commission's composition or on policy commitments. As described above, when the Barroso Commission was put forward, the Parliament forced the proposal to be withdrawn and changed to be more acceptable to Parliament.[^cite_note-IHT_Butt-49] That pressure was seen as an important sign by some of the evolving nature of the Parliament and its ability to make the Commission accountable, rather than being a rubber stamp for candidates. Furthermore, in voting on the Commission, MEPs also vote along party lines, rather than national lines, despite frequent pressure from national governments on their MEPs. This cohesion and willingness to use the Parliament's power ensured greater attention from national leaders, other institutions and the public –  reversing the previous decline in turnout for the Parliament's elections.[^cite_note-81]

The Parliament also has the power to censure the Commission by a two-thirds majority which will force the resignation of the entire Commission from office. As with approval, this power has never been explicitly used, but when faced with such a vote, the [Santer Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santer_Commission) then [resigned of their own accord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santer_Commission#Resignation).

There are other control instruments, such as: the requirement of Commission to submit reports to the Parliament and answer written and oral questions from MEPs; the requirement of the President-in-office of the Council to present its programme at the start of their [presidency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidency_of_the_Council_of_the_European_Union); the obligation on the President of the [European Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Council) to report to Parliament after each of its meetings; the right of MEPs to make requests for legislation and policy to the Commission; and the right to question members of those institutions (e.g. "Commission [Question Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Question_Time)" every Tuesday).[^cite_note-Commission_Appointment-43][^cite_note-Commission_Oversight-80] Regarding written and oral questions, MEPs voted in July 2008 to limit questions to those within the EU's mandate and ban offensive or personal questions.[^cite_note-EUO_rise-82]

### Supervisory and scrutiny powers

The Parliament also has other powers of general supervision, mainly granted by the [Maastricht Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maastricht_Treaty).[^cite_note-83] The Parliament has the power to set up a Committee of Inquiry, which it has done, for example over mad cow disease and CIA detention flights –  the former led to the creation of the [European veterinary agency](1427054.md). The Parliament can call other institutions to answer questions and if necessary to take them to [court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_Justice_of_the_European_Communities) if they break EU law or treaties.[^cite_note-supervisory-84] Furthermore, it has powers over the appointment of the members of the Court of Auditors[^cite_note-85] and the president and executive board of the [European Central Bank](9474.md). The [ECB president](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Presidents_of_the_European_Central_Bank) is also obliged to present an annual report to the parliament.[^cite_note-supervisory-84]

The [European Ombudsman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Ombudsman) is elected by the Parliament to deal with public complaints about maladministration (administrative irregularities, unfairness, discrimination, abuse of power, failure to reply, refusal of information or unnecessary delay) by any EU institution or body.[^cite_note-supervisory-84]

Petitions can be brought to the Parliament by any EU citizen on a matter within the EU's sphere of activities. The Parliament's [Committee on Petitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_on_Petitions) hears cases, some 1500 each year, sometimes presented by the citizen themselves at the Parliament. While the Parliament attempts to resolve the issue as a mediator they do resort to legal proceedings if it is necessary to resolve the citizens dispute.[^cite_note-petitions-86]

The [parliamentarians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Member_of_Parliament) are known in English as [Members of the European Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Members_of_the_European_Parliament) (MEPs). They are elected every five years by [universal suffrage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_suffrage) and sit according to political allegiance. About 40 percent are women. Before the [first direct elections, in 1979](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1979_European_Parliament_election), they were appointed by their national parliaments.[^cite_note-ENA_composition-32][^cite_note-EP_Members-87]

The Parliament has been criticized for underrepresentation of minority groups. In 2017, an estimated 17 MEPs were non-white,[^cite_note-88] and of these, three were black, a disproportionately low number.[^cite_note-89] According to activist organization [European Network Against Racism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Network_Against_Racism), while an estimated 10% of Europe is composed of racial and ethnic minorities, only 5% of MEPs were members of such groups following the [2019 European Parliament election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_European_Parliament_election).[^cite_note-Psaledakis2020-90]

Under the [Treaty of Lisbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Lisbon), [seats are allocated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apportionment_in_the_European_Parliament) to each state according to population and the maximum number of members is set at 751.[^cite_note-EUO_Lisbon-91] Since 1 February 2020 and the United Kingdom's [leaving the EU](41688778.md), 705 MEPs sit in the European Parliament. It rose to 720 after the 2024 elections.

Representation is currently limited to a maximum of 96 seats and a minimum of 6 seats per state and the seats are distributed according to "[degressive proportionality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degressive_proportionality)", i.e., the larger the state, the more citizens are represented per MEP. As a result, Maltese and Luxembourgish voters have roughly 10x more influence per voter than citizens of the six largest countries.

As of 2014, Germany (80.9 million inhabitants) has 96 seats (previously 99 seats), i.e. one seat for 843,000 inhabitants. Malta (0.4 million inhabitants) has 6 seats, i.e. one seat for 70,000 inhabitants.

The new system implemented under the Lisbon Treaty, including revising the seating well before elections, was intended to avoid political [horse trading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horse_trading_\(political\)) when the allocations have to be revised to reflect demographic changes.[^cite_note-92]

Pursuant to this apportionment, the [constituencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Parliament_constituency) are formed. In four EU member states (Belgium, Ireland, Italy and Poland), the national territory is divided into a number of constituencies. In the remaining member states, the whole country forms a single constituency. All member states hold elections to the European Parliament using various forms of [proportional representation](23298.md).

### Transitional arrangements

Due to the delay in ratifying the Lisbon Treaty, the [seventh parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/7th_European_Parliament) was elected under the lower Nice Treaty cap. A [small scale treaty amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaties_of_the_European_Union#Transitional_provisions_protocol) was ratified on 29 November 2011.[^cite_note-93] This amendment brought in transitional provisions to allow the 18 additional MEPs created under the Lisbon Treaty to be elected or appointed before the 2014 election.[^cite_note-94] Under the Lisbon Treaty reforms, Germany was the only state to lose members from 99 to 96. However, these seats were not removed until the 2014 election.[^cite_note-extra_meps-95]

### Salaries and expenses

Before 2009, members received the same salary as members of their national parliament. However, from 2009 a new members statute came into force, after years of attempts, which gave all members an equal monthly pay, of €8,484.05 each in 2016, subject to a European Union tax and which can also be taxed nationally. MEPs are entitled to a pension, paid by Parliament, from the age of 63. Members are also entitled to allowances for office costs and subsistence, and travelling expenses, based on actual cost.[^cite_note-96] Besides their pay, members are granted a number of privileges and [immunities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diplomatic_immunity). To ensure their free movement to and from the Parliament, they are accorded by their own states the facilities accorded to senior officials travelling abroad and, by other state governments, the status of visiting [foreign representatives](82433.md). When in their own state, they have all the immunities accorded to national parliamentarians, and, in other states, they have immunity from [detention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detention_\(imprisonment\)) and [legal proceedings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_procedure). They may then only be prosecuted after the European Parliament waives their immunity. However, immunity cannot be claimed when a member is found committing a criminal offence and the Parliament also has the right to strip a member of their immunity.[^cite_note-LEX_Prot-97]

### Political groups

Main article: [Political groups of the European Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_groups_of_the_European_Parliament)

MEPs in Parliament are organised into eight different [parliamentary groups](1898891.md); members who do not join a group are known as *non-attached members* or *[non-inscrits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-inscrits)*.

The two largest groups are the [European People's Party Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_People's_Party_Group) (EPP) and the [Socialists & Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_Alliance_of_Socialists_and_Democrats) (S\&D). These two groups have dominated the Parliament for much of its existence, jointly holding between 50 and 70 percent of the seats until 2019. No single group has ever held an absolute majority in Parliament.[^cite_note-autogenerated1-98]

As a result of being broad alliances of national parties, political groups in the European Parliament are decentralised and have more in common with parties in federal states like Germany or the United States than with unitary states, like the majority of the EU member states.[^cite_note-Kreppel-71] Nevertheless, the political groups were actually more cohesive than their US counterparts between 2004 and 2009.[^cite_note-99][^cite_note-100]

Half of the groups are based on a single [European political party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_political_party), such as the [European People's Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_People's_Party), with the addition of MEPs from national parties not members of a European party and independent MEPs. The others, such as the [Greens–European Free Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greens%E2%80%93European_Free_Alliance) group or the [European Conservatives and Reformists Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Conservatives_and_Reformists_Group), center on two European parties, as well as other national parties and independents.[^cite_note-101]

Groups need 23 MEPs from seven different member states to register. Following registration, groups receive public funding from the budget of the European Parliament.[^cite_note-102]

### Coalitions

Given that the Parliament does not form the government in the traditional sense of many parliamentary systems, majorities are built issue by issue. Generally, this is along broadly [consensual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consensus_democracy) lines with dynamic coalitions[^cite_note-103] rather than [majority rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majority_rule) of competing parties and coalitions.

These coalitions usually involve the [European People's Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_People's_Party) and the Socialist and Democrat Group of the [Party of European Socialists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Party_of_European_Socialists) who tend to co-operate to find compromises between them and then bring in others. Their relationship has been described as a [grand coalition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_coalition).[^cite_note-104] However, this does not always produce agreement, and each may instead try to build other alliances, the EPP normally with other centre-right or right wing Groups and the PES with centre-left or left wing groups. Sometimes, the Liberal (Renew Europe) Group is then in the pivotal position.

There are also occasions where very sharp party political divisions have emerged, for example over the [resignation of the Santer Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santer_Commission#Budget_controversy).[^cite_note-Ringe-47] When the initial allegations against the Commission emerged, they were directed primarily against [Édith Cresson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89dith_Cresson) and [Manuel Marín](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuel_Mar%C3%ADn), both socialist members. When the parliament was considering refusing to discharge the [Community budget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budget_of_the_European_Union), President [Jacques Santer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Santer) stated that a no vote would be tantamount to a [vote of no confidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vote_of_no_confidence). The Socialist Group initially supported the Commission and saw the issue as an attempt by the EPP to discredit their party ahead of the 1999 elections. Socialist leader, [Pauline Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauline_Green) MEP, attempted a vote of confidence and the EPP put forward counter motions. During this period the two parties took on similar roles to a government-[opposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opposition_\(parliamentary\)) dynamic, with the Socialists supporting the executive and EPP renouncing its previous coalition support and voting it down.[^cite_note-Ringe-47] Politicisation such as this has been increasing, in 2007 Simon Hix of the [London School of Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_School_of_Economics) noted that:[^cite_note-Farrell_Powerful-27]

> Our work also shows that politics in the European Parliament is becoming increasingly based around party and ideology. Voting is increasingly split along left-right lines, and the cohesion of the party groups has risen dramatically, particularly in the fourth and fifth parliaments. So there are likely to be policy implications here too.

During the fifth term, 1999 to 2004, there was a break in the grand coalition resulting in a centre-right coalition between the Liberal and People's parties.[^cite_note-Watson_interview-105] This was reflected in the Presidency of the Parliament with the terms being shared between the EPP and the Liberals (then called ELDR), rather than the EPP and Socialists.[^cite_note-106] In the following term the liberal group grew to hold 88 seats, the largest number of seats held by any third party in Parliament.[^cite_note-ALDE_formed-107] The EPP-S\&D coalition lost their majority after the [2019 European Parliament election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_European_Parliament_election),[^cite_note-108] requiring support by at least one other political group for a majority, most frequently the liberal Renew Group or the Greens.

## Elections

Main article: [Elections to the European Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elections_to_the_European_Parliament)

Elections have taken place, directly in every member state, every five years since 1979. As of 2019 there have been ten elections. When a nation joins mid-term, a by-election is held in that country to elect its representatives. This has happened six times, most recently when Croatia joined in 2013. Elections take place on over a four-day period from a Thursday to a Sunday (each Member State votes on the day of its choice).

Member States choose their electoral system within common guidelines, notably that the suffrage must be universal and that seats are allocated by [proportional representation](23298.md). This includes determining constituencies: while most Member States have a national constituencies, some divide their allocation between regions. Seats are [allocated to member states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apportionment_in_the_European_Parliament) according to their population, since 2014 with no state having more than 96, but no fewer than 6, to maintain proportionality.[^cite_note-109]

The most recent Union-wide elections to the European Parliament were the [European elections of 2019](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_European_Parliament_election), held from 23 to 26 May 2019. They were the largest simultaneous transnational elections ever held anywhere in the world. The first session of the ninth parliament started 2 July 2019.[^cite_note-110]

[European political parties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_political_parties) have the exclusive right to campaign during the European elections (as opposed to their corresponding EP groups).[^cite_note-111] There have been a number of proposals designed to attract greater public attention to the elections. One such innovation in the 2014 elections was that the [pan-European political parties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_political_party) announced, ahead of the elections, their candidates for [president](24468.md) of the [Commission](9974.md), the so-called *Spitzenkandidaten* (German, "leading candidates" or "top candidates"). The President of the European Commission is nominated by the European Council, representing the governments of the member states, and while there is no obligation for them to nominate the successful "candidate", the Lisbon Treaty states that they should take account of the results of the elections when choosing whom to nominate, and the candidate they propose must be endorsed by a majority of members of the European Parliament.[^cite_note-112]

The *Spitzenkandidaten* in 2014 were [Jean-Claude Juncker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Claude_Juncker) for the [European People's Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_People's_Party),[^cite_note-113] [Martin Schulz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Schulz) for the [Party of European Socialists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Party_of_European_Socialists), [Guy Verhofstadt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guy_Verhofstadt) for the [Alliance of Liberals and Democrats for Europe Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alliance_of_Liberals_and_Democrats_for_Europe_Party), [Ska Keller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ska_Keller) and [José Bové](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Bov%C3%A9) jointly for the [European Green Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Green_Party) and [Alexis Tsipras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexis_Tsipras) for the [Party of the European Left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Party_of_the_European_Left). The EPP won the most seats and Jean-Claude Juncker was nominated by the European Council and elected by the Parliament.

In 2019, the parties again announced their candidates for President of the Commission ahead of the European elections, notably [Manfred Weber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manfred_Weber) for the EPP and [Frans Timmermans](9524458.md) for the Party of European Socialists. However, after the election, there was no agreement among the parties after the election to back any of them. After a period of deadlock, the European Council proposed [Ursula von der Leyen](2991932.md) as a compromise and Parliament elected her, albeit by a narrow majority.

In 2024, the EPP decided to back Von der Leyen for a second term of office and put her forward as their "Spitzenkandidat" while the PES put forward [Nicolas Schmit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Schmit). After the election, the European Council duly nominated her and the Parliament elected her by 401 votes to 284, with support from the EPP, the Socialists, the liberal Renew Group and the Greens.[^cite_note-114]

Until 2014, turnout dropped consistently in every election since the first election, and from 1999 until 2014 was below 50%. In 2007 both [Bulgaria and Romania elected their MEPs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_European_Parliament_election) in by-elections, having joined at the beginning of 2007. The Bulgarian and Romanian elections saw two of the lowest turnouts for European elections, just 28.6%[^cite_note-115] and 28.3% respectively.[^cite_note-116] This trend was interrupted in the 2019 election, when turnout increased by 8% EU-wide, rising to 50.6%, the highest since 1994.

In England, Scotland and Wales, EP elections were originally held for a constituency MEP on a first-past-the-post basis. In 1999 the system was changed to a form of [proportional representation](23298.md) where parties put up a team of candidates in regional constituencies.[^cite_note-117] One could vote for a party's list of candidates, but not for an individual candidate (unless that party had a single candidate).

## Proceedings



These "relocation boxes" of the European Parliament, called "cantines", are ready to be transported between [Strasbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strasbourg) and [Brussels](3708.md). The EP moves back and forth to meet the EU treaty obligation to hold plenary sessions in Strasbourg.

Formally, the European Parliament has an annual "session", divided into monthly "part-sessions" and daily "sittings". In practice, this entails a monthly cycle with two committee weeks where matters are discussed in committees, political group weeks for members to discuss work within their political groups, and session weeks where members spend 3+1⁄2 days in [Strasbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strasbourg) for part-sessions. In addition six 2-day part sessions are organised in [Brussels](3708.md) throughout the year. Four weeks a year are allocated as constituency week to allow members to do exclusively constituency work, which they otherwise have to do at weekends. Finally there are no meetings planned during the summer weeks.[^cite_note-118]

The Parliament has the power to meet without being convened by another authority. Its meetings are partly set out by the treaties but are otherwise up to Parliament according to its own [Rules of Procedure of the European Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rules_of_Procedure_of_the_European_Parliament).[^cite_note-rules-119]

During sittings, members may speak after being called on by the President. Members of the Council or Commission may also attend and speak in debates.[^cite_note-120][^cite_note-121] Partly due to the need for interpretation, and the politics of consensus in the chamber, debates tend to be calmer and more polite than, say, the [Westminster system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_system).[^cite_note-122] Voting is conducted primarily by a show of hands, that may be checked on request by electronic voting.[^cite_note-123] Votes of MEPs are not recorded in either case, however; that only occurs when there is a roll-call ballot. This is required for the final votes on legislation and also whenever a political group or 30 MEPs request it. The number of roll-call votes has increased with time. Votes can also be a completely secret ballot (for example, when the president is elected).[^cite_note-124][^cite_note-125] All recorded votes, along with minutes and legislation, are recorded in the *[Official Journal of the European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Official_Journal_of_the_European_Union)* and can be accessed online.[^cite_note-126] Votes usually do not follow a debate, but rather they are grouped with other due votes on specific occasions, usually at noon on Tuesdays, Wednesdays or Thursdays. This is because the length of the vote is unpredictable and if it continues for longer than allocated it can disrupt other debates and meetings later in the day.[^cite_note-Corbett_174-127]

Members sit in a [hemicycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemicycle_\(chamber\)) according to their political groups (in the Common Assembly, prior to 1958, members sat alphabetically[^cite_note-Corbett_167-128]) who are ordered mainly by left to right. All desks are equipped with microphones, headphones for listening to interpretation, and electronic voting equipment. The leaders of the Groups sit on the front benches, and in the very centre is a podium for guest speakers. The remaining half of the circular chamber is primarily composed of the raised area where the President and staff sit. Further benches are provided between the sides of this area and the MEPs, these are taken up by the Council on the far left and the Commission on the far right. Both the Brussels and Strasbourg hemicycle roughly follow this layout with only minor differences.[^cite_note-129] The hemicycle design is a compromise between the different Parliamentary systems. The British-based system has the different groups directly facing each other while the French-based system is a semicircle (and the traditional German system had all members in rows facing a rostrum for speeches). Although the design is mainly based on a semicircle, the opposite ends of the spectrum do still face each other.[^cite_note-Corbett_167-128]

Access to the chamber limited, entrance is controlled by ushers who also aid MEPs in the chamber (for example in delivering documents). The ushers can also occasionally act as a form of police in enforcing the President, for example in ejecting an MEP who is disrupting the session (although this is rare). The first head of protocol in the Parliament was French, so many of the duties in the Parliament are based on the French model first developed following the [French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution). The ushers are highly visible in the Parliament, dressed in black [tails](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tailcoat) and wearing a silver chain, and are [recruited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EU_Concours) in the same manner as the [European civil service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Civil_Service). The President is allocated a personal usher.[^cite_note-130]

### President and organisation



[Roberta Metsola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roberta_Metsola), the current [President of the European Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_European_Parliament)

Main article: [President of the European Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_European_Parliament)

The President is essentially the [speaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speaker_\(politics\)) of the Parliament and presides over the plenary when it is in session. The President's signature is required for all acts adopted by co-decision, including the EU budget. The President is also responsible for representing the Parliament externally, including in legal matters, and for the application of the rules of procedure. The President is elected for two-and-a-half-year terms, meaning two elections per parliamentary term.[^cite_note-131][^cite_note-132] The current President of the European Parliament is [Roberta Metsola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roberta_Metsola), who was elected in January 2022.[^cite_note-133]

In most countries, the protocol of the [head of state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_of_state) comes before all others; however, in the EU the Parliament is listed as the first institution, and hence the protocol ranking of its president comes before any other European, or national, protocol. The gifts given to numerous visiting dignitaries depend upon the President. President [Josep Borrell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josep_Borrell) MEP of Spain gave his counterparts a crystal cup created by an artist from Barcelona who had engraved upon it parts of the [Charter of Fundamental Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charter_of_Fundamental_Rights_of_the_European_Union) among other things.[^cite_note-Protocol-21]

A number of notable figures have been President of the Parliament and its predecessors. The first President was [Paul-Henri Spaak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul-Henri_Spaak) MEP,[^cite_note-134] one of the [founding fathers of the Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Founding_fathers_of_the_European_Union). Other founding fathers include [Alcide de Gasperi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcide_de_Gasperi) MEP and [Robert Schuman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Schuman) MEP. The three female Presidents were [Simone Veil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simone_Veil) MEP in 1979 (first President of the elected Parliament), [Nicole Fontaine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicole_Fontaine) MEP in 1999, both Frenchwomen, and [Roberta Metsola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roberta_Metsola) from Malta in 2022.[^cite_note-135] The previous president, [Jerzy Buzek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerzy_Buzek) was the first [East-Central European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East-Central_Europe) to lead an EU institution, a former [Prime Minister of Poland](40543.md) who rose out of the [Solidarity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solidarity_\(Polish_trade_union\)) movement in Poland that helped overthrow communism in the [Eastern Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Bloc).[^cite_note-136]

During the election of a President, the previous President (or, if unable to, one of the previous vice-presidents) presides over the chamber.[^cite_note-LeP-137] Prior to 2009, the [oldest member](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_European_Parliament#Oldest_member) fulfilled this role[^cite_note-138] but the rule was changed to prevent far-right French MEP [Jean-Marie Le Pen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Marie_Le_Pen) taking the chair.[^cite_note-LeP-137]

The Parliament also elects 14 [Vice-Presidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vice_President_of_the_European_Parliament) who chair debates when the President is not in the chamber. There are a number of bodies responsible for the running of Parliament. The two main ones are the [Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_\(European_Parliament\)), which is responsible for budgetary and administration issues and is composed of the President and the Vice-Presidents, and the [Conference of Presidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conference_of_Presidents) which is the political governing body and is composed of the President of the Parliament and the leaders of each of the Parliament's political groups. Looking after the financial and administrative interests of members are five [Quaestors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaestor_\(European_Parliament\)).

As of 2014, the European Parliament budget was EUR 1.756 billion.[^cite_note-139] A 2008 report on the Parliament's finances highlighted certain overspending and miss-payments. Despite some MEPs calling for the report to be published, Parliamentary authorities had refused until an MEP broke confidentiality and leaked it.[^cite_note-140]

### Committees and delegations



A Committee room

Main article: [Committees of the European Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committees_of_the_European_Parliament)

The Parliament has 20 [Standing Committees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standing_Committee) ranging in size from 25 to 88 MEPs (each reflecting the political make-up of the whole Parliament) including a chair, a bureau and secretariat. They meet twice a month in public to draw up, amend to adopt legislative proposals and reports to be presented to the plenary.[^cite_note-Organised-141] The [rapporteurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapporteur) for a committee are supposed to present the view of the committee, although notably this has not always been the case: in the events leading to the resignation of the Santer Commission, the rapporteur went against the [Budgetary Control Committee's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_on_Budgetary_Control) narrow vote to discharge the budget, and urged the Parliament to reject it.[^cite_note-Ringe-47]

The Parliament can also set up sub-committees (e.g. the [Subcommittee on Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subcommittee_on_Human_Rights)) and temporary committees to deal with a specific topic (e.g. the Special Committee on Foreign Interference in Democratic Processes in the European Union, 2020–23). The chairs of the Committees co-ordinate their work through the "[Conference of Committee Chairmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committees_of_the_European_Parliament#Conference_of_Committee_Chairs)".[^cite_note-Organised-141]

The nature of the committees differ from their national counterparts as, although smaller in comparison to those of the [United States Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Congressional_committee), the European Parliament's committee secretariats are unusually large by European standards with between eight and twelve dedicated members of staff and three to four support staff. Considerable administration, archives and research resources are also at the disposal of the whole Parliament when needed.[^cite_note-Kreppel-71]

[Delegations of the Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Parliament_Delegations) are formed in a similar manner and are responsible for relations with Parliaments outside the EU. There are 44 delegations, mostly small (around 15 MEPs) but some larger. Chairpersons of the delegations also cooperate in a conference like the committee chairs do. Delegations include "Interparliamentary delegations" (maintain relations with Parliament outside the EU), "joint parliamentary committees" (maintaining relations with parliaments of states which are candidates or associates of the EU), the delegation to the [ACP EU Joint Parliamentary Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACP_EU_Joint_Parliamentary_Assembly) and the delegation to the [Euro-Mediterranean Parliamentary Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro-Mediterranean_Parliamentary_Assembly).[^cite_note-Organised-141] MEPs also participate in other international activities such as the [Euro-Latin American Parliamentary Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro-Latin_American_Parliamentary_Assembly), the [NATO Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NATO_Assembly), the [Transatlantic Legislators' Dialogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transatlantic_Legislators'_Dialogue) and through [election observation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_monitoring) in third countries.[^cite_note-142]

### Intergroups



[Jože Pučnik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jo%C5%BEe_Pu%C4%8Dnik) conference room

The [Intergroups in the European Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intergroups_in_the_European_Parliament) are informal fora which gather MEPs from various political groups around any topic. They do not express the view of the European Parliament. They serve a double purpose: to address a topic which is transversal to several committees and in a less formal manner. Their daily secretariat can be run either through the office of MEPs or through interest groups, be them corporate lobbies or NGOs. The favored access to MEPs which the organization running the secretariat enjoys can be one explanation to the multiplication of Intergroups in the 1990s.[^cite_note-143] They are now strictly regulated and financial support, direct or otherwise (via Secretariat staff, for example), must be officially specified in a declaration of financial interests.[^cite_note-144][^cite_note-145] Also Intergroups are established or renewed at the beginning of each legislature through a specific process. Indeed, the proposal for the constitution or renewal of an Intergroup must be supported by at least 3 political groups who can each support a limited number of proposals in proportion to their size (for example, for the legislature 2014–2019, the EPP or S\&D political groups could support 22 proposals whereas the Greens/EFA or the EFDD political groups only 7).[^cite_note-146]

### Translation and interpretation

Interpretation in the European Parliament



Interpreting booths in the hemicycle simultaneously interpret debates between 24 languages.



Interpreter at a committee hearing for the confirmation of [Thierry Breton](1545085.md) to the European Commission in 2019

See also: [Languages of the European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_the_European_Union)

Speakers in the European Parliament are entitled to speak in any of the 24 official [languages of the European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_the_European_Union), ranging from French and German to [Maltese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maltese_language) and [Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_language). Simultaneous interpreting is offered in all plenary sessions, and all final texts of legislation are translated. With twenty-four languages, the European Parliament is the most multilingual parliament in the world[^cite_note-147] and the biggest employer of interpreters in the world (employing 350 full-time and 400 freelancers when there is higher demand).[^cite_note-Interpreters-148] Citizens may also address the Parliament in [Basque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basque_language), [Catalan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalan_language)/[Valencian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valencian_language) and [Galician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galician_language).[^cite_note-149]

Usually a language is translated from a foreign tongue into a translator's native tongue. Due to the large number of languages, some being minor ones, since 1995 interpreting is sometimes done the opposite way, out of an interpreter's native tongue (the "retour" system). In addition, a speech in a minor language may be interpreted through a third language for lack of interpreters ("relay" interpreting) –  for example, when interpreting out of [Estonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estonian_language) into Maltese.[^cite_note-Interpreters-148] Due to the complexity of the issues, interpretation is not word for word. Instead, interpreters have to convey the political meaning of a speech, regardless of their own views. This requires detailed understanding of the politics and terms of the Parliament, involving a great deal of preparation beforehand (e.g. reading the documents in question). Difficulty can often arise when MEPs use profanities, jokes and word play or speak too fast.[^cite_note-Interpreters-148]

While some see speaking their native language as an important part of their identity, and can speak more fluently in debates, interpretation and its cost has been criticised by some. A 2006 report by [Alexander Stubb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Stubb) MEP highlighted that by only using English, French and German costs could be reduced from [€](9472.md)118,000 per day (for 21 languages then –  [Romanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_language), [Bulgarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgarian_language) and [Croatian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croatian_language) having not yet been included) to €8,900 per day.[^cite_note-150] There has also been a small-scale campaign to make French the reference language for all legal texts, on the basis of an argument that it is more clear and precise for legal purposes.[^cite_note-151]

Because the proceedings are translated into all of the official EU languages, they have been used to make a multilingual [corpus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Text_corpus) known as [Europarl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europarl_corpus). It is widely used to train [statistical machine translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_machine_translation) systems.[^cite_note-152]

## Corruption scandals

### Qatar related

Main article: [Qatar corruption scandal at the European Parliament](72479300.md)

Qatargate is an ongoing political scandal, involving allegations that European Parliament officials, lobbyists and their families have been influenced by the governments of Qatar, Morocco and Mauritania, engaging in corruption, money laundering, and organized crime to improve the image of these countries in the EU[^cite_note-153] amongst others to kill off six parliamentary resolutions condemning Qatar's human rights record, and working to deliver a visa-free travel deal between Doha and the EU. Law enforcement authorities in Belgium, Italy and Greece seized €1.5 million in cash, confiscated computers and mobile phones, and charged four individuals with the alleged offences:[^cite_note-154][^cite_note-155][^cite_note-156][^cite_note-157] Eva Kaili, Antonio Panzeri, an Italian former MEP, Francesco Giorgi and Luca Visentini. Later also Niccolò Figà-Talamanca, Marc Tarabella and Andrea Cozzolino were arrested. In 2023, Belgian MEP Marie Arena was charged, and in 2025, two more women: Elisabetta Gualmini and Alessandra Moretti.

### Azerbaijan related

[Azerbaijan](746.md) has been harshly criticized for bribing foreign officials and diplomats to promote its causes abroad and legitimize its elections at home, a practice termed [caviar diplomacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caviar_diplomacy).[^cite_note-158][^cite_note-159][^cite_note-160] In 2023 the Swedish-German investigative platform Blankspot published several investigative reports about freewheeling trips made by several European Parliament members to Azerbaijan.[^cite_note-161] Several publications have emphasised how these trips raise questions on the reliability of European Parliament ethics codes and the importance of enforcing them.[^cite_note-162][^cite_note-163]

### French National Front assistants affair

In December 2023, [Marine Le Pen](572897.md) was ordered to stand trial after she was charged with paying National Front party officials through funds earmarked for European Parliament assistants.[^cite_note-164] Twenty-seven others, including her father Jean Marie, served as her co-defendants.[^cite_note-165] Her trial, for misappropriation of public funds, was scheduled in March 2024 to occur between 30 September and 27 November the same year.[^cite_note-166]

On 31 March 2025, eight MEPs, 12 assistants from the National Rally, and Le Pen were found guilty of embezzling European Union funds by a Paris court.[^cite_note-167] Le Pen received a four-year prison sentence, two years of which were suspended, in addition to a €100,000 fine. She did not begin serving the two years' house arrest immediately as all appeals must be exhausted before this part of the sentence is executed. Similarly, she did not lose her seat in the lower house of the French parliament immediately. On the other hand, the court also banned her, effective immediately, from standing for political office for five years, making her ineligible to run in the 2027 French presidential election.[^cite_note-168]

### Russia related

The former British MEP [Nathan Gill](42863259.md) ([UKIP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UKIP) and later the Leader of the [Reform UK](59757202.md) party in Wales) pleaded guilty on Friday, 26 September 2025 to eight counts of bribery, under the UK's Bribery Act, 2010. He had taken payments to speak in the European Parliament to promote Russian interests, in particular in relation to Ukraine.[^cite_note-169]

### Other

A January 2024 report published by investigative outlet *Follow the Money* found that 1 in 4 EU lawmakers have been implicated in judicial cases or scandals. Out of 253 cases highlighted in the report, only 23 have resulted in convictions.[^cite_note-170]

There are also ongoing concerns related to allegations that members of the European Parliament were illegally or unethically influenced by Russia. Such concerns have been raised several times in 2023 and 2024 and dubbed "Russiagate".[^cite_note-171][^cite_note-172][^cite_note-173]

Panzeri's lobbying group, Fight Impunity, was largely funded by [Iqbal Survé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iqbal_Surv%C3%A9), a controversial South African billionaire. Survé's [Sekunjalo Development Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sekunjalo_Investments) was found to be the "most important donor" having donated €250,000 to the group.[^cite_note-174][^cite_note-175]

### Anti-corruption policies

The European Parliament has had criticism over its prodigality and for being too complacent with conflicts of interest.[^cite_note-176][^cite_note-\:0-177] Its refusal to become full member of the [GRECO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_States_against_Corruption) like all its member states is also a matter of criticism.[^cite_note-\:0-177]

The European Parliament revised its rules of procedure and its code of conduct in September 2023,[^cite_note-178] placing six main obligations on MEPs:[^cite_note-179][^cite_note-180]

- To make a detailed Declaration of private interests, including those from the 3 years prior to their election

- When total external income exceeds €5,000, all the entities from which their income is received to be disclosed

- With regard to conflicts of interest, to resolved or declare them

- Not to engage in paid lobbying activities linked to the EU's decision-making process

- To meet only with interested parties who sign up to the EU's Transparency Register and to make disclosure of such meetings, and also of meetings held with representatives of third country diplomats

- To make a Declaration of all assets and liabilities at the beginning, and again at the end, of every term of office.

## Seats

Seats of the European Parliament

Espace Léopold building in Brussels, Belgium

[Espace Léopold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Espace_L%C3%A9opold) building in [Brussels](3708.md),  [Belgium](3343.md)

Konrad Adenauer building in Luxembourg City, Luxembourg

[Konrad Adenauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konrad_Adenauer) building in [Luxembourg City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luxembourg_City),  [Luxembourg](17515.md) (seat of the Secretariat-General)

Further information: [Location of European Union institutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Location_of_European_Union_institutions), [Espace Léopold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Espace_L%C3%A9opold), and [Seat of the European Parliament in Strasbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seat_of_the_European_Parliament_in_Strasbourg)

The Parliament is based in three different cities with numerous buildings. The Member States attached a protocol to the [Treaty of Amsterdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Amsterdam) that requires 12 plenary sessions to be held in [Strasbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strasbourg) (in practice, none in August but two in October), which is described as the Parliament's official seat, while extra part sessions as well as committee meetings are held in [Brussels](3708.md). Luxembourg City hosts the [Secretariat of the European Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretariat_of_the_European_Parliament).[^cite_note-Consolidated_Treaty-24] The European Parliament is one of at least two assemblies in the world with more than one meeting place (another being the parliament of the Isle of Man, [Tynwald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tynwald)) and one of the few that does not have the power to decide its own location.[^cite_note-Strange-190]

The Strasbourg seat is considered a symbol of reconciliation between France and Germany, two countries that previously fought over the region. However, the cost and inconvenience of having two seats is questioned. While Strasbourg is the official seat, and sits alongside the [Council of Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Europe),[^cite_note-191] [Brussels is home to nearly all other major EU institutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brussels_and_the_European_Union), with the majority of Parliament's work being carried out there. Critics have described the two-seat arrangement as a "travelling circus",[^cite_note-Seat_Green-192] and there is a strong movement to establish Brussels as the sole seat. This is because the other political institutions (the Commission, Council and European Council) are located there, and hence Brussels is treated as the '[capital](181337.md)' of the EU. This movement has received strong backing from numerous figures, including [Margot Wallström](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margot_Wallstr%C3%B6m), Commission First-Vice President from 2004 to 2010, who stated that "something that was once a very positive symbol of the EU reuniting France and Germany has now become a negative symbol –  of wasting money, bureaucracy and the insanity of the Brussels institutions".[^cite_note-193] The [Green Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Green_Party) has also noted the environmental cost in a study led by [Jean Lambert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Lambert) MEP and [Caroline Lucas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caroline_Lucas) MEP; in addition to the extra 200 million euro spent on the extra seat, there are over 20,268 tonnes of additional carbon dioxide, undermining any environmental stance of the institution and the Union.[^cite_note-Seat_Green-192] The campaign is further backed by a million-strong online petition started by [Cecilia Malmström](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecilia_Malmstr%C3%B6m) MEP.[^cite_note-194] In August 2014, an assessment by the European Court of Auditors calculated that relocating the Strasbourg seat of the European Parliament to Brussels would save €113.8 million per year.[^cite_note-195] In 2006, there were allegations of irregularities in the charges made by the city of Strasbourg on buildings the Parliament rented, thus further harming the case for the Strasbourg seat.[^cite_note-196]

Most MEPs prefer Brussels as a single base.[^cite_note-197] A poll of MEPs found 89% of the respondents wanting a single seat, and 81% preferring Brussels.[^cite_note-198] Another survey found 68% support.[^cite_note-Farrell_Powerful-27] In July 2011, an absolute majority of MEPs voted in favour of a single seat.[^cite_note-199] In early 2011, the Parliament voted to scrap one of the Strasbourg sessions by holding two within a single week.[^cite_note-200] The [mayor of Strasbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roland_Ries) officially reacted by stating "we will counter-attack by upturning the adversary's strength to our own profit, as a [judoka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judo) would do".[^cite_note-201] However, as Parliament's seat is fixed by the treaties, it can only be changed by the Member States, acting unanimously and with national ratification, meaning that France could veto any move.[^cite_note-Strange-190] Former French President [Nicolas Sarkozy](557007.md) stated that the Strasbourg seat is "non-negotiable" and that France has no intention of surrendering the only EU Institution on French soil.[^cite_note-202] Given France's declared intention to veto any relocation to Brussels, some MEPs have advocated [civil disobedience](37073.md) by refusing to take part in the monthly exodus to Strasbourg.[^cite_note-203][^cite_note-204][^cite_note-205]

However, the main building in Brussels has been suffering for more than a decade from a state of degradation. Renovation or reconstruction works including a hemicycle were estimated to cost at least €500 million in 2017 with fear that the cost would be even higher[^cite_note-206] and possibly escalate up to €1 billion,[^cite_note-207] whereas the seat in Strasbourg already offers a fully-fledged hemicycle.

## &#x20;Channels of dialogue, information, and communication with European civil society

Individual MEPs are the main point of contact for citizens with the Parliament. They normally have an office in their constituency and travel back at the end of every week (and during weeks without parliamentary meetings) to participate in an array of meetings, events, interviews, visits and suchlike with voters, businesses, trade unions, local authorities and media.

The Parliament itself maintains a detailed website,[^cite_note-208] receives around one million visitors a year, and streamlines debates and committee meetings.

Over the last few years, European institutions have committed to promoting transparency, openness, and the availability of information about their work.[^cite_note-209] In particular, transparency is regarded as pivotal to the action of European institutions and a general principle of EU law, to be applied to the activities of EU institutions in order to strengthen the Union's democratic foundation.[^cite_note-210] The general principles of openness and transparency are reaffirmed in the articles 8 A, point 3 and 10.3 of the [Treaty of Lisbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Lisbon) and the [Maastricht Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maastricht_Treaty) respectively, stating that "every citizen shall have the right to participate in the democratic life of the Union. Decisions shall be taken as openly and as closely as possible to the citizen".[^cite_note-eur-lex.europa.eu-211][^cite_note-eur-lex.europa.eu-2-212] Furthermore, both treaties acknowledge the value of dialogue between citizens, representative associations, civil society, and European institutions.[^cite_note-eur-lex.europa.eu-211][^cite_note-eur-lex.europa.eu-2-212]

### Dialogue with religious and non-confessional organisations

Article 17 of the [Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_on_the_Functioning_of_the_European_Union) (TFEU) lays the juridical foundation for an open, transparent dialogue between European institutions and churches, religious associations, and non-confessional and philosophical organisations.[^cite_note-europarl.europa.eu-213] In July 2014, in the beginning of the 8th term, then President of the European Parliament [Martin Schulz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Schulz) tasked [Antonio Tajani](1767298.md), then vice-president, with implementing the dialogue with the religious and confessional organisations included in article 17.[^cite_note-214] In this framework, the European Parliament hosts high-level conferences on inter-religious dialogue, also with focus on current issues and in relation with parliamentary works.[^cite_note-europarl.europa.eu-213]

### European Parliament Mediator for International Parental Child Abduction

The chair of European Parliament Mediator for International Parental Child Abduction was established in 1987 by initiative of British MEP Charles [Henry Plumb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Plumb), with the goal of helping minor children of international couples victim of parental abduction. The Mediator finds negotiated solutions in the higher interest of the minor when said minor is abducted by a parent following separation of the couple, regardless whether married or unmarried.[^cite_note-215][^cite_note-europarl.europa.eu-2-216] Since its institution, the chair has been held by [Mairead McGuinness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mairead_McGuinness) (since 2014), Roberta Angelilli (2009–2014), Evelyne Gebhardt (2004–2009), Mary Banotti (1995–2004), and Marie-Claude Vayssade (1987–1994).[^cite_note-europarl.europa.eu-2-216] The Mediator's main task is to assist parents in finding a solution in the minor's best interest through mediation, i.e. a form of controversy resolution alternative to lawsuit. The Mediator is activated by request of a citizen and, after evaluating the request, starts a mediation process aimed at reaching an agreement. Once subscribed by both parties and the Mediator, the agreement is official. The nature of the agreement is that of a private contract between parties.[^cite_note-europarl.europa.eu-2-216] In defining the agreement, the European Parliament offers the parties the juridical support necessary to reach a sound, lawful agreement based on legality and equity. The agreement can be ratified by the competent national courts and can also lay the foundation for consensual separation or divorce.[^cite_note-europarl.europa.eu-2-216]

### Liaison Offices (EPLOs) & Antennas

The European Parliament operates Liaison Offices (EPLOs) in all Member States, as well as in the United Kingdom ([London](17867.md)) and the United States ([Washington, D.C.](108956.md)).[^cite_note-217] On 17 September 2025, European Parliament President [Roberta Metsola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roberta_Metsola) opened the Parliament's antenna office in Kyiv, co-located with the [EU delegation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delegation_of_the_European_Union_to_Ukraine) in [Kyiv](585629.md).[^cite_note-218] The [European Parliament](9581.md) also operates antennas in [New York](645042.md) (for the [United Nations](31769.md)), [Addis Ababa](52593.md) (for the [African Union](10634933.md)) [Jakarta](16275.md) (for [ASEAN](106539.md)), [Tirana](31075.md) (for the Western [Balkans](4829.md)), [Chișinău](56635.md) (for [Eastern Partnership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Partnership)). All antennas are co-located within the [EEAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EEAS) delegations.

## European Parliamentary Research Service

The [European Parliamentary Research Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Parliamentary_Research_Service) (EPRS) is the European Parliament's in-house research department and think tank. It provides [Members of the European Parliament](258909.md) –  and, where appropriate, [parliamentary committees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committees_of_the_European_Parliament) –  with independent, objective and authoritative analysis of, and research on, policy issues relating to the European Union, in order to assist them in their parliamentary work. It is also designed to increase Members' and EP committees' capacity to scrutinise and oversee the [European Commission](9974.md) and other [EU executive bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodies_of_the_European_Union).

EPRS aims to provide a comprehensive range of products and services, backed by specialist internal expertise and knowledge sources in all policy fields, so empowering Members and committees through knowledge and contributing to the Parliament's effectiveness and influence as an institution. In undertaking this work, the EPRS supports and promotes parliamentary outreach to the wider public, including dialogue with relevant stakeholders in the [EU](9317.md)'s system of multi-level governance. All EPRS publications are publicly available on the EP Think Tank platform.[^cite_note-219][^cite_note-220]

## Eurobarometer of the European Parliament

The European Parliament periodically commissions opinion polls and studies on public opinion trends in member states to survey perceptions and expectations of citizens about its work and the overall activities of the European Union. Topics include citizens' perception of the European Parliament's role, their knowledge of the institution, their sense of belonging in the European Union, opinions on European elections and European integration, identity, citizenship, political values, but also on current issues such as climate change, current economy and politics, etc. [Eurobarometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurobarometer) analyses seek to provide an overall picture of national situations, regional specificities, socio-demographic cleavages, and historical trends.[^cite_note-221][^cite_note-222]

## Prizes

### Sakharov Prize



The ceremony of the [Sakharov Prize](1055590.md) awarded to [Aung San Suu Kyi](2847.md) by [Martin Schulz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Schulz), in 2013

With the [Sakharov Prize](1055590.md), created in 1988, the European Parliament supports human rights by awarding individuals that contribute to promoting human rights worldwide, thus raising awareness on human rights violations. Priorities include: protection of human rights and fundamental liberties, with particular focus on freedom of expression; protection of minority rights; compliance with international law; and development of democracy and authentic rule of law.[^cite_note-europarl.europa.eu-3-223][^cite_note-224]

### European Charlemagne Youth Prize

The [European Charlemagne Youth Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Charlemagne_Youth_Prize) seeks to encourage youth participation in the European integration process. It is awarded by the European Parliament and the Foundation of the International Charlemagne Prize of Aachen to youth projects aimed at nurturing common European identity and European citizenship.[^cite_note-europarl.europa.eu-3-223]

### European Citizens' Prize

The [European Citizens' Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Citizens'_Prize) is awarded by the European Parliament to activities and actions carried out by citizens and associations to promote integration between the citizens of EU member states and transnational cooperation projects in the EU.[^cite_note-europarl.europa.eu-3-223]

### LUX Prize

Since 2007, the [LUX Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LUX_Prize) is awarded by the European Parliament to films dealing with current topics of public European interest that encourage reflection on Europe and its future. Over time, the Lux Prize has become a prestigious cinema award which supports European film and production also outside the EU.[^cite_note-225]

### Daphne Caruana Galizia Journalism Prize

From 2021, the Daphne Caruana Galizia Journalism prize shall be awarded by the European Parliament to outstanding journalism that reflect EU values. The prize consists in an award of 20,000 euros. This award is named after the late Maltese journalist, [Daphne Caruana Galizia](53850446.md) who was assassinated in Malta on 16 October 2017.[^cite_note-226] In October 2021 the prize was first awarded to the [Pegasus Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pegasus_Project_\(investigation\)).[^cite_note-227]
