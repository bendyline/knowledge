**Augustine of Hippo** ([/ɔːˈɡʌstɪn/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [*aw-GUST-in*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key), [US](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_English) also [/ˈɔːɡəstiːn/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [*AW-gə-steen*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key);[^cite_note-22] [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language): *Aurelius Augustinus Hipponensis*; 13 November 354 – 28 August 430),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWells200054-23] also well-known as **Saint Augustine**, was a [Christian theologian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_theologian) and philosopher from Thagaste, [Numidia Cirtensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numidia_\(Roman_province\)) and the [Bishop of Hippo Regius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippo_Regius). He is generally regarded as one of the most influential philosophers in the history of the Western world,[^cite_note-24] and is viewed as one of the most important [Church Fathers of the Latin Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Church_Fathers) in the [Patristic Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patristic_Period).

In his youth he was drawn to the [Manichaean faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manichaeism), and later to the [Hellenistic philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_philosophy) of [Neoplatonism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonism). After his conversion to Christianity and baptism in 386 by [Saint Ambrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambrose), Augustine developed his own approach to philosophy and theology, making significant contributions in a wide variety of areas.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETeSelle2002343-25] He is known as *Doctor Gratiae* ("Doctor of Grace") for the vast influence of his views on the necessity of unmerited, [prevenient grace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prevenient_grace), for salvation and the nature of [original sin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Original_sin).[^cite_note-26]

Additionally, he made important contributions in ecclesiology, noting the validity of sacraments regardless of the merit of the celebrant against the views of the [Donatists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donatism).[^cite_note-27] In his book *[The City of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_City_of_God)*, Augustine imagined the Church as a spiritual City, distinct from the material Earthly City, against the backdrop of the fall of the [Western Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Roman_Empire).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDurant1992-28] He is also widely recognized for his contributions to [just war theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Just_war_theory). His other notable works include *[On the Trinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Trinity)*, *[On Christian Doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_doctrina_Christiana)*, and *[Confessions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confessions_\(Augustine\))*, the latter of which was the first Western [autobiography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autobiography).[^cite_note-29]

Augustine is recognized as a saint in the [Catholic Church](606848.md), the [Eastern Orthodox Church](10186.md), the [Lutheran churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lutheran_churches), and the [Anglican Communion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Communion). Generally regarded as one of the greatest theologians and philosophers in the history of the Catholic Church, he is one of the four Great Latin Church Fathers, along with [Ambrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambrose), [Jerome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerome), and [Pope Gregory I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Gregory_I). His thoughts profoundly influenced the Medieval worldview, and he was the most cited author in the *[Summa Theologiae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summa_Theologica)* of [St. Thomas Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas).[^cite_note-30] Many [Protestants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism), especially [Calvinists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvinism) and [Lutherans](23371382.md), consider him one of the theological fathers of the [Protestant Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant_Reformation) due to his teachings on [salvation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvation) and [divine grace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_grace), although this interpretation is disputed by the Catholic Church.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHägglund2007139–140-31][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGonzález1987-32][^cite_note-St._Augustine_of_Hippo-33] In [Eastern Orthodoxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodoxy), he remains highly venerated, but some of his teachings, including the [filioque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filioque), are more disputed.[^cite_note-Rev._Dr.-34]

Augustine was born in 354 in the [municipium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Municipium) of [Thagaste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thagaste) (now [Souk Ahras](10799861.md), Algeria) in the [Roman province of Numidia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numidia_\(Roman_province\)).[^cite_note-Encyclopedia_Britannica-36][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMagill2003172-37][^cite_note-38][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJones201739-39][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJayapalan200151-40] His mother, [Monica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Monica) or Monnica,[^cite_note-41] was a devout Christian; his father Patricius was a [pagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paganism) who converted to Christianity on his deathbed.[^cite_note-42] He had a brother named Navigius and a sister whose name is lost but is conventionally remembered as [Perpetua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpetua_of_Hippo).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBonner1986-43]

Scholars generally agree that Augustine and his family were [Berbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berbers), an ethnic group indigenous to North Africa,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollingworth201350–51-44][^cite_note-FOOTNOTELeith199024-45][^cite_note-46] but were heavily Romanized, speaking only Latin at home as a matter of pride and dignity.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollingworth201350–51-44] In his writings, Augustine mentions in passing his identity as a [Roman African](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Africa). For example, he refers to Ponticianus as "a country man of ours, insofar as being African,"[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollingworth201350–51-44][^cite_note-47] and to [Faustus of Mileve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faustus_of_Mileve) as "an African [Gentleman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gentleman)".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollingworth201350–51-44][^cite_note-48] He writes that Apuleius "as an African is better known to us Africans".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHollingworth201350–51-44][^cite_note-49]

Augustine's family name, Aurelius, suggests his father's ancestors were [freedmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberti) of the *[gens Aurelia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurelia_\(gens\))* given full Roman citizenship by the [Edict of Caracalla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edict_of_Caracalla) in 212. Augustine's family had been Roman, from a legal standpoint, for at least a century when he was born.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELancel20025-50] It is assumed that his mother, Monica, was of Berber origin, on the basis of her name,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPower1999353–354-51][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrettFentress199671,_293-52] but as his family were *[honestiores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decurion_\(administrative\))*, an upper class of citizens known as honorable men, Augustine's first language was likely Latin.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPower1999353–354-51]

At the age of 11, Augustine was sent to school at [Madaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madaurus_University) (now [M'Daourouch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M'Daourouch)), a small Numidian city about 31 kilometres (19 miles) south of Thagaste. There he became familiar with [Latin literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_literature), as well as pagan beliefs and practices.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnowlesPenkett2004Ch._2-53] His first insight into the nature of sin occurred when he and a number of friends stole pears from a neighbourhood garden. He tells this story in his autobiography, *Confessions*. He realises that the pears were "tempting neither for its colour nor its flavour" – he was neither hungry nor poor, and he had enough of fruit which were "much better". Over the next few chapters, Augustine agonises over this past sin of his, recognising that one does not desire evil for evil's sake. Rather, "through an inordinate preference for these goods of a lower kind, the better and higher are neglected".[^cite_note-54] In other words, man is drawn to sin when grossly choosing the lesser good over a greater good. Eventually, Augustine concludes that it was the good of the "companionship" between him and his accomplices that allowed him to delight in this theft.[^cite_note-55]

At the age of 17, through the generosity of his fellow citizen Romanianus,[^cite_note-EA-56] Augustine went to [Carthage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carthage) to continue his education in [rhetoric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhetoric), though it was above the financial means of his family.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-57] Despite the good warnings of his mother, as a youth Augustine lived a [hedonistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedonism) lifestyle for a time, associating with young men who boasted of their sexual exploits. The need to gain their acceptance encouraged inexperienced boys like Augustine to seek or make up stories about sexual experiences.[^cite_note-FLNZ1-58]

It was while he was a student in Carthage that he read [Cicero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cicero)'s [dialogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialogue) *[Hortensius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hortensius_\(Cicero\))* (now lost), which he described as leaving a lasting impression, enkindling in his heart the love of wisdom and a great thirst for truth. It started his interest in philosophy.[^cite_note-59] Although raised Christian, Augustine became a [Manichaean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manichaeism), much to his mother's chagrin.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPope1911-60]

At about the age of 17, Augustine began a relationship with a young woman in Carthage. Though his mother wanted him to marry a person of his class, the woman remained his lover. He was warned by his mother to avoid fornication (sex outside marriage), but Augustine persisted in the relationship[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERanke-Heinemann1990-61] for over fifteen years,[^cite_note-utne.com-62] and the woman gave birth to his son Adeodatus (372–388), which means "Gift from God",[^cite_note-63] who was viewed as extremely intelligent by his contemporaries. In 385, Augustine ended his relationship with his lover in order to prepare to marry a teenage heiress. By the time he was able to marry her, however, he had already converted to Christianity and decided to become a Christian priest and the marriage did not happen.[^cite_note-utne.com-62][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown200063-64]

Augustine was, from the beginning, a brilliant student, with an eager intellectual curiosity, but he never mastered Greek[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEO'Donnell2005-65] – his first Greek teacher was a brutal man who constantly beat his students, and Augustine rebelled and refused to study. By the time he realized he needed to know Greek, it was too late; and although he acquired a smattering of the language, he was never eloquent with it. He did, however, become a master of Latin.

### Move to Carthage, Rome, and Milan

Augustine taught grammar at Thagaste between 373 and 374. The following year he moved to Carthage to conduct a school of rhetoric and remained there for the next nine years.[^cite_note-EA-56] Disturbed by unruly students in Carthage, he moved to establish a school in Rome, where he believed the best and brightest rhetoricians practised, in 383. However, Augustine was disappointed with the apathetic reception. It was the custom for students to pay their fees to the professor on the last day of the term, and many students attended faithfully all term, and then did not pay.

Manichaean friends introduced him to the prefect of the City of Rome, [Symmachus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quintus_Aurelius_Symmachus), who had been asked by the imperial court at [Milan](36511.md)[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPortalié1907a-20] to provide a rhetoric professor. Augustine won the job and headed north to take his position in Milan in late 384. At thirty years old, he had won the most visible academic position in the Latin world at a time when such posts gave ready access to political careers.

Although Augustine spent ten years as a Manichaean, he was never an initiate or "elect", but an "auditor", the lowest level in this religion's hierarchy.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPortalié1907a-20][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEChadwick200114-66] While still at Carthage a disappointing meeting with the Manichaean bishop, [Faustus of Mileve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faustus_of_Mileve), a key exponent of Manichaean theology, started Augustine's scepticism of Manichaeanism.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPortalié1907a-20] In Rome, he reportedly turned away from Manichaeanism, embracing the [scepticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_scepticism) of the [New Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Academy) movement. Because of his education, Augustine had great rhetorical prowess and was very knowledgeable of the philosophies behind many faiths.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKishlanskyGearyO'Brien2005142–143-67] At Milan, his mother's religiosity, Augustine's own studies in [Neoplatonism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonism), and his friend [Simplicianus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplician) all urged him towards Christianity.[^cite_note-EA-56] This was shortly after the Roman emperor [Theodosius I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodosius_I) declared Christianity to be the only legitimate religion for the Roman Empire on 27 February 380 by the [Edict of Thessalonica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edict_of_Thessalonica)[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDoniger1999689–690-68] and then issued a decree of death for all Manichaean monks in 382. Initially, Augustine was not strongly influenced by Christianity and its ideologies, but after coming in contact with [Ambrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambrose) of Milan, Augustine reevaluated himself and was forever changed.



*Saint Augustine and his mother, Saint Monica* (1846) by [Ary Scheffer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ary_Scheffer)

Augustine arrived in Milan and visited Ambrose, having heard of his reputation as an orator. Like Augustine, Ambrose was a master of rhetoric, but older and more experienced.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBeDuhn2010163-69] Soon, their relationship grew, as Augustine wrote, "And I began to love him, of course, not at the first as a teacher of the truth, for I had entirely despaired of finding that in thy Church – but as a friendly man."[^cite_note-Augustine:_Account_of_His_Own_Conversion-70] Augustine was very much influenced by Ambrose, even more than by his own mother and others he admired. In his *Confessions*, Augustine states, "That man of God received me as a father would, and welcomed my coming as a good bishop should."[^cite_note-Augustine:_Account_of_His_Own_Conversion-70] Ambrose adopted Augustine as a spiritual son after the death of Augustine's father.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilson201890-71]

Augustine's mother had followed him to Milan and arranged a respectable marriage for him. Although Augustine acquiesced, he had to dismiss his concubine and grieved for having forsaken his lover. He wrote, "My mistress being torn from my side as an impediment to my marriage, my heart, which clave to her, was racked, and wounded, and bleeding." Augustine confessed he had not been a lover of wedlock so much as a slave of lust, so he procured another concubine since he had to wait two years until his fiancée came of age.[^cite_note-73] However, his emotional wound was not healed.[^cite_note-74] It was during this period that he uttered his famously insincere prayer, "Grant me [chastity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chastity) and [continence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_abstinence), but not yet."[^cite_note-FLNZ2-75]

There is evidence Augustine may have considered this former relationship to be equivalent to marriage.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBurrus20111–20-76] In his *Confessions*, he admitted the experience eventually produced a decreased sensitivity to pain. Augustine eventually broke off his engagement to his eleven-year-old fiancée, but never renewed his relationship with either of his concubines. [Alypius of Thagaste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alypius_of_Thagaste) steered Augustine away from marriage, saying they could not live a life together in the love of wisdom if he married. Augustine looked back years later on the life at [Cassiciacum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassago_Brianza), a villa outside of Milan where he gathered with his followers, and described it as *Christianae vitae otium* – the leisure of Christian life.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFerguson1999208-77]

### Conversion to Christianity and priesthood



*The Conversion of St. Augustine* by [Fra Angelico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fra_Angelico)

In late August of 386,[^cite_note-78] at the age of 31, having heard of Ponticianus's and his friends' first reading of the life of [Anthony of the Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_the_Great), Augustine converted to Christianity. As Augustine later told it, his conversion was prompted by hearing a child's voice say "take up and read" ([Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language): *tolle, lege*). Resorting to the *[sortes biblicae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sortes_biblicae)*, he opened a book of St. Paul's writings (*Confessiones* 8.12.29) at random and read Romans 13\:13–14: "Not in rioting and drunkenness, not in chambering and wantonness, not in strife and envying, but put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh to fulfil the lusts thereof."[^cite_note-79]

He later wrote an account of his conversion in his *[Confessions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confessions_\(Augustine\))* ([Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language): *Confessiones*), which has since become a classic of Christian theology and a key text in the history of [autobiography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autobiography). This work is an outpouring of thanksgiving and penitence. Although it is written as an account of his life, the *Confessions* also talks about the nature of time, causality, free will, and other important philosophical topics. The following is taken from that work:

> Belatedly I loved thee, O Beauty so ancient and so new, belatedly I loved thee. For see, thou wast within and I was without, and I sought thee out there. Unlovely, I rushed heedlessly among the lovely things thou hast made. Thou wast with me, but I was not with thee. These things kept me far from thee; even though they were not at all unless they were in thee. Thou didst call and cry aloud, and didst force open my deafness. Thou didst gleam and shine, and didst chase away my blindness. Thou didst breathe fragrant odours and I drew in my breath; and now I pant for thee. I tasted, and now I hunger and thirst. Thou didst touch me, and I burned for thy peace.[^cite_note-80]



*The vision of St. Augustine* by [Ascanio Luciano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascanio_Luciano)

[Ambrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambrose) baptized Augustine and his son Adeodatus, in Milan on [Easter Vigil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Vigil), 24–25 April 387.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown2000117-81] A year later, in 388, Augustine completed his [apology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_apologetics) *On the Holiness of the Catholic Church*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPortalié1907a-20] That year, also, Adeodatus and Augustine returned home to Africa.[^cite_note-EA-56] Augustine's mother [Monica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Monica) died at [Ostia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ostia_Antica), Italy, as they prepared to embark for Africa.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPope1911-60] Upon their arrival, they began a life of aristocratic leisure at Augustine's family's property.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPossidius20083.1-82] Soon after, Adeodatus, too, died.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEA'Becket1907-83] Augustine then sold his patrimony and gave the money to the poor. He only kept the family house, which he converted into a [monastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monastic) foundation for himself and a group of friends.[^cite_note-EA-56] Furthermore, while he was known for his major contributions to Christian rhetoric, another major contribution was his preaching style.[^cite_note-\:4-84]

After converting to Christianity, Augustine turned against his profession as a rhetoric professor in order to devote more time to preaching.[^cite_note-\:1-85] In 391 Augustine was [ordained](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordination) a [priest](23707.md) in [Hippo Regius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippo_Regius) (now Annaba), in Algeria. He was especially interested in discovering how his previous rhetorical training in Italian schools would help the Christian Church achieve its objective of discovering and teaching the different scriptures in the Bible.[^cite_note-86] He became a famous [preacher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preacher) (more than 350 preserved sermons are believed to be authentic), and was noted for combating the [Manichaean religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manichaeism), to which he had formerly adhered.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPortalié1907a-20] He preached around 6,000 to 10,000 sermons when he was alive; however, there are only around 500 sermons that are accessible today.[^cite_note-\:2-87] When Augustine preached his sermons, they were recorded by stenographers.[^cite_note-\:4-84] Some of his sermons would last over one hour and he would preach multiple times throughout a given week.[^cite_note-\:2-87] When talking to his audience, he would stand on an elevated platform; however, he would walk towards the audience during his sermons.[^cite_note-\:2-87] When he was preaching, he used a variety of rhetorical devices that included [analogies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analogy), word pictures, [similes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simile), [metaphors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphor), [repetition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repetition_\(rhetorical_device\)), and [antithesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antithesis) when trying to explain more about the Bible.[^cite_note-\:2-87] In addition, he used questions and rhymes when talking about the differences between people's life on Earth and Heaven as seen in one of his sermons that was preached in 412 AD.[^cite_note-\:3-88] Augustine believed that the preachers' ultimate goal is to ensure the salvation of their audience.[^cite_note-89]

In 395, he was made [coadjutor Bishop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coadjutor_Bishop) of Hippo and became full Bishop shortly thereafter,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown2000-90] hence the name "Augustine of Hippo"; and he gave his property to the church of Thagaste.[^cite_note-91] He remained in that position until his death in 430. Bishops were the only individuals allowed to preach when he was alive and he scheduled time to preach after being ordained despite a busy schedule made up of preparing sermons and preaching at other churches besides his own.[^cite_note-92][^cite_note-93] When serving as the Bishop of Hippo, his goal was to minister to individuals in his congregation and he would choose the passages that the church planned to read every week.[^cite_note-\:42-94] As bishop, he believed that it was his job to interpret the work of the Bible.[^cite_note-\:42-94] He wrote his autobiographical *[Confessions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confessions_\(Augustine\))* in 397–398. His work *The City of God* was written to console his fellow Christians shortly after the [Visigoths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visigoths) had [sacked Rome in 410](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sack_of_Rome_\(410\)).[^cite_note-95] Augustine worked tirelessly to convince the people of Hippo to convert to Christianity. Though he had left his monastery, he continued to lead a monastic life in the episcopal residence.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPortalié1907b-96]

Much of Augustine's later life was recorded by his friend [Possidius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Possidius), bishop of [Calama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calama_\(Numidia\)) (present-day [Guelma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guelma), Algeria), in his *Sancti Augustini Vita*. During this latter part of Augustine's life, he helped lead a large community of Christians against different political and religious factors which had a major influence on his writings.[^cite_note-97] Possidius admired Augustine as a man of powerful intellect and a stirring orator who took every opportunity to defend Christianity against its detractors. Possidius also described Augustine's personal traits in detail, drawing a portrait of a man who ate sparingly, worked tirelessly, despised gossip, shunned the temptations of the flesh, and exercised prudence in the financial stewardship of his see.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPossidius2008-98]

### Death and sainthood



Baroque Painting of Augustine triumphantly ascending into Heaven on a cloud c. 1664

Shortly before Augustine's death, the [Vandals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vandals), a [Germanic tribe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_tribe) that had converted to [Arianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arianism), [invaded Roman Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vandalic_conquest_of_Roman_Africa). The Vandals besieged Hippo in the spring of 430 when Augustine entered his final illness. According to Possidius, one of the few miracles attributed to Augustine, the healing of an ill man, took place during the siege.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPossidius200843-99] Spending his final days in prayer and repentance, he requested the penitential [Psalms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psalms) of David be hung on his walls so he could read them, which led him to "\[weep] freely and constantly" according to Possidius's biography.[^cite_note-100] He directed the library of the church in Hippo and all the books therein should be carefully preserved. He died on 28 August 430.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPossidius200857-101] Shortly after his death, the Vandals lifted the siege of Hippo, but they returned soon after and burned the city. They destroyed all but Augustine's cathedral and library, which they left untouched.

Augustine was [canonized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonization) by popular acclaim, and recognized as a Doctor of the Church in 1298 by [Pope Boniface VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Boniface_VIII).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEOestreich1907-102] His [feast day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feast_day) is 28 August. He is considered the patron saint of brewers, printers, theologians, and a number of cities and dioceses. He is invoked against sore eyes.[^cite_note-KnoYrSaint-103]

Augustine is remembered in the [Church of England](5955.md)'s [calendar of saints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_\(Church_of_England\)) with a [lesser festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesser_Festival_\(Anglicanism\)) on 28 August.[^cite_note-104]

#### Relics



Augustine's arm bones, [Saint Augustin Basilica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Augustin_Basilica), Annaba, Algeria

According to [Bede](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bede)'s *True Martyrology*, Augustine's body was later [translated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation_\(relic\)) to [Cagliari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cagliari), [Sardinia](29376.md), by the Catholic bishops expelled from North Africa by [Huneric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huneric). Around 720, his remains were transported again by Peter, [bishop of Pavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Pavia) and uncle of the Lombard king [Liutprand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liutprand,_King_of_the_Lombards), to the church of [San Pietro in Ciel d'Oro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Pietro_in_Ciel_d'Oro) in Pavia, to save them from frequent coastal raids by [Saracens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saracen). In January 1327, [Pope John XXII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_John_XXII) issued the papal bull *Veneranda Santorum Patrum*, appointing the Augustinians guardians of the tomb of Augustine, called the *Arca*. It was remade in 1362 and elaborately carved with bas-reliefs of scenes from Augustine's life, created by [Giovanni di Balduccio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_di_Balduccio).[^cite_note-105]



[Giovanni di Balduccio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_di_Balduccio), Tomb of St Augustine, 1362–1365, [San Pietro in Ciel d'Oro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Pietro_in_Ciel_d'Oro), [Pavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pavia)

In October 1695, some workmen in the Church of San Pietro in Ciel d'Oro in Pavia discovered a marble box containing human bones (including part of a skull). A dispute arose between the Augustinian hermits (Order of Saint Augustine) and the regular canons ([Canons Regular of Saint Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canons_Regular_of_Saint_Augustine)) as to whether these were the bones of Augustine. The hermits did not believe so; the canons affirmed they were. [Pope Benedict XIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XIII) (1724–1730) directed the Bishop of Pavia, [Monsignor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monsignor) Pertusati, to make a determination. The bishop declared that, in his opinion, the bones were those of Augustine.[^cite_note-106]

The Augustinians were expelled from Pavia in 1785,[^cite_note-107] Augustine's ark and relics were brought to [Pavia Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pavia_Cathedral) in 1799.[^cite_note-108] San Pietro fell into disrepair but was restored in the 1870s at the urging of [Agostino Gaetano Riboldi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agostino_Gaetano_Riboldi). It was reconsecrated in 1896 when the relics of Augustine and the shrine were once again reinstalled.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDale200155-109][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStone2002-110]

In 1842, a portion of Augustine's right arm (cubitus) was secured from Pavia and returned to [Annaba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annaba).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnaubeltVan_Fleteren1999165-111] It now rests in the [Saint Augustin Basilica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Augustin_Basilica) within a glass tube inserted into the arm of a life-size marble statue of Augustine.

Augustine's large contribution of writings covered diverse fields including theology, philosophy and sociology. Along with [John Chrysostom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Chrysostom), Augustine was among the most prolific scholars of the early church by quantity.

### Christian anthropology

Augustine was one of the first Christian [ancient Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Latin) authors with a very clear vision of [theological anthropology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_anthropology).[^cite_note-112] He saw the human being as a perfect unity of soul and body. In his late treatise *[On Care to Be Had for the Dead, section 5](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Nicene%20and%20Post-Nicene%20Fathers:%20Series%20I/Volume%20III/Moral%20Treatises%20of%20St.%20Augustin/On%20Care%20to%20Be%20Had%20for%20the%20Dead/Section%205)* (420) he exhorted respect for the body on the grounds it belonged to the very nature of the human [person](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Person).[^cite_note-113] Augustine's favourite figure to describe *body–soul* unity is marriage: *caro tua, coniunx tua – your body is your wife*.[^cite_note-114][^cite_note-115][^cite_note-116]

Augustine believed that though initially the two elements of body and soul were in perfect harmony, after the [fall of humanity](#Original_sin) they came into dramatic combat with one another. He wrote of them as two categorically different things: the body as a three-dimensional object composed of the four elements, and the soul as spatially dimensionless.[^cite_note-117] He further defined the soul as a kind of substance, participating in reason, fit for ruling the body.[^cite_note-118]

Augustine was not preoccupied, as [Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato) and [Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descartes) were, in detailed efforts to explain the [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics) of the soul-body union. It sufficed for him to admit they are metaphysically distinct: to be a human is to be a composite of soul and body, with the soul superior to the body. The latter statement is grounded in his [hierarchical classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hierarchical_classification) of things into those that merely exist, those that exist and live, and those that exist, live, and have intelligence or reason.[^cite_note-119][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMann1999141–142-120]

Sermons directed at Manichaeism focused on their error of teaching the soul was part of God instead of admission that the soul is 'full of illusions'.[^cite_note-121]

Like other Church Fathers such as [Athenagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenagoras_of_Athens),[^cite_note-122] [Tertullian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertullian),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFlinn20074-123] [Clement of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clement_of_Alexandria) and [Basil of Caesarea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basil_of_Caesarea),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELuker198512-124] Augustine "vigorously condemned the practice of induced [abortion](765.md)", and although he disapproved of abortion during any stage of pregnancy, he made a distinction between early and later abortions.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBauerschmidt19991-125] He acknowledged the distinction between "formed" and "unformed" fetuses mentioned in the [Septuagint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Septuagint) translation of Exodus 21\:22–23, which incorrectly translates the word "harm" (from the original Hebrew text) as "form" in the [Koine Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koine_Greek) of the Septuagint. His view was based on the Aristotelian distinction "between the fetus before and after its supposed 'vivification'". Therefore, he did not classify the abortion of an "unformed" fetus as murder since he thought it could not be known with certainty the fetus had received a soul.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBauerschmidt19991-125][^cite_note-126]

Augustine held that "the timing of the infusion of the soul was a mystery known to God alone".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELysaughtKotvaLammersVerhey2012676-127] However, he considered procreation as "one of the goods of marriage; abortion figured as a means, along with drugs which cause sterility, of frustrating this good. It lay along a continuum which included infanticide as an instance of 'lustful cruelty' or 'cruel lust.' Augustine called the use of means to avoid the birth of a child an 'evil work:' a reference to either abortion or contraception or both."[^cite_note-Vasa-128]

See also: [Allegorical interpretations of Genesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allegorical_interpretations_of_Genesis)



Mosaic of Augustine from the [Cappella Palatina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cappella_Palatina) in [Palermo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palermo), Italy (1140)

In *City of God*, Augustine rejected both the contemporary ideas of ages (such as those of certain Greeks and Egyptians) that differed from the Church's sacred writings.[^cite_note-129] In *The Literal Interpretation of Genesis*, Augustine argued that God had created everything in the universe simultaneously and not over a period of six days. He argued the six-day structure of creation presented in the Book of Genesis represents a [logical framework](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Framework_interpretation_\(Genesis\)), rather than the passage of time in a physical way – it would bear a spiritual, rather than physical, meaning, which is no less literal. One reason for this interpretation is the passage in [Sirach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sirach) 18\:1, *creavit omnia simul* ("He created all things at once"), which Augustine took as proof that the days of Genesis 1 had to be taken non-literalistically.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETeske1999377–378-130] As additional support for describing the six days of creation as a [heuristic device](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic_device), Augustine thought the actual event of creation would be incomprehensible by humans and therefore needed to be translated.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFranklin-Brown2012280-131]

Augustine also does not envision original sin as causing structural changes in the universe, and even suggests that the bodies of [Adam and Eve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_and_Eve) were already created mortal before [the Fall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_man).[^cite_note-132][^cite_note-133][^cite_note-134] Apart from his specific views, Augustine recognized that interpreting the creation story was difficult, and remarked that interpretations could change should new information come up.[^cite_note-135]

### Ecclesiology

See also: [Ecclesiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesiology)



*St. Augustine* by [Carlo Crivelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlo_Crivelli)

Augustine developed his doctrine of the Church principally in reaction to the [Donatist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donatist) sect. He taught there is one Church, but within this Church there are two realities, namely, the visible aspect (the institutional [hierarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hierarchy_of_the_Catholic_Church), the [Catholic sacraments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacraments_of_the_Catholic_Church), and the [laity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laity)) and the invisible (the souls of those in the Church, who are either dead, sinful members or elect predestined for Heaven). The former is the institutional body established by Christ on earth which proclaims salvation and administers the sacraments, while the latter is the invisible body of the elect, made up of genuine believers from all ages, who are known only to God. The Church, which is visible and societal, will be made up of "wheat" and "tares", that is, good and wicked people (as per Mat. 13\:30), until the end of time. This concept countered the Donatist claim that only those in a [state of grace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_\(theology\)) were the "true" or "pure" church on earth, and that priests and bishops who were not in a state of grace had no authority or ability to confect the sacraments.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGonzález198728-136]

Augustine's ecclesiology was more fully developed in *City of God*. There he conceives of the church as a heavenly city or kingdom, ruled by love, which will ultimately triumph over all earthly empires which are self-indulgent and ruled by pride. Augustine followed [Cyprian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprian) in teaching that bishops and priests of the Church are the [successors of the Apostles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostolic_succession),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGonzález1987-32] and their authority in the Church is God-given.

The concept of [Church invisible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_invisible) was advocated by Augustine as part of his refutation of the Donatist sect, though he, as other Church Fathers before him, saw the invisible Church and visible Church as one and the same thing, unlike the later Protestant reformers who did not identify the Catholic Church as the [true church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_true_church).[^cite_note-137] He was strongly influenced by the [Platonist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonism) belief that true reality is invisible and that, if the visible reflects the invisible, it does so only partially and imperfectly (see [Theory of Forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_Forms)).[^cite_note-138] Others question whether Augustine really held to some form of an "invisible true Church" concept.[^cite_note-139]

### Eschatology

Augustine originally believed in [premillennialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premillennialism), namely that Christ would establish a literal 1,000-year kingdom prior to the general [resurrection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resurrection), but later rejected the belief, viewing it as carnal. During the medieval period, the Catholic Church built its system of eschatology on Augustinian [amillennialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amillennialism), where Christ rules the earth spiritually through his triumphant church.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlomberg2006519-140]

During the [Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reformation), theologians such as [John Calvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Calvin) accepted amillennialism. Augustine taught that the eternal fate of the soul is determined at death,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone2005-141][^cite_note-142] and that [purgatorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purgatory) fires of the [intermediate state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermediate_state_\(Christianity\)) purify only those who died in communion with the Church. His teaching provided fuel for later theology.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone2005-141]

### Mariology

Although Augustine did not develop an independent [Mariology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariology), his statements on Mary surpass in number and depth those of other early writers. Even before the [Council of Ephesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Ephesus), he defended the [Ever-Virgin Mary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpetual_virginity_of_Mary) as the [Mother of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theotokos), believing her to be "full of grace" (following earlier Latin writers such as [Jerome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerome)) on account of her sexual integrity and innocence.[^cite_note-143] Likewise, he affirmed that the Virgin Mary "conceived as virgin, gave birth as virgin and stayed virgin forever".[^cite_note-144]

### Natural knowledge and biblical interpretation

Augustine took the view that, if a literal interpretation contradicts science and humans' God-given reason, the biblical text should be interpreted metaphorically. While each passage of Scripture has a literal sense, this "literal sense" does not always mean the Scriptures are mere history; at times they are rather an [extended metaphor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extended_metaphor).[^cite_note-145]

### Original sin

See also: [Original sin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Original_sin)



Painting of Augustine (1458) by Tomás Giner, tempera on panel, Diocesan Museum of Zaragoza, Aragon, Spain

Augustine taught that the sin of Adam and Eve was either an act of foolishness (*insipientia*) followed by pride and disobedience to God or that pride came first.[^cite_note-146] The first couple disobeyed God, who had told them not to eat of the [Tree of the knowledge of good and evil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_of_the_knowledge_of_good_and_evil) (Gen 2\:17).[^cite_note-147] The tree was a symbol of the order of creation.[^cite_note-148] Self-centeredness made Adam and Eve eat of it, thus failing to acknowledge and respect the world as it was created by God, with its hierarchy of beings and values.[^cite_note-149]

Augustine wrote that Adam and Eve would not have fallen into pride and lack of wisdom if [Satan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satan) had not sown into their senses "the root of evil" (*radix Mali*).[^cite_note-150] Their nature was wounded, according to Augustine, by [concupiscence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concupiscence) or [libido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libido), which affected human intelligence and will, as well as affections and desires, including sexual desire.[^cite_note-151] In terms of [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics), Augustine found concupiscence to be not a state of being but a bad quality, the privation of good or a wound.[^cite_note-152]

Augustine's understanding of the consequences of original sin and the necessity of redeeming grace was developed in the struggle against [Pelagius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelagius) and his [Pelagian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelagianism) disciples, [Caelestius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caelestius) and [Julian of Eclanum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_of_Eclanum),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGonzález1987-32] who had been inspired by [Rufinus of Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rufinus_of_Syria), a disciple of [Theodore of Mopsuestia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_of_Mopsuestia).[^cite_note-153][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBonner198735-154] They refused to agree original sin wounded human will and mind, insisting human nature was given the power to act, to speak, and to think when God created it. Human nature cannot lose its moral capacity for doing good, but a person is free to act or not act in a righteous way. Pelagius gave an example of eyes: they have capacity for seeing, but a person can make either good or bad use of it.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBonner1986355–356-155][^cite_note-156]

Pelagians insisted human affections and desires were not touched by the fall either. In the Pelagian view, immorality, e.g. [fornication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fornication), is exclusively a matter of will, i.e. a person does not use natural desires in a proper way. In opposition, Augustine pointed out the apparent disobedience of the flesh to the spirit, and explained it as one of the results of original sin, punishment of Adam and Eve's disobedience to God.[^cite_note-157]

Augustine had served as a "Hearer" for the Manichaeans for about nine years,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown200035-158] who taught that the original sin was [carnal knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnal_knowledge).[^cite_note-159] But his struggle to understand the cause of evil in the world started before that, at the age of nineteen.[^cite_note-160] By *malum* (evil) he understood most of all concupiscence, which he interpreted as a vice dominating people and causing in men and women moral disorder. Agostino Trapè insists Augustine's personal experience cannot be credited for his doctrine about concupiscence. He considers Augustine's marital experience to be quite normal, and even exemplary, aside from the absence of Christian wedding rites.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETrapè1987113–114-161] As J. Brachtendorf showed, Augustine used Ciceronian [Stoic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoicism) concept of passions, to interpret [Paul's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_the_Apostle) doctrine of universal sin and redemption.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrachtendorf1997307-162]



*St. Augustine* by [Peter Paul Rubens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Paul_Rubens)

The view that not only human [soul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul) but also senses were influenced by the fall of Adam and Eve was prevalent in Augustine's time among the [Fathers of the Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fathers_of_the_Church).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESfameni_Gasparro2001250–251-163][^cite_note-FOOTNOTESomers1961115-164][^cite_note-165] It is clear the reason for Augustine's distancing from the affairs of the flesh was different from that of [Plotinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plotinus), a [Neoplatonist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonist)[^cite_note-166] who taught that only through disdain for fleshly desire could one reach the ultimate state of mankind.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGerson1999203-167] Augustine taught the redemption, i.e. transformation and purification, of the body in the resurrection.[^cite_note-168]

Some authors perceive Augustine's doctrine as directed against [human sexuality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_sexuality) and attribute his insistence on continence and devotion to God as coming from his need to reject his own highly sensual nature as described in the *Confessions*.[^cite_note-170] Augustine taught that human sexuality has been wounded, together with the whole of human nature, and requires [redemption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redemption_\(theology\)) of Christ. That healing is a process realized in conjugal acts. The virtue of continence is achieved thanks to the grace of the sacrament of Christian marriage, which becomes therefore a *remedium concupiscentiae* – remedy of concupiscence.[^cite_note-171][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBurke2006481–536-172] The redemption of human sexuality will be, however, fully accomplished only in the resurrection of the body.[^cite_note-173]

Augustine also taught that the sin of Adam is inherited by all human beings. Already in his pre-Pelagian writings, Augustine taught that Original Sin is transmitted to his descendants by concupiscence,[^cite_note-174] which he regarded as the passion of both soul and body,[^cite_note-175] making humanity a *massa damnata* (mass of perdition, condemned crowd) and much enfeebling, though not destroying, the freedom of the will.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone20051200–1204-176] Although earlier Christian authors taught the elements of physical death, moral weakness, and a sin propensity within original sin, Augustine was the first to add the concept of inherited guilt (*reatus*) from Adam whereby an infant was eternally damned at birth.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilson201893,_127,_140,_146,_231–233,_279–280-177]

Although Augustine's anti-Pelagian defence of original sin was confirmed at numerous councils, i.e. [Carthage (418)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Carthage_\(418\)), [Ephesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Council_of_Ephesus) (431), [Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Orange_\(529\)) (529), [Trent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Trent) (1546) and by popes, i.e. [Pope Innocent I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Innocent_I) (401–417) and [Pope Zosimus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Zosimus) (417–418), his inherited guilt eternally damning infants was omitted by these councils and popes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilson2018221,_231,_267,_296-178] [Anselm of Canterbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anselm_of_Canterbury) established in his *[Cur Deus Homo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cur_Deus_Homo)* the definition that was followed by the great 13th-century Schoolmen, namely that Original Sin is the "privation of the righteousness which every man ought to possess," thus separating it from *concupiscence,* with which some of Augustine's disciples had identified it,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBonner1986371-179][^cite_note-FOOTNOTESouthern1978234–237-180] as later did Luther and Calvin.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone20051200–1204-176] In 1567, [Pope Pius V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Pius_V) condemned the identification of Original Sin with concupiscence.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone20051200–1204-176]

### Predestination

Main article: [Augustinian soteriology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustinian_soteriology)

Augustine taught that God orders all things while preserving human freedom.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELevering201144-181] Prior to 396, he believed [predestination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predestination) was based on God's foreknowledge of whether individuals would believe in Christ, that God's grace was "a reward for human assent".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELevering201148–49-182] Later, in response to [Pelagius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelagius), Augustine said that the sin of [pride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pride) consists in assuming "we are the ones who choose God or that God chooses us (in his foreknowledge) because of something worthy in us", and argued that God's grace causes the individual act of faith.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELevering201147–48-183]

Scholars are divided over whether Augustine's teaching implies [double predestination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_predestination), or the belief God chooses some people for damnation as well as some for salvation. Catholic scholars tend to deny he held such a view while some Protestants and secular scholars have held that Augustine did believe in double predestination.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJames1998102-184] About 412, Augustine became the first Christian to understand predestination as a divine unilateral pre-determination of individuals' eternal destinies independently of human choice, although his prior Manichaean sect did teach this concept.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWidengren197763–65,_90-185][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStroumsa1992344–345-186][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilson2018286–293-187][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEvan_Oort2010520-188] Some Protestant theologians, such as [Justo L. González](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justo_L._Gonz%C3%A1lez)[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGonzález198744-189] and [Bengt Hägglund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengt_H%C3%A4gglund),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHägglund2007139–140-31] interpret Augustine's teaching that grace is [irresistible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irresistible_grace), results in conversion, and leads to [perseverance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perseverance_of_the_saints).

In *On Rebuke and Grace* (*De correptione et gratia*), Augustine wrote: "And what is written, that He wills all men to be saved, while yet all men are not saved, may be understood in many ways, some of which I have mentioned in other writings of mine; but here I will say one thing: He wills all men to be saved, is so said that all the predestinated may be understood by it, because every kind of men is among them."[^cite_note-St._Augustine_of_Hippo-33]

Speaking of the twins Jacob and Esau, Augustine wrote in his book *On the Gift of Perseverance*, "\[I]t ought to be a most certain fact that the former is of the predestinated, the latter is not."[^cite_note-190]

### Sacramental theology



*[St. Augustine in His Study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Augustine_in_His_Study_\(Carpaccio\))* by [Vittore Carpaccio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vittore_Carpaccio), 1502

Also in reaction to the Donatists, Augustine developed a distinction between the "regularity" and "validity" of the [sacraments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrament). Regular sacraments are performed by clergy of the Catholic Church, while sacraments performed by schismatics are considered irregular. Nevertheless, the validity of the sacraments does not depend upon the holiness of the priests who perform them (*[ex opere operato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ex_opere_operato)*); therefore, irregular sacraments are still accepted as valid provided they are done in the name of Christ and in the manner prescribed by the Church. On this point, Augustine departs from the earlier teaching of [Cyprian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprian), who taught that converts from schismatic movements must be re-baptised.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGonzález1987-32] Augustine taught that sacraments administered outside the Catholic Church, though true sacraments, avail nothing. However, he also stated that baptism, while it does not confer any grace when done outside the Church, does confer grace as soon as one is received into the Catholic Church.[^cite_note-191]

Augustine believed that in a [real presence of Christ in the Eucharist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_presence_of_Christ_in_the_Eucharist), saying that Christ's statement, "This is my body" referred to the bread he carried in his hands,[^cite_note-192][^cite_note-193] and that Christians must have faith the bread and wine are in fact the body and blood of Christ, despite what they see with their eyes.[^cite_note-194] For instance, he stated that "He \[Jesus] walked here in the same flesh, and gave us the same flesh to be eaten unto salvation. But no one eats that flesh unless first he adores it; and thus it is discovered how such a footstool of the Lord's feet is adored; and not only do we not sin by adoring, we do sin by not adoring."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJurgens197020§ 1479a-195] Presbyterian professor and author John Riggs argued that Augustine held that Christ is really present in the elements of the Eucharist, but not in a bodily manner, because his body remains in [Heaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heaven_in_Christianity).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERiggs201515-196]

Against the [Pelagians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelagians), Augustine strongly stressed the importance of [infant baptism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infant_baptism). About the question whether baptism is an absolute necessity for salvation, however, Augustine appears to have refined his beliefs during his lifetime, causing some confusion among later theologians about his position. He said in one of his sermons that only the baptized are saved.[^cite_note-197] This belief was shared by many early Christians. However, a passage from his *City of God*, concerning the [Apocalypse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apocalypse), may indicate Augustine did believe in an exception for children born to Christian parents.[^cite_note-198]

### Astrology



Saint Augustine in the *[Nuremberg Chronicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuremberg_Chronicle)*&#x20;

Augustine's contemporaries often believed [astrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology) to be an exact and genuine science. Its practitioners were regarded as true men of learning and called *mathematici*. Astrology played a prominent part in Manichaean doctrine, and Augustine himself was attracted by their books in his youth, being particularly fascinated by those who claimed to foretell the future. Later, as a bishop, he warned that one should avoid astrologers who combine science and [horoscopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horoscope). (Augustine's term "mathematici", meaning "astrologers", is sometimes mistranslated as "mathematicians".) According to Augustine, they were not genuine students of [Hipparchus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hipparchus) or [Eratosthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eratosthenes) but "common swindlers".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEVan_Der_Meer196160-199][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBonner198663-200][^cite_note-FOOTNOTETestard1958100–106-201][^cite_note-202]

### Epistemology

[Epistemological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology) concerns shaped Augustine's intellectual development. His early dialogues *Contra academicos* (386) and *De Magistro* (389), both written shortly after his conversion to Christianity, reflect his engagement with sceptical arguments and show the development of his doctrine of [divine illumination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_illumination). The doctrine of illumination claims God plays an active and regular part in human perception and understanding by illuminating the mind so human beings can recognize intelligible realities God presents (as opposed to God designing the human mind to be reliable consistently, as in, for example, Descartes's idea of clear and distinct perceptions). According to Augustine, illumination is obtainable to all rational minds and is different from other forms of [sense perception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sense_perception). It is meant to be an explanation of the conditions required for the mind to have a connection with intelligible entities.[^cite_note-encyclopedia-203]

Augustine also posed the [problem of other minds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Problem_of_other_minds) throughout different works, most famously perhaps in *[On the Trinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Trinity)* (VIII.6.9), and developed what has come to be a standard solution: the argument from analogy to other minds.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMatthews1992-204] In contrast to Plato and other earlier philosophers, Augustine recognized the centrality of [testimony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_problems_of_testimony) to human knowledge and argued that what others tell us can provide knowledge even if we do not have independent reasons to believe their testimonial reports.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKingBallantyne2009195-205]

### Just war theory

See also: [Just war theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Just_war_theory)

Augustine asserted Christians should be [pacifists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacifists) as a personal, philosophical stance.[^cite_note-206] However, peacefulness in the face of a grave wrong that could only be stopped by violence would be a sin. Defence of one's self or others could be a necessity, especially when authorized by a legitimate authority. While not breaking down the conditions necessary for war to be just, Augustine coined the phrase in his work *The City of God*.[^cite_note-207] In essence, the pursuit of peace must include the option of fighting for its long-term preservation.[^cite_note-208] Such a war could not be pre-emptive, but defensive, to restore peace.[^cite_note-209] [Thomas Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas), centuries later, used the authority of Augustine's arguments in an attempt to define the conditions under which a war could be just.[^cite_note-210][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGonzalez2010-211]

### Free will

Included in Augustine's earlier [theodicy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodicy) is the claim God created humans and angels as rational beings possessing [free will](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_will). Free will was not intended for sin, meaning it is not equally predisposed to both good and evil. A will defiled by sin is not considered as "free" as it once was because it is bound by material things, which could be lost or be difficult to part with, resulting in unhappiness. Sin impairs free will, while grace restores it. Only a will that was once free can be subjected to sin's corruption.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMeisterCopan2013-212] After 412, Augustine changed his theology, teaching that humanity had no free will to believe in Christ but only a free will to sin: "I in fact strove on behalf of the free choice of the human 'will,' but God's grace conquered" (*Retract*. 2.1).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilson2018285-213]

The early Christians opposed the deterministic views (e.g., fate) of Stoics, Gnostics, and Manichaeans prevalent in the first four centuries.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcIntire20053206–3209-214] Christians championed the concept of a relational God who interacts with humans rather than a Stoic or Gnostic God who unilaterally foreordained every event (yet Stoics still claimed to teach free will).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDihle1982152-215] [Patristics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patristics) scholar Ken Wilson argues that every early Christian author with extant writings who wrote on the topic prior to Augustine of Hippo (412) advanced human free choice rather than a deterministic God.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilson201893–94,_273–274-216] According to Wilson, Augustine taught traditional free choice until 412, when he reverted to his earlier Manichaean and Stoic deterministic training when battling the Pelagians.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilson2018281–294-217] Only a few Christians accepted Augustine's view of free will until the Protestant Reformation when both Luther and Calvin embraced Augustine's deterministic teachings wholeheartedly.[^cite_note-218][^cite_note-219]

The [Catholic Church](606848.md) considers Augustine's teaching to be consistent with free will. He often said that anyone can be saved if they wish.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPortalié1907b-96] While God knows who will and will not be saved, with no possibility for the latter to be saved in their lives, this knowledge represents God's perfect knowledge of how humans will freely choose their destinies.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPortalié1907b-96]

### Ethics

#### Natural law

Augustine was among the earliest to examine the legitimacy of the laws of man, and attempt to define the boundaries of what laws and rights occur naturally, instead of being arbitrarily imposed by mortals. All who have wisdom and conscience, he concludes, are able to use reason to recognize the *lex naturalis*, [natural law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_law). Mortal law should not attempt to force people to do what is right or avoid what is wrong, but simply to remain just. Therefore "[an unjust law is no law at all](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_unjust_law_is_no_law_at_all)". People are not obligated to obey laws that are unjust, those that their conscience and reason tell them violate natural law and [rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_rights).[^cite_note-220]

#### Slavery

Augustine led many clergy under his authority at Hippo to free their slaves as a "pious and holy" act.[^cite_note-221] He boldly wrote a letter urging the emperor to set up a new law against slave traders and was very much concerned about the sale of children. Christian emperors of his time for 25 years had permitted the sale of children, not because they approved of the practice, but as a way of preventing [infanticide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infanticide) when parents were unable to care for a child. Augustine noted that the tenant farmers in particular were driven to hire out or to sell their children as a means of survival.[^cite_note-222]

In his book, *The City of God*, he presents the development of slavery as a product of sin and as contrary to God's divine plan. He wrote that God "did not intend that this rational creature, who was made in his image, should have dominion over anything but the irrational creation – not man over man, but man over the beasts". Thus he wrote that righteous men in primitive times were made shepherds of cattle, not kings over men. "The condition of slavery is the result of sin", he declared.[^cite_note-223] In *The City of God*, Augustine wrote he felt the existence of slavery was a punishment for the existence of sin, even if an individual enslaved person committed no sin meriting punishment. He wrote: "Slavery is, however, penal, and is appointed by that law which enjoins the preservation of the natural order and forbids its disturbance."[^cite_note-\:0-224] Augustine believed slavery did more harm to the slave owner than the enslaved person himself: "the lowly position does as much good to the servant as the proud position does harm to the master."[^cite_note-\:10-225] Augustine proposes as a solution to sin a type of cognitive reimagining of one's situation, where slaves "may themselves make their slavery in some sort free, by serving not in crafty fear, but in faithful love," until the end of the world eradicated slavery for good: "until all unrighteousness pass away, and all principality and every human power be brought to nothing, and God be all in all."[^cite_note-\:0-224]

#### Jews

After [Christianity became the state religion of the Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_as_the_Roman_state_religion), it was seen as the fulfillment of Old Testament prophecies and as evidence of Christianity's truth over Judaism.[^cite_note-BenYohanan-226] The [destruction of the Second Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Destruction_of_the_Second_Temple) and the resulting Jewish exile were often viewed as divine punishment for the Jewish rejection of Jesus and led to Christian thinkers to grapple with the presence of Jewish continued existence in their midst.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGarroway202199–102-227] In this context, Augustine developed a theological justification of Jewish presence within a Christian society that came to be known as the witness theory of the Jewish diaspora.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGarroway202199-228] Against certain Christian movements, some of which rejected the use of [Hebrew Scripture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Scripture), Augustine countered that God had chosen the [Jews](25955086.md) as a special people,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMacCulloch20108-229] and he considered the scattering of Jewish people by the Roman Empire to be a fulfilment of prophecy as well as punishment for their rejection of Christ.[^cite_note-230] Nevertheless, Jews should not be slain or forcibly converted because they provide Christianity with an ever-present witness to its own validity as their own scriptures, the Old Testament, foretold the coming and resurrection of Christ.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGarroway202199-228] Their blind adherence to the Old Testament were proof that Christians had not faked the claims in the New Testament, although the Jews remained blind to the truth. Especially relevant for Augustine's justification was [Psalm 59](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psalm_59)\:11 "Slay them not, lest at some time they for get your Law".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGarroway2021104-231] Augustine, who believed Jewish people would be converted to Christianity at "the end of time", argued God had allowed them to survive their dispersion as a warning to Christians; as such, he argued, they should be permitted to dwell in Christian lands.[^cite_note-232][^cite_note-233]

The sentiment sometimes attributed to Augustine that Christians should let the Jews "survive but not thrive" (it is repeated by the author [James Carroll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Carroll_\(author\)) in his book *Constantine's Sword*, for example)[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECarroll2002219-234] is apocryphal and is not found in any of his writings.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEVan_Biema2008-235] Augustine's theological justification became the Catholic Church's official policy through the endorsement of [pope Gregory the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Gregory_the_Great) in the sixth century and allowed Jews to live in relative safety among their Christian hosts up until the twelfth century.[^cite_note-BenYohanan-226][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGarroway2021105-236]

#### Sexuality and the sexes

For Augustine, the evil of sexual immorality was not in the sexual act itself, but in the emotions that typically accompany it. In *On Christian Doctrine* Augustine contrasts love, which is enjoyment on account of God, and lust, which is not on account of God.[^cite_note-237] Augustine claims that, following the Fall, sexual lust (*concupiscentia*) has become necessary for copulation (as required to stimulate male erection), sexual lust is an evil result of the Fall, and therefore, evil must inevitably accompany sexual intercourse (*On marriage and concupiscence*[^cite_note-238]). Therefore, following the Fall, even marital sex carried out merely to procreate inevitably perpetuates evil (*On marriage and concupiscence* 1.27; *A Treatise against Two Letters of the Pelagians* 2.27). For Augustine, proper love exercises a denial of selfish pleasure and the subjugation of corporeal desire to God. The only way to avoid evil caused by sexual intercourse is to take the "better" way (*Confessions* 8.2) and abstain from marriage (*On marriage and concupiscence* 1.31). Sex within marriage is not, however, for Augustine a sin, although necessarily produces the evil of sexual lust. Based on the same logic, Augustine also declared the pious virgins raped during the sack of Rome to be innocent because they did not intend to sin nor enjoy the act.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERussell1945356-239][^cite_note-240]

Before the Fall, Augustine believed sex was a passionless affair, "just like many a laborious work accomplished by the compliant operation of our other limbs, without any lascivious heat",[^cite_note-241] that the seed "might be sown without any shameful lust, the genital members simply obeying the inclination of the will".[^cite_note-242] After the Fall, by contrast, the penis cannot be controlled by mere will, subject instead to both unwanted impotence and involuntary erections. Augustine censured those who try to prevent the creation of offspring when engaging in sexual relations, saying that though they may be nominally married they are not really, but are using that designation as a cloak for turpitude.[^cite_note-243]

Augustine believed Adam and Eve had both already chosen in their hearts to disobey God's command not to eat of the Tree of Knowledge before Eve took the fruit, ate it, and gave it to Adam.[^cite_note-ReferenceC-244][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEClark1996-245] Accordingly, Augustine did not believe Adam was any less guilty of sin.[^cite_note-ReferenceC-244][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEClark1986139–162-246] Augustine praises women and their role in society and in the Church. Augustine believed that "woman has been made for man" and that "in sex she is physically subject to him in the same way as our natural impulses need to be subjected to the reasoning power of the mind, in order that the actions to which they lead may be inspired by the principles of good conduct".[^cite_note-247] Women were created as a "helper" to men for Augustine.[^cite_note-248]

#### Pedagogy



*[Saint Augustine in His Study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Augustine_in_His_Cell_\(Botticelli\))*, by [Sandro Botticelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandro_Botticelli), 1494, [Uffizi Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uffizi_Gallery)

Augustine is considered an influential figure in the history of education. A work early in Augustine's writings is *De Magistro* (On the Teacher), which contains insights into education. His ideas changed as he found better directions or better ways of expressing his ideas. In the last years of his life, Augustine wrote his *Retractationes* (*Retractations*), reviewing his writings and improving specific texts. Henry Chadwick believes an accurate translation of "retractationes" may be "reconsiderations". Reconsiderations can be seen as an overarching theme of the way Augustine learned. Augustine's understanding of the search for understanding, meaning, and truth as a restless journey leaves room for doubt, development, and change.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcCloskey2008-249]

Augustine was a strong advocate of [critical thinking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_thinking) skills. Because written works were limited during this time, spoken communication of knowledge was very important. His emphasis on the importance of community as a means of learning distinguishes his pedagogy from some others. Augustine believed dialectic is the best means for learning and that this method should serve as a model for learning encounters between teachers and students. Augustine's dialogue writings model the need for lively interactive dialogue among learners.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcCloskey2008-249] He recommended adapting educational practices to fit the students' educational backgrounds:

- the student who has been well-educated by knowledgeable teachers;

- the student who has had no education; and

- the student who has had a poor education, but believes himself to be well-educated.

If a student has been well educated in a wide variety of subjects, the teacher must be careful not to repeat what they have already learned, but to challenge the student with material they do not yet know thoroughly. With the student who has had no education, the teacher must be patient, willing to repeat things until the student understands, and sympathetic. Perhaps the most difficult student, however, is the one with an inferior education who believes he understands something when he does not. Augustine stressed the importance of showing this type of student the difference between "having words and having understanding"[^cite_note-250] and of helping the student to remain humble with his acquisition of knowledge.

Under the influence of [Bede](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bede), [Alcuin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcuin), and [Rabanus Maurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabanus_Maurus), *De catechizandis rudibus* came to exercise an important role in the education of clergy at the monastic schools, especially from the eighth century onwards.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHowie1969150–153-251]

Augustine believed students should be given an opportunity to apply learned theories to practical experience. Yet another of Augustine's major contributions to education is his study on the styles of teaching. He claimed there are two basic styles a teacher uses when speaking to the students: *mixed* and *grand*. The *mixed style* includes complex and sometimes showy language to help students see the artistry of the subject they are studying, whereas the *grand style* is not considered quite as elegant. Augustine balanced his teaching philosophy with the traditional [Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible)-based practice of strict discipline.

Augustine knew [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) and [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek). He had a long correspondence with St Jerome regarding the textual differences existing between the [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible) and the Greek [Septuagint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Septuagint), concluding that the original Greek manuscripts were closely similar to the other Hebrew ones, and also that even the differences in the two original versions of the Holy Scripture could enlight its spiritual meaning to have been unitarily inspired by God.[^cite_note-252]

Augustine's theological views extended into the centuries that came after his life and were very influential throughout various religious clergy in Europe, and eventually those in the New World. In the American colonies his teachings were foundational to the religious precepts among various prominent figures in [The First Great Awakening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_First_Great_Awakening) of the eighteenth century, including [Jonathan Edwards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Edwards_\(theologian\)) and [George Whitefield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Whitefield).[^cite_note-253] [John Wesley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wesley), principle founder of the [Methodist Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodist_Church), also drew heavily on Augustine's teachings.[^cite_note-254]

#### Coercion

Augustine of Hippo is one of the very few authors in Antiquity to theoretically examine the ideas of religious freedom and coercion.[^cite_note-Brown1964-255]<sup>: 107 </sup> Augustine handled the infliction of punishment and the exercise of power over law-breakers by analyzing these issues in ways similar to modern debates on penal reform.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown1964115-256] His teaching on coercion has "embarrassed his modern defenders and vexed his modern detractors,"[^cite_note-Markus-257]<sup>: 116 </sup> because it is seen as making him appear "to generations of religious liberals as *le prince et patriarche de persecuteurs.*"[^cite_note-Brown1964-255]<sup>: 107 </sup> Brown asserts that, at the same time, Augustine becomes "an eloquent advocate of the ideal of corrective punishment" and reformation of the wrongdoer.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown1964116-258] Russell says Augustine's theory of coercion "was not crafted from dogma, but in response to a unique historical situation" and is, therefore, context-dependent, while others see it as inconsistent with his other teachings.[^cite_note-Russell2-259]<sup>: 125 </sup>

##### Context

During the [Great Persecution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocletianic_persecution), "When Roman soldiers came calling, some of the \[Catholic] officials handed over the sacred books, vessels, and other church goods rather than risk legal penalties" over a few objects.[^cite_note-Tilley1996-260]<sup>: ix </sup> Maureen Tilley says this was a problem by 305, that became a schism by 311, because many of the North African Christians had a long established tradition of a "physicalist approach to religion."[^cite_note-Tilley1996-260]<sup>: xv </sup> The sacred scriptures were not simply books to them, but were the Word of God in physical form, therefore they saw handing over the Bible, and handing over a person to be martyred, as "two sides of the same coin."[^cite_note-Tilley1996-260]<sup>: ix </sup> Those who cooperated with the authorities became known as *traditores.* The term originally meant *one who hands over a physical object*, but it came to mean "traitor".[^cite_note-Tilley1996-260]<sup>: ix </sup>

According to Tilley, after the persecution ended, those who had apostatized wanted to return to their positions in the church.[^cite_note-Tilley1996-260]<sup>: xiv </sup> The North African Christians, the rigorists who became known as Donatists, refused to accept them.[^cite_note-Tilley1996-260]<sup>: ix, x </sup> Catholics were more tolerant and wanted to wipe the slate clean.[^cite_note-Cameron1993-261]<sup>: xiv, 69 </sup> For the next 75 years, both parties existed, often directly alongside each other, with a double line of bishops for the same cities.[^cite_note-Tilley1996-260]<sup>: xv </sup> Competition for the loyalty of the people included multiple new churches and violence.[^cite_note-262]<sup>: 334 </sup> No one is exactly sure when the [Circumcellions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcellions) and the Donatists allied, but for decades, they fomented protests and street violence, accosted travellers and attacked random Catholics without warning, often doing serious and unprovoked bodily harm such as beating people with clubs, cutting off their hands and feet, and gouging out eyes.[^cite_note-Frend1-263]<sup>: 172, 173, 222, 242, 254 </sup>

Augustine became [coadjutor Bishop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coadjutor_Bishop) of Hippo in 395, and since he believed that conversion must be voluntary, his appeals to the Donatists were verbal. For several years, he used popular propaganda, debate, personal appeal, General Councils, appeals to the emperor and political pressure to bring the Donatists back into union with the Catholics, but all attempts failed.[^cite_note-Frend1-263]<sup>: 242, 254 </sup> The harsh realities Augustine faced can be found in his Letter 28 written to bishop Novatus around 416. Donatists had attacked, cut out the tongue and cut off the hands of a Bishop Rogatus who had recently converted to Catholicism. An unnamed count of Africa had sent his agent with Rogatus, and he too had been attacked; the count was "inclined to pursue the matter."[^cite_note-Markus-257]<sup>: 120 </sup> Russell says Augustine demonstrates a "hands-on" involvement with the details of his bishopric, but at one point in the letter, he confesses he does not know what to do. "All the issues that plague him are there: stubborn Donatists, Circumcellion violence, the vacillating role of secular officials, the imperative to persuade, and his own trepidations."[^cite_note-Markus-257]<sup>: 120, 121 </sup> The empire responded to the civil unrest with the law and its enforcement, and thereafter, Augustine changed his mind about using verbal arguments alone. Instead, he came to support the state's use of coercion.[^cite_note-Brown1964-255]<sup>: 107–116 </sup> Augustine did not believe the empire's enforcement would "make the Donatists more virtuous" but he did believe it would make them "less vicious."[^cite_note-Russell2-259]<sup>: 128 </sup>

##### Theology

The primary 'proof-text' of what Augustine thought concerning coercion is from Letter 93, written in 408, as a reply to bishop Vincentius, of Cartenna (Mauretania, North Africa). This letter shows that both practical and biblical reasons led Augustine to defend the legitimacy of coercion. He confesses that he changed his mind because of "the ineffectiveness of dialogue and the proven efficacy of laws."[^cite_note-Marcos-264]<sup>: 3 </sup> He had been worried about false conversions if force was used, but "now," he says, "it seems imperial persecution is working." Many Donatists had converted.[^cite_note-Russell2-259]<sup>: 116 </sup> "Fear had made them reflect, and made them docile."[^cite_note-Marcos-264]<sup>: 3 </sup> Augustine continued to assert that coercion could not directly convert someone, but concluded it could make a person ready to be reasoned with.[^cite_note-Park-265]<sup>: 103–121 </sup>

According to Mar Marcos, Augustine made use of several biblical examples to legitimize coercion, but the primary analogy in Letter 93 and in Letter 185, is the parable of the Great Feast in Luke 14.15–24 and its statement *compel them to come in.*[^cite_note-Marcos-264]<sup>: 1 </sup> Russell says, Augustine uses the Latin term *cogo*, instead of the *compello* of the Vulgate, since to Augustine, *cogo* meant to "gather together" or "collect" and was not simply "compel by physical force."[^cite_note-Russell2-259]<sup>: 121 </sup>



*Saint Augustine of Hippo*, Gerard Seghers

In 1970, Robert Markus[^cite_note-266] argued that, for Augustine, a degree of external pressure being brought for the purpose of reform was compatible with the exercise of free will.[^cite_note-Markus-257] Russell asserts that *Confessions 13* is crucial to understanding Augustine's thought on coercion; using Peter Brown's explanation of Augustine's view of salvation, he explains that Augustine's past, his own sufferings and "conversion through God's pressures," along with his biblical hermeneutics, is what led him to see the value in suffering for discerning truth.[^cite_note-Russell2-259]<sup>: 116–117 </sup> According to Russell, Augustine saw coercion as one among many conversion strategies for forming "a pathway to the inner person."[^cite_note-Russell2-259]<sup>: 119 </sup>

In Augustine's view, there is such a thing as just and unjust persecution. Augustine explains that when the purpose of persecution is to lovingly correct and instruct, then it becomes discipline and is just.[^cite_note-Marcos-264]<sup>: 2 </sup> He said the church would discipline its people out of a loving desire to heal them, and that, "once compelled to come in, heretics would gradually give their voluntary assent to the truth of Christian orthodoxy."[^cite_note-Russell2-259]<sup>: 115 </sup> Frederick H. Russell[^cite_note-Russell-267] describes this as "a pastoral strategy in which the church did the persecuting with the dutiful assistance of Roman authorities,"[^cite_note-Russell2-259]<sup>: 115 </sup> adding that it is "a precariously balanced blend of external discipline and inward nurturance."[^cite_note-Russell2-259]<sup>: 125 </sup>

Augustine placed limits on the use of coercion, recommending fines, imprisonment, banishment, and moderate floggings, preferring beatings with rods which was a common practice in the ecclesial courts.[^cite_note-Hughes-268]<sup>: 164 </sup> He opposed severity, maiming, and the execution of heretics.[^cite_note-269] While these limits were mostly ignored by Roman authorities, Michael Lamb says that in doing this, "Augustine appropriates republican principles from his Roman predecessors..." and maintains his commitment to liberty, legitimate authority, and the rule of law as a constraint on arbitrary power. He continues to advocate holding authority accountable to prevent domination but affirms the state's right to act.[^cite_note-270]

[Herbert A. Deane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_A._Deane),[^cite_note-271] on the other hand, says there is a fundamental inconsistency between Augustine's political thought and "his final position of approval of the use of political and legal weapons to punish religious dissidence" and others have seconded this view.[^cite_note-272] Brown asserts that Augustine's thinking on coercion is more of an attitude than a doctrine since it is "not in a state of rest," but is instead marked by "a painful and protracted attempt to embrace and resolve tensions."[^cite_note-Brown1964-255]<sup>: 107 </sup>

According to Russell, it is possible to see how Augustine himself had evolved from his earlier *Confessions* to this teaching on coercion and the latter's strong patriarchal nature: "Intellectually, the burden has shifted imperceptibly from discovering the truth to disseminating the truth."[^cite_note-Russell2-259]<sup>: 129 </sup> The bishops had become the church's elite with their own rationale for acting as "stewards of the truth." Russell points out that Augustine's views are limited to time and place and his own community, but later, others took what he said and applied it outside those parameters in ways Augustine never imagined or intended.[^cite_note-Russell2-259]<sup>: 129 </sup>

## Works

Main article: [Augustine of Hippo bibliography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo_bibliography)



*Saint Augustine*, painting by Antonio Rodríguez

Augustine was one of the most prolific Latin authors in terms of surviving works, and the list of his works consists of more than one hundred separate titles.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWrightSinclair193156–-273] They include [apologetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apologetics) works against the heresies of the [Arians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arianism), [Donatists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donatists), [Manichaeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manichaeans) and [Pelagians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelagians); texts on Christian [doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctrine), notably *[De Doctrina Christiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Doctrina_Christiana)* (*On Christian Doctrine*); [exegetical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exegesis) works such as commentaries on [Genesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Genesis), the [Psalms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psalms) and [Paul's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_of_Tarsus) [Letter to the Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letter_to_the_Romans); many [sermons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sermon) and [letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letter_\(message\)); and the *Retractationes*, a review of his earlier works which he wrote near the end of his life.

Apart from those, Augustine is probably best known for his *[Confessions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confessions_\(Augustine\))*, which is a personal account of his earlier life, and for *De civitate Dei* (*[The City of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_City_of_God)*, consisting of 22 books), which he wrote to restore the confidence of his fellow Christians, which was badly shaken by the [sack of Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sack_of_Rome_\(410\)) by the [Visigoths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visigoths) in 410. His *[On the Trinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Trinity)*, in which he developed what has become known as the 'psychological analogy' of the [Trinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity), is also considered to be among his masterpieces, and arguably of more [doctrinal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctrinal) importance than the *Confessions* or the *City of God*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHill1961540–548-274] He also wrote *On Free Choice of the Will* (*[De libero arbitrio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_libero_arbitrio_\(Augustine\))*), addressing why God gives humans free will that can be used for evil.

## Legacy



*Saint Augustine Disputing with the Heretics*, painting by Vergós Group

In both his philosophical and theological reasoning, Augustine was greatly influenced by [Stoicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoicism), [Platonism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonism) and [Neoplatonism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonism), particularly by the work of [Plotinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plotinus), author of the *[Enneads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enneads)*, probably through the mediation of [Porphyry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porphyry_\(philosopher\)) and [Victorinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaius_Marius_Victorinus) (as [Pierre Hadot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Hadot) has argued). Some Neoplatonic concepts are still visible in Augustine's early writings.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERussell1945Book_II,_Chapter_IV-275] His early and influential writing on the [human will](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will_\(philosophy\)), a central topic in [ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics), would become a focus for later philosophers such as [Schopenhauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Schopenhauer), [Kierkegaard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%B8ren_Kierkegaard), and [Nietzsche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Nietzsche). He was also influenced by the works of [Virgil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgil) (known for his teaching on language), and [Cicero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cicero) (known for his teaching on argument).[^cite_note-encyclopedia-203] Augustine, along with [Ambrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambrose), [Jerome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerome) and pope [Gregory the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregory_the_Great), is considered one of the four Great Latin [Church Fathers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_Fathers).[^cite_note-276]

### In philosophy

[Philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher) [Bertrand Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertrand_Russell) was impressed by Augustine's meditation on the nature of time in the *Confessions*, comparing it favourably to [Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant)'s version of the view that time is subjective.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERussell1945352–353-277] Catholic theologians generally subscribe to Augustine's belief that God exists [outside of time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eternity) in the "eternal present"; that time only exists within the created universe because only in space is time discernible through motion and change. His meditations on the nature of time are closely linked to his consideration of the human ability of [memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory). [Frances Yates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frances_Yates) in her 1966 study *[The Art of Memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Art_of_Memory)* argues that a brief passage of the *Confessions*, 10.8.12, in which Augustine writes of walking up a flight of stairs and entering the vast fields of memory[^cite_note-278] clearly indicates that the ancient Romans were aware of how to use explicit spatial and architectural metaphors as a [mnemonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mnemonic) technique for organizing large amounts of information.

Augustine's philosophical method, especially demonstrated in his *Confessions*, had a continuing influence on Continental philosophy throughout the 20th century. His descriptive approach to intentionality, memory, and language as these phenomena are experienced within consciousness and time anticipated and inspired the insights of modern [phenomenology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenology_\(philosophy\)) and [hermeneutics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermeneutics).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDe_Paulo2006-279] [Edmund Husserl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Husserl) writes: "The analysis of time-consciousness is an age-old crux of descriptive psychology and theory of knowledge. The first thinker to be deeply sensitive to the immense difficulties to be found here was Augustine, who laboured almost to despair over this problem."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHusserl201921-280]

[Martin Heidegger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Heidegger) refers to Augustine's descriptive philosophy at several junctures in his influential work *[Being and Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Being_and_Time)*.[^cite_note-281] [Hannah Arendt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hannah_Arendt) began her philosophical writing with a dissertation on Augustine's concept of love, *Der Liebesbegriff bei Augustin* (1929): "The young Arendt attempted to show that the philosophical basis for *vita socialis* in Augustine can be understood as residing in neighbourly love, grounded in his understanding of the common origin of humanity."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEChiba1995507-282]

Jean Bethke Elshtain in *Augustine and the Limits of Politics* tried to associate Augustine with Arendt in their concept of evil: "Augustine did not see evil as glamorously demonic but rather as absence of good, something which paradoxically is really nothing. Arendt ... envisioned even the extreme evil which produced [the Holocaust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Holocaust) as merely banal \[in *[Eichmann in Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eichmann_in_Jerusalem)*]."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETinder1997432–433-283] Augustine's philosophical legacy continues to influence contemporary critical theory through the contributions and inheritors of these 20th-century figures. Seen from a historical perspective, there are three main perspectives on the political thought of Augustine: first, political Augustinianism; second, Augustinian [political theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_theology); and third, Augustinian political theory.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWoo2015421–441-284]

The historian [Diarmaid MacCulloch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diarmaid_MacCulloch) has written: "Augustine's impact on Western Christian thought can hardly be overstated; only his beloved example, [Paul of Tarsus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_the_Apostle), has been more influential, and Westerners have generally seen Paul through Augustine's eyes."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMacCulloch2010319-285]

[Thomas Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas) was influenced heavily by Augustine. On the topic of original sin, Aquinas proposed a more optimistic view of man than that of Augustine in that his conception leaves to the reason, will, and passions of fallen man their natural powers even after the Fall, without "supernatural gifts".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone20051203-286] While in his pre-Pelagian writings Augustine taught that Adam's guilt as transmitted to his descendants much enfeebles, though does not destroy, the freedom of their will, Protestant reformers Martin Luther and John Calvin affirmed that Original Sin completely destroyed liberty (see [total depravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_depravity)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECrossLivingstone20051200–1204-176]

According to [Leo Ruickbie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Ruickbie), Augustine's arguments against [magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_\(paranormal\)), differentiating it from a miracle, were crucial in the early Church's fight against [paganism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paganism) and became a central thesis in the later denunciation of witches and [witchcraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witchcraft). According to Professor Deepak Lal, Augustine's vision of the heavenly city has influenced the secular projects and traditions of the [Enlightenment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment), [Marxism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism), [Freudianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freudianism) and eco-fundamentalism.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELal2002-287] Post-Marxist philosophers Antonio Negri and Michael Hardt rely heavily on Augustine's thoughts, particularly *The City of God*, in their book of political philosophy *Empire*.

Augustine has influenced many modern-day theologians and authors such as [John Piper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Piper_\(theologian\)). [Hannah Arendt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hannah_Arendt), an influential 20th-century political theorist, wrote her doctoral dissertation in philosophy on Augustine, and continued to rely on his thought throughout her career. [Ludwig Wittgenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Wittgenstein) extensively quotes Augustine in *[Philosophical Investigations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_Investigations)* for his approach to language, both admiringly, and as a sparring partner to develop his own ideas, including an extensive opening passage from the *[Confessions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confessions_\(Augustine\))*. Contemporary linguists have argued that Augustine has significantly influenced the thought of [Ferdinand de Saussure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_de_Saussure), who did not 'invent' the modern discipline of [semiotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiotics), but rather built upon [Aristotelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotelianism) and Neoplatonic knowledge from the Middle Ages, via an Augustinian connection: "as for the constitution of Saussurian semiotic theory, the importance of the Augustinian thought contribution (correlated to the Stoic one) has also been recognized. Saussure did not do anything but reform an ancient theory in Europe, according to the modern conceptual exigencies."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMunteanu199665-288]

[Pope Benedict XVI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XVI), in his autobiographical book *Milestones*, notes that Augustine was one of the deepest influences in his thought. [Pope Francis](1687680.md) suggests that Augustine's reflections on the relationship between the "[beloved disciple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beloved_disciple)" and Jesus ([John 13\:23](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_13#Verse_23): "reclining next to him") contributed to the development of devotion to the [Sacred Heart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacred_Heart),[^cite_note-289] and [Pope Leo XIV](54520372.md), a member of the Augustinian order, refers to his patron as "a vigilant pastor and theologian of rare insight".[^cite_note-290]
