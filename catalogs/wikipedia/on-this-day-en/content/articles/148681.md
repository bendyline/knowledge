**Life imprisonment** (or a **life sentence**) is any [sentence](870351.md) of [imprisonment](213806.md) in which the convicted individual will remain incarcerated for the rest of their natural life (or until [pardoned](288593.md) or [commuted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutation_\(law\)) to a fixed term), with or without the possibility of release. Crimes that are punishable by life imprisonment are considered extremely serious and usually violent. Examples of these crimes are [murder](20171.md), [torture](47702.md), [terrorism](30636.md), [child abuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_abuse) [resulting in death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_manslaughter), [rape](7980471.md), [espionage](10501.md), [treason](31292.md), [illegal drug trade](218449.md), [human trafficking](19333662.md), severe [fraud](58790.md) and [financial crimes](11656148.md), [aggravated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aggravation_\(law\)) [property damage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Property_damage), [arson](184695.md), [hate crime](13547.md), [kidnapping](169376.md), [burglary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burglary), [robbery](54849.md), [piracy](50715.md), [aircraft hijacking](2075.md), [genocide](12441.md), [war crimes](48626.md), and [crimes against humanity](61099.md).

## Law passes

Common law murder is a crime for which life imprisonment is mandatory in several countries, including some states of the [United States](3434750.md). Life imprisonment (as a maximum term) can also be imposed, in certain countries, for traffic offences causing death.[^cite_note-1] Life imprisonment is not used in all countries; [Portugal](23033.md) was the first country to abolish life imprisonment, in 1884, and is the only country in the world that considers this type of punishment for the duration of a convict's natural life – both for [minors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_\(law\)) and adults, with or without the possibility of [parole](342305.md) – a violation of human rights.[^cite_note-2] All other [Portuguese-speaking countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese-speaking_countries) and [Macao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macao) also have maximum imprisonment lengths, as do all [Spanish-speaking countries in the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispanic_America) except for Cuba, Peru, Argentina, Chile and the Mexican state of [Chihuahua](23962301.md). Other countries that do not practice life sentences include Mongolia in Asia and Norway, Iceland, Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Slovenia, Andorra and Montenegro in Europe. Where life imprisonment is a possible sentence, there may also exist formal mechanisms for requesting parole after a certain period of prison time. This means that a convict could be entitled to spend the rest of the sentence (until that individual dies) outside prison. Early release is usually conditional on past and future conduct, possibly with certain restrictions or obligations. In contrast, when a fixed term of imprisonment has ended, the convict is free. The length of time served and the conditions surrounding parole vary. Being eligible for parole does not necessarily ensure that parole will be granted. In some countries, including Sweden, parole does not exist but a life sentence may – after a successful application – be commuted to a fixed-term sentence, after which the offender is released as if the sentence served was that originally imposed.

In some countries around the world, courts have been given the authority to pass prison terms that may amount to *de facto* life imprisonment, meaning that the sentence would last longer than the human life expectancy. For example, in one American case a court imposed a sentence of life plus 1,000 years.[^cite_note-3] Courts in South Africa have handed out at least two sentences that have exceeded a century, while in [Tasmania](29944.md), Australia, [Martin Bryant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Bryant), the perpetrator of the [Port Arthur massacre](39737448.md) in 1996, received 35 life sentences plus 1,035 years without parole. In the United States, [James Holmes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Holmes_\(mass_murderer\)), the perpetrator of the [2012 Aurora theater shooting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Aurora_theater_shooting), received 12 consecutive life sentences plus 3,318 years without the possibility of parole.[^cite_note-4] In the case of mass murder in the US, Parkland mass murderer [Nikolas Cruz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolas_Cruz) was sentenced to 34 consecutive terms of life imprisonment (without parole) for murdering 17 people and injuring another 17 at a school.[^cite_note-5] Any sentence without parole effectively means a sentence cannot be suspended; a life sentence without parole, therefore, means that in the absence of circumstances such as [pardon](288593.md), [amnesty](18940624.md) or [humanitarian grounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compassionate_release) (e.g. imminent death), the prisoner will spend the rest of their natural life in prison.

In several countries where *de facto* life terms are used, a release on humanitarian grounds (also known as compassionate release) is commonplace, such as in the case of [Abdelbaset al-Megrahi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdelbaset_al-Megrahi). Since the behaviour of a prisoner serving a life sentence without parole is not relevant to the execution of such sentence, many people among lawyers, penitentiary specialists, [criminologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminology), but most of all among [human rights organizations](14923539.md) oppose that punishment. In particular, they emphasize that when faced with a prisoner with no hope of ever being released, the prison has no means to discipline such a prisoner effectively. The [European Court of Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Court_of_Human_Rights) (ECtHR) has considered the issue of life imprisonment without the possibility of parole, particularly in relation to Article 3 of the [European Convention on Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Convention_on_Human_Rights), which prohibits inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment. The Court has ruled that *irreducible* life sentences (i.e. an imprisonment for life-regime without parole) violate Article 3. However, the Court has also stated that life sentences can be imposed without breaching Article 3 if there are guarantees of review and release.[^cite_note-6]

In some countries, young adults (18-21) may be able to receive life sentences without the possibility of parole. The use of those sentences has been the subject of continuous ethical, scientific, and legal debate. There has been research in developmental neuroscience[^cite_note-\:0-7] on maturity levels in the adolescent brain. Most of the research signifies that adolescent regions of the brain involved in impulse control and judgment are not developed to the extent they need to be to make sound decisions. These findings bring up discussions about how appropriate it is to send youth to life without parole.[^cite_note-\:0-7]

A few countries allow for a minor to be given a life sentence without parole; these include but are not limited to: Antigua and Barbuda, Argentina (only over the age of 16),[^cite_note-8] Australia, Belize, Brunei, Cuba, Dominica, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, the Solomon Islands, Sri Lanka, and the United States. According to a [University of San Francisco School of Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_San_Francisco_School_of_Law) study, only the US had minors serving such sentences in 2008.[^cite_note-9] In 2009, [Human Rights Watch](46530.md) estimated that there were 2,589 youth offenders serving life sentences without the possibility for parole in the US.[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11] Since the start of 2020, that number has fallen to 1,465.[^cite_note-12][^cite_note-13] The United States has the highest population of prisoners serving life sentences for both adults and minors, at a rate of 50 people per 100,000 (1 out of 2,000) residents imprisoned for life.[^cite_note-14]

## By country

In several countries, life imprisonment has been effectively abolished. Many of the countries whose governments have abolished both life imprisonment and indefinite imprisonment have been culturally influenced or colonized by [Spain](26667.md) or [Portugal](23033.md) and have written such prohibitions into their current constitutional laws (including Portugal itself but not Spain).[^cite_note-rtve-15][^cite_note-efe-16]

### Detailed analysis

#### Europe

A number of European countries have abolished all forms of indefinite imprisonment. [Croatia](5573.md) set maximum prison sentence at 40 years (50 years in exceptional circumstances). In the [Bosnia and Herzegovina](3463.md) the maximum prison sentence is set at 45 years on federal level, in [Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federation_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) and [Brčko Distrikt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Br%C4%8Dko_District), while [Republika Srpska](347422.md), which previously also set maximum prison term at 45 years, has reinstated a life sentence. Portugal abolished all forms of life imprisonment with the [prison reforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prison_reform) of Sampaio e Melo in 1884 and has a maximum sentence of 25 years.[^cite_note-105][^cite_note-106]

Life imprisonment in Spain was abolished in 1928, but reinstated in 2015 and upheld by the [Constitutional Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_Court_of_Spain) in 2021.[^cite_note-rtve-15][^cite_note-efe-16][^cite_note-pais-107] [Serbia](29265.md) previously had a maximum prison sentence of 40 years; life imprisonment was instated in 2019 by amendments to the country's criminal code, alongside a [three-strikes law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-strikes_law).[^cite_note-Krivični_zakonik-108] In [Kosovo](17391.md), life imprisonment was also reinstated in 2019.[^cite_note-109]

##### Recent reforms and human rights perspectives

In [Europe](9239.md), there are many jurisdictions where the law expressly provides for life sentences without the possibility of parole. These are [England and Wales](275187.md) (within the [United Kingdom](31717.md); see [Life imprisonment in England and Wales](3275619.md)), the [Netherlands](21148.md), [Moldova](19260.md), [Bulgaria](3415.md),[^cite_note-110] [Italy](14532.md) (only for persons who refuse to cooperate with authorities and are sentenced for [mafia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mafia) activities or [terrorism](30636.md)), [Ukraine](31750.md), [Turkey](11125639.md) and [Serbia](29265.md).[^cite_note-Krivični_zakonik-108]

In [Sweden](5058739.md), although the law does not expressly provide for life without the possibility of release, some convicted persons may never be released, on the grounds that they are too dangerous. In [Italy](14532.md), persons who refuse to cooperate with authorities and are sentenced for mafia activities or terrorism are ineligible for parole and thus will spend the rest of their lives in prison. In Austria, life imprisonment will mean imprisonment for the remainder of the offender's life unless clemency is granted by the [President of Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_Austria) or it can be assumed that the convicted person will not commit any further crimes; the probationary period is ten years.[^cite_note-111] In Malta, prior to 2018, there was no possibility of parole for any person sentenced to life imprisonment, and any form of release from a life sentence was only possible by clemency granted by the [President of Malta](348075.md). In [France](5843419.md), while the law does not expressly provide for life imprisonment without any possibility of parole, a court can rule in exceptionally serious circumstances that convicts are ineligible for automatic parole consideration after 30 years if convicted of child murder involving rape or torture, premeditated murder of a state official or terrorism resulting in death. In [Moldova](19260.md), there is never a possibility of parole for anyone sentenced to life imprisonment, as life imprisonment is defined as the "deprivation of liberty of the convict for the entire rest of his/her life". Where mercy is granted in relation to a person serving life imprisonment, imprisonment thereof must not be less than 30 years. In [Ukraine](31750.md), life imprisonment means for the rest of one's life with the only possibilities for release being a terminal illness or a presidential pardon.[^cite_note-112] In Albania, while no person sentenced to life imprisonment is eligible for standard parole, a conditional release is still possible if the prisoner is found not likely to re-offend and has displayed good behaviour, and has served at least 25 years.

Before 2016 in the [Netherlands](21148.md), there was never a possibility of parole for any person sentenced to life imprisonment, and any form of release for life convicted in the country was only possible when granted royal decree by the [King of the Netherlands](23416611.md), with the last granting of a pardon taking place in 1986 when a terminally ill convict was released. As of 1970, the Dutch monarch has pardoned a total of three convicts. Although there is no possibility of parole eligibility, since 2016 prisoners sentenced to life imprisonment in the Netherlands are eligible to have their cases reviewed after serving at least 25 years. This change in law was because the [European Court of Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Court_of_Human_Rights) stated in 2013 that lifelong imprisonment without the chance of being released is inhumane.[^cite_note-113]

Even in other European countries that do provide for life without parole, courts continue to retain judicial discretion to decide whether a sentence of life should include parole or not. In [Albania](738.md), the decision of whether or not a life-convicted person is eligible for parole is up to the prison complex after 25 years have been served, and release eligibility depends on the prospect of [rehabilitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rehabilitation_\(penology\)) and how likely they are to re-offend. In Europe, only Ukraine and Moldova explicitly exclude parole or any form of sentence commutation for life sentences in all cases.

#### South America

In South and Central America, Honduras, Nicaragua, Costa Rica, Venezuela, Colombia, Uruguay, Bolivia, Ecuador, and the Dominican Republic have all abolished life imprisonment. The maximum sentence is 60 years in Colombia, 50 years in Costa Rica and Panama, 40 years in Honduras and Brazil,[^cite_note-114] 30 years in Nicaragua, Bolivia, Uruguay, Venezuela and the Dominican Republic, and 25 years in Paraguay and Ecuador.

Countries in the region where life imprisonment is a legal sentence include Argentina,[^cite_note-115][^cite_note-116] Guyana,[^cite_note-117] Peru,[^cite_note-118] and El Salvador, which introduced the maximum sentence of life amid [its gang crackdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvadoran_gang_crackdown).[^cite_note-119][^cite_note-120]

#### Canada

This section is an excerpt from [Life imprisonment in Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_imprisonment_in_Canada).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Life_imprisonment_in_Canada\&action=edit)]

[Life imprisonment in Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_imprisonment_in_Canada) is a criminal sentence for certain offences that lasts for the offender’s life. Parole is possible, but even if paroled, the offender remains under the supervision of [Corrections Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correctional_Service_of_Canada) for their lifetime, and can be returned to prison for parole violations.

A person serving a life sentence must serve for a certain length of time before becoming eligible for parole. First degree murder and high treason carry the longest period of parole ineligibility in the *[Criminal Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_Code_\(Canada\))*, at 25 years. A statutory amendment to allow periods of parole ineligibility greater than 25 years was held to be unconstitutional by the [Supreme Court of Canada](123468.md) in *[R v Bissonnette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R_v_Bissonnette)* (2022 SCC 23), as contrary to [section 12 of the *Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Section_12_of_the_Canadian_Charter_of_Rights_and_Freedoms), which prohibits cruel and unusual punishment. Parole eligibility for second degree murder typically varies between 10 and 25 years, and is set by the sentencing judge.

A life sentence is the most severe punishment for any crime in Canada. Criminal laws are enacted by the Parliament of Canada and apply uniformly across the country.[^cite_note-Life_imprisonment_in_Canada_auto1-121]

#### El Salvador

On March 17, 2026, the [Legislative Assembly of El Salvador](1608500.md) approved a constitutional amendment to allow life imprisonment for [murder](20171.md), [rape](7980471.md), and [acts of terrorism](30636.md). Specifically, the second paragraph of Article 27 of the Constitution of the Republic of [El Salvador](9356.md) was amended, stating that "life imprisonment shall only be imposed on murders, rapists and terrorists".[^cite_note-122] This following reform allowed children under the age of 18 to be imprisoned, with many questioning the ethics of this decision including [UNICEF](19698110.md).[^cite_note-123]

#### Philippines

Life imprisonment is a penalty imposed in the Philippines where a convict is imprisoned indefinitely. It is often confused for *[reclusión perpetua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reclusi%C3%B3n_perpetua)* which entails imprisonment of at least 20 years and one day to a maximum of 40 years, after which the convicted would be eligible for parole.[^cite_note-124]

#### United States

Main article: [Life imprisonment in the United States](15549479.md)

**Legal Framework and Key Court Rulings**

In 2011, the [Supreme Court of the United States](31737.md) ruled that sentencing minors to life without parole, automatically (as the result of a statute) or as the result of a judicial decision, for crimes other than intentional homicide, violated the [Eighth Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eighth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution)'s ban on "[Cruel and unusual punishments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cruel_and_unusual_punishment)", in the case of *[Graham v. Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_v._Florida)*.[^cite_note-125]



Mugshot of [Burton Phillips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burton_Phillips), sentenced to life imprisonment for bank robbery, 1935

*Graham v. Florida* was a significant case in juvenile justice. In [Jacksonville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacksonville,_Florida), Florida, Terrence J. Graham tried to rob a restaurant along with three adolescent accomplices. During the robbery, one of Graham's accomplices had a metal bar that he used to hit the restaurant manager twice in the head. Once arrested, Graham was charged with attempted armed robbery and armed burglary with assault/battery. The maximum sentence he faced for these charges was life without the possibility of parole, and the prosecutor wanted to charge him as an adult. During the trial, Graham pleaded guilty to the charges, resulting in three years of probation, one year of which had to be served in jail. Since he had been awaiting trial in jail, he already served six months and, therefore, was released after six additional months.[^cite_note-Drinan,_C.H._2012-126]

Within six months of his release, Graham was involved in another robbery. Since he violated the conditions of his probation, his probation officer reported to the trial court about his probation violations a few weeks before Graham turned 18 years old. It was a different judge presiding over his trial for the probation violations a year later. While Graham denied any involvement in the robbery, he did admit to fleeing from the police. The trial court found that Graham violated his probation by "committing a home invasion robbery, possessing a firearm, and associating with persons engaged in criminal activity",[^cite_note-Drinan,_C.H._2012-126] and sentenced him to 15 years for the attempted armed robbery plus life imprisonment for the armed burglary. The life sentence Graham received meant he had a life sentence without the possibility of parole, "because Florida abolished their parole system in 2003".[^cite_note-Drinan,_C.H._2012-126]

Graham's case was presented to the [Supreme Court of the United States](31737.md), with the question of whether juveniles should receive life without the possibility of parole in non-homicide cases. The Justices eventually ruled that such a sentence violated the juvenile's 8th Amendment rights, protecting them from punishments that are disproportionate to the crime committed,[^cite_note-Drinan,_C.H._2012-126] resulting in the abolition of life sentences without the possibility of parole in non-homicide cases for juveniles.

In 2012, the Supreme Court ruled in the case of *[Miller v. Alabama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller_v._Alabama)* in a 5–4 decision and with the majority opinion written by Associate Justice [Elena Kagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elena_Kagan) that mandatory sentences of life in prison without parole for juvenile offenders are unconstitutional. The majority opinion stated that barring a judge from considering mitigating factors and other information, such as age, maturity, and family and home environment violated the [Eighth Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eighth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution) ban on cruel and unusual punishment. Sentences of life in prison without parole can still be given to juveniles for aggravated first-degree murder, as long as the judge considers the circumstances of the case.[^cite_note-127][^cite_note-128]

In 2016 the Supreme Court ruled in the case of *[Montgomery v. Louisiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montgomery_v._Louisiana)* that the rulings imposed by *[Miller v. Alabama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller_v._Alabama)* were to apply retroactively, causing a substantial amount of appeals to decade-old sentences for then-juvenile offenders.

In 2021, the Supreme Court ruled in *[Jones v. Mississippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jones_v._Mississippi)* that sentencers are not required to make a separate finding of the defendant to be "permanently incorrigible" prior to sentencing a juvenile to life without parole.

#### Vatican City

[Pope Francis](1687680.md) called for the abolition of both [capital punishment](5902.md) and life imprisonment in a meeting with representatives of the [International Association of Penal Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Association_of_Penal_Law). He also stated that life imprisonment, removed from the [Vatican City](32408.md) penal code in 2013, is just a variation of the death penalty.[^cite_note-129]

#### Malaysia

Originally in Malaysia, life imprisonment was construed as a jail term lasting the remainder of a convict's natural life, either with or without the possibility of parole. In April 2023, the Malaysian government officially abolished natural life imprisonment and instead redefined a life sentence as a jail term between 30 and 40 years. At the time of the reform, at least 117 prisoners were serving natural life imprisonment, consisting of 70 whose original death sentences were commuted to life (without parole) prior to the reform, and another 47 whose sentences of life were imposed by the courts, and all of these life convicts were allowed to have their jail terms reduced to between 30 and 40 years in jail.[^cite_note-130] In November 2023, four drug traffickers - Zulkipli Arshad, Wan Yuriilhami Wan Yaacob, Ghazalee Kasim and Mohamad Junaidi Hussin - became the first group of people to have their natural life sentences reduced to 30 years’ imprisonment after a re-sentencing hearing by the [Federal Court of Malaysia](14222422.md), which was followed by many more such commutations in the months to come.[^cite_note-131][^cite_note-132]

#### Singapore

Main article: [Life imprisonment in Singapore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_imprisonment_in_Singapore)

In [Singapore](27318.md), before 20 August 1997, the law decreed that life imprisonment is a fixed sentence of 20 years with the possibility of one-third reduction of the sentence (13 years and 4 months) for good behaviour. It was an appeal by [Abdul Nasir bin Amer Hamsah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriental_Hotel_murder) on 20 August 1997 that led to the law in Singapore to change the definition of life imprisonment into a sentence that lasts the remainder of the prisoner's natural life, with the possibility of parole after at least 20 years. Abdul Nasir was a convicted robber and kidnapper who was, in two separate High Court trials, sentenced to 18 years' imprisonment and 18 strokes of the cane for robbery with hurt resulting in a female Japanese tourist's death at [Oriental Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandarin_Oriental,_Singapore) in 1994 and a consecutive sentence of life imprisonment with 12 strokes of the cane for kidnapping two police officers for ransom in 1996, which totalled up to 38 years' imprisonment and 30 strokes of the cane.[^cite_note-auto2-133]

Abdul Nasir's appeal for the two sentences to run concurrently led to the [Court of Appeal of Singapore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_Appeal_of_Singapore), which dismissed Abdul Nasir's appeal, to decide that it would be wrong to consider life imprisonment as a fixed jail term of 20 years and thus changed it to a jail term to be served for the rest of the prisoner's remaining lifespan.[^cite_note-auto2-133] The amended definition is applied to future crimes committed after 20 August 1997. Since Abdul Nasir committed the crime of kidnapping and was sentenced before 20 August 1997, his life sentence remained as a prison term of 20 years and thus he still had to serve 38 years behind bars.[^cite_note-134][^cite_note-135][^cite_note-136][^cite_note-137]

The appeal of Abdul Nasir, titled [*Abdul Nasir bin Amer Hamsah v Public Prosecutor* \[1997\] SGCA 38](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriental_Hotel_murder),[^cite_note-auto2-133] was since regarded as a landmark in Singapore's legal history as it changed the definition of life imprisonment from "life" to "natural life" under the law.
