**Central Europe** is a geographical region of [Europe](9239.md) between [Eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Europe), [Southern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Europe), [Western](33800.md) and [Northern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Europe) Europe.[^cite_note-3][^cite_note-Economist-4] Central Europe is known for its cultural diversity;[^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6] however, countries in this region also share certain historical and cultural similarities.[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-\:0-8]

The region is variously defined but often includes [Austria](26964606.md), [Croatia](5573.md), the [Czech Republic](5321.md), [Germany](11867.md), [Hungary](13275.md), [Liechtenstein](17810.md), [Lithuania](17675.md), [Poland](22936.md), [Slovakia](26830.md), [Slovenia](27338.md) and [Switzerland](26748.md).[^cite_note-Fact-2][^cite_note-Jordan_2005-1] Throughout much of the [Early Modern period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Modern_period), the territories of Poland and Lithuania were part of the [Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Lithuanian_Commonwealth). Meanwhile, the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md) came to occupy most of present-day Croatia and present-day Hungary,[^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11] and southern parts of present-day Slovakia.[^cite_note-12][^cite_note-13][^cite_note-14] The various [German Principalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany_in_the_early_modern_period), the [Kingdom of Bohemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Bohemia) (present-day Czech Republic), the [Duchy of Carniola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Carniola) (part of present-day Slovenia), and the [Old Swiss Confederacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Swiss_Confederacy) were within the [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire). During the 18th century, the [Habsburg monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habsburg_monarchy) came to reign over the territories of Austria, Croatia, the Czech Republic, Hungary, Slovakia and Slovenia, alongside parts of [Serbia](29265.md), Germany, [Italy](14532.md) and Switzerland.[^cite_note-15] Between the early 18th and the early 20th centuries, Central Europe had a substantial [Jewish](25955086.md) population.[^cite_note-\:14-16]

Since the [Cold War](325329.md), the countries that make up Central Europe have historically been and in some cases continue to be[^cite_note-UNM49-17] divided into either Eastern or Western Europe.[^cite_note-\:1-18][^cite_note-\:2-19] After World War II, Europe was divided by the [Iron Curtain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Curtain)[^cite_note-20] into two parts, the [capitalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalism) [Western Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Bloc) and the [socialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_socialism) [Eastern Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Bloc), although Austria, Switzerland and [Yugoslavia](34244.md) (encompassing the territories of present-day Croatia, Slovenia and various other South Slavic nations) declared neutrality. The [Berlin Wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Wall) was one of the most visible symbols of this division.[^cite_note-21] Respectively, countries in Central Europe have historical, cultural and [geopolitical](163225.md) ties with these wider regions of Europe.[^cite_note-\:4-22][^cite_note-\:6-23][^cite_note-\:7-24][^cite_note-25]

Central Europe began a "strategic awakening" in the late 20th and early 21st centuries,[^cite_note-26] with initiatives such as the [Central European Defence Cooperation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_European_Defence_Cooperation), the [Central European Initiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_European_Initiative), [Centrope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centrope), and the [Visegrád Four Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visegr%C3%A1d_Group). That awakening was accelerated by writers and other intellectuals, who recognized the societal paralysis of decaying dictatorships and felt compelled to speak up against [Soviet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) oppression.[^cite_note-europarl-27]

## Historical perspective

### Middle Ages and early modern period

In the early Middle Ages, Central Europe had a diverse landscape, with various ethnic groups inhabiting the region. Following the fall of [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire) rule in the 5th century, [Germanic tribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_tribes) conquered parts of the west.[^cite_note-28] Among them were the [Franks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franks), [Alemans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alemanni) and [Bavarians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bavarians). [Slavic tribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_early_Slavic_peoples) were predominantly in the east, with the [West Slavs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Slavs) having migrated from the Slavic *[urheimat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urheimat)* in the area of modern-day [Ukraine](31750.md) and [Belarus](3457.md).[^cite_note-29][^cite_note-30] The region encompassed a wide spectrum of additional tribes and communities.

From the late 6th century to the early 9th century, the area roughly corresponding to the [Carpathian Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pannonian_Basin) was part of the Avar Khaganate, the realm of the [Carpathian Avars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpathian_Avars).[^cite_note-31][^cite_note-32] While the Avars dominated the east of what is now Austria, its north and south were under Germanic and Slavic influence, respectively.[^cite_note-33] Meanwhile, the territories now comprising Germany and Switzerland were under the influence of the [Merovingian dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merovingian_dynasty), and later the [Carolingian dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carolingian_dynasty).[^cite_note-34][^cite_note-35] Various Slavic tribes that inhabited eastern Central Europe established settlements during this period, primarily in present-day Croatia, Czech Republic, Poland, Slovakia and Slovenia.[^cite_note-\:9-36] In the 7th century, the [South Slavic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Slavic_languages) principality of [Carantania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carantania) was established in the area of modern-day southern Austria and northeastern Slovenia. The territory of Lithuania was inhabited by [Baltic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balts) tribes. Amongst them were the [Samogitians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samogitians), [Lithuanians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuanians_\(tribe\)) and [Curonians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curonians).[^cite_note-\:14-16]

The [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire) was founded at the turn of the 9th century, following the coronation of [Charlemagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlemagne) by [Pope Leo III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Leo_III).[^cite_note-37] At its inception, it incorporated present-day Germany and nearby regions, including parts of what is now Austria, the Czech Republic, Slovenia and Switzerland. Three decades later, [Great Moravia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Moravia), centred on present-day Czech Republic and Slovakia, became one of the first West Slavic states to be founded in Central Europe. The state was dominated by the [Moravians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moravians) and [Slovaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slovaks), also incorporating [Bohemians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohemians_\(tribe\)) when it expanded into the Bohemian lands. Great Moravia reached its height in the late 9th Century. It was at this time that the [Hungarian tribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magyar_tribes), having migrated westward on the [Eurasian Steppe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasian_Steppe),[^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39] overcame advances from the [First Bulgarian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Bulgarian_Empire), Great Moravia and the [East Franks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Franks), and settled in the Carpathian Basin.[^cite_note-40] Following from the earlier [Duchy of Croatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Croatia), the [Kingdom of Croatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Croatia_\(925%E2%80%931102\)) was established in 925, liberating the territory from Frankish suzerainty. The Kingdom of Croatia cooperated with the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire) against the expansion of the First Bulgarian Empire. In 966, the first Polish duchy, [Civitas Schinesghe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civitas_Schinesghe), was established. This is considered the predecessor state of the [Kingdom of Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Poland).

Following the Christianization of various Central European countries, elements of cultural unity emerged within the region, specifically [Catholicism](606848.md) and [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin). Eastern and Southeastern Europe remained predominantly [Eastern Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox), and were dominated by [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire) cultural influence. After the [East–West Schism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East%E2%80%93West_Schism) in 1054, significant parts of Eastern Europe developed cultural unity and resistance to Catholic Western and Central Europe within the framework of the [Eastern Orthodox Church](10186.md), [Church Slavonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_Slavonic) and the [Cyrillic alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrillic_alphabet).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMagocsi2002chapter_11-41][^cite_note-Greyerz-42][^cite_note-Sedlar-43][^cite_note-44]

According to the historian [Jenő Szűcs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jen%C5%91_Sz%C5%B1cs), Central Europe at the end of the 1st millennium became influenced by Western European developments. Szűcs argued that between the 11th and 15th centuries, Christianization influenced the cultures within Central Europe, and well-defined social features were also implemented in the region based on Western characteristics. The keyword of Western social development after the turn of the millennium was the spread of [Magdeburg rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magdeburg_rights) in some cities and towns of Western Europe. They began to spread in the mid-13th century in Central European countries and brought about self-governments of towns and counties.[^cite_note-45]

In 1335, the [Kings of Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_of_Poland), [Bohemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_of_Bohemia) and [Hungary and Croatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_I_of_Hungary) met in the castle of [Visegrád](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visegr%C3%A1d)[^cite_note-Halman-46] and agreed to cooperate closely in the field of politics and commerce. That has inspired the post-[Cold War](325329.md) [Visegrád Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visegr%C3%A1d_Group).[^cite_note-Halman-46]

In 1386, [Jogaila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W%C5%82adys%C5%82aw_II_Jagie%C5%82%C5%82o), the [Grand Duke of Lithuania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Duke_of_Lithuania), converted to Christianity (specifically Catholicism) and subsequently became King of Poland through marriage to [Queen Jadwiga of Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jadwiga_of_Poland). That initiated the [Christianization of Lithuania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianization_of_Lithuania) and resulted in the [Union of Krewo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_of_Krewo), signifying a personal union between the [Grand Duchy of Lithuania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Duchy_of_Lithuania) and the Kingdom of Poland. The union commenced an enduring political alliance between the two entities and laid the foundations for the later establishment of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth in 1569.[^cite_note-47]

Between the 15th and the early 16th centuries, the Kingdom of Croatia, which was then in [personal union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croatia_in_personal_union_with_Hungary) with the Kingdom of Hungary, served as a significant maritime gateway of Central Europe, with its ports facilitating key trade routes between Central Europe and the Mediterranean.[^cite_note-\:12-48][^cite_note-49] The [Republic of Ragusa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Ragusa) emerged as a prominent hub for cultural exchange during this time.[^cite_note-\:12-48] Following the Ottoman and Habsburg wars of the 16th and 17th centuries, the Kingdom of Croatia, [under Habsburg rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Croatia_\(Habsburg\)), began to regain its position as a significant trade route, restoring ports and revitalising commercial activity.

Before 1870, the industrialization that had started to develop in Northwestern and Central Europe and the United States did not extend in any significant way to the rest of the world. Even in [Eastern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Europe), industrialization lagged far behind. [Russia](25391.md), for example, remained largely rural and agricultural, and its autocratic rulers kept the peasants in serfdom.[^cite_note-51] The concept of Central Europe was already known at the beginning of the 19th century,[^cite_note-essex.ac.uk-52] but it developed further and became an object of intensive interest towards the 20th century. However, the first concept mixed science, politics, and economy and was strictly connected with the aspirations of German states to dominate a part of European continent called *Mitteleuropa*. At the [Frankfurt Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankfurt_Parliament), which was established in the wake of the [March Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_revolutions_of_1848%E2%80%931849) of 1848, there were multiple competing ideas for the integration of German-speaking areas, including the *mitteleuropäische Lösung* (Central European Solution) propagated by Austria, which sought to merge the smaller German-speaking states with the multi-ethnic Habsburg monarchy, but was opposed by Prussia and others. An imperialistic idea of *Mitteleuropa* also became popular in the [German Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Empire), which was established in 1871 and experienced intensive economic growth. The term was used when the [Union of German Railway Administrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_of_German_Railway_Administrations) established the *Mitteleuropäische Eisenbahn-Zeit* (Central European Railway Time) [time zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_zone), which was applied by the railways from 1 June 1891 and was later widely adopted in civilian life; the time zone's name has been shortened to the present-day [Central European Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_European_Time).[^cite_note-Zeit-MEZ-53]

The German term denoting Central Europe was so fashionable that other languages started referring to it when indicating territories from [Rhine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhine) to [Vistula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vistula) or even the [Dnieper](380138.md) and from the [Baltic Sea](3335.md) to the [Balkans](4829.md).[^cite_note-54] An example of this vision of Central Europe may be seen in [Joseph Partsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Partsch)'s book of 1903.[^cite_note-55]

On 21 January 1904, *Mitteleuropäischer Wirtschaftsverein* (Central European Economic Association) was established in [Berlin](3354.md) with economic integration of Germany and Austria (with eventual extension to Switzerland, [Belgium](3343.md) and the [Netherlands](21148.md)) as its main aim. Another time, the term Central Europe became connected to the German plans of political, economic, and cultural domination. The "bible" of the concept was [Friedrich Naumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Naumann)'s book *Mitteleuropa*[^cite_note-56] in which he called for an economic federation to be established after World War I. Naumann's proposed a federation with Germany and the Habsburg monarchy as its centre that would eventually unite all external European nations through economic prosperity.[^cite_note-57] The concept failed after the German defeat in [World War I](4764461.md). The revival of the idea may be observed during the [Hitler era](21212.md).

### Interwar period



[Interwar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interwar_period) Central Europe according to Emmanuel de Martonne (1927)

[^cite_note-Between_Worlds_–_The_MIT_Press-58]

The [interwar period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interwar_period) (1918–1938) brought a new geopolitical system, as well as economic and political problems, and the concept of Central Europe took on a different character. The centre of interest was moved to its eastern part, particularly to the countries that had reappeared on the map of Europe. Central Europe ceased to be the area of German aspiration to lead or dominate and became a territory of various integration movements aiming at resolving political, economic, and national problems of "new" states, being a way to face German and Soviet pressures. However, the conflict of interests was too major, and neither the [Little Entente](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Entente) nor [Intermarium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermarium) (*Międzymorze*) ideas succeeded. The Hungarian historian [Magda Ádám](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magda_%C3%81d%C3%A1m) wrote in her study *Versailles System and Central Europe* (2006): "Today we know that the bane of Central Europe was the [Little Entente](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Entente), military alliance of [Czechoslovakia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czechoslovakia), Romania and [Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Serbs,_Croats_and_Slovenes) (later Yugoslavia), created in 1921 not for Central Europe's cooperation nor to fight German expansion, but in a wrong perceived notion that a completely powerless Hungary must be kept down".[^cite_note-59] The [events preceding World War II in Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Events_preceding_World_War_II_in_Europe), including the so-called [Western betrayal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_betrayal) such as the [Munich Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munich_Agreement), were very much enabled by the rising nationalism and ethnocentrism that typified that period.

The interwar period brought new elements to the concept of Central Europe. Before World War I, it embraced mainly German-speaking states, and non-German speaking territories were an area of intended German penetration and domination, with German leadership being the 'natural' result of economic dominance.[^cite_note-essex.ac.uk-52] Post-war, the Eastern part of Central Europe was placed at the centre of the concept. At the time, the scientists took an interest in the idea: the International Historical Congress in [Brussels](3708.md) in 1923 was committed to Central Europe, and the 1933, Congress continued the discussions.[^cite_note-ehr.oxfordjournals.org-60]

According to [Emmanuel de Martonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmanuel_de_Martonne), in 1927, Central Europe encompassed Austria, Czechoslovakia, Germany, Hungary, Poland, Romania and Switzerland, northern Italy and northern Yugoslavia. The author uses both Human and Physical Geographical features to define Central Europe but failed to take into account the legal development or the social, cultural, economic, and infrastructural developments in those countries.[^cite_note-61]

The avant-garde movements of Central Europe contributed to the evolution of modernism and reached their its peak throughout the continent during the 1920s. The *Sourcebook of Central European avantgards* ([Los Angeles County Museum of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Angeles_County_Museum_of_Art)) contains primary documents of the avant-gardes in the territories of Austria, Germany, Poland (including western parts of present-day [Belarus](3457.md) and [Ukraine](31750.md), and southern parts of Lithuania), Czechoslovakia (including the Czech Republic and Slovakia), Hungary, Romania and Yugoslavia (including present-day Bosnia and Herzegovina, Croatia, [Montenegro](20760.md), [North Macedonia](23564616.md), [Serbia](29265.md) and Slovenia) from 1910 to 1930.[^cite_note-Between_Worlds_–_The_MIT_Press-58]

### Mitteleuropa

With the [dissolution of the Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Holy_Roman_Empire) around 1800, there was a consolidation of power among the [Habsburgs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habsburgs) and the [Hohenzollerns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hohenzollerns) as the two major states in the area. They had much in common and occasionally cooperated in various channels, but more often competed. One approach in the various attempts at cooperation, was the conception of a set of supposed common features and interests, and this idea led to the first discussions of a *Mitteleuropa* in the mid-nineteenth century, as espoused by [Friedrich List](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_List) and [Karl Ludwig Bruck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Ludwig_Bruck). These were mostly based on economic issues.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEvans2006296–297-62]

*[Mitteleuropa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitteleuropa)* may refer to a historical concept or a contemporary German definition of Central Europe. As a historical concept, the German term *Mitteleuropa* (or alternatively its literal translation into English, *Middle Europe*[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJohnson1996165-63]) is an ambiguous German concept.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJohnson1996165-63] According to [Fritz Fischer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Fischer_\(historian\)) *Mitteleuropa* was a scheme in the era of the [Reich of 1871–1918](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Empire) by which the old imperial elites had allegedly sought to build a system of German economic, military and political domination from the northern seas to the Near East and from the Low Countries through the steppes of Russia to the Caucasus.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHayes199416-64] Later on, Professor Fritz Epstein argued the threat of a Slavic "Drang nach Westen" (Western expansion) had been a major factor in the emergence of a *Mitteleuropa* ideology before the Reich of 1871 ever came into being.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHayes199417-65]

In Germany, the word's connotation was also sometimes linked to the pre-war German provinces east of the [Oder-Neisse line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oder-Neisse_line).[^cite_note-Sinnhuber1954-66]

The term "Mitteleuropa" conjures up negative historical associations among some people although the Germans have not played an exclusively negative role in the region.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJohnson19966-67] Most Central European Jews embraced the enlightened German humanistic culture of the 19th century.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJohnson19967-68] Jews at the turn of the 20th century became representatives of what many consider to be Central European culture at its best, but the Nazi conceptualisation of "Mitteleuropa" sought to destroy that culture.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJohnson19967,_165,_170-69] The term "Mitteleuropa" is widely used in German education and media without a negative meaning, especially since the end of communism.[^cite_note-70] Many people from the [new states of Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_states_of_Germany) do not identify themselves as being part of Western Europe and therefore prefer the term "Mitteleuropa".[^cite_note-71]

### Central Europe during World War II



[German-occupied Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German-occupied_Europe) at the height of the Axis conquests in 1942

During World War II, Central Europe was largely occupied by Nazi Germany. Many areas were a battle area and were devastated. The mass murder of the Jews depopulated many of their centuries-old settlement areas or settled other people there and their culture was wiped out. Both [Adolf Hitler](2731583.md) and [Joseph Stalin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Stalin) diametrically opposed the centuries-old Habsburg principles of "live and let live" with regard to ethnic groups, peoples, minorities, religions, cultures and languages and tried to assert their own ideologies and power interests in Central Europe.[^cite_note-72] There were various Allied plans for state order in Central Europe for post-war. While Stalin tried to get as many states under his control as possible, [Winston Churchill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winston_Churchill) preferred a Central European Danube Confederation to counter those countries against Germany and Russia. There were also plans to add Bavaria and Württemberg to an enlarged Austria.[^cite_note-73] There were also various resistance movements around [Otto von Habsburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_von_Habsburg) that pursued that goal. The group around the Austrian priest [Heinrich Maier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Maier) also planned in that direction, which also successfully helped the Allies to wage war by, among other things, forwarding production sites and plans for [V-2 rockets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V-2_rocket), [Tiger tanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiger_tanks?action=edit\&redlink=1) and aircraft to the United States.[^cite_note-74][^cite_note-75] Otto von Habsburg tried to relieve Austria, Czechoslovakia, Hungary and northern Yugoslavia (particularly the territories of present-day Croatia and Slovenia) from German and Soviet influence and control.[^cite_note-76][^cite_note-77] There were various considerations to prevent German and Soviet power in Europe after the war. Churchill's idea of reaching the area around Vienna before the Russians via an operation from the Adriatic had not been approved by the Western Allied chiefs of staff.[^cite_note-78] As a result of the military situation at the end of the war, Stalin's plans prevailed and much of Central Europe came under Soviet control.[^cite_note-79][^cite_note-80]

### Central Europe behind the Iron Curtain

&#x20;  [Neutral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutral_country): [Austria](26964606.md), [Finland](10577.md), Sweden and Switzerland

&#x20; [Non-Aligned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Aligned_Movement): [Cyprus](5593.md), [Malta](19137.md) and [Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_Federal_Republic_of_Yugoslavia)

Following [World War II](32927.md), parts of Central Europe became part of the [Eastern Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Bloc). The boundary between the two blocks was called the [Iron Curtain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Curtain). Austria, Switzerland and Yugoslavia remained neutral.

The post-World War II period brought blocking of research on Central Europe in the [Eastern Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Bloc) countries, as its every result proved the dissimilarity of Central Europe, which was inconsistent with the [Stalinist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stalinist) doctrine. On the other hand, the topic became popular in Western Europe and the United States, much of the research being carried out by immigrants from Central Europe.[^cite_note-81] Following the [Fall of Communism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Communism), publicists and historians in Central Europe, especially the anti-communist opposition, returned to their research.[^cite_note-82]

According to Karl A. Sinnhuber (*Central Europe: Mitteleuropa: Europe Centrale: An Analysis of a Geographical Term*)[^cite_note-Sinnhuber1954-66] most Central European states were unable to preserve their political independence and became [Soviet satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_satellites). Besides Austria, Switzerland and Yugoslavia, only the marginal European states of [Cyprus](5593.md), [Finland](10577.md), [Malta](19137.md) and Sweden preserved their political sovereignty to a certain degree, being left out of any military alliances in Europe.

The opening of the [Iron Curtain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Curtain) between Austria and Hungary at the [Pan-European Picnic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-European_Picnic) on 19 August 1989 then set in motion a peaceful chain reaction, at the end of which there was no longer an [East Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Germany) and the Eastern Bloc had disintegrated.[^cite_note-83][^cite_note-84] It was the largest escape movement from East Germany since the Berlin Wall was built in 1961. After the picnic, which was based on an idea by [Otto von Habsburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_von_Habsburg) to test the reaction of the USSR and Mikhail Gorbachev to an opening of the border, tens of thousands of media-informed East Germans set off for Hungary.[^cite_note-85] The leadership of the GDR in East Berlin did not dare to completely block the borders of their own country and the USSR did not respond at all. This broke the bracket of the Eastern Bloc and Central Europe subsequently became free from communism.[^cite_note-86][^cite_note-87][^cite_note-88]

## Definitions

The issue of how to name and define the Central European area is subject to debates. Very often, the definition depends on the nationality and historical perspective of its author. The concept of "Central Europe" appeared in the 19th century. It was understood as a contact zone between the Southern and Northern areas, and later the Eastern and Western areas of Europe. Thinkers portrayed "Central Europe" either as a separate region, or a buffer zone between these regions.

In the early nineteenth century, the terms "Middle" or "Central" Europe (known as "Mitteleuropa" in German and "Europe centrale" in French) were introduced in geographical scholarship in both German and French languages. At first, these terms were linked to the regions spanning from the Pyrenees to the Danube, which, according to German authors, could be united under German authority. However, after the Franco-Prussian war of 1870, the French began to exclude France from this area, and later the Germans also adopted this perspective by the end of World War I.[^cite_note-Aleksov_Piahanau_2020_pp7-8-92]

The concept of "Central" or "Middle Europe", understood as a region with German influence, lost a significant part of its popularity after WWI and was completely dismissed after WWII. Two defeats of Germany in the world wars, combined with the division of Germany, an almost complete disappearance of German-speaking communities in these countries, and the Communist-led isolation of Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Lithuania, Poland and Yugoslavia from the Western world, turned the concept of "Central/Middle Europe" into an anachronism. On the other side, the non-German areas of Central Europe were almost universally regarded as "Eastern European" primarily associated with the Soviet sphere of influence in the late 1940s–1980s.

For the most part, this geographical framework lost its attraction after the end of the Cold War. A number of Post-Communist countries rather re-branded themselves in the 1990s as "Central European.", while avoiding the stained wording of "Middle Europe," which they associated with German influence in the region. This reinvented concept of "Central Europe" excluded Germany, Austria and Switzerland, reducing its coverage chiefly to Poland, the Czech Republic, Slovakia, Hungary, Lithuania and Yugoslavia.[^cite_note-Aleksov_Piahanau_2020_pp7-8-92]

### Academic

The main proposed regional definitions, gathered by Polish historian [Jerzy Kłoczowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerzy_K%C5%82oczowski) and others, include:[^cite_note-93]

- West-Central and [East-Central Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East-Central_Europe) – this conception, presented in 1950,

  [^cite_note-94]

  &#x20;distinguishes two regions in Central Europe: the German West-Centre and the East-Centre covered by a variety of nations *from [Finland](10577.md) to [Greece](12108.md)*, placed between the great empires of [Scandinavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scandinavia), Germany, Italy and the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union).

- Central Europe as a region comprising countries in [mainland Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_Europe) speaking [West Germanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Germanic_languages) (Austria, [Belgium](3343.md), Germany, the [Netherlands](21148.md) and Switzerland) and [West Slavic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Slavic_languages) languages (the Czech Republic, Slovakia and Poland).

  [^cite_note-ReferenceA-95]

- Central Europe as the area of the cultural heritage of the [Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Lithuanian_Commonwealth) – Ukrainian, Belarusian and Lithuanian historians, in cooperation (since 1990) with Polish historians, insist on the importance of this concept.



[Habsburg-ruled lands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria-Hungary) (divided between [Cisleithanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cisleithanian)/Austrian-administered and [Transalthanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lands_of_the_Crown_of_Saint_Stephen)/Hungarian-administered)

- Central Europe as the area of the former Habsburg monarchy

  [^cite_note-96]

  &#x20;– a concept which is popular in large parts of [Bosnia and Herzegovina](3463.md).

  [^cite_note-97]

- A concept underlining the links connecting [Belarus](3457.md), [Moldova](19260.md) and [Ukraine](31750.md) with Russia and treating the [Russian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire) together with the whole [Slavic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavic_peoples) [Orthodox](10186.md) population as one entity – this position is taken by Russian historiographers.

- A concept putting the accent on links with the West, especially from the 19th century and the grand period of liberation and formation of Nation-states, an idea that is represented by the [South-Eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South-Eastern_Europe) states, which prefer the enlarged concept of the "East Centre" expressing their links with [Western culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_culture).

Former University of Vienna professor Lonnie R. Johnson points out criteria to distinguish Central Europe from Western, Northern, Eastern and Southern Europe:[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJohnson1996\[\[Category\:Wikipedia_articles_needing_page_number_citations_from_October_2023\]\]<sup_class="noprint_Inline-Template_"_style="white-space\:nowrap;">\[<i>\[\[Wikipedia\:Citing_sources|<span_title="This_citation_requires_a_reference_to_the_specific_page_or_range_of_pages_in_which_the_material_appears. - One criterion for defining Central Europe is the frontiers of medieval empires and kingdoms that largely correspond to the religious frontiers between [Catholic](606848.md) Western and Central Europe and [Orthodox](10186.md) Eastern Europe.

  [^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJohnson19964-99]

  &#x20;Following that criterion, the pagans of Central Europe were converted to Catholicism, but in Eastern and Southeastern Europe, they were brought into the fold of the [Eastern Orthodox Church](10186.md).

  [^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJohnson19964-99]

He also thinks that Central Europe is a dynamic historical concept, not a static spatial one. For example, a fair share of [Belarus](3457.md) and [Right-bank Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-bank_Ukraine) are in Eastern Europe today, but 240 years ago, they were in the [Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Lithuanian_Commonwealth).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJohnson19964-99] Johnson's study on Central Europe received acclaim and positive reviews in the scientific community.[^cite_note-100][^cite_note-101] However, according to the Romanian researcher [Maria Bucur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Bucur), the very ambitious project suffers from the weaknesses imposed by its scope (almost 1600 years of history).[^cite_note-102]

### Encyclopedias, gazetteers, dictionaries

[The World Factbook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_World_Factbook)[^cite_note-Fact-2] defines Central Europe as: Austria, the Czech Republic, Germany, Hungary, Liechtenstein, Poland, Slovakia, Slovenia and Switzerland. *[The Columbia Encyclopedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Columbia_Encyclopedia)* includes: Austria, the Czech Republic, Germany, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, Slovakia and Switzerland. While it does not have a single article defining Central Europe, [Encyclopædia Britannica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica) includes the following countries in Central Europe in one or more of its articles: Austria, Bosnia and Herzegovina,[^cite_note-103] Croatia,[^cite_note-104] the Czech Republic, Germany,[^cite_note-105] Hungary, Lithuania,[^cite_note-\:13-106] Poland, Romania,[^cite_note-\:13-106] Slovakia, Slovenia and Switzerland.

The [French](5843419.md) *Encyclopédie Larousse* defines Central Europe as a region comprising Austria, the Czech Republic, Germany, Liechtenstein, Slovakia, Slovenia and Switzerland.[^cite_note-107]

The German Encyclopaedia *Meyers Grosses Taschenlexikon* (*Meyers Big Pocket Encyclopedia*), 1999, defines Central Europe as the central part of Europe with no precise borders to the East and West. The term is mostly used to denominate the territory between the [Schelde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schelde) to [Vistula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vistula) and from the [Danube](21209639.md) to the [Moravian Gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moravian_Gate).

The German *[Ständige Ausschuss für geographische Namen](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/St%C3%A4ndiger%20Ausschuss%20f%C3%BCr%20geographische%20Namen)* (Standing Committee on Geographical Names), which develops and recommends rules for the uniform use of geographical names, proposes two sets of boundaries. The first follows international borders of current countries. The second subdivides and includes some countries based on cultural criteria. In comparison to some other definitions, it is broader, including Luxembourg, Estonia, Latvia, and in the second sense, the [Kaliningrad Oblast](17311.md) and parts of Belarus, Ukraine, Romania, Serbia, Italy, and France.[^cite_note-Jordan_2005-1]

According to *Meyers Enzyklopädisches Lexikon*,[^cite_note-108] Central Europe is a part of Europe composed of Austria, [Belgium](3343.md), the Czech Republic, Slovakia, Germany, Hungary, [Luxembourg](17515.md), [Netherlands](21148.md), Poland, [Romania](25445.md) and Switzerland, and northern marginal regions of Italy and Yugoslavia (northern states – [Croatia](5573.md) and [Slovenia](27338.md)), as well as northeastern France.

## Geography



The [Danube](21209639.md) watercourse system throughout Central and Southeastern Europe

Geography defines Central Europe's natural borders with the neighbouring regions to the north across the [Baltic Sea](3335.md), namely Northern Europe (or [Scandinavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scandinavia)), and to the south across the [Alps](981.md), the [Apennine Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apennine_Peninsula) (or Italy), and the [Balkan Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balkan_Peninsula)[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMagocsi200220-109] across the [Soča](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/So%C4%8Da)–[Krka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krka_\(Sava\))–[Sava](587845.md)–Danube line. The borders to Western Europe and Eastern Europe are geographically less defined, and for this reason the [cultural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture) and historical boundaries migrate more easily west–east than south–north.

Southwards, the [Pannonian Plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pannonian_Plain) is bounded by the rivers [Sava](587845.md) and [Danube](21209639.md) – and their respective floodplains.[^cite_note-152] The [Pannonian Plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pannonian_Plain) stretches over the following countries: [Austria](26964606.md), [Croatia](5573.md), [Hungary](13275.md), [Romania](25445.md), [Serbia](29265.md), [Slovakia](26830.md) and [Slovenia](27338.md), and touches borders of [Bosnia and Herzegovina](3463.md) and [Ukraine](31750.md) ("peri- Pannonian states").

South of the [Eastern Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Alps) (spanning Austria, Germany, Italy, Liechtenstein, Slovenia and Switzerland),[^cite_note-153] the [Dinaric Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinaric_Alps) extend for 650 kilometres along the coast of the [Adriatic Sea](23275478.md) (northwest-southeast), from the [Julian Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_Alps) in the northwest down to the Šar-Korab massif, north–south. According to the [Freie Universität Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_University_of_Berlin), this [mountain chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinaric_Alps) is classified as [South Central European](981.md).[^cite_note-154] The city of [Trieste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trieste) in this area, for example, expressly sees itself as a *città mitteleuropea*. This is particularly because it lies at the interface between the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_languages), [Slavic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavs), [Germanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_German-speaking_Europe), [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Greece) and [Jewish culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_culture) on the one hand and the geographical area of the [Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean) and the [Alps](981.md) on the other. A geographical and cultural assignment is made.

The Central European [flora region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phytochorion) stretches from Central France (the [Massif Central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massif_Central)) to the Northern [Balkans](4829.md), Central [Romania](25445.md) ([Carpathians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpathians)) and Southern [Scandinavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scandinavia).[^cite_note-155]

## Demography



Population density in Central European countries

Central Europe is one of the continent's most populous regions. It includes countries of varied sizes, ranging from tiny Liechtenstein to Germany, the second largest European country by population. Demographic figures for countries entirely located within notion of Central Europe ("the core countries") number around 173 million people, out of which around 82 million are residents of Germany.[^cite_note-156] Other populations include: Poland with around 38.5 million residents,[^cite_note-157] Czech Republic at 10.5 million,[^cite_note-158] Hungary at 10 million,[^cite_note-159] Austria with 9.2 million, Switzerland with 8.5 million,[^cite_note-160] Slovakia at 5.4 million,[^cite_note-161] Croatia with 4.3 million,[^cite_note-162] Lithuania with 2.9 million, Slovenia with 2.1 million[^cite_note-163] and Liechtenstein at a bit less than 40,000.[^cite_note-164]

If the countries which are sometimes also included in Central Europe were counted in, partially or in whole – Romania (20 million), Latvia (2 million), Estonia (1.3 million), Serbia (7.1 million)[^cite_note-165] – this would contribute around an additional 30.4 million, although this figure would vary depending on whether a regional or integral approach is used.[^cite_note-166] If smaller, western and eastern historical parts of Central Europe would be included in the demographic corpus, a further 20 million people of different nationalities would also be added in the overall count, surpassing a total of 200 million people.

Central Europe contains the continent's earliest railway systems, whose greatest expansion was recorded in Austrian, Czech, [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Empire), Hungarian and Swiss territories between 1860-1870s.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMagocsi20021758-170] By the mid-19th century Berlin, Vienna, [Zurich](40334603.md), [Pest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pest,_Hungary) and [Prague](23844.md) were focal points for network lines connecting industrial areas of [Saxony](28395.md), [Silesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silesia), Bohemia, Moravia and Lower Austria with the Baltic ([Kiel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiel), [Szczecin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Szczecin)) and Adriatic ([Rijeka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rijeka), Trieste).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMagocsi2002\[\[Category\:Wikipedia_articles_needing_page_number_citations_from_October_2023\]\]<sup_class="noprint_Inline-Template_"_style="white-space\:nowrap;">\[<i>\[\[Wikipedia\:Citing_sources|<span_title="This_citation_requires_a_reference_to_the_specific_page_or_range_of_pages_in_which_the_material_appears. By 1936, 70% of the [Swiss Federal Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_Federal_Railways) network had undergone electrification.[^cite_note-172]

Rail infrastructure in Central Europe remains the densest in the world. #### Universities

The first university established east of France and north of the Alps was in [Prague](23844.md) in 1348 by [Charles IV, Holy Roman Emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_IV,_Holy_Roman_Emperor). The [Charles University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_University) was modeled upon the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris) and initially included the faculty of law, medicine, philosophy, and theology.[^cite_note-185]

#### Central European University



The entrance of the [Central European University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_European_University) in [Budapest](36787.md)

In 1991, [Ernest Gellner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Gellner) proposed the establishment of a Central European institution of higher learning in Prague (1991–1995).[^cite_note-186] Eventually, the [Central European University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_European_University) (CEU) project was taken on and financially supported by the philanthropist [George Soros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Soros), who provided an endowment of US\$880 million, making the university one of the wealthiest in Europe.[^cite_note-187] Over its 30-year history, CEU has become one of the most internationally diverse universities in the world. As of 2019, 1217 students were enrolled in the university, of which 962 were international students, making the student body the fourth most international in the world.[^cite_note-188] CEU offers highly selective programs with a student-to-faculty ratio of 7\:1.[^cite_note-189] In 2021, the admission rate into its programs was 13%.[^cite_note-190] CEU promotes a distinctively Central European perspective while emphasizing academic rigor, applied research, and academic integrity.[^cite_note-191] CEU is a founding member of CIVICCA, a group of prestigious European higher education institutions in the social sciences, humanities, business management and public policy, including [Sciences Po](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sciences_Po) (France), [The London School of Economics and Political Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_London_School_of_Economics_and_Political_Science) (UK), [Bocconi University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bocconi_University) (Italy) and the [Stockholm School of Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm_School_of_Economics) (Sweden).[^cite_note-192]

### Religion



Adherence to Catholicism in Europe



Adherence to Protestantism in Europe

Central European major Christian denomination is Catholicism as well as large [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant) populations. Click map to see legend.

Central European countries are mostly [Catholic](606848.md) (Austria, Croatia, Liechtenstein, Lithuania, Poland and Slovenia) or historically both Catholic and [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant) (the [Czech Republic](5321.md), Germany, Hungary, Slovakia and Switzerland). Large Protestant groups include [Lutheran](23371382.md), [Calvinist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvinism), and the [Unity of the Brethren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moravian_Church) affiliates. Significant populations of [Eastern Catholicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Catholicism) and [Old Catholicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Catholic) are also prevalent throughout Central Europe. [Orthodox](10186.md) Christianity is a minority denomination observed to varying extents across Central Europe.

Central Europe has been the center of the [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant) movement for centuries, with the majority of Protestants suppressed and annihilated during the [Counterreformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterreformation).[^cite_note-196][^cite_note-197]

Historically, people in [Bohemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohemia) in today's Czech Republic were some of the first Protestants in Europe. As a result of the [Thirty Years' War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirty_Years'_War) following the [Bohemian Revolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohemian_Revolt), many [Czechs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czechs) were either killed, executed (see for [Old Town Square execution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Town_Square_execution)), forcibly turned into Roman Catholics, or emigrated to [Scandinavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scandinavia) and the [Low Countries](51402.md). In the aftermath of the [Thirty Years' War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirty_Years'_War), the number of inhabitants in the [Kingdom of Bohemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Bohemia) decreased from three million to only 800,000 from multiple factors, including devastating ongoing battles such as the significant [Battle of White Mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_White_Mountain) and the [Battle of Prague (1648)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Prague_\(1648\)). However, in recent years, most Czechs report as overwhelmingly non-religious, with some describing themselves as Catholic (10.3%).

Islam has had a notable historical presence in Croatia and Hungary. [Islam in Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_Hungary) dates back to the foundation of the state, from which a significant Muslim community persisted for several centuries. Islam was revived there under [Ottoman](22278.md) rule. [Islam in Croatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_Croatia) dates back to Ottoman rule, and today is represented mainly by ethnic [Bosniak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosniaks), [Albanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albanians) and [Turkish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_people) Muslims. Both countries have long absorbed migrations of Muslims from these populations. Additionally, Islam has had a historical presence in Poland and Lithuania, albeit limited. Communities of [Tatar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tatars) Muslims settled in the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth during the 15th and 16th centuries. Tatar Muslim minorities remain in Poland and Lithuania to this day.

Before the [Holocaust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocaust) (1941–45), there was also a sizeable [Ashkenazi Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashkenazi_Jewish) community in the region, numbering approximately 16.7 million people.[^cite_note-198] Poland and Lithuania had the largest Jewish populations in Europe as a percentage of their total populations, with Jews constituting 9.5% of the Polish population and 7.6% of the Lithuanian population in 1933.[^cite_note-199]

Certain countries in Central Europe, particularly the Czech Republic, Germany and Switzerland have sizeable [atheist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheist) and [non-religious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreligion) populations. In 2021, 48% of the Czech population declared that they had no religion.[^cite_note-200] In 2022, 43.8% of the German population declared that they had no religion.[^cite_note-201] Meanwhile, 33.5% of the Swiss population stated that they were not affiliated with any religion.[^cite_note-202]

### Cuisine

[Central European cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_European_cuisine) has evolved over centuries because of social and political change and is generally diverse. However, the national cuisines of western Central Europe share notable similarities, as do the cuisines of eastern Central Europe. Sausages, salamis and cheeses are popular in most of Central Europe, with the earliest evidence of cheesemaking in the archaeological record dates back to 5,500 BCE ([Kuyavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuyavia) region, Poland).[^cite_note-203] Other popular food items in Central Europe include soups, stews, pickled and fermented vegetables. [Schnitzel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schnitzel) and variations of [spätzle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sp%C3%A4tzle) are popular in the region.

Another common feature among Central European cuisines, particularly Austrian, Croatian, Lithuanian, Slovenian and Swiss cuisine, is the use of wild ingredients in traditional dishes, spanning from wild herbs to mushrooms and berries. Beer consumption is also prominent in parts of Central Europe, where the Czech Republic has the highest [beer consumption per capita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_beer_consumption_per_capita) globally, followed by Austria, with Germany coming 4th. The cuisines of Central European countries that are included in broader definitions of Eastern Europe share similarities and traditions with other Eastern European cuisines. This is particularly evident in the cuisines of Czech Republic, Lithuania, Poland and Slovakia, which feature dishes such as [borscht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borscht) and [pierogi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierogi). The cuisines of Croatia, Hungary and Slovenia feature a number of culinary traditions common within the Balkans, including dishes such as [stuffed peppers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuffed_peppers), [stuffed cabbage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuffed_cabbage), [bean soup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasulj), [pogača](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poga%C4%8Da) and [ajvar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ajvar)/[zacuscă](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zacusc%C4%83).

### Human rights

Generally, the countries in the region have been progressive on the issue of human rights: death penalty is illegal in all of them, corporal punishment is outlawed in most of them and people of both genders can vote in elections. However, Central European countries are divided on the subject of [same-sex marriage](92656.md) and abortion. Austria, the Czech Republic, Germany, and Poland also have a history of participation in the CIA's extraordinary rendition and detention program, according to the [Open Society Foundations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_Society_Foundations).[^cite_note-204][^cite_note-205]

### Literature

Regional writing tradition revolves around the turbulent history of the region, as well as its cultural diversity.[^cite_note-206][^cite_note-207] Its existence is sometimes challenged.[^cite_note-208] Specific courses on Central European literature are taught at [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University),[^cite_note-209] [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University)[^cite_note-210] and [Jagiellonian University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jagiellonian_University)[^cite_note-211] as well as cultural magazines dedicated to regional literature.[^cite_note-212] Angelus Central European Literature Award is an award worth 150,000.00 PLN (about \$50,000 or £30,000) for writers originating from the region.[^cite_note-213] Likewise, the [Vilenica International Literary Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vilenica_International_Literary_Festival) is awarded to a Central European author for "outstanding achievements in the field of literature and essay writing".[^cite_note-vilenica-214]

Further information: [List of central European countries by development indexes § Democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_central_European_countries_by_development_indexes#Democracy)



The [Economist Intelligence Unit](15303116.md) Democracy index map for 2022, with greener colours representing more democratic countries

Central Europe is a home to some of world's oldest democracies. However, most of them have been impacted by [totalitarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totalitarianism), particularly [Fascism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fascism) and [Nazism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazism). Germany and Italy occupied all Central European countries, except Switzerland. In all occupied countries, the [Axis powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_powers) suspended democracy and installed puppet regimes loyal to the occupation forces. Also, they forced conquered countries to apply racial laws and formed military forces for helping German and Italian struggle against Communists. After [World War II](32927.md), almost the whole of Central Europe (the Eastern and Middle part) had been transformed into [communist states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_states), most of which had been occupied and later allied with the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union), often against their will through forged referendum (such as the [Polish people's referendum in 1946](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1946_Polish_people's_referendum)) or force (northeast Germany, Poland, Hungary et alia). Nevertheless, those experiences have been dealt in most of them. Most Central European countries score very highly in the [Democracy Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy_Index).[^cite_note-216]

In spite of its turbulent history, Central Europe is currently one of world's safest regions. Most Central European countries are in top 20%.[^cite_note-217]

## In popular culture

Central Europe is mentioned in the 35th episode of *[Lovejoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lovejoy_\(TV_series\))*, entitled "The Prague Sun", filmed in 1992. While walking over the well-regarded and renowned [Charles Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Bridge) in Prague, the main character, Lovejoy, says: "I've never been to Prague before. Well, it is one of the great unspoiled cities in Central Europe. Notice: I said: 'Central', not 'Eastern'! The [Czechs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czechs) are a bit funny about that, they think of Eastern Europeans as *turnip heads*."[^cite_note-218]

[Wes Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wes_Anderson)'s Oscar-winning film *[The Grand Budapest Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Grand_Budapest_Hotel)* depicts a fictional grand hotel located somewhere in Central Europe, which is in actuality modelled on the [Grandhotel Pupp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grandhotel_Pupp) in [Karlovy Vary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karlovy_Vary) in the [Czech Republic](5321.md). The film is a celebration of 1920s and 1930s Central Europe with its artistic splendor and societal sensibilities.[^cite_note-219][^cite_note-\:15-220]
