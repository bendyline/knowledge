The **watt** (symbol: **W**) is the unit of [power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_\(physics\)) or [radiant flux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiant_flux) in the [International System of Units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units) (SI), equal to 1 [joule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joule) per [second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second) or 1 kg⋅m<sup>2</sup>⋅s<sup>−3</sup>.[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3] It is used to [quantify](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantification_\(science\)) the rate of [energy transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Work_\(physics\)). The watt is named in honor of [James Watt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Watt) (1736–1819), an 18th-century [Scottish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_people) [inventor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inventor), [mechanical engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_engineer), and [chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemist) who in 1776 improved the [Newcomen engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newcomen_engine) with his own [steam engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watt_steam_engine), which became fundamental for the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution).

## Overview

When an object's [velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velocity) is held constant at one [meter per second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meter_per_second) against a constant opposing force of one [newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton_\(unit\)), the rate at which [work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Work_\(physics\)) is done is one watt.      1   W = 1   J   /   s = 1   N  ⋅  m   /   s = 1   k g  ⋅   m  2    ⋅   s  − 3    .   {\displaystyle \mathrm {1\~W=1\~J{/}s=1\~N{\cdot }m{/}s=1\~kg{\cdot }m^{2}{\cdot }s^{-3}} .}  {\displaystyle \mathrm {1\~W=1\~J{/}s=1\~N{\cdot }m{/}s=1\~kg{\cdot }m^{2}{\cdot }s^{-3}} .}

In terms of [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism), one watt is the rate at which [electrical work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_work) is performed when a current of one [ampere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampere) (A) flows across an electrical [potential difference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_difference) of one [volt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volt) (V), meaning the watt is equivalent to the [volt-ampere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volt-ampere) (the latter unit, however, is used for a different quantity from the [real power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_power) of an electrical circuit).      1   W = 1   V  ⋅  A  .   {\displaystyle \mathrm {1\~W=1\~V{\cdot }A} .}  {\displaystyle \mathrm {1\~W=1\~V{\cdot }A} .}

Two additional [unit conversions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_conversion) for watt can be found using the above equation and [Ohm's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohm's_law).      1   W = 1    V  2    /  Ω = 1    A  2    ⋅  Ω  ,   {\displaystyle \mathrm {1\~W=1\~V^{2}/\Omega =1\~A^{2}{\cdot }\Omega } ,}  {\displaystyle \mathrm {1\~W=1\~V^{2}/\Omega =1\~A^{2}{\cdot }\Omega } ,} where [ohm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohm) (    Ω   {\displaystyle \Omega }  {\displaystyle \Omega }) is the [SI derived unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI_derived_unit) of [electrical resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_resistance).

### Examples

- A person having a mass of 100 kg who climbs a 3-meter-high ladder in 5 seconds is doing work at a rate of about 600 watts. Mass times acceleration due to [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity) times height divided by the time it takes to lift the object to the given height gives the *rate of doing work* or *power*.

  [^cite_note-4]

- A laborer over the course of an eight-hour day can sustain an average output of about 75 watts; higher power levels can be achieved for short intervals and by athletes.

  [^cite_note-5]

## History

The watt is named after the Scottish inventor [James Watt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Watt).[^cite_note-Klein-6] The unit name was proposed by [C. William Siemens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Wilhelm_Siemens) in August 1882 in his President's Address to the Fifty-Second Congress of the [British Association for the Advancement of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Association_for_the_Advancement_of_Science).[^cite_note-Siemens-7] Noting that units in the [practical system of units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CGS#Practical_CGS_units) were named after leading physicists, Siemens proposed that *watt* might be an appropriate name for a unit of power.[^cite_note-8] Siemens defined the unit within the existing system of practical units as "the power conveyed by a current of an [Ampère](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampere) through the difference of potential of a Volt".[^cite_note-9]

In October 1908, at the International Conference on Electric Units and Standards in London,[^cite_note-10] so-called *international* definitions were established for practical electrical units.[^cite_note-EB11-742-11] Siemens' definition was adopted as the *international* watt. (Also used: 1 A<sup>2</sup> × 1 Ω.)[^cite_note-Klein-6] The watt was defined as equal to 10<sup>7</sup> units of power in the *practical system* of units.[^cite_note-EB11-742-11] The ["international units"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Electrical_and_Magnetic_Units#Overdefinition_and_the_1908_modification) were dominant from 1909 until 1948. After the 9th [General Conference on Weights and Measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Conference_on_Weights_and_Measures) in 1948, the *international* watt was redefined from practical units to absolute units (i.e., using only length, mass, and time). Concretely, this meant that 1 watt was defined as the quantity of energy transferred in a unit of time, namely 1 J/s. In this new definition, 1 *absolute* watt = 1.00019 *international* watts. Texts written before 1948 are likely to be using the *international* watt, which implies caution when comparing numerical values from this period with the post-1948 watt.[^cite_note-Klein-6] In 1960, the 11th General Conference on Weights and Measures adopted the *absolute* watt into the [International System of Units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units) (SI) as the unit of power.[^cite_note-12]

Attowatt

The sound intensity in water corresponding to the international standard reference [sound pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_pressure) of 1 [μPa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micropascal) is approximately 0.65 aW/m

2

.

[^cite_note-13]

Femtowatt

Powers measured in femtowatts are typically found in references to [radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio) and [radar](25676.md) receivers. For example, meaningful [FM tuner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FM_tuner) performance figures for sensitivity, quieting and [signal-to-noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal-to-noise) require that the [RF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RF) energy applied to the antenna input be specified. These input levels are often stated in dBf ([decibels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decibel) referenced to 1 femtowatt). This is 0.2739 microvolts across a 75-ohm load or 0.5477 microvolt across a 300-ohm load; the specification takes into account the RF [input impedance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Input_impedance) of the tuner.

Picowatt

Powers measured in picowatts are typically used in reference to radio and radar receivers, [acoustics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acoustics) and in the science of [radio astronomy](82961.md). One picowatt is the international standard reference value of [sound power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_power) when this quantity is expressed in decibels.

[^cite_note-14]

Nanowatt

Powers measured in nanowatts are also typically used in reference to radio and radar receivers.

Microwatt

Powers measured in microwatts are typically stated in [medical instrumentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_instrument) systems such as the [electroencephalograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroencephalograph) (EEG) and the [electrocardiograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrocardiograph) (ECG), in a wide variety of scientific and engineering instruments, and in reference to radio and radar receivers. Compact [solar cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_cell) for devices such as [calculators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar-powered_calculator) and [watches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar-powered_watch) are typically measured in microwatts.

[^cite_note-15]

Milliwatt

A typical [laser pointer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_pointer) outputs about five milliwatts of light power, whereas a typical [hearing aid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hearing_aid) uses less than one milliwatt.

[^cite_note-16]

&#x20;[Audio signals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audio_signal) and other electronic signal levels are often measured in [dBm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DBm), referenced to one milliwatt.

Watt

PC [power supply units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_supply_unit_\(computer\)) are typically specified in watts; modern [graphics cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphics_card) usually have [TDPs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_design_power) of a few hundred watts.

Kilowatt

"Kilowatt" and "Kilowatts" redirect here. For the musician James Watts, see [KiloWatts (musician)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KiloWatts_\(musician\)).

"KW" redirects here. For other uses, see [KW (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KW_\(disambiguation\)).

The kilowatt is typically used to express the output power of [engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engine) and the power of [electric motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_motor), tools, machines, and heaters. It is also a common unit used to express the [electromagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_radiation) power output of broadcast radio and television [transmitters](61164.md).&#x20;

&#x20;One kilowatt is approximately equal to 1.34 [horsepower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horsepower). A small electric heater with one [heating element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heating_element) can use 1 kilowatt. The average [electric power consumption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_power_consumption) of a household in the United States is about 1 kilowatt.

[\[ii\]](#cite_note-18)

&#x20;A surface area of 1 square meter on Earth receives typically about one kilowatt of sunlight from the Sun (the [solar irradiance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_irradiance)) (on a clear day at midday, close to the equator).

[^cite_note-19]

Megawatt

Many events or machines produce or sustain the conversion of energy on this scale, including large electric motors; large warships, aircraft carriers, cruisers, and submarines; large [server farms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Server_farm) or [data centers](579730.md#Energy_use); and some scientific research equipment, such as [supercolliders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercollider), and the output pulses of very large lasers. A large residential or commercial building may use several megawatts in electric power. On railways, modern high-powered [electric locomotives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_locomotive) typically have a peak power output of 5 or 6 MW, while some produce much more. The [Eurostar e300](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurostar_e300), for example, uses more than 12 MW, while heavy [diesel–electric locomotives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diesel%E2%80%93electric_locomotive) typically produce and use 3 and 5 MW. U.S. [nuclear power plants](207347.md) have net summer capacities between about 500 and 1300 MW.

## Conventions in the electric power industry

In the [electric power industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_power_industry), *megawatt electrical* (*MWe*[^cite_note-34] or MW<sub>e</sub>)[^cite_note-35] refers by convention to the [electric power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_power) produced by a generator, while *megawatt thermal* or *thermal megawatt*[^cite_note-36] (MWt, MW<sub>t</sub>, or MWth, MW<sub>th</sub>) refers to [thermal power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_power) produced by the plant and *megawatt* *mechanical* (MW<sub>m</sub>) to [mechanical power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_power).[^cite_note-37] For example, the [Embalse nuclear power plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embalse_nuclear_power_plant) in Argentina uses a [fission reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fission_reactor) to generate 2,109 MW<sub>t</sub> (i.e. heat), which creates steam to drive a turbine, which generates 648 MW<sub>e</sub> (i.e. electricity). Other [SI prefixes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI_prefix) are sometimes used, for example *gigawatt electrical* (GW<sub>e</sub>). The [International Bureau of Weights and Measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Bureau_of_Weights_and_Measures), which maintains the SI-standard, states that further information about a quantity should not be attached to the unit symbol but instead to the quantity symbol (e.g., *P*<sub>th</sub> = 270 W rather than *P* = 270 W<sub>th</sub>) and so these unit symbols are non-SI.[^cite_note-38] In compliance with SI, the energy company [Ørsted A/S](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%98rsted_\(company\)) uses the unit megawatt for produced electrical power and the equivalent unit [megajoule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megajoule) per second for delivered heating power in a [combined heat and power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combined_heat_and_power) station such as [Avedøre Power Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aved%C3%B8re_Power_Station).[^cite_note-39]

When describing [alternating current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_current) (AC) electricity, another distinction is made between the watt and the [volt-ampere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volt-ampere). While these units are equivalent for simple [resistive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resistor) [circuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_network), they differ when loads exhibit [electrical reactance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_reactance).

## Radio transmission

Main article: [Effective radiated power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_radiated_power)

[Radio stations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_stations) usually report the power of their [transmitters](61164.md) in units of watts, referring to the [effective radiated power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_radiated_power). This refers to the power that a [half-wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-wave_antenna) [dipole antenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipole_antenna) would need to radiate to match the intensity of the transmitter's [main lobe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_lobe).

## Distinction between watts and watt-hours

The terms [power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_\(physics\)) and [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) are closely related but distinct physical quantities. Power is the rate at which energy is generated or consumed and hence is measured in units (e.g. watts) that represent energy *per unit time*.

For example, when a [light bulb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_bulb) with a [power rating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_rating) of 100W is turned on for one hour, the energy used is 100 [watt hours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watt_hour) (W·h), 0.1 kilowatt hour, or 360 [kJ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joule#Kilojoule). This same amount of energy would light a 40-watt bulb for 2.5 hours, or a 50-watt bulb for 2 hours.

[Power stations](212141.md) are rated using units of power, typically megawatts or gigawatts (for example, the [Three Gorges Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Gorges_Dam) in China is rated at approximately 22 gigawatts). This reflects the maximum power output it can achieve at any point in time. A power station's annual energy output, however, would be recorded using units of energy (not power), typically gigawatt hours. Major energy production or consumption is often expressed as [terawatt hours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terawatt_hour) for a given period; often a calendar year or financial year. One terawatt hour of energy is equal to a sustained power delivery of one terawatt for one hour, or approximately 114 megawatts for a period of one year:

Power output = energy / time

1 terawatt hour per year = 1×10<sup>12</sup> W·h / (365 days × 24 hours per day) ≈ 114 million watts,

equivalent to approximately 114 megawatts of constant power output.

The [watt-second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watt-second) is a unit of energy, equal to the [joule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joule). One kilowatt hour is 3,600,000 watt seconds.

While a watt per hour is a unit of rate of change of power with time,[^cite_note-40] it is not correct to refer to a watt (or watt-hour) as a watt per hour.[^cite_note-41]
