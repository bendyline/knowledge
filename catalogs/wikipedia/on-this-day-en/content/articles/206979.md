**Colorectal cancer**, also known as **bowel cancer**, **colon cancer**, **intestinal cancer**, or **rectal cancer**, is the development of [cancer](105219.md) from the [colon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colon_\(anatomy\)) or [rectum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectum) (parts of the [large intestine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_intestine)), characterized by the uncontrolled growth of colon cells that can invade or spread to other parts of the body.[^cite_note-NCI2014PtTx-5] Signs and symptoms may include [blood in the stool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_gastrointestinal_bleeding), a change in [bowel movements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowel_movements), weight loss, abdominal pain, and fatigue.[^cite_note-8] Most colorectal cancers are due to lifestyle factors and genetic disorders.[^cite_note-WCR2014_5.5-2][^cite_note-NCI2014Pre-3] Risk factors include diet, [obesity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obesity), [smoking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoking), and lack of [physical activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_activity).[^cite_note-WCR2014_5.5-2] Dietary factors that increase the risk include [red meat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_meat), [processed meat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Processed_meat), and [alcohol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcohol_\(drug\)).[^cite_note-WCR2014_5.5-2][^cite_note-Theodoratou2017-4] Another risk factor is [inflammatory bowel disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflammatory_bowel_disease), which includes [Crohn's disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crohn's_disease) and [ulcerative colitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulcerative_colitis).[^cite_note-WCR2014_5.5-2] Some of the inherited genetic disorders that can cause colorectal cancer include [familial adenomatous polyposis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Familial_adenomatous_polyposis) and [hereditary non-polyposis colon cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hereditary_non-polyposis_colon_cancer); however, these represent less than 5% of cases.[^cite_note-WCR2014_5.5-2][^cite_note-NCI2014Pre-3] It typically starts as a [benign tumor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenoma), often in the form of a [polyp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorectal_polyp), which over time becomes [cancerous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carcinoma).[^cite_note-WCR2014_5.5-2]

Colorectal cancer may be diagnosed by [obtaining a sample](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biopsy) of the colon during a [sigmoidoscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmoidoscopy) or [colonoscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonoscopy).[^cite_note-NCI2014Pt-1] This is then followed by [medical imaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_imaging) to determine whether the cancer has spread beyond the colon or is *[in situ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_situ)*.[^cite_note-NCI2014PtTx-5] [Screening](22848246.md) is effective for preventing and decreasing deaths from colorectal cancer.[^cite_note-USPSTF2016-9] Colorectal cancer screening is recommended for average-risk adults aged 45-75, as screening effectively prevents and decreases related mortality.[^cite_note-USPSTF2016-9] During colonoscopy, small polyps may be removed if found.[^cite_note-WCR2014_5.5-2] If a large polyp or tumor is found, a [biopsy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biopsy) may be performed to check if it is cancerous.

Treatments used for colorectal cancer may include some combination of surgery, [radiation therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_therapy), [chemotherapy](7172.md), and [targeted therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Targeted_therapy).[^cite_note-NCI2014PtTx-5] Cancers that are confined within the wall of the colon may be curable with surgery, while [cancer that has spread widely](152509.md) is usually not curable, with management being directed towards improving [quality of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quality_of_life) and symptoms.[^cite_note-NCI2014PtTx-5] The [five-year survival rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five-year_survival_rate) in the United States is approximately 65.4% (based on data from 2016-2022)[^cite_note-SEER2014-6] The chances of survival depend on how advanced the cancer is, whether all of the cancer can be removed with surgery, and the person's overall health.[^cite_note-NCI2014Pt-1] Globally, colorectal cancer is the third-most common type of cancer, making up about 10% of all cases.[^cite_note-WCR2014Epi-10] In 2022, there were 1.93 million new cases and 903,859 deaths from the disease.[^cite_note-Bray2022-7] It is more common in [developed countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developed_country), where more than 65% of cases are found.[^cite_note-WCR2014_5.5-2]

## Signs and symptoms

At least half of colorectal cancer cases are [asymptomatic](602790.md), showing no signs or symptoms before they are discovered on a routine [screening](3211372.md).[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12] Due to the increasing [incidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incidence_\(epidemiology\)) and high [prevalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prevalence) of colorectal cancer worldwide, and the greatly improved [prognosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prognosis) associated with asymptomatic discovery, most nations screen average-risk adults at regular intervals starting at age 45, until age 75.[^cite_note-13]

The signs and symptoms of colorectal cancer depend on the location of the [tumor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tumour) in the [bowel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowel) and whether it has spread elsewhere in the body ([metastasis](152509.md)). The classic warning signs include worsening [constipation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constipation), blood in the [stool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stool_test), a decrease in stool caliber (thickness), loss of appetite, loss of weight, and [nausea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nausea) or [vomiting,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vomiting) particularly in adults aged 45 and older.[^cite_note-14]

[Rectal bleeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectal_bleeding) or [anemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anemia) are high-risk symptoms in people over the age of 50.[^cite_note-Sym11-15] Weight loss and changes in a person's bowel habit are typically only concerning if they are associated with rectal bleeding.[^cite_note-Sym11-15][^cite_note-pmid21624112-16]

## Cause

75–95% of colorectal cancer cases occur in people with little or no genetic risk.[^cite_note-Cause11-17][^cite_note-Lancet10-18] Risk factors include older age,[^cite_note-Lancet10-18] high intake of fat, [sugar](27712.md), [alcohol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcohol_\(drug\)), [red meat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_meat), [processed meats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Processed_meat), [obesity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obesity), [smoking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoking), and a lack of [physical exercise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_exercise).[^cite_note-Cause11-17][^cite_note-WCRF2011-19] Approximately 10% of cases are linked to insufficient physical activity.[^cite_note-20]

### Diet

Diet is the largest environmental risk factor.[^cite_note-Cause11-17] Alcohol use is the most evidence-supported dietary risk factor.[^cite_note-Theodoratou2017-4][^cite_note-\:1-21] Alcohol use above one drink per day increases risk.[^cite_note-Fedirko2011-22]

[Whole grain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whole_grain) intake is inversely related to risk.[^cite_note-Theodoratou2017-4] Drinking five glasses of water a day may be linked to a decrease in the risk of colorectal cancer and [adenomatous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenomatous) polyps.[^cite_note-23] The consumption of calcium (e.g. [dairy products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dairy_product)) is protective against colorectal cancer.[^cite_note-\:1-21]

Associated with a diet high in [saturated fats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturated_fat), elevated levels of [bile acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bile_acid) appear to increase the risk of colorectal cancer.[^cite_note-Fogelson2023-24][^cite_note-Bernstein2023-25] The bile acid [deoxycholic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deoxycholic_acid) particularly is elevated in the colonic contents of humans in response to a high fat diet.[^cite_note-Fogelson2023-24][^cite_note-Bernstein2023-25] In populations that have a high incidence of colorectal cancer, fecal concentrations of bile acids, particularly deoxycholic acid, are higher.[^cite_note-Fogelson2023-24][^cite_note-Bernstein2023-25]

Higher fecal concentrations of the bile acids [cholic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cholic_acid) and [chenodeoxycholic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chenodeoxycholic_acid) are associated with a high risk and higher incidence of colorectal cancer.[^cite_note-26]

### Bacteria

*[Streptococcus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streptococcus) gallolyticus* is associated with colorectal cancer.[^cite_note-BoleijvanGelder2011-27] Some strains of *[Streptococcus bovis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streptococcus_bovis)/[Streptococcus equinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streptococcus_equinus)* complex are consumed by millions of people daily and thus may be safe.[^cite_note-JansMeile2015-28] 25 to 80% of people with *Streptococcus bovis/gallolyticus* bacteremia have concomitant colorectal tumors.[^cite_note-AbdulamirHafidh2011-29] Seroprevalence of *Streptococcus bovis/gallolyticus* is considered as a candidate practical marker for the early prediction of an underlying bowel lesion at high-risk population.[^cite_note-AbdulamirHafidh2011-29] It has been suggested that the presence of antibodies to *Streptococcus bovis/gallolyticus* antigens or the antigens themselves in the bloodstream may act as markers for the carcinogenesis in the colon.[^cite_note-AbdulamirHafidh2011-29]

[Pathogenic *Escherichia coli*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathogenic_Escherichia_coli) may increase the risk of colorectal cancer by producing the [genotoxic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genotoxicity) [metabolite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolite) [colibactin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colibactin).[^cite_note-pmid32317778-30]

### Inflammatory bowel disease

People with [inflammatory bowel disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflammatory_bowel_disease) ([ulcerative colitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulcerative_colitis) and [Crohn's disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crohn's_disease)) are at increased risk of colon cancer.[^cite_note-IBD11-31][^cite_note-pmid26004415-32] The risk increases the longer a person has the disease, and the worse the severity of inflammation.[^cite_note-IBD09-33] In these high risk groups, both prevention with [aspirin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspirin) and regular [colonoscopies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonoscopies) are recommended.[^cite_note-Bye-2017-34] [Endoscopic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endoscopic) surveillance in this high-risk population may reduce the development of colorectal cancer through early diagnosis and may also reduce the chances of dying from colon cancer.[^cite_note-Bye-2017-34] People with inflammatory bowel disease account for less than 2% of colon cancer cases yearly.[^cite_note-IBD09-33] In those with Crohn's disease (with colonic involvement), 2% get colorectal cancer after 10 years, 8% after 20 years, and 18% after 30 years.[^cite_note-IBD09-33] In people who have ulcerative colitis, approximately 16% develop either a [cancer precursor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dysplasia) or cancer of the colon over 30 years.[^cite_note-IBD09-33]

### Genetics

Those with a family history in two or more [first-degree relatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_of_relationship) (such as a parent or sibling) have a two to threefold greater risk of disease. This group accounts for about 20% of all cases. Several genetic syndromes are also associated with higher rates of colorectal cancer. The most common of these is [hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hereditary_nonpolyposis_colorectal_cancer) (HNPCC, or Lynch syndrome), which is present in about 3% of people with colorectal cancer.[^cite_note-Lancet10-18] Other syndromes that are strongly associated with colorectal cancer include [Gardner syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gardner_syndrome) and [familial adenomatous polyposis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Familial_adenomatous_polyposis) (FAP).[^cite_note-pmid20141232-35] For people with these syndromes, cancer almost always occurs and makes up 1% of the cancer cases.[^cite_note-pmid19822006-36] A total [proctocolectomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proctocolectomy) may be recommended for people with FAP as a preventive measure due to the high risk of malignancy. Colectomy, the removal of the colon, may not suffice as a preventive measure because of the high risk of rectal cancer if the rectum remains.[^cite_note-37] The most common polyposis syndrome affecting the colon is [serrated polyposis syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serrated_polyposis_syndrome),[^cite_note-Mankaney-38] which is associated with a 25–40% risk of colorectal cancer (CRC).[^cite_note-Fan-39]

Mutations in the pair of genes *[POLE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA_polymerase_epsilon)* and *[POLD1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/POLD1)* have been associated with familial colon cancer.[^cite_note-Bourdais2017-40]

Most deaths due to colon cancer are associated with metastatic disease. A gene that appears to contribute to the risk of metastatic disease, metastasis-associated in colon cancer 1 (*MACC1*), has been isolated.[^cite_note-Stein2009-41] It is a [transcriptional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcriptional) factor that influences the expression of [hepatocyte growth factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepatocyte_growth_factor). This gene is associated with the proliferation, invasion, and scattering of colon cancer cells in [cell culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_culture), and tumor growth and metastasis in mice. MACC1 may be a potential target for cancer intervention, but this possibility needs to be confirmed with clinical studies.[^cite_note-Stein2013-42]

[Epigenetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epigenetic) factors, such as abnormal [DNA methylation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA_methylation) of [tumor suppressor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tumor_suppressor) promoters, play a role in the development of colorectal cancer.[^cite_note-43]

The Rectal Cancer Survival Calculator developed by the [MD Anderson Cancer Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MD_Anderson_Cancer_Center) also considers race a risk factor; however, there are equity issues concerning whether this might lead to inequity in clinical decision making.[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45] [Ashkenazi Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashkenazi_Jews) have a 6% higher risk rate of getting [adenomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenoma) and then colon cancer due to mutations in the [APC gene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/APC_gene) being more common.[^cite_note-46]

## Pathogenesis

Colorectal cancer is a disease originating from the [epithelial cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epithelium) lining the colon or rectum of the [gastrointestinal tract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_gastrointestinal_tract), most frequently as a result of genetic mutations in the [Wnt signaling pathway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wnt_signaling_pathway) that increases signaling activity.[^cite_note-Tabibzadeh-2020-47] The Wnt signaling pathway normally plays an important role for normal function of these cells including maintaining this lining. Mutations can be [inherited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hereditary_disease) or [acquired](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somatic_cell), and most probably occur in the [intestinal crypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intestinal_crypt) [stem cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stem_cell).[^cite_note-48][^cite_note-49][^cite_note-50] The most commonly mutated gene in all colorectal cancer is the *[APC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenomatous_polyposis_coli)* gene, which produces the APC protein.[^cite_note-Tabibzadeh-2020-47] The APC protein prevents the accumulation of [β-catenin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%CE%92-catenin) protein. Without APC, [β-catenin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%CE%92-catenin) accumulates to high levels and translocates (moves) into the [nucleus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_nucleus), binds to DNA, and activates the [transcription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcription_\(genetics\)) of proto-[oncogenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oncogenes). These genes are normally important for stem cell renewal and differentiation, but when inappropriately expressed at high levels, they can cause cancer.[^cite_note-Tabibzadeh-2020-47] While APC is mutated in most colon cancers, some cancers have increased β-catenin because of mutations in β-catenin (CTNNB1) that block its breakdown, or have mutations in other genes with function similar to APC such as [AXIN1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AXIN1), [AXIN2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AXIN2), [TCF7L2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TCF7L2), or [NKD1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naked_cuticle_1).[^cite_note-markowitz-51]

Beyond the defects in the [Wnt signaling pathway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wnt_signaling_pathway), other mutations must occur for the cell to become cancerous. The [p53](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P53) protein, produced by the *TP53* gene, normally monitors [cell division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_division) and induces [programmed death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apoptosis) if Wnt pathway defects are present. Eventually, a [cell line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_line) acquires a mutation in the *TP53* gene and transforms the tissue from a [benign epithelial tumor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenoma) into an invasive [epithelial cell cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carcinoma). Sometimes the gene encoding p53 is not mutated, but another protective protein named [BAX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BAX_\(gene\)) is mutated instead.[^cite_note-markowitz-51]

Other proteins responsible for programmed cell death that are commonly deactivated in colorectal cancers are [TGF-β](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TGF-%CE%B2) and DCC ([Deleted in Colorectal Cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deleted_in_Colorectal_Cancer)). TGF-β has a deactivating mutation in at least half of colorectal cancers. Sometimes TGF-β is not deactivated, but a downstream protein named [SMAD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMAD_\(protein\)) is deactivated.[^cite_note-markowitz-51] DCC commonly has a deleted segment of a chromosome in colorectal cancer.[^cite_note-52]

Approximately 70% of all [human genes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_gene) are expressed in colorectal cancer, with just over 1% having increased expression in colorectal cancer compared to other forms of cancer.[^cite_note-Uh2017-53] Some genes are [oncogenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oncogenes): they are overexpressed in colorectal cancer. For example, genes encoding the proteins [KRAS](9065739.md), [RAF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C-Raf), and [PI3K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PI3K), which normally stimulate the [cell to divide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_cycle) in response to growth factors, can acquire mutations that result in over-activation of cell proliferation. The chronological order of mutations is sometimes important. If a previous APC mutation occurred, a primary KRAS mutation often progresses to cancer rather than a self-limiting [hyperplastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperplastic) or borderline lesion.[^cite_note-54] [PTEN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PTEN_\(gene\)), a tumor suppressor, normally inhibits PI3K, but can sometimes become mutated and deactivated.[^cite_note-markowitz-51]

Comprehensive, [genome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genome)-scale analysis has revealed that colorectal carcinomas can be categorized into hypermutated and non-hypermutated tumor types.[^cite_note-Muzny-2012-55] In addition to the oncogenic and inactivating mutations described for the genes above, non-hypermutated samples also contain mutated [CTNNB1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CTNNB1), [FAM123B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FAM123B), [SOX9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SOX9), [ATM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ataxia_telangiectasia_mutated), and [ARID1A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ARID1A). Progressing through a distinct set of genetic events, hypermutated tumors display mutated forms of [ACVR2A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACVR2A), [TGFBR2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TGFBR2), [MSH3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MSH3), [MSH6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MSH6), SLC9A9, [TCF7L2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TCF7L2), and [BRAF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BRAF_\(gene\)). The common theme among these genes, across both tumor types, is their involvement in Wnt and TGF-β signaling pathways, which results in increased [MYC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MYC) activity, a central player in colorectal cancer.[^cite_note-Muzny-2012-55]

[Mismatch repair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA_mismatch_repair) (MMR) deficient tumors are characterized by a relatively high number of poly-nucleotide [tandem repeats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tandem_repeat).[^cite_note-56] This is caused by a deficiency in MMR proteins – which are typically caused by [epigenetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epigenetics) silencing and/or inherited mutations (*e.g.*, [Lynch syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hereditary_nonpolyposis_colorectal_cancer)).[^cite_note-Ryan-2017-57] 15 to 18 percent of colorectal cancer tumors have MMR deficiencies, with 3 percent developing due to Lynch syndrome.[^cite_note-58] The role of the mismatch repair system is to protect the integrity of the genetic material within cells (*i.e.*, error detecting and correcting).[^cite_note-Ryan-2017-57] Consequently, a deficiency in MMR proteins may lead to an inability to detect and repair genetic damage, allowing for further cancer-causing mutations to occur and colorectal cancer to progress.[^cite_note-Ryan-2017-57]

The polyp to cancer progression sequence is the classical model of colorectal cancer [pathogenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathogenesis).[^cite_note-Grady-2015-59] In this **adenoma-carcinoma sequence**,[^cite_note-60] normal epithelial cells progress to [dysplastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dysplastic) cells such as [adenomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenoma), and then to carcinoma, by a process of progressive genetic mutation.[^cite_note-61] Central to the polyp to CRC sequence are gene mutations, epigenetic alterations, and local inflammatory changes.[^cite_note-Grady-2015-59] The polyp to CRC sequence can be used as an underlying framework to illustrate how specific molecular changes lead to various cancer subtypes.[^cite_note-Grady-2015-59]

### Field defects



Longitudinally opened freshly resected colon segment showing a cancer and four polyps. Plus a schematic diagram indicating a likely field defect (a region of tissue that precedes and predisposes to the development of cancer) in this colon segment. The diagram indicates sub-clones and sub-sub-clones that were precursors to the tumors.

The term "field cancerization" was first used in 1953 to describe an area or "field" of epithelium that had been preconditioned (by largely unknown processes at the time) to predispose it toward the development of cancer.[^cite_note-62] Since then, the terms "field cancerization", "field carcinogenesis", "field defect", and "[field effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_cancerization)" have been used to describe pre-malignant or pre-neoplastic tissue in which new cancers are likely to arise.[^cite_note-63]

Field defects are important in the progression of colon cancer.[^cite_note-64][^cite_note-65]

However, as pointed out by Rubin, "The vast majority of studies in cancer research has been done on well-defined tumors *in vivo*, or on discrete neoplastic foci *in vitro*. Yet there is evidence that more than 80% of the somatic mutations found in mutator phenotype human colorectal tumors occur before the onset of terminal clonal expansion."[^cite_note-66][^cite_note-67] Similarly, Vogelstein et al.[^cite_note-Vogelstein-68] pointed out that more than half of somatic mutations identified in tumors occurred in a pre-neoplastic phase (in a field defect), during growth of apparently normal cells. Likewise, epigenetic alterations present in tumors may have occurred in pre-neoplastic field defects.[^cite_note-Bernstein-2013-69]

An expanded view of field effect has been termed "etiologic field effect", which encompasses not only molecular and pathologic changes in pre-neoplastic cells but also the influence of exogenous environmental factors and molecular changes in the local [microenvironment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tumor_microenvironment) on neoplastic evolution from tumor initiation to death.[^cite_note-70]

### Epigenetics

As described by Vogelstein et al.,[^cite_note-Vogelstein-68] an average cancer of the colon has only 1 or 2 oncogene mutations and 1 to 5 tumor suppressor mutations (together designated "driver mutations"), with about 60 further "passenger" mutations. The oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes are well-studied and described above under [Pathogenesis](#Pathogenesis).[^cite_note-71][^cite_note-72]

[Epigenetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epigenetics) alterations are much more frequent in colon cancer than genetic (mutational) alterations. Epigenetic alterations, distinct from mutations, change how genes express proteins without changing the DNA sequence. One frequent type of epigenetic alteration in colorectal cancers is changed expression levels of particular [microRNAs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MicroRNA). microRNAs (miRNAs) are small RNAs that bind the [3′ untranslated regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_prime_untranslated_region) of their target [messenger RNAs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messenger_RNA) and cause suppression of protein translation.[^cite_note-pmid34722255-73] Down-regulation and up-regulation of microRNAs are epigenetic alterations since their altered regulation of messenger RNAs does not directly involve changing the DNA sequence. MicroRNAs are important epigenetic factors in colorectal cancer, with 164 microRNAs significantly altered in colorectal cancers.[^cite_note-pmid22647361-74] miRNAs have an average of 300 target genes per miRNA.[^cite_note-pmid19167326-75] About 60% of human protein-coding genes appear to be under the epigenetic control of miRNAs.[^cite_note-pmid18955434-76] As an example, [miRNA-143](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mir-143) is downregulated in 88% of colorectal colon cancers[^cite_note-Ng-77] and down-regulation of miRNA-143 causes up-regulation of protein expression of its target oncogene [KRAS](9065739.md) as well as its target DNA methylating protein [DNMT3A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA_\(cytosine-5\)-methyltransferase_3A)[^cite_note-pmid19137007-78][^cite_note-Ng-77]

In addition to epigenetic changes in miRNA expression, other common epigenetic alterations in cancers that influence gene expression levels include direct hypermethylation or hypomethylation of CpG islands of protein-encoding genes and alterations in histones and chromosomal architecture that influence gene expression.[^cite_note-79] As an example, 147 hypermethylations and 27 hypomethylations of protein-coding genes were frequently associated with colorectal cancers. Of the hypermethylated genes, 10 were hypermethylated in 100% of colon cancers, and many others were hypermethylated in more than 50% of colon cancers.[^cite_note-Sch-80] In addition, 11 hypermethylations and 96 hypomethylations of miRNAs were also associated with colorectal cancers.[^cite_note-Sch-80] Abnormal (aberrant) [methylation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methylation) occurs as a normal consequence of normal aging, and the risk of colorectal cancer increases as a person gets older.[^cite_note-Epigenetics-81] The source and trigger of this age-related methylation is unknown.[^cite_note-Epigenetics-81][^cite_note-82] Approximately half of the genes that show age-related methylation changes are the same genes that have been identified to be involved in the development of colorectal cancer.[^cite_note-Epigenetics-81] These findings may suggest a reason for age being associated with the increased risk of developing colorectal cancer.[^cite_note-Epigenetics-81]

Epigenetic reductions in DNA repair enzyme expression may likely lead to the genomic and epigenomic instability characteristic of cancer.[^cite_note-83][^cite_note-84][^cite_note-Bernstein-2013-69] As summarized in the articles [Carcinogenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carcinogenesis) and [Neoplasm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplasm), for sporadic cancers in general, a deficiency in DNA repair is occasionally due to a mutation in a DNA repair gene, but is much more frequently due to epigenetic alterations that reduce or silence expression of DNA repair genes.[^cite_note-85]

Epigenetic alterations involved in the development of colorectal cancer may affect a person's response to chemotherapy.[^cite_note-86]

### Genomics and epigenomics

Consensus Molecular Subtypes (CMS) classification of colorectal cancer was first introduced in 2015. CMS classification is considered the most robust classification system available for CRC. It has a clear biological interpretability and basis for future clinical stratification and subtype-based targeted interventions.[^cite_note-87]

A novel Epigenome-based Classification (EpiC) of colorectal cancer was proposed in 2021, introducing 4 enhancer subtypes in people with CRC. Chromatin states using 6 histone marks are characterized to identify EpiC subtypes. A combinatorial therapeutic approach based on the previously introduced consensus molecular subtypes (CMSs) and EpiCs could significantly enhance current treatment strategies.[^cite_note-88]

### Microbiome and infectious agents

Several studies show that tumors are consistently associated with reduced microbial diversity and the enrichment of specific taxa that promote inflammation and immune modulation. *Fusobacterium* species are enriched in colorectal cancer tissue compared with adjacent normal mucosa, suggesting a selective tumor microenvironment for these bacteria.[^cite_note-89] Recent experimental work has shown that membrane vesicles derived from selected *Clostridioides difficile* strains can induce epithelial–mesenchymal transition in colonic epithelial cells, providing mechanistic insights into how this pathogen may contribute to colorectal carcinogenesis.[^cite_note-90]

Other pathogens have also been linked to colorectal carcinogenesis. Studies report enrichment of Clostridia and other pro-inflammatory taxa in tumor tissue, accompanied by production of metabolites with mutagenic potential and changes in host gene expression.[^cite_note-91] Other studies similarly suggest that patients with a higher "dysbiosis index", characterized by expansion of pathogenic taxa such as *Escherichia coli* and *Fusobacterium nucleatum* and depletion of beneficial genera like *Bifidobacterium* and *Lactobacillus*, may carry an increased risk of developing colorectal cancer.[^cite_note-92] Collectively, these findings support the view that disturbances in the gut microbiome can influence colorectal carcinogenesis through multiple mechanisms.

## Diagnosis



Colon cancer with extensive metastases to the liver

Colorectal cancer diagnosis is performed by sampling areas of the colon suspicious for possible tumor development, typically during colonoscopy or sigmoidoscopy, depending on the lesion's location.[^cite_note-Lancet10-18]

### Medical imaging

A colorectal cancer is sometimes initially discovered on [CT scan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CT_scan).[^cite_note-Colorectal_Cancer-93]

The presence of [metastases](152509.md) is determined by a [CT scan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_computed_tomography) of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis.[^cite_note-Lancet10-18] Other potential imaging tests, such as [PET](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positron_emission_tomography) and [MRI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MRI), may be used in certain cases.[^cite_note-Lancet10-18] MRI is particularly useful to determine the local stage of the tumor and to plan the optimal surgical approach.[^cite_note-Colorectal_Cancer-93]

MRI is also performed after completion of neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy to identify patients who achieve a complete response. Patients with a complete response on both MRI and endoscopy may not require surgical resection and can avoid unnecessary surgical morbidity and complications.[^cite_note-94] Patients selected for non-surgical treatment of rectal cancer should have periodic MRI scans, receive physical examinations, and undergo endoscopy procedures to detect any tumor re-growth, which can occur in a minority of these patients. When local recurrence occurs, periodic follow-up can detect it when it is still small and curable with salvage surgery. In addition, MRI tumor regression grades (mrTRG vs. pTRG = pathological tumor regression grade) can be assigned after chemoradiotherapy, which correlate with patients' long-term survival outcomes.[^cite_note-95]

### Histopathology

Further information: [Histopathology of colorectal adenocarcinoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Histopathology_of_colorectal_adenocarcinoma)



Relative incidence of various histopathological types of colorectal cancer. The vast majority of colorectal cancers are [adenocarcinomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenocarcinoma).

[^cite_note-96]



Micrograph of colorectal adenocarcinoma, showing "dirty necrosis"

The [histopathologic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Histopathology) characteristics of the [tumor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tumor) are reported from the analysis of tissue taken from a biopsy or surgery. A pathology report describes the microscopic characteristics of the tumor tissue, including tumor cells and how the tumor invades into healthy tissues, and finally, whether the tumor appears to be completely removed. The most common form of colon cancer is [adenocarcinoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenocarcinoma), constituting between 95%[^cite_note-97] and 98%[^cite_note-98] of all cases of colorectal cancer. Other, rarer types include [lymphoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lymphoma), adenosquamous, and [squamous cell carcinoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squamous_cell_carcinoma). Some subtypes are more aggressive.[^cite_note-99] [Immunohistochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunohistochemistry) may be used in uncertain cases.[^cite_note-100]

### Staging

Main article: [Colon cancer staging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colon_cancer_staging)

[Staging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cancer_staging) of the cancer is based on both radiological and pathological findings. As with most other forms of cancer, tumor staging is based on the [TNM system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TNM_staging_system), which considers how much the initial tumor has spread and the presence of metastases in lymph nodes and more distant organs.[^cite_note-Lancet10-18] The AJCC 8th edition was published in 2018.[^cite_note-101]

## Prevention

It has been estimated that about half of colorectal cancer cases are due to lifestyle factors, and about a quarter of all cases are preventable.[^cite_note-102] Increasing surveillance, engaging in physical activity, consuming a diet high in fiber, quitting smoking, and limiting alcohol consumption decrease the risk.[^cite_note-103][^cite_note-104]

### Lifestyle

Lifestyle risk factors with strong evidence include lack of exercise, cigarette smoking, alcohol, and obesity.[^cite_note-NCI2018Overview-105][^cite_note-WHOcancerprevention-106][^cite_note-107] The risk of colon cancer can be reduced by maintaining a normal body weight through a combination of sufficient exercise and eating a [healthy diet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Healthy_diet).[^cite_note-108]

Current research consistently links eating more red meat and processed meat to a higher risk of the disease.[^cite_note-109] Starting in the 1970s, dietary recommendations to prevent colorectal cancer often included increasing the consumption of [whole grains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whole_grain), fruits and vegetables, and reducing the intake of [red meat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_meat) and [processed meats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Processed_meat). This was based on animal studies and retrospective observational studies. However, large-scale prospective studies have failed to demonstrate a significant protective effect, and due to the multiple causes of cancer and the complexity of studying correlations between diet and health, it is uncertain whether any specific dietary interventions will have significant protective effects.[^cite_note-WCR2014Willett-110]<sup>: 432–433 </sup>[^cite_note-WCR2014_2.6-111]<sup>: 125–126 </sup> In 2018 the [National Cancer Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Cancer_Institute) stated that "There is no reliable evidence that a diet started in adulthood that is low in fat and meat and high in fiber, fruits, and vegetables reduces the risk of CRC by a clinically important degree."[^cite_note-NCI2018Overview-105][^cite_note-NCI2018Evidence-112]

Consuming alcoholic drinks and processed meat is associated with higher risk of colorectal cancer.[^cite_note-w151-113]

The 2014 World Health Organization cancer report noted that dietary fiber has been hypothesized to help prevent colorectal cancer, but that most studies at the time had not yet studied the correlation.[^cite_note-WCR2014_2.6-111] A 2019 review, however, found evidence of benefit from dietary fiber and whole grains.[^cite_note-114] The [World Cancer Research Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Cancer_Research_Fund) listed the benefit of fiber for prevention of colorectal cancer as "probable" as of 2017.[^cite_note-Song2018-115] A 2022 [umbrella review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umbrella_review) says there is "convincing evidence" for that association.[^cite_note-116]

Higher physical activity is recommended.[^cite_note-WCRF2011-19][^cite_note-117] [Physical exercise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_exercise) is associated with a modest reduction in colon but not rectal cancer risk.[^cite_note-pmid19207713-118][^cite_note-119] High levels of physical activity reduce the risk of colon cancer by about 21%.[^cite_note-BMJ2016-120] [Sitting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sitting) regularly for prolonged periods is associated with higher mortality from colon cancer. Regular exercise does not negate the risk but does lower it.[^cite_note-Biswas-121]

### Medication and supplements

[Aspirin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspirin)[^cite_note-122] and [celecoxib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celecoxib) appear to decrease the risk of colorectal cancer in those at high risk.[^cite_note-pmid20594533-123][^cite_note-124] Aspirin is recommended in those who are 50 to 60 years old, do not have an increased risk of bleeding, and are at risk for cardiovascular disease to prevent colorectal cancer.[^cite_note-125] It is not recommended in those at average risk.[^cite_note-126][^cite_note-127]

Adequate [Vitamin D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitamin_D) intake and blood levels are associated with a lower risk of colon cancer.[^cite_note-pmid21876081-128][^cite_note-pmid21672549-129]

### Screening

As more than 80% of colorectal cancers arise from [adenomatous polyps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenomatous_polyps), screening for this cancer is effective for both early detection and prevention.[^cite_note-Lancet10-18][^cite_note-cdcprevent-130] Diagnosis of cases of colorectal cancer through screening tends to occur 2–3 years before diagnosis of cases with symptoms.[^cite_note-Lancet10-18] Any polyps that are detected can be removed, usually by [colonoscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonoscopy) or [sigmoidoscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmoidoscopy), and thus prevent them from turning into cancer. Screening has the potential to reduce colorectal cancer deaths by 60%.[^cite_note-Screen11-131]

The three main screening tests are colonoscopy, [fecal occult blood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fecal_occult_blood) testing, and [flexible sigmoidoscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flexible_sigmoidoscopy). Of the three, only sigmoidoscopy cannot screen the [right side of the colon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colon_\(anatomy\)#Right_and_Left_Colon), where 42% of cancers are found.[^cite_note-Siegel2012Intro-132] Flexible sigmoidoscopy, however, has the best evidence for decreasing the risk of death from any cause.[^cite_note-133]

Fecal occult blood (FOB)[^cite_note-134] testing of the stool is typically recommended every two years and can be either [guaiac-based](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stool_guaiac_test) or [immunochemical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunochemistry).[^cite_note-Lancet10-18] If abnormal FOB testing results are found, participants are typically referred for a follow-up colonoscopy examination. When conducted once every 1–2 years, FOB screening reduces colorectal cancer deaths by 16% with the intention to screen and by up to 25% with actual participation in screening; however, it has not been proven to reduce all-cause mortality.[^cite_note-135] Immunochemical tests are accurate and do not require dietary or medication changes before testing.[^cite_note-136] However, research in the UK has found that for these immunochemical tests, the threshold for further investigation is set at a point that may miss more than half of colorectal cancer cases. The research suggests that the [NHS England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NHS_England)'s Bowel Cancer Screening Programme could make better use of the test's ability to provide the exact concentration of blood in feces (rather than only whether it is above or below a cutoff level).[^cite_note-NIHCR_New_screening_pathways-137][^cite_note-Li-2021-138]

Other options include [virtual colonoscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_colonoscopy) and stool DNA screening testing (FIT-DNA). Virtual colonoscopy via a [CT scan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CT_scan) appears as good as standard colonoscopy for detecting cancers and large adenomas. However, it is expensive, associated with radiation exposure, and cannot remove any detected abnormal growths as a standard colonoscopy.[^cite_note-Lancet10-18] Stool DNA screening test looks for [biomarkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomarker) associated with colorectal cancer and precancerous lesions, including altered [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA) and [blood hemoglobin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemoglobin_A). Although it is not used as a first choice test, blood DNA testing is also available as a non-invasive screening option. An advancement in blood plasma testing improved the testing sensitivity, Shield offers 83% sensitivity detecting colorectal cancer cells.[^cite_note-139] A positive result should be followed by [colonoscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonoscopy). FIT-DNA has more false positives than FIT and thus results in more adverse effects.[^cite_note-USPSTF2016-9] Further study is required as of 2016 to determine whether a three-year screening interval is correct.[^cite_note-USPSTF2016-9]

In the United States, screening is recommended for all average-risk adults between the ages of 45 and 75 years.[^cite_note-140][^cite_note-141] For those between 76 and 85 years old, the decision to screen should be individualized.[^cite_note-USPSTF2016-9] For those at high risk, screenings usually begin at around 40.[^cite_note-Lancet10-18][^cite_note-USPTF2008-142]

Several screening methods are recommended including stool-based tests every 2 years, [sigmoidoscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmoidoscopy) every 10 years with [fecal immunochemical testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fecal_immunochemical_testing) every two years, and [colonoscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonoscopy) every 10 years.[^cite_note-Qa2019-143] It is unclear which of these two methods is better.[^cite_note-Bren2014-144] Colonoscopy may find more cancers in the first part of the colon, but is associated with greater cost and more complications.[^cite_note-Bren2014-144] For people with average risk who have had a high-quality colonoscopy with normal results, the [American Gastroenterological Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Gastroenterological_Association) does not recommend any type of screening in the 10 years following the colonoscopy.[^cite_note-AGAfive-145][^cite_note-coloscreen-146] For people over 75 or those with a life expectancy of less than 10 years, screening is not recommended.[^cite_note-147] It takes about 10 years after screening for one out of a 1000 people to benefit.[^cite_note-148] The USPSTF list seven potential strategies for screening, with the most important thing being that at least one of these strategies is appropriately used.[^cite_note-USPSTF2016-9]

In Canada, among those 50 to 75 years old at normal risk, fecal immunochemical testing or FOBT is recommended every two years or sigmoidoscopy every 10 years.[^cite_note-CMAJ2016-149] Colonoscopy is less preferred.[^cite_note-CMAJ2016-149] However, beginning in 2026, the provinces of [Prince Edward Island](23071.md) and [Ontario](22218.md) reduced the screenings to age 45, with Ontario doing so beginning on July 1.[^cite_note-150][^cite_note-151]

Some countries have national colorectal screening programs that offer FOBT screening for all adults within a certain age group, typically starting between ages 50 and 60. Examples of countries with organised screening include the United Kingdom,[^cite_note-152] the Netherlands,[^cite_note-153] Hong Kong, and Taiwan.[^cite_note-154]

The UK Bowel Cancer Screening Programme aims to find warning signs in people aged 50 to 74 by recommending a fecal immunochemical test (FIT) every two years. FIT measures blood in feces, and people with levels above a certain threshold may have bowel tissue examined for signs of cancer. Growths having cancerous potential are removed.[^cite_note-NIHCR_New_screening_pathways-137][^cite_note-Li-2021-138]

## Treatment

The treatment of colorectal cancer can be aimed at cure or palliation. The decision on which aim to adopt depends on various factors, including the person's health and preferences, as well as the tumor stage.[^cite_note-pmid21943995-155][^cite_note-156] Assessment in multidisciplinary teams is a critical part of determining whether the patient is suitable for surgery or not.[^cite_note-157] When colorectal cancer is caught early, surgery can be curative. However, when it is detected at later stages (for which [metastases](152509.md) are present), this is less likely and treatment is often directed at palliation, to relieve symptoms caused by the tumor and keep the person as comfortable as possible.[^cite_note-Lancet10-18]

### Surgery



A diagram of a local resection of early stage colon cancer



A diagram of local surgery for rectal cancer



Margins of a colonic resection

At an early stage, colorectal cancer may be removed during a colonoscopy using one of several techniques, including [endoscopic mucosal resection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endoscopic_mucosal_resection) or [endoscopic submucosal dissection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endoscopic_submucosal_dissection).[^cite_note-NCI2014PtTx-5] Endoscopic resection is possible if there is a low possibility of lymph node metastasis and the size and location of the tumor make en bloc resection possible.[^cite_note-158] For people with localized cancer, the preferred treatment is complete surgical removal with adequate [margins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resection_margin), with the attempt of achieving a cure. The procedure of choice is a partial [colectomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colectomy) (or proctocolectomy for rectal lesions) where the affected part of the colon or rectum is removed along with parts of its [mesocolon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesocolon) and [blood supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesenteric_arteries) to facilitate removal of draining [lymph nodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lymph_node). This can be done either by an open [laparotomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laparotomy) or [laparoscopically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laparoscopic), depending on factors related to the individual and the lesion.[^cite_note-Lancet10-18] The colon may then be reconnected, or a person may have a [colostomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colostomy).[^cite_note-NCI2014PtTx-5]

If there are only a few metastases in the liver or lungs, these may also be removed. [Chemotherapy](7172.md) may be used before surgery to shrink the cancer before attempting to remove it. The two most common sites of recurrence of colorectal cancer are the [liver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liver) and [lungs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lungs).[^cite_note-Lancet10-18] For [peritoneal carcinomatosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peritoneal_carcinomatosis), [cytoreductive surgery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cytoreductive_surgery)—sometimes in combination with [HIPEC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperthermic_intraperitoneal_chemotherapy)—can be used in an attempt to remove the cancer.[^cite_note-McCarthy-2012-159]

### Chemotherapy

In both cancer of the [colon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colon_\(anatomy\)) and [rectum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectum), [chemotherapy](7172.md) may be used in addition to surgery in certain cases. The decision to add chemotherapy to the management of colon and rectal cancer depends on the stage of the disease.[^cite_note-Colorectal_Colon_Cancer-160]

In Stage I colon cancer, no chemotherapy is offered, and surgery is the definitive treatment. The role of chemotherapy in Stage II colon cancer is debatable. It is usually not offered unless risk factors such as T4 tumor, undifferentiated tumor, vascular and perineural invasion, or inadequate lymph node sampling are identified.[^cite_note-161] It is also known that the people who carry abnormalities of the mismatch repair genes do not benefit from chemotherapy. For Stage III and Stage IV colon cancer, chemotherapy is an integral part of treatment.[^cite_note-Lancet10-18]

If cancer has spread to the [lymph nodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lymph_nodes) or distant organs, which is the case with Stage III and Stage IV colon cancer respectively, adding chemotherapy agents [fluorouracil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorouracil), [capecitabine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capecitabine) or [oxaliplatin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxaliplatin) increases life expectancy. If the lymph nodes do not contain cancer, the benefits of chemotherapy are controversial. If the cancer is widely metastatic or unresectable, treatment is then [palliative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palliative). Typically in this setting, several different chemotherapy medications may be used.[^cite_note-Lancet10-18] Chemotherapy drugs for this condition may include [capecitabine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capecitabine), [fluorouracil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorouracil), [irinotecan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irinotecan), [oxaliplatin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxaliplatin) and [UFT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tegafur-Uracil).[^cite_note-162] The drugs capecitabine and fluorouracil are interchangeable, with capecitabine being an oral medication and fluorouracil being an intravenous medicine. Some specific [regimens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemotherapy_regimen) used for CRC are [CAPOX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CAPOX), [FOLFOX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FOLFOX), [FOLFOXIRI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FOLFOXIRI), and [FOLFIRI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FOLFIRI).[^cite_note-Fakih-163] Antiangiogenic drugs such as [bevacizumab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bevacizumab) are often added in first line therapy.[^cite_note-l758-164] Another class of drugs used in the second line setting are [epidermal growth factor receptor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epidermal_growth_factor_receptor) inhibitors, of which the three FDA approved ones are [aflibercept](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aflibercept), [cetuximab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cetuximab) and [panitumumab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panitumumab).[^cite_note-Shaib2013-165][^cite_note-166]

The primary difference in the approach to low-stage rectal cancer is the incorporation of radiation therapy. Often, it is used in conjunction with chemotherapy in a [neoadjuvant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoadjuvant) fashion to enable surgical resection, so that ultimately a [colostomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colostomy) is not required. However, it may not be possible in tumors close to the anal opening, in which case, a permanent colostomy may be required. Stage IV rectal cancer is treated similarly to Stage IV colon cancer.

Stage IV colorectal cancer due to [peritoneal carcinomatosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peritoneal_carcinomatosis) can be treated using [HIPEC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperthermic_intraperitoneal_chemotherapy) combined with cytoreductive surgery, in some people.[^cite_note-167][^cite_note-168][^cite_note-169] Also, T4 colorectal cancer can be treated with HIPEC to avoid future relapses.[^cite_note-170]

### Radiation therapy

While a combination of [radiation](25856.md) and chemotherapy may be useful for [rectal cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectal_cancer),[^cite_note-Lancet10-18] for some people requiring treatment, chemoradiotherapy can increase acute treatment-related toxicity and has not been shown to improve survival rates compared to radiotherapy alone. It is associated with less local recurrence.[^cite_note-McCarthy-2012-159] For squamous cell carcinoma of the anal canal, chemoradiation therapy (CRT) with 5-FU and mitomycin C is preferred over radiation alone, offering improved survival outcomes but with increased risks of [acute hematological toxicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hematologic_disease).[^cite_note-djae195-171]

The use of radiotherapy in colon cancer is not routine due to the sensitivity of the bowels to radiation.[^cite_note-172] Radiation therapy's side effects (and occurrence rates) include acute (27%) and late (17%) [dermatological toxicities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skin_condition), acute (14%) and late (27%) [gastrointestinal toxicities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastrointestinal_disease),[^cite_note-djae195-171] and late [pelvic radiation disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_enteropathy) (1-10%), e.g., [irreversible lumbosacral plexopathy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation-induced_lumbar_plexopathy).[^cite_note-CCR-19-2744-173]

As with chemotherapy, radiotherapy can be used as a neoadjuvant for clinical stages T3 and T4 for rectal cancer.[^cite_note-Neoadjuvant_radiotherapy_for_rectal-174] This results in downsizing or downstaging of the tumor, preparing it for surgical resection, and also decreases local recurrence rates.[^cite_note-Neoadjuvant_radiotherapy_for_rectal-174] For locally advanced rectal cancer, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy has become the standard treatment.[^cite_note-175] Additionally, when surgery is not possible, radiation therapy has been suggested to be an effective treatment against CRC pulmonary metastases, which develop in 10–15% of people with CRC.[^cite_note-176]

### Immunotherapy

[Immunotherapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunotherapy) with immune checkpoint inhibitors is useful for a type of colorectal cancer with [mismatch repair deficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mismatch_repair_deficiency) and [microsatellite instability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsatellite_instability).[^cite_note-Bo2017-177][^cite_note-Nic2017-178][^cite_note-Borras2023-179] [Pembrolizumab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pembrolizumab) is approved for advanced CRC tumors that are MMR deficient.[^cite_note-180] Most people who do improve, however, still worsen after months or years.[^cite_note-Nic2017-178]

On the other hand, in a prospective phase 2 study published in June 2022 in The New England Journal of Medicine, 12 patients with Deficient Mismatch Repair (dMMR) stage II or III rectal adenocarcinoma were administered single-agent [dostarlimab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dostarlimab), an anti–PD-1 monoclonal antibody, every three weeks for six months. After a median follow-up of 12 months (range, 6 to 25 months), all 12 patients had a complete clinical response with no evidence of tumor on MRI, 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose–positron-emission tomography, endoscopic evaluation, digital rectal examination, or biopsy. Moreover, no patient in the trial needed chemoradiotherapy or surgery, and no patient reported grade 3 or higher adverse events. However, although the results of this study are promising, the study is small and has uncertainties about long-term outcomes.[^cite_note-dostarlimab_2022-181]

### Palliative care

[Palliative care](275509.md) can be used at the same time as the cancer treatment and is recommended for any person who has advanced colon cancer or who has significant symptoms.[^cite_note-182][^cite_note-183] Involvement of palliative care may be beneficial to improve the quality of life for both the person and his or her family, by improving symptoms, anxiety and preventing admissions to the hospital.[^cite_note-184]

In people with incurable colorectal cancer, palliative care can consist of procedures that relieve symptoms or complications from the cancer but do not attempt to cure the underlying cancer, thereby improving [quality of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quality_of_life). Surgical options may include non-curative surgical removal of some of the cancer tissue, bypassing part of the intestines, or stent placement. These procedures can be considered to improve symptoms and reduce complications such as bleeding from the tumor, abdominal pain, and intestinal obstruction.[^cite_note-185] Non-operative methods of symptomatic treatment include radiation therapy to decrease tumor size as well as pain medications.[^cite_note-186]

### Psychosocial intervention

In addition to medical intervention, psychosocial interventions have been implemented to address psychosocial concerns for colorectal cancer.[^cite_note-Son-2018-187] Depression and anxiety are highly prevalent in patients diagnosed with CRC, therefore psychosocial interventions can help alleviate psychological distress.[^cite_note-Peng-2019-188][^cite_note-auto1-189] Many patients continue to experience symptoms of anxiety and depression following treatment, regardless of treatment outcome.[^cite_note-Peng-2019-188][^cite_note-190] Societal stigmas associated with colorectal cancer present further psychosocial challenges for CRC patients and their families.[^cite_note-Reynolds-2013-191][^cite_note-Phelan-2013a-192]

#### Depression and anxiety

Colorectal cancer patients have a 51% higher risk of experiencing depression than individuals without the disease.[^cite_note-auto1-189] Additionally, CRC patients are at high risk of experiencing severe anxiety, low self-esteem, poor self-concept, and social anxiety.[^cite_note-Peng-2019-188][^cite_note-auto2-193]

#### Post-treatment distress

Regardless of treatment outcome, many CRC patients experience ongoing symptoms of anxiety, depression, and distress.[^cite_note-Peng-2019-188]

Survivorship of CRC can involve significant lifestyle adjustments.[^cite_note-Phelan-2013a-192] Postoperative afflictions may include stomas, bowel issues, incontinence, odor, and changes to sexual functioning.[^cite_note-Phelan-2013a-192][^cite_note-auto2-193] These changes can result in distorted body image, social anxiety, depression, and distress—all of which contribute to a poorer quality of life.[^cite_note-Phelan-2013a-192][^cite_note-194]

Colorectal cancer is the second leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide.[^cite_note-auto-195] CRC patients and their loved ones often struggle with the severity and difficulty of colorectal cancer in particular.[^cite_note-Lancet10-18].

#### Stigma

Colorectal cancer is highly stigmatized and can elicit feelings of disgust from patients, healthcare professionals, families, intimate partners, and the general public.[^cite_note-Reynolds-2013-191] Patients with stomas are especially vulnerable to stigmatization due to unavoidable odors, gas, and unpleasant noises from stoma bags.[^cite_note-Reynolds-2013-191] Additionally, associated CRC risk factors like poor diet, alcohol consumption, and lack of physical activity prompt negative assumptions of blame and personal responsibility onto CRC patients.[^cite_note-Phelan-2013a-192] Judgement from others, along with internalized self-blame and embarrassment, can negatively affect self-esteem, sociability, and quality of life.[^cite_note-Phelan-2013a-192]

#### Methods of intervention

Face-to-face interventions such as clinician-patient talk therapy, body-mind-spirit practices, and support group sessions have been identified as most effective in reducing anxiety and depression in CRC patients.[^cite_note-Son-2018-187] Additionally, journaling exercises and over-the-phone talk therapy sessions have been implemented.[^cite_note-Son-2018-187] Though deemed less effective, these non-face-to-face interventions are economically inclusive and have been found to reduce both depression and anxiety in CRC patients.[^cite_note-Son-2018-187]

### Follow-up

The U.S. [National Comprehensive Cancer Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Comprehensive_Cancer_Network) and [American Society of Clinical Oncology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Society_of_Clinical_Oncology) provide guidelines for the follow-up of colon cancer.[^cite_note-NCCNguidelines-196][^cite_note-pmid16260687-197] A [medical history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_history) and [physical examination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_examination) are recommended every 3 to 6 months for 2 years, then every 6 months for 5 years. [Carcinoembryonic antigen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carcinoembryonic_antigen) blood level measurements follow the same timing but are only advised for people with T2 or greater lesions who are candidates for intervention. A [CT-scan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computed_tomography) of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis can be considered annually for the first 3 years for people who are at high risk of recurrence (for example, those who had poorly differentiated tumors or venous or lymphatic invasion) and are candidates for curative surgery (to cure). A [colonoscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonoscopy) can be done after 1 year, except if it could not be done during the initial staging because of an obstructing mass, in which case it should be performed after 3 to 6 months. If a villous polyp, a polyp >1 centimeter, or high-grade dysplasia is found, it can be repeated after 3 years, then every 5 years. For other abnormalities, the colonoscopy can be repeated after 1 year.[^cite_note-Colorectal_Colon_Cancer-160]

Routine [PET](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positron_emission_tomography) or [ultrasound scanning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_ultrasonography), [chest X-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chest_X-ray), [complete blood count](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_blood_count) or [liver function tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liver_function_tests) are not recommended.[^cite_note-NCCNguidelines-196][^cite_note-pmid16260687-197]

For people who have undergone curative surgery or adjuvant therapy (or both) to treat non-metastatic colorectal cancer, intense surveillance and close follow-up have not been shown to provide additional survival benefits.[^cite_note-198] A phase 3 randomized trial of patients who had completed adjuvant chemotherapy for stage II–III colon cancer found that a structured 3-year exercise program improved disease-free survival and was associated with lower all-cause mortality at a median 7.9-year follow-up.[^cite_note-199][^cite_note-200]

## Prognosis

Fewer than 600 genes are linked to outcomes in colorectal cancer.[^cite_note-Uh2017-53] These include both unfavorable genes, where high expression is related to poor outcome, for example the [heat shock 70 kDa protein 1 (HSPA1A)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HSPA1A), and favorable genes where high expression is associated with better survival, for example the [putative RNA-binding protein 3 (RBM3)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RBM3).[^cite_note-Uh2017-53]

### Recurrence rates

Main article: [Cancer recurrence § Rectal cancer](71993835.md#Rectal_cancer)

The average five-year recurrence rate in people with *colon cancer* where surgery is successful is 5% for [stage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cancer_staging) I cancers, 12% in stage II, and 33% in stage III. However, depending on the number of risk factors it ranges from 9–22% in stage II and 17–44% in stage III.[^cite_note-201] The average five-year recurrence rate in people with *rectal cancer* where surgery is successful is 9% for stage 0 [(after pre-treatment)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoadjuvant_therapy) cancers, 8% for stage I cancers, 18% in stage II, and 34% in stage III. Depending on the number of risk factors (0–2) the risk for distant metastasis in *rectal cancer* ranges from 4–11% in stage 0, 6–12% in stage I, 11–28% in stage II, and 15–43% in stage III.[^cite_note-\:0-202]

The recurrence rates have decreased over the past decades as a result of improvements in the colorectal cancer management.[^cite_note-203] The risk of recurrence after five years of surveillance remain very low.[^cite_note-204]

### Survival rates

In Europe, the [five-year survival rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five-year_survival_rate) for colorectal cancer is less than 60%. In the [developed world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developed_world), about a third of people who get the disease die from it.[^cite_note-Lancet10-18]

Survival is directly related to detection and the type of cancer involved, but is overall poor for symptomatic cancers, as they are typically quite advanced. Survival rates for colorectal cancers detected at an early stage are about five times that of late-stage cancers. People with a tumor that has not breached the [muscularis mucosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muscularis_mucosa) (TNM stage Tis, N0, M0) have a five-year survival rate of 100%. Those with invasive cancer of T1 (within the submucosal layer) or T2 (within the muscular layer) have an average five-year survival rate of approximately 90%. Those with a more invasive tumor yet without node involvement (T3–4, N0, M0) have an average five-year survival rate of approximately 70%. People with positive regional lymph nodes (any T, N1–3, M0) have an average five-year survival rate of approximately 40%. Those with distant metastases (any T, any N, M1) have a poor prognosis and the five-year survival ranges from <5% to 31%.[^cite_note-pmid20332485-205][^cite_note-agabegi2nd-206][^cite_note-207][^cite_note-208][^cite_note-209]

[Five-year overall survival (OS)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survival_rate) in rectal cancer after modern [preoperative treatment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoadjuvant_therapy) and surgery was 90% for stage 0, 86% for stage I, 78% for stage II, and 67% for stage III according to a nationwide, population-based study.[^cite_note-\:0-202]

While the impact of colorectal cancer on those who survive varies greatly, there will often be a need to adapt to both physical and psychological outcomes of the illness and its treatment.[^cite_note-210] For example, it is common for people to experience incontinence,[^cite_note-211] sexual dysfunction,[^cite_note-212] problems with stoma care[^cite_note-213] and fear of cancer recurrence[^cite_note-214] after primary treatment has concluded.

A qualitative systematic review published in 2021 highlighted three main factors influencing adaptation to living with and beyond colorectal cancer: support mechanisms, severity of late treatment effects, and psychosocial adjustment. Therefore, people must be offered appropriate support to help them better adapt to life after treatment.[^cite_note-215]

A 2026 study in the *[Journal of the American Medical Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_the_American_Medical_Association)* shows that colorectal cancer is now the primary cause of cancer-related mortality among Americans younger than age 50.[^cite_note-216] Colorectal cancer has overtaken all other malignancies in this age group and the analysis found that deaths from colon and rectal cancers among people under 50 have increased by approximately 1.1% per year since 2005, driving a striking shift as the disease ranked fifth among cancer killers in younger adults in the early 1990s is now in 2026 is the leading cause of cancer mortality.[^cite_note-217]

### Health related quality of life (HRQoL)

Previous studies have shown that Health related quality of life are an important prognostic factor when it comes to disease free survival and Overall survival <https://pubmed-ncbi-nlm-nih-gov.vastman.idm.oclc.org/40495145/>[^cite_note-218]

Globally, more than 1 million people get colorectal cancer every year,[^cite_note-Lancet10-18] resulting in about 715,000 deaths as of 2010, up from 490,000 in 1990.[^cite_note-219]

As of 2012, it is the second most common cause of cancer in women (9.2% of diagnoses) and the third most common in men (10.0%)[^cite_note-WCR2014Epi-10]<sup>: 16 </sup> with it being the fourth most common cause of cancer death after [lung](18450.md), [stomach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stomach_cancer), and [liver cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liver_cancer).[^cite_note-WHO-220] It is more common in developed than developing countries.[^cite_note-pmid20952724-221] Global incidence varies 10-fold, with highest rates in Australia, New Zealand, Europe and the US and lowest rates in Africa and South-Central Asia.[^cite_note-GLOBOCAN-222]

### United States

In 2022, the incidence of colorectal cancer in the United States was anticipated to be about 151,000 adults, including over 106,000 new cases of colon cancer (some 54,000 men and 52,000 women) and about 45,000 new cases of rectal cancer.[^cite_note-asco-223] Since the 1980s, the incidence of colorectal cancer decreased, dropping by about 2% annually from 2014 to 2018 in adults aged 50 and older, due mainly to improved screening.[^cite_note-asco-223] However, the incidence of colorectal cancer has increased in individuals aged 25 to 50. In early 2023, the American Cancer Society (ACS) reported that 20% of diagnoses (of colon cancer) in 2019 were in patients under age 55, which is about double the rate in 1995, and rates of advanced disease increased by about 3% annually in people younger than 50. It predicted that, in 2023, an estimated 19,550 diagnoses and 3,750 deaths would be in people younger than 50.[^cite_note-224] Colorectal cancer also disproportionately affects black Americans, where the rates are the highest of any racial/ethnic group in the US. Black Americans are about 20% more likely to get colorectal cancer and about 40% more likely to die from it than most other groups.

### United Kingdom

In the UK, about 41,000 people a year get colon cancer, making it the fourth most common type.[^cite_note-225]

### Australia

One in 19 men and one in 28 women in Australia will develop colorectal cancer before the age of 75; one in 10 men and one in 15 women will develop it by 85 years of age.[^cite_note-226]

### Papua New Guinea

In [Papua New Guinea](22943.md) and other Pacific Island States, including the [Solomon Islands](265083.md), colorectal cancer is a rare cancer compared to lung, stomach, liver, or breast cancer. It is estimated that 8 in 100,000 people are likely to develop colorectal cancer every year, while 24 in 100,000 women are likely to develop breast cancer.[^cite_note-227]

## Early-onset colorectal cancer

A diagnosis of colorectal cancer in patients under 50 years of age is referred to as early-onset colorectal cancer (EOCC).[^cite_note-auto-195][^cite_note-Puzzono-2021a-228] Instances of EOCC have increased over the last decade, specifically in patient populations aged 20 to 40 years old throughout North America, Europe, Australia, and China.[^cite_note-Puzzono-2021a-228][^cite_note-Vuik-2019-229]

A 2026 report found colorectal cancer to be the leading cause of cancer-related deaths for both men and women under 50 years old in the United States.[^cite_note-siegel-230]

### Incidence by age

The incidence of colorectal cancer in younger populations has increased over the last decade.[^cite_note-auto-195][^cite_note-Puzzono-2021a-228][^cite_note-Vuik-2019-229] While advancements in the diagnostic procedure may have some impact, the reduced likelihood of screening among these populations suggests detection bias is not a major contributor to this trend. It is more likely that [cohort effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohort_effect) are contributing.[^cite_note-Vuik-2019-229]

The population experiencing the greatest rise in EOCC cases is men and women aged 20 to 29 years old, with incidence increasing by 7.9% per year between 2004 and 2016.[^cite_note-Vuik-2019-229] Similarly, though less severe, men and women aged 30 to 39 experienced an increase in cases at a rate of 3.4% per year during that same period. Despite these increases, the mortality rate for colorectal cancer has remained the same.[^cite_note-Vuik-2019-229]

### Risk factors

Risk factors associated with EOCC are akin to those of all colorectal cancer cases.[^cite_note-Puzzono-2021a-228] Observed cohort effects are likely the product of generational shifts in lifestyle and environmental factors.[^cite_note-auto-195][^cite_note-Puzzono-2021a-228]

### Preventive screening

In 2018, the American Cancer Society modified its previous screening guideline for colorectal cancer from age 50 down to age 45 following the recognition of increasing cases of EOCC.[^cite_note-Vuik-2019-229] Individuals under the age of 60 have been identified as most susceptible to non-participation in colorectal cancer screening.[^cite_note-231]

## Research

Low-quality studies of early rectal cancer indicated that it is unclear if local surgery affects the risk of recurrent cancer, survival, and complications.[^cite_note-236]

### Exercise

A 2020 Cochrane review was uncertain whether physical activity interventions such as walking, cycling, resistance exercise, or yoga had any effect on physical and mental health in people with non‐advanced colorectal cancer.[^cite_note-mcget-237] Prehabilitation programs may improve the 6‐minute walk test postoperatively, but the effect on complications, postoperative emergency department visits, and readmissions was uncertain. Results for the specific amounts of exercise needed to observe a benefit were conflicting.[^cite_note-mcget-237]

Physical activity improves aerobic fitness, cancer-related fatigue, and health-related quality of life in the short term.[^cite_note-mcget-237] However, these improvements were not observed at the level of disease-related mental health, such as anxiety and depression.[^cite_note-mcget-237]
