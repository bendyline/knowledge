**Global Affairs Canada** (**GAC**; [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language): *Affaires mondiales Canada*; **AMC**)[^cite_note-4] is the [department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_\(government_department\)) of the [Government of Canada](616255.md) that manages Canada's diplomatic and consular relations, promotes Canadian international trade, and leads Canada's international development and humanitarian assistance. It is also responsible for maintaining Canadian government offices abroad with diplomatic and consular status on behalf of all government departments and contains within it Canada's official chief officer and supportive staff for Canada's Office of Protocol.[^cite_note-5]

According to the [OECD](33853117.md), Canada's total official development assistance (ODA) (US\$7.8 billion, preliminary data) increased in 2022 due to exceptional support to Ukraine and its pandemic response in developing countries, increased costs for in-donor refugees as well as higher contributions to international organizations, representing 0.37% of [gross national income](1242956.md) (GNI).[^cite_note-6]

## History

The department has undergone numerous name changes and re-organizations since its founding in 1909. Originally established as the "Department of External Affairs", GAC has been known by a variety of names throughout its lifetime. Its current legal name is the **Department of Foreign Affairs, Trade and Development**, but its "applied" name used within government is Global Affairs Canada[^cite_note-7] often shorted in the Canadian media to simply "Global Affairs".

### Origins (early 20th century)

GAC was first founded as the **Department of External Affairs** on 1 June 1909.[^cite_note-8]

During and after [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canada_in_World_War_II), Canada assumed greater control over its foreign relations, with its full autonomy in this field confirmed by the [Statute of Westminster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute_of_Westminster_1931) in 1931. For historical reasons, the name External Affairs was retained.[^cite_note-9]

The Department of Trade and Commerce, which included the [Trade Commissioner Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_Commissioner_Service), had been created in 1892. In 1969, it was combined with the [Department of Industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Industry_\(Canada\)) to form the Department of Industry Trade and Commerce (ITC).[^cite_note-10] Both External Affairs and ITC maintained networks of offices abroad, with varying degrees of coordination among them. The [Department of Citizenship and Immigration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizenship_and_Immigration_Canada) also had offices abroad, in some cases dating back to [Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Confederation).

### Reorganization (1970s–80s)

In the 1970s and early 1980s, there were growing efforts to ensure coordination among all Canadian government offices outside Canada and to strengthen the leadership role and authority of heads of post ([ambassadors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lists_of_ambassadors_of_Canada), [high commissioners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Ambassadors_and_High_Commissioners_of_Canada), and [consuls general](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_diplomatic_missions_of_Canada)) over all Canadian government staff in their areas of accreditation. This led to a 1979 decision by [Prime Minister](24135.md) [Joe Clark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Clark) to consolidate the various streams of the [Canadian Foreign Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Foreign_Service), including the "political" (traditional diplomatic) stream, the [Trade Commissioner Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_Commissioner_Service), and the Immigration Foreign Service.

This was followed in 1982 by Prime Minister [Pierre Trudeau's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre-Elliott_Trudeau) decision to combine External Affairs and International Trade into a single department. It initially retained the name of Department of External Affairs, but was subsequently renamed **External Affairs and International Trade**.[^cite_note-11] The change was reflected in a new *Department of External Affairs Act* passed in 1983.[^cite_note-12] The 1982 merger was part of larger reorganization of government that also combined the Industry component of ITC with the Department of Regional Economic Expansion.

### Department of Foreign Affairs and International Trade (1993–95)

The department's name was changed to the **Department of Foreign Affairs and International Trade** (**DFAIT**) in 1993, about 60 years after Canada had gained control over [its foreign policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_foreign_policy) in 1931—though this change was only formalized by an [Act of Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Act_of_Parliament) in 1995. DFAIT maintained two separate ministers: the [Minister of Foreign Affairs](270882.md), with lead responsibility for the portfolio, and the [Minister of International Trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_of_International_Trade_\(Canada\)). The [Minister for International Cooperation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_International_Cooperation_\(Canada\)), who was responsible for agencies such as the [Canadian International Development Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_International_Development_Agency) (CIDA), also fell under DFAIT. Moreover, the responsibilities of DFAIT would include [Canadian relations with Commonwealth nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canada_and_the_Commonwealth_of_Nations)—though such nations are not considered 'foreign' to one another.[^cite_note-13][^cite_note-14]

CIDA had been formally established in 1968, although a preceding External Aid Office was created as a branch of the External Affairs Department in 1960,[^cite_note-15] building on roots that go back to the [Colombo Plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colombo_Plan) in the early 1950s.

### Recent developments (21st century)

Through an administrative separation of the Department of Foreign Affairs and International Trade, two separate departments named **Foreign Affairs Canada** (**FAC**) and **International Trade Canada** (**ITCan**) were created in December 2003. However, legislation to formally abolish DFAIT and provide a statutory basis for the separate departments failed to pass a first vote in the [House of Commons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Commons_of_Canada) on 15 February 2005. The government, nonetheless, maintained the administrative separation of the two departments despite neither having been established through an Act of Parliament.

In early 2006, under the new government of Prime Minister [Stephen Harper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Harper),[^cite_note-16] Foreign Affairs Canada and International Trade Canada were re-joined to again form a single department known as **Foreign Affairs and International Trade Canada**.

In 2013, included within the Conservative government's [omnibus budget bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omnibus_spending_bill), *An Act to implement certain provisions of the budget tabled in Parliament on March 21, 2013 and other measures* (Bill C-60), was a section that would fold [CIDA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_International_Development_Agency) into the department, creating the **Department of Foreign Affairs, Trade and Development** (**DFATD**). The bill received [royal assent](153067.md) on 26 June 2013.[^cite_note-17]

On 4 November 2015, Prime Minister [Justin Trudeau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justin_Trudeau)'s new [Liberal government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justin_Trudeau_ministry) again modified the name of the department.[^cite_note-18] While its legal name remains the Department of Foreign Affairs, Trade and Development, its public designation (applied title) under the [Federal Identity Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Identity_Program) is **Global Affairs Canada** (**GAC**).[^cite_note-\:0-19] Despite the change to the applied title of the department, the senior minister responsible is still called the [Minister of Foreign Affairs](270882.md), rather than "Minister of Global Affairs", in line with the terminology used in other jurisdictions and in international law.

## Nomenclature

The change of terminology from *external affairs* to *foreign affairs* recognized, albeit belatedly, a shift that had occurred many years before.

At the time that the external affairs portfolio was created in 1909, Canada was a self-governing [dominion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominion) in the [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire) and did not have an independent foreign policy. The term *external affairs* avoided the question of whether a colony or dominion—self-governing and hence sovereign in some respects—could, by definition, have foreign affairs. Implicitly, since the department was responsible for affairs with both [Commonwealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_of_Nations) and non-Commonwealth countries, all external relations were of a type, even when the head of state was shared with other nations.

Under section 132 of the *[British North America Act, 1867](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_Act,_1867)*, the federal government had authority to conduct and implement relations with other parts of the British Empire, which were not considered foreign lands. The United Kingdom and other colonial powers still routinely divided their conduct of overseas policy into foreign affairs (e.g. the [Foreign Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Office)) and domestic or colonial affairs (the [Colonial Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonial_Office) or [Dominion Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominion_Office), which were later reorganized and combined into one department: the [Foreign and Commonwealth Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_and_Commonwealth_Office)). Canadian interests outside the empire (e.g. between Canada and its non-empire neighbours, the [United States](3434750.md), [Russia](25391.md), [St. Pierre and Miquelon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Pierre_and_Miquelon), and [Greenland](12118.md)) were under the purview of the UK Foreign Office. Informally, however, Canada had had relations with the United States in particular, with trade and other relationships pre-dating Confederation.[^cite_note-28]

## Foreign relations

Main article: [Foreign relations of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_Canada)

Canada's management of its own foreign relations evolved over time, with key milestones including: the [First World War](4764461.md) (at the conclusion of which Canada was a signatory of the [Treaty of Versailles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Versailles) and a member of the [League of Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Nations)); the [Balfour Declaration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balfour_Declaration_1926); increased direct conduct of [bilateral](429154.md) matters with the [United States](3434750.md) (where Canada had its own [representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Canadian_ambassadors_to_the_United_States) since at least 1927); and finally, the [Statute of Westminster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute_of_Westminster_1931) and the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War). In terms of Canada's commercial relations, the first trade commissioner, [John Short Larke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Short_Larke), was named following a successful trade delegation to Australia led by Canada's first minister of trade and commerce, [Mackenzie Bowell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mackenzie_Bowell).[^cite_note-29]

The Statute of Westminster clarified that Canada (and certain other dominions, such as [Australia](4689264.md) and [New Zealand](4913064.md)) were primarily responsible for, among other things, the conduct of their own foreign affairs. After World War II, Canada was a founding member of the [United Nations](31769.md) and participant in its own right in post-war settlement talks and other international fora, and in most respects the conduct of foreign affairs was no longer colonial.

Over the years after the Second World War, a number of other historical traditions were slowly abolished or brought into accordance with reality, such as the practice of Canadian [ambassadors](68651.md) presenting diplomatic [credentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credentials) signed by the [monarch of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarchy_of_Canada) (including, on occasion, credentials written in French as an [official language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Official_language) of Canada); Canadian ambassadors now present credentials signed by the [governor general of Canada](38152.md) as representative of the Canadian monarch. Other traditions remain, such as the exchange of [high commissioners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Commissioner), instead of ambassadors, between Commonwealth countries. (High commissioners present credentials from the [head of government](37570.md) \[the prime minister], as the head of state was historically shared, and would not accredit a representative to one's self.) Nonetheless, by the time the change in terminology was effected in 1993, Canada's foreign affairs had been conducted separately from the [United Kingdom](31717.md) in most significant respects for the entire post-war period, or over 60 years since the Statute of Westminster.

This process was paralleled in other areas over this period, including the establishment of Canada's own [supreme court](123468.md) as the [court of last resort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_last_resort), the [patriation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriation) of [the constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Canada), and [Canadian citizenship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_citizenship) (Canadians had been [British subjects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_subject), and no citizenship *per se* existed until 1947).



[John G. Diefenbaker Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_G._Diefenbaker_Building), 111 Sussex Avenue, is home to most of the employees working on international trade. It also hosts a number of secondary and support offices.

In September 2012, the Canadian Department of Foreign Affairs and the [UK Foreign and Commonwealth Office](59653.md) signed a memorandum of understanding on diplomatic cooperation, which promotes the co-location of embassies, the joint provision of consular services, and common crisis response. The project has been criticized by leading Canadian foreign affairs scholars for undermining Ottawa's foreign policy independence.[^cite_note-30]

### International Development and Humanitarian Assistance

Global Affairs Canada funds humanitarian projects, contributes to the United Nations Central Emergency Response Fund, makes smaller emergency contributions through the [Canadian Red Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Red_Cross) and Canadian Foodgrains Bank and funds Canadian humanitarian organisations via the [Humanitarian Coalition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Humanitarian_Coalition).[^cite_note-31]

Support is guided by Canada's Feminist International Assistance Policy.[^cite_note-32]

### International trade

See also: [Free trade agreements of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_trade_agreements_of_Canada) and [List of the largest trading partners of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_the_largest_trading_partners_of_Canada)

Within Global Affairs Canada, there are several bodies that facilitate Canada's [international trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_trade) system, including the [Trade Controls Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_Controls_Bureau_\(Canada\)), [Export Development Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Export_Development_Canada), [Canadian Commercial Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Commercial_Corporation), and the [Trade Commissioner Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_Commissioner_Service). Other organizations that facilitate international trade and foreign investment in Canada include the [Canada Border Services Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canada_Border_Services_Agency) (CBSA), [Invest in Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invest_in_Canada) (formerly [Foreign Investment Review Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Investment_Review_Agency)), and the [Canadian International Trade Tribunal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_International_Trade_Tribunal), which is an independent [quasi-judicial body](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-judicial_body). The CBSA and [Statistics Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistics_Canada) collect information on all items exported from Canada, and classify these items using categories negotiated by the [World Customs Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Customs_Organization).[^cite_note-33]
