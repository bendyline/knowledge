&#x20;A **meteotsunami** or **meteorological tsunami**[^cite_note-1] is a [tsunami](31161.md)-like [sea wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_wave) of [meteorological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteorological) origin. Meteotsunamis are generated when rapid changes in [barometric pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barometric_pressure) cause the displacement of a body of water. In contrast to impulse-type tsunami sources, a traveling atmospheric disturbance normally interacts with the ocean over a limited period of time (from several minutes to several hours).[^cite_note-Monserrat-2] Tsunamis and meteotsunamis are otherwise similar enough that it can be difficult to distinguish one from the other, as in cases where there is a tsunami wave but there are no records of an earthquake, landslide, or volcanic eruption.[^cite_note-Monserrat-2]<sup>: 1036 </sup> Meteotsunamis, rather, are triggered due to [extreme weather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_weather) events including severe thunderstorms, squalls and storm fronts; all of which can quickly change atmospheric pressure. Meteotsunamis typically occur when severe weather is moving at the same speed and direction of the local wave action towards the coastline. The size of the wave is enhanced by coastal features such as shallow continental shelves, bays and inlets.[^cite_note-What_is-3]

Only about 3% of historical tsunami events (from 2000 BC through 2014) are known to have meteorological origins, although their true prevalence may be considerably higher than this because 10% of historical tsunamis have unknown origins, tsunami events in the past are often difficult to validate, and meteotsunamis may have previously been misclassified as [seiche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seiche) waves.[^cite_note-NOAA_Examination-4] Seiches are classified as a long-standing wave with longer periods and slower changes in water levels. They are also restricted to enclosed or partially enclosed basins.

## Characteristics

Meteotsunamis are restricted to local effects because they lack the energy available to significant seismic tsunami. However, when they are amplified by [resonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resonance) they can be hazardous.[^cite_note-Monserrat-2] Meteotsunami events can last anywhere from a few minutes to a couple of hours. Their size, length and [period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelength) is heavily dependent on the speed and severity of the storm front. They are progressive waves which can affect enclosed basins and also large areas of coastline. These events have produced waves over 6 feet (1.8 m) in height and can resemble [storm surge](449744.md) flooding.[^cite_note-What_is-3]

## Frequency of events

In April 2019, [NOAA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NOAA) determined that 25 meteotsunamis, on average, strike the [East Coast of the United States](89126.md) every year. In the Great Lakes, even more of these events occur; on average, 126 times a year.[^cite_note-Events-5] In some parts of the world, they are common enough to have local names: *rissaga* or *rissague* ([Catalan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalan_language)), *ressaca* or *resarca* ([Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_language)), *milgħuba* ([Maltese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maltese_language)), *marrobbio* or *marrubio* ([Italian](14708.md)), *Seebär* ([German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language)), *sjösprång* ([Swedish](26689.md)), *Sea Bar* ([Scots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scots_Language)), *abiki* or *yota* ([Japanese](15606.md)), *šćiga* ([Croatian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croatian_language)).[^cite_note-Monserrat-2] Some bodies of water are more susceptible than others, including anywhere that the natural resonance frequency matches that of the waves, such as in long and narrow bays, particularly when the [inlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inlet) is aligned with the oncoming wave.[^cite_note-NOAA_Examination-4]<sup>: 4 </sup> Examples of particularly susceptible areas include Nagasaki Bay,[^cite_note-Monserrat-2]<sup>: 1038–1040 </sup>[^cite_note-NOAA_Examination-4]<sup>: 8 </sup> the eastern [Adriatic Sea](23275478.md),[^cite_note-Monserrat-2]<sup>: 1046 </sup>[^cite_note-NOAA_Examination-4]<sup>: 8 </sup> and the Western Mediterranean.[^cite_note-Monserrat-2]<sup>: 1044 </sup>

## Other notable events

In 1929, a wave 6 meters (20 ft) in height pulled ten people from the shore, to their deaths in [Grand Haven, Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Haven,_Michigan). A three-meter wave that hit the Chicago waterfront in 1954 swept people off of piers, drowning seven.[^cite_note-NOAA_Examination-4]<sup>: 10 </sup> A meteotsunami that struck Nagasaki Bay on 31 March 1979 achieved a maximum wave height of 5 meters (16 ft); there were three fatalities.[^cite_note-NOAA_Examination-4] In June 2013, a [derecho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derecho) off the New Jersey coast triggered a widespread meteotsunami event, where tide gauges along the East Coast, Puerto Rico and Bermuda reported "tsunami-like" conditions. The peak wave [amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplitude) was 1 foot (0.30 m) above normal sea level in Newport, Rhode Island. In New Jersey, divers were pulled over a [breakwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breakwater_\(structure\)) and three people were swept off a jetty, two seriously injured, when a six-foot wave struck the [Barnegat Inlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnegat_Inlet).[^cite_note-Events-5][^cite_note-Examples-16]

In June, 2025, a suspected meteotsunami occurred in [Lake Superior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Superior) and was videoed on the eastern side of [Thunder Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thunder_Bay) in [Ontario](22218.md).[^cite_note-ThunderBay2025-17] Canadian scientists are analyzing the data, which included water levels on the lake moving up to a meter, to determine if it was a meteotsunami, a [seiche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seiche), or some other phenomenon.[^cite_note-ThunderBay2025-17]
