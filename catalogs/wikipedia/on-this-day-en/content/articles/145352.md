# Iron ore



[Hematite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hematite), the main iron ore found in [Brazilian](3383.md) mines



Stockpiles of iron ore pellets like this one are used in [steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel) production.



An illustration of iron ore being unloaded at docks in [Toledo, Ohio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toledo,_Ohio)

**Iron ores**[^cite_note-1] are [rocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_\(geology\)) and [minerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineral) from which [metallic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal) [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron) can be economically extracted. The [ores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ore) are usually rich in [iron oxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_oxide) and vary in color from dark grey, bright yellow, or deep purple to rusty red. The iron is usually found in the form of [magnetite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetite) (Fe<sup></sup>\
<sub>3</sub>O<sup></sup>\
<sub>4</sub>, 72.4% Fe), [hematite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hematite) (Fe<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub>O<sup></sup>\
<sub>3</sub>, 69.9% Fe), [goethite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goethite) (FeO(OH), 62.9% Fe), [limonite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limonite) (FeO(OH)·n(H<sub>2</sub>O), 55% Fe), or [siderite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siderite) (FeCO<sub>3</sub>, 48.2% Fe).

Ores containing very high quantities of hematite or magnetite (typically greater than about 60% iron) are known as natural ore or [direct shipping ore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_shipping_ore), and can be fed directly into iron-making [blast furnaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blast_furnace). Iron ore is the [raw material](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raw_material) used to make [pig iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pig_iron), which is one of the primary raw materials to make [steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel) — 98% of the mined iron ore is used to make steel.[^cite_note-\:4-2] In 2011 the *[Financial Times](136566.md)* quoted Christopher LaFemina, mining analyst at Barclays Capital, saying that iron ore is "more integral to the [global economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_economy) than any other commodity, except perhaps [oil](23195.md)."[^cite_note-Iron_ore_pricing-3]

Elemental iron is virtually absent on the [Earth](9228.md)'s surface except as iron-nickel [alloys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alloy) [from meteorites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteoric_iron) and sporadic forms of deep mantle [xenoliths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenolith). Although iron is the fourth most abundant element in [Earth's crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_crust), composing about 5% by weight,[^cite_note-4] the vast majority is bound in [silicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicate) or, more rarely, [carbonate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonate) minerals, and smelting pure iron from these minerals would require a prohibitive amount of energy. Therefore, all sources of iron used by human industry exploit comparatively rarer iron [oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxide) minerals, primarily [hematite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hematite).

Prehistoric societies used [laterite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laterite) as a source of iron ore.[^cite_note-5] Before the industrial revolution, most iron was obtained from widely available [goethite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goethite) or [bog ore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bog_ore), for example, during the [American Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolution) and the [Napoleonic Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars). Historically, much of the iron ore used by [industrialized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrialized) societies has been mined from predominantly hematite deposits with grades of around 70% Fe. These deposits are commonly referred to as "direct shipping ores" or "natural ores". Increasing iron ore demand, coupled with the depletion of high-grade hematite ores in the United States, led after [World War II](32927.md) to the development of lower-grade iron ore sources, principally the use of [magnetite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetite) and [taconite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taconite).

Iron ore mining methods vary by the type of ore being mined. There are four main types of iron ore deposits worked currently, depending on the [mineralogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineralogy) and geology of the ore deposits. These are magnetite, [titanomagnetite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titanomagnetite), hematite, and [pisolitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisolite) ironstone deposits.[^cite_note-\:4-2]

The origin of iron can be ultimately traced to its formation through nuclear fusion in stars. Most of the iron is thought to have originated in dying stars that are large enough to explode as [supernovae](27680.md).[^cite_note-6] The [Earth's core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_structure_of_Earth) is thought to consist mainly of iron, but this is inaccessible from the surface. Some [iron meteorites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_meteorite) are thought to have originated from [asteroids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M-type_asteroid) 1,000 km (600 mi) in diameter or larger.[^cite_note-7]

### Banded iron formations

Main article: [Banded iron formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banded_iron_formation)



Banded iron rock, estimated at being 2.1 billion years old



Processed [taconite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taconite) pellets with reddish surface oxidation used in [steelmaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steelmaking) with a [US quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarter_\(United_States_coin\)) (24 mm \[0.94 in] diameter) shown for scale

[Banded iron formations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banded_iron_formation) (BIFs) are [sedimentary rocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedimentary_rock) containing more than 15% iron composed predominantly of thinly-bedded iron minerals and [silica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silica) (as [quartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartz)). Banded iron formations occur exclusively in [Precambrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precambrian) rocks, and are commonly weakly-to-intensely [metamorphosed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metamorphism). Banded iron formations may contain iron in [carbonates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonate_mineral) ([siderite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siderite) or [ankerite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ankerite)) or [silicates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicate_mineral) ([minnesotaite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minnesotaite), [greenalite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenalite), or [grunerite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grunerite)), but in those mined as iron ores, [oxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxide_mineral) ([magnetite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetite) or [hematite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hematite)) are the principal iron mineral.[^cite_note-8] Banded iron formations are known as *[taconite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taconite)* within North America.

The mining involves moving tremendous amounts of ore and waste. The waste comes in two forms: non-ore bedrock in the mine ([overburden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overburden) or interburden locally known as mullock), and unwanted minerals, which are an intrinsic part of the ore rock itself ([gangue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gangue)). The mullock is mined and piled in [waste dumps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overburden), and the gangue is separated during the [beneficiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beneficiation) process and is removed as [tailings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tailings). Taconite tailings are mostly the mineral [quartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartz), which is chemically inert. This material is stored in large, regulated water settling ponds.

### Magnetite ores

The key parameters for magnetite ore being economic are the [crystallinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallinity) of the magnetite, the grade of the iron within the banded iron formation host rock, and the contaminant elements which exist within the magnetite concentrate. The size and strip ratio of most magnetite resources are irrelevant, as a banded iron formation can be hundreds of meters thick, extend hundreds of kilometers along [strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strike_and_dip), and can easily come to more than three billion or more tonnes of contained ore.

The typical grade of iron at which a magnetite-bearing banded iron formation becomes economic is roughly 25% iron, which can generally yield a 33% to 40% recovery of magnetite by weight, to produce a concentrate grading over 64% iron by weight. The typical magnetite iron ore concentrate has less than 0.1% [phosphorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphorus), 3–7% [silica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silica), and less than 3% [aluminium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium).

As of 2019, magnetite iron ore is mined in [Minnesota](19590.md) and [Michigan](18859.md) in the [United States](3434750.md), eastern [Canada](5042916.md), and northern [Sweden](5058739.md).[^cite_note-9] Magnetite-bearing banded iron formation is mined extensively in [Brazil](3383.md) as of 2019, which exports significant quantities to [Asia](689.md), and there is a nascent and large magnetite iron ore industry in [Australia](4689264.md).[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11]

### Direct-shipping (hematite) ores

Direct-shipping iron ore (DSO) deposits (typically composed of [hematite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hematite)) are currently exploited on all continents except [Antarctica](18959138.md), with the largest intensity in [South America](26769.md), Australia, and Asia. Most large hematite iron ore deposits are sourced from altered banded iron formations and (rarely) igneous accumulations.

DSO deposits are typically rarer than the magnetite-bearing BIF or other rocks which form their primary source, or protolith rock, but are considerably cheaper to mine and process as they require less [beneficiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beneficiation) due to the higher iron content. However, DSO ores can contain significantly higher concentrations of penalty elements, typically being higher in phosphorus, water content (especially [pisolite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisolite) sedimentary accumulations), and aluminium ([clays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clay_mineral) within pisolites). Export-grade DSO ores are generally in the 62–64% Fe range.[^cite_note-12]

### Magmatic magnetite ore deposits

[Granite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granite) and [ultrapotassic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrapotassic) [igneous rocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igneous_rock) were sometimes used to segregate [magnetite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetite) crystals and form masses of magnetite suitable for economic concentration.[^cite_note-13] A few iron ore deposits, notably in [Chile](5489.md), are formed from [volcanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic) flows containing significant accumulations of magnetite [phenocrysts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenocryst).[^cite_note-ChileIronOxideLava-14]

## Mine tailings

For every one ton of iron ore concentrate produced, approximately 2.5–3.0 tons of iron ore [tailings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tailings) will be discharged. Statistics show that there are 130 million tons of iron ore tailings discharged every year. If, for example, the mine tailings contain an average of approximately 11% iron, there would be approximately 1.41 million tons of iron wasted annually.[^cite_note-\:0-15] These tailings are also high in other useful metals such as [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper), [nickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel), and [cobalt](24580536.md),[^cite_note-16] and they can be used for [road-building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Road_building) materials like pavement and filler and building materials such as cement, low-grade glass, and wall materials.[^cite_note-\:0-15][^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18] While tailings are a relatively low-grade ore, they are also inexpensive to collect, as they do not have to be mined. Because of this, companies such as [Magnetation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetation_\(iron_ore\)) have started reclamation projects where they use iron ore tailings as a source of metallic iron.[^cite_note-\:0-15]

The two main methods of recycling iron from iron ore tailings are magnetizing roasting and direct reduction. Magnetizing roasting uses temperatures between 700 and 900 °C (1,300 and 1,700 °F) for a time of under 1 hour to produce an iron concentrate (Fe<sub>3</sub>O<sub>4</sub>) to be used for iron smelting. For magnetizing roasting, it is important to have a reducing atmosphere to prevent oxidization and the formation of [Fe<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron\(III\)_oxide) because it is harder to separate as it is less magnetic.[^cite_note-\:0-15][^cite_note-19] Direct reduction uses hotter temperatures of over 1,000 °C (1,800 °F) and longer times of 2–5 hours. Direct reduction is used to produce [sponge iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_reduced_iron) (Fe) to be used for steel-making. Direct reduction requires more energy, as the temperatures are higher and the time is longer, and it requires more reducing agent than magnetizing roasting.[^cite_note-\:0-15][^cite_note-20][^cite_note-21]

Lower-grade sources of iron ore generally require [beneficiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beneficiation), using techniques like crushing, [milling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mill_\(grinding\)), [gravity or heavy media separation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_separation), screening, and silica [froth flotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Froth_flotation) to improve the concentration of the ore and remove impurities. The results, high-quality fine ore powders, are known as fines.

### Magnetite

[Magnetite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetite) is [magnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic), and hence easily separated from the [gangue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gangue) minerals and capable of producing a high-grade concentrate with very low levels of impurities.

The grain size of the magnetite and its degree of commingling with the silica [groundmass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_\(geology\)) determine the grind size to which the rock must be comminuted to enable efficient magnetic separation to provide a high-purity magnetite concentrate. This determines the energy inputs required to run a milling operation.

Mining of banded iron formations involves coarse crushing and screening, followed by rough crushing and fine grinding to [comminute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comminution) the ore to the point where the crystallized magnetite and quartz are fine enough that the quartz is left behind when the resultant powder is passed under a magnetic separator.

Generally, most magnetite banded iron formation deposits must be ground to between 32 and 45 μm (0.0013 and 0.0018 in) to produce a low-silica magnetite concentrate. Magnetite concentrate grades are generally more than 70% iron by weight and usually are low in phosphorus, aluminium, titanium, and silica, and demand a premium price.

### Hematite

Due to the high [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density) of [hematite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hematite) relative to associated [silicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicate) gangue, hematite beneficiation usually involves a combination of beneficiation techniques. One method relies on passing the finely-crushed [ore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ore) over a slurry containing [magnetite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetite) or other agent such as [ferrosilicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferrosilicon), which increases its density. When the density of the slurry is calibrated correctly, the hematite will sink and the [silicate mineral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicate_mineral) fragments will float and can be removed.[^cite_note-22]

Iron ore represents 93% of metals mined worldwide in 2021.[^cite_note-27] Steel, of which iron is the key ingredient, represents almost 95% of all metal used per year.[^cite_note-Iron_ore_pricing-3] It is used primarily in structures, ships, automobiles, and machinery.

Iron-rich rocks are common worldwide, but ore-grade commercial [mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining) operations are dominated by the countries listed in the table aside. The major constraint to economics for iron ore deposits is not necessarily the grade or size of the deposits, because it is not particularly hard to geologically prove enough tonnage of the rocks exists. The primary constraint is the position of the iron ore relative to the market, the cost of rail infrastructure to get it to market, and the energy cost required to do so.

Mining iron ore is a high-volume, low-margin business, as the value of iron is significantly lower than that of base metals.[^cite_note-Iron_ore_interactive-28] It is highly capital-intensive and requires significant investment in infrastructure, such as rail, to transport the ore from the mine to a freight ship.[^cite_note-Iron_ore_interactive-28] For these reasons, iron ore production is concentrated in the hands of a few major players.

World production averages 2 billion tonnes (2.0 billion long tons; 2.2 billion short tons) of raw ore annually. The world's largest producer of iron ore is the Brazilian mining corporation [Vale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vale_\(company\)), followed by Australian companies [Rio Tinto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rio_Tinto_\(corporation\)) and [BHP](200825.md). A further Australian supplier, [Fortescue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortescue_\(company\)), has helped bring Australia's production to first in the world.

The seaborne trade in iron ore—that is, iron ore to be shipped to other countries—was 849 million tonnes (836 million long tons; 936 million short tons) in 2004.[^cite_note-Iron_ore_interactive-28] Australia and Brazil dominate the seaborne trade, with 72% of the market.[^cite_note-Iron_ore_interactive-28] BHP, Rio and Vale control 66% of this market between them.[^cite_note-Iron_ore_interactive-28]

In [Australia](4689264.md), iron ore is won from three primary sources: pisolite "[channel iron deposit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel-iron_deposits)" ore derived by mechanical erosion of primary banded-iron formations and accumulated in alluvial channels such as at [Pannawonica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pannawonica); and the dominant metasomatically altered [banded iron formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banded_iron_formation)-related ores such as at [Newman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newman,_Western_Australia), the [Chichester Range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chichester_Range), the [Hamersley Range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamersley_Range) and [Koolyanobbing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koolyanobbing), [Western Australia](33613.md). Other types of ore are coming to the fore recently, such as oxidised ferruginous hardcaps, for instance [laterite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laterite) iron ore deposits near [Lake Argyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Argyle) in Western Australia.

The total recoverable reserves of iron ore in [India](14533.md) are about 9.6 billion tonnes (9.4 billion long tons; 10.6 billion short tons) of [hematite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hematite) and 3.4 billion tonnes (3.3 billion long tons; 3.7 billion short tons) of [magnetite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetite).[^cite_note-29] [Chhattisgarh](47734.md), [Madhya Pradesh](47945.md), [Karnataka](16880.md), [Jharkhand](197225.md), [Odisha](250724.md), [Goa](40010153.md), [Maharashtra](20629.md), [Andhra Pradesh](2377.md), [Kerala](4349459.md), [Rajasthan](26291.md), and [Tamil Nadu](29918.md) are the principal Indian producers of iron ore. World consumption of iron ore grows 10% per year  on average, with the main consumers being China, Japan, Korea, the United States, and the European Union.

China is currently the largest consumer of iron ore, which translates to being the world's largest steel-producing country. It is also the largest importer, buying 52% of the seaborne trade in iron ore in 2004.[^cite_note-Iron_ore_interactive-28] China is followed by Japan and Korea, which consume a significant amount of raw iron ore and [metallurgical coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metallurgical_coal). In 2006, China produced 588 million tonnes (579 million long tons; 648 million short tons) of iron ore, with an annual growth of 38%.

Over the last 40 years, iron ore prices have been decided in closed-door negotiations between the small handful of miners and [steelmakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steelmaking), which dominate both spot and contract markets. Until 2006, prices were determined in annual benchmark negotiations between the leading iron ore producers ([BHP](200825.md), [Rio Tinto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rio_Tinto_\(corporation\)), and [Vale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vale_\(company\))) and Japanese importers.[^cite_note-\:02-32]<sup>: 31 </sup> In 2006, Chinese company [Baosteel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baosteel) began handling negotiations for the importer side.[^cite_note-\:02-32]<sup>: 31 </sup> The Chinese government replaced Baosteel with China Iron and Steel Association as lead negotiator in 2009.[^cite_note-\:02-32]<sup>: 109 </sup> Traditionally, the first deal reached between the major producers and the major importers sets a benchmark to be followed by the rest of the industry.[^cite_note-Iron_ore_pricing-3]

[Singapore Mercantile Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singapore_Mercantile_Exchange) (SMX) has launched the world's first global iron ore futures contract, based on the [Metal Bulletin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal_Bulletin) Iron Ore Index (MBIOI) which uses daily price data from a broad spectrum of industry participants and independent Chinese steel consultancy and data provider Shanghai Steelhome's widespread contact base of steel producers and iron ore traders across China.[^cite_note-33] The futures contract has seen monthly volumes over 1.5 million tonnes (1.5 million long tons; 1.7 million short tons) after eight months of trading.[^cite_note-34]

This move follows a switch to index-based quarterly pricing by the world's three largest iron ore miners—[Vale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vale_\(company\)), [Rio Tinto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rio_Tinto_\(corporation\)), and [BHP](200825.md)—in early 2010, breaking a 40-year tradition of benchmark annual pricing.[^cite_note-35]

### Fe benchmark

The global iron ore market has historically used a [benchmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benchmark_price) based on ore with an iron (Fe) content of 62 percent to set pricing standards. However, in recent decades, the average iron content of mined ore has gradually declined, while the presence of impurities has increased. Consequently, the 62 percent Fe benchmark no longer accurately represents the typical ore traded in the market, which is more commonly closer to 61 percent Fe. In response to these changes, the iron ore industry is scheduled to transition to a new pricing benchmark based on 61 percent Fe ore starting in 2026.[^cite_note-36]

## Abundance by country

### Available world iron ore resources

Iron is the most abundant element on Earth, but not in the crust.[^cite_note-pnas71_12_6973-37] The extent of the accessible iron ore reserves is not known, though [Lester Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lester_R._Brown) of the [Worldwatch Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worldwatch_Institute) suggested in 2006 that iron ore could run out within 64 years (that is, by 2070), based on 2% growth in demand per year.[^cite_note-Brown-38]

### Australia

In 2018, [Geoscience Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoscience_Australia) calculated that the country's "[economic demonstrated resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_demonstrated_resources)" of iron currently amount to 33 billion tonnes (32 billion long tons; 36 billion short tons).[^cite_note-39] Another 2021 estimate places Australia's reserves of iron ore at 52 billion tonnes (51 billion long tons; 57 billion short tons), or 30% of the world's estimated 170 billion tonnes (170 billion long tons; 190 billion short tons), of which Western Australia accounts for 28 billion tonnes (28 billion long tons; 31 billion short tons).[^cite_note-40] The current production rate from the [Pilbara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilbara) region of [Western Australia](33613.md) is approximately 844 million tonnes (831 million long tons; 930 million short tons) per year and rising.[^cite_note-41] [Gavin Mudd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gavin_Mudd) ([RMIT University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RMIT_University)) and Jonathon Law ([CSIRO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CSIRO)) expect it to be gone within 30–50 years and 56 years, respectively.[^cite_note-42] These 2010 estimates require ongoing review to take into account shifting demand for lower-grade iron ore and improving mining and recovery techniques (allowing deeper mining below the groundwater table).

### Brazil

[Brazil](3383.md) is the second-largest producer of iron ore after Australia, accounting for 16% of the world's iron ore production. After a somewhat sluggish production volume 2010-2020, partly due to the [Mariana dam disaster](48475234.md) in 2015 and the [Brumadinho dam disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brumadinho_dam_disaster) in 2019, which halted the production at the two involved mines, production has increased steadily since 2021, when Brazil produced 431 million tonnes (424 million long tons; 475 million short tons). In 2022 it increased to 435 million tonnes (428 million long tons; 480 million short tons) and in 2023 to 440 million tonnes (430 million long tons; 490 million short tons).[^cite_note-43]

The Brazilian production is expected to rise by a [CAGR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CAGR) of 2% between 2023 and 2027,[^cite_note-44] and industry analyst [Fitch Solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitch_Ratings#Fitch_Solutions) forecasted in 2021 that Brazil's annual production will reach 592 million tonnes (583 million long tons; 653 million short tons) by 2030.[^cite_note-45]

### Canada

In 2017, Canadian iron ore mines produced 49 million tonnes (48 million long tons; 54 million short tons) of iron ore in concentrate pellets and 13.6 million tons of crude steel. Of the 13.6 million tonnes (13.4 million long tons; 15.0 million short tons) of steel 7 million tonnes (6.9 million long tons; 7.7 million short tons) was exported, and 43.1 million tonnes (42.4 million long tons; 47.5 million short tons) of iron ore was exported at a value of \$4.6 billion. Of the iron ore exported, 38.5% of the volume was iron ore pellets with a value of \$2.3 billion, and 61.5% was iron ore concentrates with a value of \$2.3 billion.[^cite_note-\:2-46] 46% of Canada's iron ore comes from the [Iron Ore Company of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Ore_Company_of_Canada) mine, in [Labrador City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labrador_City), [Newfoundland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newfoundland), with secondary sources including the [Mary River Mine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_River_Mine) in [Nunavut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nunavut).[^cite_note-\:2-46][^cite_note-47]

### India

According to the US Geological Survey's 2021 Report on iron ore,[^cite_note-\:3-48] India is estimated to produce 59 million tonnes (58 million long tons; 65 million short tons) of iron ore in 2020, placing it as the seventh-largest global center of iron ore production, behind Australia, Brazil, China, Russia, South Africa, and Ukraine.

India's iron ore production in 2023 was 285 million tonnes (280 million long tons; 314 million short tons) and was the fourth largest producer in the world.[^cite_note-49]

### Ukraine

According to the US Geological Survey's 2021 report on iron ore,[^cite_note-\:3-48] Ukraine is estimated to have produced 62 million tonnes (61 million long tons; 68 million short tons) of iron ore in 2020, placing it as the seventh largest global center of iron ore production, behind Australia, Brazil, China, India, Russia, and South Africa. Producers of iron ore in Ukraine include [Ferrexpo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferrexpo), [Metinvest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metinvest), and [ArcelorMittal Kryvyi Rih](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ArcelorMittal_Kryvyi_Rih).

### United States

In 2014, mines in the [United States](3434750.md) produced 57.5 million tonnes (56.6 million long tons; 63.4 million short tons) of iron ore with an estimated value of \$5.1 billion.[^cite_note-\:1-50] [Iron mining in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_mining_in_the_United_States) is estimated to have accounted for 2% of the world's iron ore output. In the United States there are twelve iron ore mines, with nine being [open pit mines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-pit_mining) and three being reclamation operations. There were also ten pelletizing plants, nine concentration plants, two direct-reduced iron (DRI) plants, and one iron nugget plant that were operating in 2014.[^cite_note-\:1-50] In the United States, the majority of iron ore mining is in the [iron ranges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Range) around [Lake Superior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Superior). These iron ranges occur in [Minnesota](19590.md) and Michigan, which combined accounted for 93% of the usable iron ore produced in the United States in 2014. Seven of the nine operational open-pit mines in the United States are located in Minnesota, as well as two of the three tailings reclamation operations. The other two active open-pit mines were located in [Michigan](18859.md). In 2016, one of the two mines shut down.[^cite_note-\:1-50] There have also been iron ore mines in [Utah](31716.md) and [Alabama](303.md); however, the last iron ore mine in Utah shut down in 2014[^cite_note-\:1-50] and the last iron ore mine in Alabama shut down in 1975.[^cite_note-51]

## Smelting

Main articles: [blast furnace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blast_furnace) and [bloomery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloomery)

Iron ores consist of [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen) and iron atoms bonded together into molecules. To convert it to metallic iron, it must be [smelted](28716.md) or sent through a [direct reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_reduction) process to remove the oxygen. Oxygen-iron bonds are strong, and to remove the iron from the oxygen, a stronger elemental bond must be presented to attach to the oxygen. Carbon is used because the strength of a [carbon-oxygen bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon-oxygen_bond) is greater than that of the iron-oxygen bond at high temperatures. Thus, the iron ore must be powdered and mixed with [coke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coke_\(fuel\)) to be burnt in the smelting process.

[Carbon monoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide) is the primary ingredient of chemically stripping oxygen from iron. Thus, the iron and carbon smelting must be kept in an oxygen-deficient (reducing) state to promote the burning of carbon to produce CO and not CO<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub>.

### Trace elements

The inclusion of even small amounts of some elements can have profound effects on the behavioral characteristics of a batch of iron or the operation of a smelter. These effects can be both good and bad, some catastrophically harmful. Some chemicals are deliberately added, such as flux, which makes a blast furnace more efficient. Others are added because they make the iron more fluid, harder, or give it some other desirable quality. The choice of ore, fuel, and flux determines how the slag behaves and the operational characteristics of the iron produced. Ideally, iron ore contains only iron and oxygen. In reality, this is rarely the case. Typically, iron ore contains a host of elements that are often unwanted in modern steel.

#### Silicon

Silica (SiO<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub>) is almost always present in iron ore. Most of it is slagged off during the smelting process. At temperatures above 1,300 °C (2,400 °F), some will be reduced and form an alloy with the iron. The hotter the furnace, the more silicon will be present in the iron. It is not uncommon to find up to 1.5% Si in European cast iron from the 16th to 18th centuries.

The significant effect of silicon is to promote the formation of grey iron. Grey iron is less brittle and easier to finish than white iron. It is preferred for casting purposes for this reason. British metallurgist [Thomas Turner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Turner_\(metallurgist\)) reported that silicon also reduces shrinkage and the formation of blowholes, lowering the number of bad castings. However, too much silicon present in the iron leads to increased brittleness and moderate hardness.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETurner1900287-52]

#### Phosphorus

[Phosphorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphorus) (P) has four significant effects on iron: increased hardness and strength, lower [solidus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquidus_and_solidus), improved fluidity, and cold shortness. Depending on the use intended for the iron, these effects are either good or bad. Bog ore often has a high phosphorus content.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGordon199657-53]

The strength and hardness of iron increase with the concentration of phosphorus. 0.05% phosphorus in wrought iron makes it as hard as medium-carbon steel. High-phosphorus iron can also be hardened by cold hammering. The hardening effect is true for any concentration of phosphorus. The more phosphorus, the harder the iron becomes, and the more it can be hardened by hammering. Modern steel makers can increase hardness by as much as 30%, without sacrificing shock resistance by maintaining phosphorus levels between 0.07 and 0.12%. It also increases the depth of hardening due to quenching, but at the same time, it also decreases the solubility of carbon in iron at high temperatures. This would reduce its usefulness in making blister steel ([cementation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cementation_process)), where the speed and amount of carbon absorption are the overriding considerations.

The addition of phosphorus has a downside. At concentrations higher than 0.2%, iron becomes increasingly cold short or brittle at low temperatures. Cold short is especially important for bar iron. Although bar iron is usually worked hot, its uses often require it to be tough, bendable, and resistant to shock at room temperature. A nail that shatters when hit with a hammer or a carriage wheel that breaks when it hits a rock would not sell well. High enough concentrations of phosphorus render any iron unusable.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERostokerBronson199022-54] The effects of cold shortness are magnified by temperature. Thus, a piece of iron that is perfectly serviceable in summer might become extremely brittle in winter. There is some evidence that during the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) the very wealthy may have had a high-phosphorus sword for summer and a low-phosphorus sword for winter.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERostokerBronson199022-54]

Careful control of phosphorus can be of great benefit in casting operations. Phosphorus depresses the liquidus, allowing the iron to remain molten for longer and increasing fluidity. The addition of 1% can double the distance molten iron will flow.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERostokerBronson199022-54] The maximum effect, about 500 °C (930 °F), is achieved at a concentration of 10.2%.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERostokerBronson1990194-55] For foundry work, Turner[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETurner1900-56] felt the ideal iron had 0.2–0.55% phosphorus. The resulting iron-filled molds with fewer voids also shrank less. In the 19th century, some producers of decorative cast iron used iron with up to 5% phosphorus. The extreme fluidity allowed them to make very complex and delicate castings, but they could not be weight-bearing, as they had no strength.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETurner1900202–204-57]

There are two remedies for high-phosphorus iron. The oldest, easiest, and cheapest is avoidance. If the iron that the ore produced was cold short, one would search for a new source of iron ore. The second method involves oxidizing the phosphorus during the fining process by adding iron oxide. This technique is usually associated with puddling in the 19th century, and may not have been understood earlier. For instance, Isaac Zane, owner of Marlboro Iron Works, did not appear to know about it in 1772. Given Zane's reputation for keeping abreast of the latest developments, the technique was probably unknown to the ironmasters of [Virginia](32432.md) and [Pennsylvania](23332.md).

[Phosphorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphorus) is generally considered to be a deleterious contaminant because it makes steel brittle, even at concentrations of as little as 0.6%. When the [Gilchrist–Thomas process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilchrist%E2%80%93Thomas_process) allowed the removal of bulk amounts of the element from cast iron in the 1870s, it was a significant development because most of the iron ores mined in continental Europe at the time were phosphorus. However, removing all the contaminants by fluxing or smelting is complicated, and so desirable iron ores must generally be low in phosphorus to begin with.

#### Aluminium

Small amounts of [aluminium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium) (Al) are present in many ores, including iron ore, sand, and some limestones. The former can be removed by washing the ore before smelting. Until the introduction of brick-lined furnaces, the amount of aluminium contamination was small enough that it did not affect either the iron or slag. However, when brick began to be used for hearths and the interior of blast furnaces, the amount of aluminium contamination increased dramatically. This was due to the erosion of the furnace lining by the liquid slag.

Aluminium is difficult to reduce. As a result, aluminium contamination of the iron is not a problem. However, it does increase the viscosity of the slag.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKatoMinowa196937-58][^cite_note-FOOTNOTERosenqvist1983311-59] This will have several adverse effects on furnace operation. The thicker slag will slow the descent of the charge, prolonging the process. High aluminium will also make it more difficult to tap off the liquid slag. At the extreme, this could lead to a frozen furnace.

There are a number of solutions to a high-aluminium slag. The first is avoidance: do not use ore or a lime source with a high aluminium content. Increasing the ratio of lime flux will decrease the viscosity.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERosenqvist1983311-59]

#### Sulfur

[Sulfur](27127.md) (S) is a frequent contaminant in coal. It is also present in small quantities in many ores, but can be removed by [calcining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcining). Sulfur dissolves readily in both liquid and solid iron at the temperatures present in iron smelting. The effects of even small amounts of sulfur are immediate and serious. They were one of the first worked out by iron makers. Sulfur causes iron to be red or hot short.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGordon19967-60]

Hot short iron is brittle when hot. This was a serious problem, as most iron used during the 17th and 18th centuries was bar or wrought iron. Wrought iron is shaped by repeated blows with a hammer while hot. A piece of hot, short iron will crack if worked with a hammer. When a piece of hot iron or steel cracks, the exposed surface immediately oxidizes. This layer of oxide prevents the mending of the crack by welding. Large cracks cause the iron or steel to break up. Smaller cracks can cause the object to fail during use. The degree of hot shortness is in direct proportion to the amount of sulfur present. Today, iron with over 0.03% sulfur is avoided.

Hot short iron can be worked, but it must be worked at low temperatures. Working at lower temperatures requires more physical effort from the smith or forgeman. The metal must be struck more often and harder to achieve the same result. A mildly sulfur-contaminated bar can be worked, but it requires a great deal more time and effort.

In cast iron, sulfur promotes the formation of white iron. As little as 0.5% can counteract the effects of slow cooling and a high silicon content.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERostokerBronson199021-61] White cast iron is more brittle, but also harder. It is generally avoided because it is difficult to work, except in China, where high-sulfur cast iron, some as high as 0.57%, made with coal and coke, was used to make bells and chimes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERostokerBronsonDvorak1984760-62] According to [Turner (1900](#CITEREFTurner1900), pp. 200), good foundry iron should have less than 0.15% sulfur. In the rest of the world, a high-sulfur cast iron can be used for making castings, but it will make poor wrought iron.

There are a number of remedies for sulfur contamination. The first, and the one most used in historic and prehistoric operations, is avoidance. Coal was not used in Europe (unlike China) as a fuel for smelting because it contains sulfur and therefore causes hot short iron. If an ore resulted in hot short metal, [ironmasters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ironmaster) looked for another ore. When mineral coal was first used in European blast furnaces in 1709 (or perhaps earlier), it was [coked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coke_\(fuel\)). Only with the introduction of [hot blast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hot_blast) from 1829 was raw coal used.

#### Ore roasting

Sulfur can be removed from ores by [roasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roasting_\(metallurgy\)) and washing. Roasting oxidizes sulfur to form [sulfur dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfur_dioxide) (SO<sub>2</sub>), which either escapes into the atmosphere or can be washed out. In warm climates, it is possible to leave [pyritic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrite) ore out in the rain. The combined action of rain, [bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteria), and heat [oxidize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidation) the sulfides to [sulfuric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfuric_acid) and [sulfates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfate), which are water-soluble and leached out.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETurner190077-63] However, historically (at least), iron sulfide (iron [pyrite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrite) FeS<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub>), though a common iron mineral, has not been used as an ore for the production of iron metal. Natural weathering was also used in Sweden. The same process, at geological speed, results in the [gossan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gossan) [limonite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limonite) ores.

The importance attached to low-sulfur iron is demonstrated by the consistently higher prices paid for the iron of Sweden, Russia, and Spain from the 16th to 18th centuries. Today, sulfur is no longer a problem. The modern remedy is the addition of [manganese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manganese), but the operator must know how much sulfur is in the iron because at least five times as much manganese must be added to neutralize it. Some historic irons display manganese levels, but most are well below the level needed to neutralize sulfur.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERostokerBronson199021-61]

Sulfide inclusion as [manganese sulfide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manganese_sulfide) (MnS) can also be the cause of severe [pitting corrosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitting_corrosion) problems in low-grade [stainless steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stainless_steel) such as [AISI 304 steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SAE_304_stainless_steel).[^cite_note-StewartWilliams1992-64][^cite_note-WilliamsKilburn2010-65] Under oxidizing conditions and in the presence of moisture, when [sulfide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfide) oxidizes, it produces [thiosulfate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thiosulfate) anions as intermediate species, and because the thiosulfate anion has a higher equivalent electromobility than the [chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloride) anion due to its double negative electrical charge, it promotes pit growth.[^cite_note-NewmanIsaacs1982-66] Indeed, the positive electrical charges born by Fe<sup>2+</sup> cations released in solution by Fe [oxidation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidation) on the [anodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anode) zone inside the pit must be quickly compensated/neutralized by negative charges brought by the [electrokinetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrokinetic_phenomena) migration of anions in the capillary pit. Some of the [electrochemical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemistry) processes occurring in a capillary pit are the same as those encountered in [capillary electrophoresis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capillary_electrophoresis). The higher the anion electrokinetic migration rate, the higher the rate of pitting corrosion. [Electrokinetic transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrokinetic_phenomena) of ions inside the pit can be the rate-limiting step in the pit growth rate.
