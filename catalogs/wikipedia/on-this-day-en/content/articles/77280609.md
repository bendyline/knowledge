[Keir Starmer](18681491.md)'s tenure as [Prime Minister of the United Kingdom](24150.md) began on 5 July 2024 when he accepted an invitation from [King Charles III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Charles_III) to form a government, succeeding [Rishi Sunak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rishi_Sunak) of the [Conservative Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Party_\(UK\)), and concluded upon his resignation on 20 July 2026. As prime minister Starmer also served simultaneously as [First Lord of the Treasury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Lord_of_the_Treasury), [Minister for the Civil Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_the_Civil_Service) and [Minister for the Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_the_Union).

Starmer, who had been [Leader of the Labour Party](19043368.md) since 2020, was appointed prime minister after his party had won a [landslide victory](41053306.md) at the [2024 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_Kingdom_general_election), ending fourteen years of Conservative governance, becoming the first Labour prime minister since [Gordon Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Brown) in 2010 and the first Labour leader to win a general election since [Tony Blair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Blair) in 2005, but with the smallest share of the electoral vote of any majority government since record-keeping of the popular vote began [in 1830](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1830_United_Kingdom_general_election). Under Starmer's premiership, [the government](77280613.md) ended certain [Winter Fuel Payments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_Fuel_Payment) for around 10 million people, implemented an early-release scheme for thousands of prisoners to reduce [prison overcrowding](76650342.md), settled a number of [public-sector strikes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_industrial_disputes_and_strikes_\(2022%E2%80%93present\)) and announced the establishment of [Great British Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_British_Energy). He also in order to stop the industry from collapsing announced the nationalisation of Great British Steel. [Rachel Reeves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rachel_Reeves) introduced the largest tax rises at a budget since [March 1993](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_1993_United_Kingdom_budget) in [her October 2024 budget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/October_2024_United_Kingdom_budget). Starmer announced the [Border Security Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Border_Security_Command) to replace the defunct [Rwanda asylum plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rwanda_asylum_plan) and the National Violent Disorder Programme in response to the [2024 riots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_Kingdom_riots), as well as reforms to [workers' rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Workers'_rights). In foreign policy, Starmer [supported Ukraine against the Russian invasion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_and_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war) and initially [supported Israel in the Gaza war](76740255.md), but later called for a ceasefire in the [Gaza Strip](12047.md) and condemned Israel's actions. In September 2025, his government [formally recognised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_recognition_of_Palestine) the [State of Palestine](241405.md). Starmer [responded to the 2026 Iran war](82581844.md) by shooting down Iranian drones and missiles over allied countries in the Middle East, but denied a request from US president [Donald Trump](4848272.md) to use British military bases and criticised opposition leaders [Nigel Farage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Farage) and [Kemi Badenoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kemi_Badenoch) for their immediate support for the war.

Starmer was viewed unfavourably by the British public during his premiership, due to a prolonged [cost-of-living crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_cost-of-living_crisis) and controversies surrounding the government ending certain [Winter Fuel Payments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_Fuel_Payment), the [freebies controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Labour_Party_freebies_controversy) involving donations to Starmer, raising [employers' national insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Insurance), the changes to [farmers' inheritance tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024%E2%80%932026_United_Kingdom_farmers'_protests), and the [Peter Mandelson appointment scandal](81477249.md). Starmer's approval ratings fell to historical lows for a British prime minister, drawing comparisons to [Liz Truss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liz_Truss).[^cite_note-1] [His net approval rating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leadership_approval_opinion_polling_for_the_next_United_Kingdom_general_election#Leadership_approval) began slightly positive, but fell from +5% after the election to −30% by January 2025 before levelling off until April 2025 when it began to decline further, reaching −32% by November that year. A poll found 35% of the public were satisfied with Starmer's job performance, 65% dissatisfied, giving a net approval rating of minus 30.[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3][^cite_note-IpsosSep20253-4] Starmer's average net approval remained higher than [Boris Johnson](19065069.md)'s during the [Partygate scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partygate_scandal), [Jeremy Corbyn](415036.md)'s when he resigned as Labour leader and when Truss resigned as prime minister. Starmer's unpopularity was tied to poor results for Labour in the [2025 United Kingdom local elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_United_Kingdom_local_elections). Likewise, the party garnered poor polling numbers ahead of the [2026 United Kingdom local elections](79139854.md), the [2026 Scottish Parliament election](65934215.md) and the [2026 Senedd election](68245236.md), which culminated in a [leadership crisis](83157483.md) beginning in May 2026. On 22 June 2026, days following [Andy Burnham](414818.md)'s return to parliament, Starmer announced his resignation as Prime Minister and as leader of the Labour Party, and was succeeded as prime minister by Burnham, who won the leadership election.

## Background

### Labour leadership bid

Main article: [2020 Labour Party leadership election (UK)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Labour_Party_leadership_election_\(UK\))

On 4 January 2020 [Keir Starmer](18681491.md) announced his candidacy for the [Labour Party leadership election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Labour_Party_leadership_election_\(UK\)).[^cite_note-\:5-5] By 8 January it was reported that he had gained enough nominations from Labour [members of Parliament](3776360.md) (MPs) and [members of the European Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Members_of_the_European_Parliament) (MEPs) to get onto the ballot paper, and that the trade union [Unison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unison_\(trade_union\)) was backing him. Unison, with 1.3 million members, said Starmer was the best-placed candidate to unite the party and regain public trust.[^cite_note-\:4-6] He was also supported by the former prime minister [Gordon Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Brown) and the [mayor of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor_of_London), [Sadiq Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sadiq_Khan).[^cite_note-LPLE20202-7]

During the leadership election, Starmer stood on a [left-wing](18499.md) platform. He positioned himself in opposition to [austerity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_government_austerity_programme), stating that the outgoing leader, [Jeremy Corbyn](415036.md), was "right" to position Labour as the "party of [anti-austerity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-austerity)".[^cite_note-\:13-8][^cite_note-9] He indicated he would continue with the Labour policy of scrapping [tuition fees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuition_fees_in_the_United_Kingdom) as well as pledging "common ownership" of rail, mail, energy and water companies and called for ending outsourcing in the [National Health Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Health_Service) (NHS), local governments and the justice system.[^cite_note-\:16-10] Starmer was announced as the winner of the leadership contest on 4 April 2020, defeating his rivals, [Rebecca Long-Bailey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebecca_Long-Bailey) and [Lisa Nandy](27266258.md), with 56.2% of the vote in the first round.[^cite_note-\:7-11] As [Leader of the Opposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keir_Starmer_as_Leader_of_the_Opposition), contrary to his leadership campaign, he moved the party towards the [political centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_centre), and also emphasised the project of eliminating [antisemitism within the party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antisemitism_in_the_British_Labour_Party). In 2023 he set out five missions for his government, targeting issues such as economic growth, health, clean energy, crime and education.

### 2024 general election

Main article: [2024 United Kingdom general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_Kingdom_general_election)

On 22 May 2024, Prime Minister [Rishi Sunak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rishi_Sunak) unexpectedly announced that a [general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_Kingdom_general_election) would be held on 4 July 2024. Labour entered the general election with a large lead over the [Conservative Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Party_\(UK\)) in [opinion polls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opinion_polling_for_the_2024_United_Kingdom_general_election), and the potential scale of the party's victory was a topic of discussion during the campaign period.[^cite_note-Walker_2024232-12][^cite_note-Hunt_2024232-13] In June 2024 Starmer released the Labour Party's manifesto, *[Change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Change_\(manifesto\))*, which focuses on economic growth, planning system reforms, infrastructure, what Starmer describes as "clean energy", healthcare, education, childcare and strengthening workers' rights.[^cite_note-Change-14][^cite_note-x11-15] It pledges a new publicly owned energy company ([Great British Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_British_Energy)), a "Green Prosperity Plan", reducing patient waiting times in the NHS, and [renationalisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renationalisation) of the [railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railways_in_the_United_Kingdom) network ([Great British Railways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_British_Railways)).[^cite_note-Reid-16] It includes wealth creation and "pro-business and pro-worker" policies.[^cite_note-nz.news.yahoo.com-17] The manifesto also pledged to give votes to 16 year olds, reform the [House of Lords](13658.md), and to [tax private schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxation_of_private_school_fees_in_the_United_Kingdom), with money generated going into improving state education.[^cite_note-x14-18][^cite_note-x15-19]

In July 2024 Starmer led Labour to a landslide victory in the general election, ending fourteen years of Conservative government with Labour becoming the largest party in the House of Commons.[^cite_note-\:112-20] Labour achieved a 174-seat simple majority and a total of 411 seats,[^cite_note-Speaker-21] the party's third-best result in terms of seat share following the [1997](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997_United_Kingdom_general_election) and [2001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001_United_Kingdom_general_election) general elections. The party had the most seats in England for the first time since 2005, [in Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_Kingdom_general_election_in_Scotland) for the first time [since 2010](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_United_Kingdom_general_election_in_Scotland), and retained its status as the largest party [in Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_Kingdom_general_election_in_Wales).[^cite_note-bbc.co.uk222-22]

In his victory speech Starmer thanked party workers for their work – including nearly five years of revamping and rebranding Labour in the face of Conservative dominance – and urged them to savour the moment, but warned them of challenges ahead and pledged his government would work for "national renewal":[^cite_note-23][^cite_note-x19-24]

> We did it! You campaigned for it, you fought for it, you voted for it and now it has arrived. Change begins now. And it feels good, I have to be honest. Four-and-a-half years of work changing the party. This is what it is for – a changed Labour Party ready to serve our country, ready to restore Britain to the service of working people. And across our country people will be waking up to the news, relieved that a weight has been lifted, a burden finally removed from the shoulders of this great nation. And now we can look forward. Walk into the morning, the sunlight of hope, pale at first but getting stronger through the day, shining once again, on a country with the opportunity after 14 years to get its future back. We said we would end the chaos and we will. We said we would turn the page and we have. Today we start the next chapter, begin the work of change, the mission of national renewal and start to rebuild our country.

## Premiership

### Appointment as prime minister



Starmer giving his first speech as prime minister at [10 Downing Street](226294.md), 5 July 2024

As the leader of the majority party in the [House of Commons](13828.md), Starmer was appointed as prime minister by King [Charles III](125248.md) on 5 July 2024, becoming the first Labour prime minister since [Gordon Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Brown), the first Labour leader to win a general election since [Tony Blair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Blair), and the first prime minister to enter office with a knighthood since [Alec Douglas-Home](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alec_Douglas-Home).[^cite_note-25] He and his wife, [Victoria Starmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_Starmer), were driven from [Buckingham Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckingham_Palace) to [Downing Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downing_Street). Starmer stopped the car on the way back from the palace and went on a walkabout outside Downing Street to meet cheering crowds. In his first speech as prime minister, Starmer paid tribute to the previous prime minister Rishi Sunak, saying "His achievement as the first British Asian Prime Minister of our country should not be underestimated by anyone," and also recognised "the dedication and hard work he brought to his leadership" but said that the people of Britain had voted for change:[^cite_note-26][^cite_note-x19-24]

> You have given us a clear mandate, and we will use it to deliver change. To restore service and respect to politics, end the era of noisy performance, tread more lightly on your lives, and unite our country. Four nations, standing together again, facing down, as we have so often in our past, the challenges of an insecure world. Committed to a calm and patient rebuilding. So with respect and humility, I invite you all to join this government of service in the mission of national renewal. Our work is urgent and we begin it today.

### State Opening of Parliament



Starmer at [Prime Minister's Questions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Minister's_Questions), 11 September 2024

The [2024 State Opening of Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_State_Opening_of_Parliament) outlined 39 pieces of legislation that Labour proposed to introduced in the months ahead, including [bills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_\(law\)) to [renationalise the railways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renationalisation_of_British_Rail), to strengthen the rights of workers, tackle [illegal immigration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illegal_immigration_to_the_United_Kingdom), reform the [House of Lords](13658.md) and undertake a programme to hasten the delivery of "high quality infrastructure" and housing. In addition, a number of bills proposed by Sunak's Conservative administration were also included, particularly the [Tobacco and Vapes Bill](78470987.md), which had appeared in the [2023 King's Speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_State_Opening_of_Parliament), but had been abandoned after the election was called.[^cite_note-Growth2-27][^cite_note-Key_points2-28]

#### Immigration

Main articles: [Border Security Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Border_Security_Command) and [Modern immigration to the United Kingdom](1247786.md)

One of Starmer's first acts as prime minister was cancelling the controversial [Rwanda asylum plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rwanda_asylum_plan), claiming it to be "dead and buried."[^cite_note-x26-39][^cite_note-40] The [home secretary](149104.md), [Yvette Cooper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yvette_Cooper), set out the first steps towards establishing a [Border Security Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Border_Security_Command), to tackle [smuggling gangs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_smuggler) which facilitate [illegal migrant crossings over the English Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Channel_migrant_crossings_\(2018%E2%80%93present\)).[^cite_note-x26-39][^cite_note-41] The command would be funded by money previously earmarked for the Rwanda plan and would be responsible for coordinating the activities of [Immigration Enforcement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration_Enforcement), [MI5](100076.md), the [Border Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Border_Force) and the [National Crime Agency](28153284.md) in tackling smuggling gangs which facilitate illegal migrant crossings over the [English Channel](9230.md).[^cite_note-\:3-42][^cite_note-\:12-43] The new office of Border Security Commander was also established, whose remit would be to lead the new command and its members, with the government expecting the appointment of the first officeholder to be made in the coming weeks.[^cite_note-\:12-43][^cite_note-44] A team in the [Home Office](225695.md) was tasked with setting out the remit of the command, as well as its governance structure and its strategic direction.[^cite_note-\:12-43][^cite_note-45]

In the year ending June 2025, total UK immigration was around 898,000, with non-EU nationals making up the majority (75%, or 670,000). This total included 143,000 British nationals and 85,000 EU nationals.[^cite_note-Office_for_National_Statistics_data_released_November_2025-46][^cite_note-47] In the year ending June 2025, total UK emigration was a provisional 693,000, with EU+ nationals and British nationals making up significant portions.[^cite_note-Office_for_National_Statistics_data_released_November_2025-46] More EU+ and British citizens left than arrived, contributing to net negative migration for these groups.[^cite_note-48] For the year ending (YE) June 2025, the top three nationalities from non-EU+ countries immigrating on work-related visas were [Indian](14533.md), [Pakistani](23235.md), and [Nigerian](21383.md).[^cite_note-Office_for_National_Statistics_data_released_November_2025-46]

In May 2025, following [Reform UK](59757202.md)'s electoral success at the local elections, Starmer gave a press conference where he promised a significant fall in net migration by the end of the parliament and unveiled plans to curb judges' powers to block deportations under the [European Convention on Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Convention_on_Human_Rights) (ECHR).[^cite_note-49] Starmer promised to reduce the [number of migrants coming to the UK](1247786.md) "significantly", by around 100,000 per year,[^cite_note-50] stating: "Make no mistake, this plan means migration will fall. That is a promise." Starmer refused to commit to an annual cap or target as he argued such an approach would not be "sensible". Asked to give voters more than just a promise, he said: "We do want to significantly reduce migration. I am absolutely making clear that is what it will do and that if we need to go further, we will." He also cast doubt on any threat from Reform's electoral success, claiming: "I know on a day like today, people who like politics will try to make this all about politics, about this or that strategy, targeting these voters, responding to that party. No. I am doing this because it is right, because it is fair and because it is what I believe in." Starmer's line "we risk becoming an island of strangers" when discussing tighter immigration controls was criticised by media outlets and Labour backbencher [Zarah Sultana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zarah_Sultana) as echoing [Enoch Powell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enoch_Powell)'s "strangers in their own country" line from his "[rivers of blood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rivers_of_blood)" speech. He was also criticised for using the phrase "[take back control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Take_back_control)" five times, which had been used as [Vote Leave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vote_Leave)'s slogan during the [2016 European Union membership referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_European_Union_membership_referendum).[^cite_note-51] Farage accused Starmer of being "insincere" and warned he was "playing catch up" on the issue of immigration, stating "I was using take back control in 2004".[^cite_note-52]

On 10 July 2025, Starmer announced that a new ['one in, one out' migrant deal with France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom%E2%80%93France_one_in,_one_out_plan) would begin within weeks.[^cite_note-53] In early September 2025, it was reported that 3,567 people had arrived on the UK's shores since the deal with France was ratified in August, however returns had not yet commenced.[^cite_note-54] On 15 September 2025, the first "one in, one out" migrant flight, which was due to fly one migrant from London to Paris on an [Air France](14998012.md) flight, was cancelled due to protests from charities and threats of legal action.[^cite_note-55] On 16 September 2025, deportation flights were cancelled again due to legal challenges and protests.[^cite_note-56] Further that day, it was reported that a 25-year-old [Eritrean](17238590.md) asylum seeker who had arrived on a small boat on 12 August 2025 would not be deported as planned on 17 September, after he won his High Court bid to have the removal temporarily blocked.[^cite_note-57][^cite_note-58]

#### Economy

Main article: [Chancellorship of Rachel Reeves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancellorship_of_Rachel_Reeves)



Starmer with his [Chancellor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancellor_of_the_Exchequer), [Rachel Reeves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rachel_Reeves), 6 July 2024

Starmer has suggested that he favours [green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_politics) policies to tackle [climate change](5042951.md) and [decarbonise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decarbonise) the [British economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_economy). He has committed to eliminate [fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel) from the [UK electricity grid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Grid_\(Great_Britain\)) by 2030.[^cite_note-59] In 2021 Starmer and [Rachel Reeves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rachel_Reeves) pledged that a Labour government would invest an extra £28 billion per year in green industries; in June 2023 this was changed to £28 billion per year by the middle of their first term of government.[^cite_note-60]

Since 2022 Reeves has adhered to "modern [supply-side economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supply-side_economics)", an economic policy which focuses on infrastructure, education and labour supply by rejecting tax cuts and [deregulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deregulation).[^cite_note-61][^cite_note-62] In May 2023, Reeves coined the term "securonomics" to refer to her version of this economic policy, originally in a public address at the [Peterson Institute for International Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peterson_Institute_for_International_Economics).[^cite_note-newstatesman-20230607-63][^cite_note-\:02-64] It was heavily inspired by [the economic policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_policy_of_the_Joe_Biden_administration) of [Joe Biden](145422.md), the former [president of the United States](24113.md), particularly his [Inflation Reduction Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation_Reduction_Act).[^cite_note-\:1-65][^cite_note-66]

Securonomics is based on the belief that [globalisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globalisation) has failed to achieve its stated aims and that economies in the Western world must adapt in response.[^cite_note-67] Reeves suggested that the active state is part of an "emerging global consensus" which will replace the [neoliberal economic consensus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_Consensus), and that economic policy must be driven by the need for security.[^cite_note-newstatesman-20230607-63][^cite_note-\:2-68]

On taking office as [Chancellor of the Exchequer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancellor_of_the_Exchequer), Reeves stated that since there is "not a huge amount of money" her focus will be on "unlocking" private-sector investment, as she believes "private-sector investment is the lifeblood of a successful economy."[^cite_note-bbcmoney-69][^cite_note-\:52-70][^cite_note-71] She made her first statement as Chancellor two days later, announcing measures she said were aimed to grow the economy. On 29 July 2024 Reeves conducted a spending review, arguing there was a need to make "necessary and urgent decisions" because of an "unfunded" and "undisclosed" overspending of £21.9 billion by the previous Conservative government. Among the decisions she made were to axe [winter fuel payments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_fuel_payment) for pensioners not receiving pension credit (roughly around 10 million people), which received [widespread criticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_fuel_payment_abolition_backlash). She also announced the cancellation of several infrastructure projects, and selling surplus public sector buildings and land.[^cite_note-bbcnews-72] Reeves' [first budget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/October_2024_United_Kingdom_budget) took place on 30 October 2024, which introduced the largest tax rises at a budget since [March 1993](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_1993_United_Kingdom_budget) and is forecast to set the tax burden to its highest level in recorded history.[^cite_note-bbcnews-72][^cite_note-73] Starmer and Reeves met with senior bondholders including Larry Fink on 21 November 2024.[^cite_note-74]



Starmer with Saudi Crown Prince [Mohammed bin Salman](38689358.md) in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, December 2024

Starmer identified economic growth (or wealth creation) as his "number one mission" during his official visit to [Saudi Arabia](349303.md) in December 2024.[^cite_note-75]

In January 2025 Reeves announced plans for a new runway at [Heathrow Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heathrow_Airport) as part of a push for "growth".[^cite_note-76] Regulators were also asked to "tear down regulatory barriers" to promote economic growth.[^cite_note-77] On 25 February 2025, Starmer announced plans to [increase the defence budget](79277430.md) to 2.5 per cent of the British [gross domestic product](12594.md) (GDP) by 2027—paid for by cutting the UK's international aid budget to 0.3 per cent.[^cite_note-78] [Save the Children](436885.md) described the cuts as "a betrayal of the world's most vulnerable children and the UK's national interest".[^cite_note-79]

In the second quarter GDP grew by 0.3% after 0.7% growth in the first quarter.[^cite_note-80]

In April 2025 Starmer recalled Parliament from recess for a Saturday sitting to debate the [Steel Industry (Special Measures) Bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel_Industry_\(Special_Measures\)_Bill), emergency legislation to prevent the closure of [Scunthorpe Steelworks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scunthorpe_Steelworks).[^cite_note-81] The bill is seen as a first step towards the nationalisation of [British Steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Steel_\(2016%E2%80%93present\)).[^cite_note-82]

#### Budget

Main article: [October 2024 United Kingdom budget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/October_2024_United_Kingdom_budget)



Reeves holding the red box

The October 2024 budget was presented by Reeves on 30 October 2024. In it she announced tax rises worth £40 billion, the biggest tax rise at a budget since [March 1993](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_1993_United_Kingdom_budget).[^cite_note-83] Amongst the measures she announced were an increase in employers' [National Insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Insurance) to 15 per cent on salaries above £5,000 from April 2025,[^cite_note-84] income tax thresholds to rise in-line with inflation after 2028, changes to farm inheritance tax meaning that the inheritance tax of 20 per cent would effectively apply to rural estates above the value of £1,000,000 from April 2026,[^cite_note-85] and a rise in the single bus fare cap to £3 from January 2025.[^cite_note-86] The forecast from the [Office for Budget Responsibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_for_Budget_Responsibility) (OBR) stated that the budget would mean the tax burden would be set to its highest ever level in recorded history.[^cite_note-87][^cite_note-88][^cite_note-89][^cite_note-90] The director of the [Institute for Fiscal Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Fiscal_Studies), [Paul Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Johnson_\(economist\)), accused Reeves of further undermining trust in politicians.[^cite_note-91] Reeves later said that it was not a budget she would want to repeat,[^cite_note-92] and accepted that the tax rises would likely hit wage growth for workers.[^cite_note-93]

#### 2025 spring statement

Main article: [March 2025 United Kingdom spring statement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_2025_United_Kingdom_spring_statement)

The 2025 spring budget statement was delivered to the House of Commons by Reeves on 26 March 2025.[^cite_note-94] There was emphasis on increasing defence spending.[^cite_note-defence1-95] Major welfare announcements included restrictions on universal credit and its health-related element in particular, and eligibility for personal independence payments. The health-related element of universal credit will no longer be available to young people aged under 22.[^cite_note-96] 10,000 [Civil Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Service_\(United_Kingdom\)) jobs are set to be cut.[^cite_note-97] Late payment penalties related to VAT and self-assessed [income tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Income_Tax_in_the_United_Kingdom) liabilities will be increased from 6 April 2025: the new rates will be 3% of the tax outstanding where tax is overdue by 15 days, a further 3% where either tax is overdue by 30 days, and an additional 10% per annum where either tax is overdue by 31 days or more.[^cite_note-98] The budget statement raised concerns that it could risk pushing up further Britain's \$143 billion per annum in interest costs on government debt. The government is expected to spend £9.4 million on interest in the 2025 fiscal year than it did in the time up to the autumn budget in 2024.[^cite_note-99]

#### Early release of prisoners



Starmer gives his Inheritance speech in the garden of 10 Downing Street, 27 August 2024

Shortly after taking office, Starmer said that there were "too many prisoners",[^cite_note-100] and described the previous government as having acted with "almost beyond recklessness".[^cite_note-101] In order to manage the prison overcrowding, his [Justice Secretary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_State_for_Justice), [Shabana Mahmood](27266407.md), announced the implementation of an early release scheme which allowed for prisoners in England and Wales to be released after serving 40 per cent of their sentences rather than the 50 per cent previously introduced under the last government.[^cite_note-102] Over 1,700 prisoners were released in September, with further releases expected in the following year.[^cite_note-103] One prisoner released early under the scheme was charged with sexual assault relating to an alleged offence against a woman on the same day he was freed.[^cite_note-104] Starmer has defended the releasing of prisoners, and accused the previous government of having "broke the prison system."[^cite_note-105]

#### Tobacco and Vapes Bill

Main article: [Tobacco and Vapes Bill](78470987.md)

On 24 October 2024 the government announced a ban on the sale of single-use [electronic cigarettes](11996885.md) (vapes), effective from June 2025, in an effort to address environmental concerns and the rising prevalence of vaping amongst children.[^cite_note-106] On 5 November 2024 the government introduced the Tobacco and Vapes Bill, aiming to protect public health and reduce the harm caused by smoking. The bill proposed measures to phase out tobacco sales for those under 15, extend the indoor smoking ban to outdoor areas and introduce stricter regulations on vaping, including a ban on vape advertising and limitations on flavours and packaging.[^cite_note-107]

The bill was supported by health organisations,[^cite_note-108] including [Cancer Research UK](615793.md), [Action on Smoking and Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_on_Smoking_and_Health) and [Royal College of Paediatrics and Child Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_College_of_Paediatrics_and_Child_Health).[^cite_note-109] In a poll conducted by [YouGov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/YouGov) in August 2024, 61 per cent of UK adults supported the efforts to phase out smoking by banning it for people born after 1 January 2009, while 27 per cent opposed it. 81 per cent supported the banning of disposable vapes, while 13 per cent opposed it.[^cite_note-110]

#### Assisted dying bill

Main article: [Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminally_Ill_Adults_\(End_of_Life\)_Bill)

Starmer is a longtime supporter of the campaign for [assisted dying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assisted_suicide) or doctor-assisted voluntary [euthanasia](9587.md) in the UK. On 13 March 2024 he pledged to give MPs a vote on assisted suicide if Labour won the 2024 general election.[^cite_note-Riley-Smith_2024-111] He phoned the pro-assisted dying campaigner [Esther Rantzen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esther_Rantzen) and said to her: "I'm personally in favour of changing the law. I think we need to make time. We will make the commitment. Esther, I can give you that commitment right now\... For people who are going through this or are likely to go through it in the next few months or years, this matters hugely and delay just prolongs the agony."[^cite_note-Riley-Smith_2024-111]

In September 2024 [Kim Leadbeater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kim_Leadbeater), the Labour MP for [Spen Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spen_Valley_\(UK_Parliament_constituency\)), was drawn first in the ballot for [private members' bills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_members'_bills_in_the_Parliament_of_the_United_Kingdom). She announced on 3 October 2024 that she would introduce a bill on assisted dying, and on 16 October 2024 the [Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminally_Ill_Adults_\(End_of_Life\)_Bill) was introduced to the House of Commons. The full text of the bill (as presented for [second reading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_reading)) was published on 11 November 2024. Starmer acknowledged the vote for Leadbeater's bill as being "very important".[^cite_note-112] Additionally, Starmer noted Parliament's future intentions to strengthen NHS care for all needs, "including end-of-life care".[^cite_note-113]

#### Law and order

Main articles: [2024 Southport stabbings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Southport_stabbings) and [2024 United Kingdom riots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_Kingdom_riots)



Starmer speaks to the media following an earlier [Cobra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_Office_Briefing_Rooms) meeting to discuss [the violent disorder across the country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_Kingdom_riots), 6 August 2024

On 29 July 2024, following [a mass stabbing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Southport_stabbings) occurred at a dance studio in [Southport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southport) in which a 17-year-old boy named Axel Rudakubana killed three children and injured ten others – eight of whom were children at a [Taylor Swift](5422144.md)–themed yoga and dance workshop. Starmer described the murders as horrendous and shocking and thanked emergency services for their swift response.[^cite_note-BBC_cevwgqz0x41t_145222-116] He later wrote amidst [the riots across the country following the stabbing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_Kingdom_riots) that those who had "hijacked the vigil for the victims" had "insulted the community as it grieves" and that rioters would feel the full force of the law.[^cite_note-117] On 1 August, following a meeting with senior police officers, Starmer announced the establishment of the National Violent Disorder Programme to facilitate greater cooperation between police forces when dealing with violent disorder.[^cite_note-118] On 4 August Starmer said that rioters "will feel the full force of the law" and told them "You will regret taking part in this, whether directly or those whipping up this action online and then running away themselves". He added "I won't shy away from calling it what it is – far-right thuggery".[^cite_note-Whitehead-202423-119][^cite_note-120] Starmer later called an emergency response meeting of [Cobra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_Office_Briefing_Rooms).[^cite_note-121] After the Cobra meeting he announced the establishment of a "standing army" of specialist police officers to address the violence, and help bring it to an end.[^cite_note-122]

Starmer rejected calls from some MPs – including Labour MP [Diane Abbott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diane_Abbott), [Reform UK](59757202.md) leader [Nigel Farage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Farage) and Conservative [Priti Patel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priti_Patel) – to [recall Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recall_of_parliament) to [Westminster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_Westminster).[^cite_note-123] Farage condemned the violence as having "no place in a functioning democracy" but suggested "soft policing" during [Black Lives Matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Lives_Matter) protests had contributed to a "sense of injustice" and an impression of "two-tier policing".[^cite_note-124] Patel called Farage's comments deeply misleading and "simply not relevant". She said: "There's a clear difference between effectively blocking streets or roads being closed to burning down libraries, hotels, food banks and attacking places of worship. What we have seen is thuggery, violence, racism." Patel also wrote to the prime minister and home secretary, asking them to recall parliament.[^cite_note-125] Following the 2024 UK riots, the Starmer government announced that it would be reviewing the [Online Safety Act 2023](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_Safety_Act_2023) with the possibility of strengthening its provisions. Critics, including officials and stakeholders, characterized the legislation passed by the Conservatives as "not fit for purpose."[^cite_note-126]

After Starmer said "large social media companies and those who run them" were contributing to the disorder, the American businessman and political figure [Elon Musk](909036.md), the owner of [Twitter](9988187.md), criticised him for not condemning all participants in the riots and only blaming the [far-right](221220.md).[^cite_note-127][^cite_note-128] Musk also responded to a tweet which said the riots were due to "mass migration and open borders" by tweeting, "Civil war is inevitable". His comments were condemned by Starmer's official spokesman.[^cite_note-129][^cite_note-130][^cite_note-131] Musk further said Starmer was responsible for a "two-tier" policing system which did not protect all communities in the United Kingdom,[^cite_note-132][^cite_note-133][^cite_note-134] and subsequently shared a [conspiracy theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conspiracy_theory) that Starmer's government was planning to build detainment camps in the [Falkland Islands](7550182.md) to hold far-right rioters. In response, Starmer said: "my focus is on ensuring our communities are safe. That is my sole focus. I think it's very important for us all to support the police in what they're doing".[^cite_note-135][^cite_note-136]

After Rudakubana's guilty plea on 20 January 2025, Cooper announced a [public inquiry](826604.md), stating that the victims' families "needed answers about what had happened leading up to the attack".[^cite_note-BBC_cj3exxl8zp8o-137] This was followed by Starmer's promise to overhaul terrorism laws to reflect the type of non-ideological killings characterised by individuals like Rudakubana, stressing the threat from "acts of extreme violence perpetrated by loners, misfits, young men in their bedroom, accessing all manner of material online, desperate for notoriety, sometimes inspired by traditional terrorist groups, but fixated on that extreme violence, seemingly for its own sake".[^cite_note-\:222-138] Significant attention was drawn to Prevent for failing to accept referrals of Rudakubana on the basis of him lacking a terrorist ideology. Although an emergency review found that Prevent had followed correct procedures on each referral, Cooper concluded "that too much weight was placed on the absence of ideology" in the programme. Cooper announced that there would be a review on the threshold at which Prevent intervenes, with the senior lawyer [David Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Anderson,_Baron_Anderson_of_Ipswich) being assigned by Starmer as the Independent Prevent Commissioner to perform the review.[^cite_note-\:222-138][^cite_note-139]

#### Railways

On 25 May 2025, Labour's [renationalisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renationalisation) of the UK's railways began as the train operator [South Western Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Western_Railway) was taken into [public ownership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_ownership).[^cite_note-140]

Passenger train operators continue to be renationalised, with services to be managed at some future time by [Great British Railways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_British_Railways). As of October 2025, [c2c](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C2c_\(1996%E2%80%932025\)) (on 20 July 2025[^cite_note-141]) and [Greater Anglia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Anglia_\(2012-2025\)) (on 12 October 2025[^cite_note-142]) have also been taken into public ownership.

Seven operators remain in the private sector:[^cite_note-143] [Avanti West Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avanti_West_Coast), [Chiltern Railways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiltern_Railways), [CrossCountry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CrossCountry), [East Midlands Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Midlands_Railway), [Govia Thameslink Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Govia_Thameslink_Railway), [Great Western Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Western_Railway_\(train_operating_company\)) and [West Midlands Trains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Midlands_Trains). West Midlands Trains is expected to be the next operator to be taken back into public ownership in February 2026, and all seven are expected to have been taken into public ownership by 2027.

### Defence





Starmer visiting a [Vanguard-class submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanguard-class_submarine) which provide [Britain's nuclear deterrent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trident_\(UK_nuclear_programme\))

In general, Starmer continued the policies of the previous Conservative government, with [promises to increase national security spending](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agreement_on_5%25_NATO_defence_spending_by_2035)[^cite_note-bbc-20260623-147] aiming to [keep the US in NATO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_NATO#2024%E2%80%93present:_American_detachment_from_NATO), act as a bridge between the US and Europe, and to [support Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_and_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war) in the [Russo-Ukrainian war](42085878.md). He has generally supported the existing orthodoxy of Britain's defence establishment including continuing the expensive [renewal of UK's ageing nuclear weapons capabilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trident_\(UK_nuclear_programme\)#Renewal),[^cite_note-dorman-20251211-148] with the costs of the entire [Defence Nuclear Enterprise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defence_Nuclear_Enterprise) expected to increase to between 20% and 25% of the overall [Ministry of Defence](203793.md) budget in coming years.[^cite_note-pac-20260607-149]

On 25 February 2025, Starmer announced that the previous government's commitment to reaching 2.5% of GDP spending on defence by 2030 would be brought forward to April 2027. This would be funded by reducing the [official development assistance](1316305.md) budget from 0.5% to 0.3%, transferring £6.1 billion annually to defence.[^cite_note-govuk-20250225-150][^cite_note-fullfact-20260115-151] [George Robertson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Robertson,_Baron_Robertson_of_Port_Ellen), a former [Secretary General of NATO](22194433.md) and Labour politician, led the [2025 Strategic Defence Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_Defence_Review_\(2025\)) to guide the future increased spending, with Starmer commenting that the UK was moving to "war-fighting readiness" and that "everything we do will add to the strength of [NATO](21133.md)".[^cite_note-ph-20250602-152]

In June 2025, Starmer announced the purchase of [F-35A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-35A) aircraft capable of deploying US nuclear weapons as part of NATO's [dual capable aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_capable_aircraft) nuclear mission, which his office said would be "the biggest strengthening of the [UK's nuclear posture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_of_the_United_Kingdom) in a generation".[^cite_note-bbc-20250624-153][^cite_note-govuk-20250624-154] The [Royal Air Force](25679.md) stated that it would reintroduce a RAF nuclear role for the first time since the UK retired [nuclear bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WE.177) after the end of the [Cold War](325329.md), and allows them to contribute to the [NATO nuclear burden-sharing arrangement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_sharing).[^cite_note-raf-20250625-155]

The [Defence Investment Plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Defence_Investment_Plan) to implement the review was expected to be issued in autumn 2025. However this was delayed amid warnings that there was a £28 billion funding gap.[^cite_note-guardian-20260205-156][^cite_note-bbc-20260109-157] In June 2026, *[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times)* reported that the delay was due to the [Ministry of Defence](203793.md) identifying nine months after the review that it needed the extra £28 billion over the next four years because of shortfalls in its ability to meet previous commitments.[^cite_note-times-20260604-158] The [Public Accounts Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Accounts_Committee_\(United_Kingdom\)) reported that the delay was undermining the UK's credibility with NATO allies and confidence within the [defence industrial base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defence_industrial_base).[^cite_note-guardian-20260607-159] On 11 June 2026, just before the planned Defence Investment Plan publication, Defence Secretary [John Healey](434625.md) and his junior minister [Al Carns](77290491.md) resigned citing disagreement with the very limited spending increase given in the plan beyond the 2.5% of GDP already committed to.[^cite_note-guardian-20260611-160]

The Defence Investment Plan was announced by Starmer on 30 June 2026, after he had announced his [resignation as prime minister and Leader of the Labour Party](83157483.md#Starmer's_resignation).[^cite_note-161] The plan increased spending by about £1.5 billion over the previous draft, though funding for all the spending was not initially allocated.[^cite_note-162][^cite_note-163] [The Treasury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HM_Treasury) had not carried out the analysis of how to reach the 3.5% of GDP NATO national security spending promise, and this would be a matter for the next prime minister.[^cite_note-guardian-20260708-164]

### Foreign affairs

Starmer with Syrian President [Ahmed al-Sharaa](44007699.md) at [10 Downing Street](226294.md), 31 March 2026

Biden also congratulated Starmer on "a hell of a victory".[^cite_note-169]

Starmer with Ukraine President [Volodymyr Zelenskyy](59210523.md), February 2025

At the 2024 NATO summit, Starmer signalled that Ukraine could use Britain's [Storm Shadow](306908.md) missile [donations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_military_aid_to_Ukraine_during_the_Russo-Ukrainian_War) to [strike military targets](70539619.md) inside Russia.[^cite_note-171] In a meeting with Zelensky, Starmer called for an "irreversible" [membership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukraine%E2%80%93NATO_relations) strategy for Ukraine to [join](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enlargement_of_NATO) [NATO](21133.md).[^cite_note-172]

Starmer's government has significantly expanded economic and military ties with [Gulf Cooperation Council](220950.md) (GCC) nations, primarily focusing on securing infrastructure investment and finalizing a region-wide [free trade agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_Cooperation_Council%E2%80%93United_Kingdom_Free_Trade_Agreement). Starmer defended the engagement with these leaders—despite human rights concerns—as necessary for both British security and jobs.[^cite_note-173][^cite_note-174] In early December 2024, Starmer conducted a multi-day diplomatic visit to the Gulf, meeting with Sheikh [Mohamed bin Zayed Al Nahyan](2121945.md) in the UAE and Crown Prince [Mohammed bin Salman](38689358.md) in Saudi Arabia.[^cite_note-175]

Starmer described this as the UK's best trade deal since Brexit.[^cite_note-176] Starmer faced questions regarding India's continued [purchase of Russian fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India_and_the_Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine), saying he respected India's "strategic independence".[^cite_note-177]

Later in May, the UK and the EU agreed on a deal in London to 'reset' relations between them following [Brexit](41688778.md). The deal included provisions on defence, fishing rights and youth mobility.[^cite_note-178]

Foreign Secretary [Yvette Cooper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yvette_Cooper) has declined to commit to halting [British arms exports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_arms_export) to the UAE, instead focusing on diplomatic pressure and humanitarian aid.[^cite_note-181][^cite_note-182]

In October 2025, Starmer finalized a landmark defence agreement with [Turkey](11125639.md) for the sale of 20 [Eurofighter Typhoon](167667.md) fighter jets, valued at approximately £8 billion (\$10.7 billion).[^cite_note-183]

#### Russian invasion of Ukraine

Main article: [United Kingdom and the Russo-Ukrainian war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_and_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war)



Starmer and Zelenskyy honoring fallen Ukrainian soldiers at [The Wall of Remembrance of the Fallen for Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wall_of_Remembrance_of_the_Fallen_for_Ukraine) in Kyiv, 16 January 2025

In January 2025, Starmer announced that the UK would provide £4.5 billion in [military support to Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_military_aid_to_Ukraine_during_the_Russo-Ukrainian_War) throughout 2025.[^cite_note-211] On 16 January 2025, Starmer and Zelenskyy signed a landmark 100-Year Partnership Agreement in Kyiv.[^cite_note-212] The government has stated it will sustain £3 billion a year in military aid until 2031, and 229,900 [Ukrainian refugees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_refugees) have been supported in the UK. The UK has also made a loan of £2.26 billion to Ukraine as its share of the [G7](1196634.md) Extraordinary Revenue Acceleration \$50 billion loan.[^cite_note-govuk-20251223-213][^cite_note-hocl-20261017-214]

On 16 February 2025 Starmer said he is ready to deploy British peacekeeping units in Ukraine if there is a [peace deal to end the Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_negotiations_in_the_Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine).[^cite_note-215]



Starmer with international leaders at the [2025 London Summit on Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_London_Summit_on_Ukraine)

In addition, Starmer hosted the [2025 London Summit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_London_Summit_on_Ukraine) on Ukraine with 18 world leaders, and announced the creation of a [Coalition of the willing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coalition_of_the_willing_\(Russo-Ukrainian_War\)) to support peace in Ukraine and to end the [Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine). The stated aim of the initiative is to facilitate the [peace negotiation attempts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_negotiations_in_the_Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine) launched and mediated by the [United States](3434750.md) between Ukraine and Russia in February 2025, by helping to build up strong enough [security guarantees](4991894.md) for Ukraine to ensure that a potential reached ceasefire or peace deal would be lasting.[^cite_note-216] Besides serving the role as building up a potential peacekeeping force, the coalition have also expressed readiness to increase military support for Ukraine and strengthen economic sanctions against Russia, in the event that the ongoing negotiations for a "comprehensive ceasefire" or "peace deal" would fail. As of 20 March 2025, the exact shape and function of the coalition was described as still being subject to ongoing planning, but moved into an "operational phase".[^cite_note-217][^cite_note-218]

Starmer has actively urged NATO allies to "ramp up" the provision of long-range weapons to Ukraine.[^cite_note-219] In 2025, Starmer's government significantly expanded the UK's commitment to [Storm Shadow](306908.md) missiles through both replenishment of national stockpiles and new supplies for Ukraine.[^cite_note-220] Starmer championed a plan to use the profits from [frozen Russian sovereign assets](78521030.md) to provide reparations loans to Ukraine.[^cite_note-221]

#### Israel-Palestine conflict

Main article: [United Kingdom and the Gaza war](76740255.md)



Starmer meeting with [Mahmoud Abbas](232595.md), [President of Palestine](30580840.md), New York, September 2024



Keir Starmer meeting with Israeli President [Isaac Herzog](4645588.md), 10 Downing Street, 10 September 2025



Starmer with international leaders at the [2025 Gaza peace summit](81324529.md)

Starmer's government supports Israel's right to self-defence and has not blocked the sale of weapons and military equipment to Israel. Starmer has also condemned some of Israel's actions and called for a ceasefire in the [Gaza war](74998519.md), sent humanitarian aid to Gaza, and supported a [two-state solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-state_solution).

Under Starmer's premiership, the licences of some [British arms sales to Israel](76740255.md#Military_exports) were suspended in September 2024 because of a "clear risk" that the weapons could be used to violate international law. Lammy announced the suspension of 30 out of 350 arms export licences to Israel, affecting equipment such as parts for fighter jets, helicopters and drones.[^cite_note-222] However, the value of arms exports to Israel rose in the three months after the partial ban. A spokesman for the Department for Business and Trade (DBT) said that most of these licenses were "for items being produced in Israel for third countries".[^cite_note-223][^cite_note-224][^cite_note-225]

After [Israel's parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel's_parliament) banned [UNRWA](32270.md) in October 2024,[^cite_note-226] Starmer issued a statement saying he was "gravely concerned". He said "The humanitarian situation in Gaza is simply unacceptable" and "Only UNRWA can deliver humanitarian aid at the scale and pace needed".[^cite_note-227][^cite_note-228]

In November 2024, Starmer refused to call Israel's actions in Gaza "[genocide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestinian_genocide_accusation)".[^cite_note-229] Later that month, Starmer's government stated that Netanyahu would be arrested if he travels to the UK, after the [International Criminal Court](14880.md) issued [an international arrest warrant for him](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Criminal_Court_arrest_warrants_for_Israeli_leaders) for alleged war crimes.[^cite_note-230]

On 18 March 2025 Foreign Secretary [David Lammy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Lammy) said that Israel had breached international law by [blocking aid to the Gaza Strip](75013719.md). However, Starmer's government rejected Lammy's statement. A spokesman for Starmer's government said: "Our position remains that Israel's actions in Gaza are at clear risk of breaching international humanitarian law ... The government is not an international court, and, therefore, it is up to courts to make judgments". Asked whether Lammy should apologise, they added: "I'll leave that to the Foreign Office".[^cite_note-231]

[Stephen Flynn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Flynn), who supported an amendment by the [SNP](28887.md) to scrap the [two child benefit cap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_child_benefit_cap), leading to Labour [withdrawing the whip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Withdrawal_of_the_whip) from 7 of its MPs who had supported it

Starmer and Reeves initially refused to scrap the [benefit cap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benefit_cap) introduced by the [Cameron–Clegg coalition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cameron%E2%80%93Clegg_coalition), citing financial reasons.[^cite_note-243][^cite_note-244][^cite_note-245] The cap was introduced in 2013 as part of the coalition government's wide-reaching welfare reform agenda which included the introduction of [Universal Credit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Credit) and reforms of housing benefit and disability benefits.[^cite_note-bbc-246] Starmer's government cited wide public support for the measure, despite it being highly controversial.[^cite_note-247] The benefit cap primarily affects families with children, high rents, or both.[^cite_note-IFS-248] By 2024, the year Starmer and Reeves entered government, two-thirds of the families affected by the cap were single-parent families, half of which had a child under five.[^cite_note-Butler-249]

Starmer launched a Child Poverty Taskforce, in which expert officials from across government would work together on how best to support more than four million children living in poverty.[^cite_note-bbc.com-35] On 26 November 2025, Reeves announced that the two child benefit cap will be scrapped in 2026 in the [2025 budget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/November_2025_United_Kingdom_budget).

During the Starmer leadership, 30 Labour MPs had the whip withdrawn, which was more than under any other Labour leader since 1988.[^cite_note-guardian-20260719-251]

##### Conversion of Winter Fuel Payment to a means-tested benefit

Main article: [Winter Fuel Payment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_Fuel_Payment)



[Gordon Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Brown) (then chancellor) introduced the policy.

In July 2024, Chancellor [Rachel Reeves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rachel_Reeves) announced that the [Winter Fuel Payment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_Fuel_Payment), a welfare payment for elderly people introduced in 1997 by former chancellor and prime minister [Gordon Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Brown) intended to cover the additional costs of [heating](57169.md) over the winter months, would only be given to those in receipt of [Pension Credit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pension_Credit) or other [means-tested benefits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Means-tested_benefit). This removed the benefit from around 10 million pensioners.[^cite_note-252] The announcement of this policy to remove fuel payments from pensioners took the nation by surprise as it had not been publicised in advance or included in [Labour's manifesto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Change_\(manifesto\)) for the election. With the announcement being made shortly before the Commons party conference season recess, there was only a limited initial response. However, several Labour MPs returned to Westminster complaining that their constituents were "furious and, in some cases, deeply worried".[^cite_note-auto-253]

On 5 September, Starmer conceded to demands and promised a binding vote in the Commons on whether the changes to the fuel payment would be implemented. The change of heart came after unease amongst Labour MPs, with many of them signing an [early day motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_day_motion) challenging the changes, and the opposition [Conservative Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Party_\(UK\)) submitted a motion to annul the government's change to regulations.[^cite_note-auto1-254] The Conservative motion was debated on 10 September, and in the vote that followed, 348 MPs backed the government and 228 supported the opposition motion. With a majority of 120 to the government, the policy was implemented. A total of 52 Labour MPs, including seven ministers, did not participate in the vote. Labour MP [Jon Trickett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jon_Trickett) voted against the government. BBC News said that around 20 of the Labour MPs who did not participate had publicly expressed opposition to the policy previously.[^cite_note-255]

The government was put under pressure to abandon this policy by the [Trades Union Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trades_Union_Congress) (TUC) as it started its annual conference on 9 September. The TUC general secretary, [Paul Nowak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Nowak_\(trade_unionist\)), said that he was concerned about the removal of the universal payment for all but the poorest pensioners. The general secretary of [Unite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unite_the_Union), [Sharon Graham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharon_Graham), repeated her call for the decision to be reversed. [Fran Heathcote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran_Heathcote) of the [PCS union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_and_Commercial_Services_Union) said that the plan was a "misstep" and it needed to be "put right".[^cite_note-256][^cite_note-257] At the [Labour Party conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_Party_conference), held between 23 and 25 September, Unite and the [Communication Workers Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication_Workers_Union_\(United_Kingdom\)), managed to reserve time for a motion opposing the government's fuel payment policy to be debated and a non-binding vote taken.[^cite_note-\:14-258] The motion was scheduled to be debated on the first day of the conference, but on the day, the same day that Reeves was due to give a speech, the vote was postponed until the last day of the conference – after Starmer had left. The announcement of the move was greeted with loud boos and jeers from the conference attendees.[^cite_note-\:14-258][^cite_note-259][^cite_note-260] The Unite union said that by rescheduling their motion to the very end of the conference, the conference organisers had sought to silence them.[^cite_note-261] The Labour leadership lost the vote, with delegates supporting the motion to scrap the government's policy.[^cite_note-262]

The announcement of this policy to remove fuel payments from pensioners took the nation by surprise as it had not been publicised in advance or included in Labour's manifesto for the election. With the announcement being made shortly before the Commons party conference season recess, there was only a limited initial response. However, several Labour MPs returned to Westminster complaining that their constituents were "furious and, in some cases, deeply worried".[^cite_note-auto-253] In August, consumer journalist and founder of [MoneySavingExpert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MoneySavingExpert), [Martin Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Lewis_\(financial_journalist\)), suggested that the government should rethink their plans to restrict who would get the payment saying they had gone too far by limiting it to only the "absolute poorest pensioners on the very lowest income".[^cite_note-263] Labour MP [Rachael Maskell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rachael_Maskell) said, "Being cold at home can lead to stroke, heart attack, hypothermia, pneumonia and other such illnesses" and recommended that the government should read the work of [Professor Sir Michael Marmot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Marmot) and [Sir Chris Whitty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Chris_Whitty) with respect to this "so that we can take a [public health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_health) approach to people being warm at home, to mitigate the cost that could come without putting right mitigation around the winter fuel payments".[^cite_note-auto1-254] On 9 September it was reported that Labour MPs, including [frontbenchers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frontbencher), were worried that Reeves's "brutal" plan for the fuel allowance would result in more older people ending up in hospital over the winter.[^cite_note-264] The UK's leading charity for older people, [Age UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_UK), wrote to Reeves with its proposal which it says would prevent around two million pensioners, for whom the payment is badly needed, from having the payment stopped.[^cite_note-265] At Prime Minister's Questions on 11 September, the former prime minister, [leader of the opposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leader_of_the_Opposition_\(United_Kingdom\)), Rishi Sunak, accused Starmer of covering up the [impact assessment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_assessment) for the policy, asking him if the estimate for the number of deaths was higher or lower than the 3,850 Labour had previously forecast would result from this policy. Starmer did not answer that question directly.[^cite_note-266]

##### Free gifts and hospitality controversy

Main article: [2024 Labour Party freebies controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Labour_Party_freebies_controversy)



[Waheed Alli, Baron Alli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waheed_Alli,_Baron_Alli) pictured in 2010

On 24 August 2024 *[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times)* reported that, shortly after Starmer became the prime minister, [Waheed Alli, Baron Alli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waheed_Alli,_Baron_Alli), Starmer's biggest personal donor, had been given a security pass. The pass gave Alli unrestricted access to Downing Street, and he then hosted a party there for other Labour Party donors. This gave rise to suspicions of "cash for access" and claims of "cronyism".[^cite_note-267][^cite_note-268] On 15 September 2024 reports emerged in the British media that Starmer had initially failed to declare £5,000 of gifts used to purchase clothes for his wife, [Victoria Starmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_Starmer).[^cite_note-269] The gifts had been given by Alli, who had also given Starmer a number of clothing-related gifts, including £2,435 worth of eyeglasses, and had given Labour deputy leader [Angela Rayner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angela_Rayner) gifts worth £3,550 of clothes in June 2024.[^cite_note-Guardian_20_Sept-270] It was subsequently reported that Starmer had accepted over £107,145 worth of gifts, benefits, and hospitality since the [2019 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_United_Kingdom_general_election), including tickets to [Arsenal F.C.](2174.md) matches and [Taylor Swift](5422144.md) concerts, two-and-a-half times more than any other MP.[^cite_note-271][^cite_note-Reuters-272] It was also reported that Health Secretary [Wes Streeting](17169295.md) had been gifted four Taylor Swift concert tickets, worth a total of £1,160, by [The Football Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Football_Association),[^cite_note-273] and that Chancellor of the Exchequer [Rachel Reeves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rachel_Reeves) had accepted £7,500 worth of clothes in 2024 from [Juliet Rosenfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Rosenfeld),[^cite_note-Guardian_20_Sept-270] which were registered as donations "to support the shadow chancellor's office".[^cite_note-274] On 17 September 10 Downing Street announced that [Parliamentary Commissioner for Standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_Commissioner_for_Standards) [Daniel Greenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Greenberg_\(lawyer\)) would not investigate the gifts.[^cite_note-275]

On 20 September 2024, as the [Labour Party Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_Party_Conference) was set to begin, Starmer as well as Reeves and Rayner announced they would no longer accept clothes from donors.[^cite_note-276] That weekend, *[The Mail on Sunday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mail_on_Sunday)* reported that Secretary of State for Education [Bridget Phillipson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridget_Phillipson) had received a £14,000 gift from Alli for her 40th birthday party. Starmer's use of accommodation provided by Alli sparked further controversy when it was reported that a video calling for Brits to stay at home that Starmer had filmed in December 2021, during the third wave of the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_the_United_Kingdom), had been filmed in a flat belonging to Alli.[^cite_note-281] On 27 September *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)* reported that Starmer had received an additional £16,000 worth of clothes as a gift from Alli in late 2023 and early 2024. Although those gifts had been declared in time, they had been declared as money "for the private office" of Starmer, and not as clothing.[^cite_note-282]



In October 2024, it was reported that the [Special Escort Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Escort_Group_\(Metropolitan_Police\)) would be provided for [Taylor Swift](5422144.md) for the remainder of [The Eras Tour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Eras_Tour) shows in London.

In October 2024 it was reported that the [Metropolitan Police](192450.md)'s [Special Escort Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Escort_Group_\(Metropolitan_Police\))—a top-level security usually provided for [members of the British royal family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_royal_family) and [heads of state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heads_of_state)—would be provided for Swift and crew for the remainder of [The Eras Tour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Eras_Tour) shows in London. ##### Louise Haigh situation



It emerged in November 2024 that [Louise Haigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louise_Haigh) had pleaded guilty to [fraud by false representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraud_by_false_representation) in 2014 after falsely reporting in 2013 to police that her work phone had been stolen; she subsequently resigned as Transport Secretary

On 28 November 2024, it emerged that then Transport Secretary [Louise Haigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louise_Haigh) had pleaded guilty to [fraud by false representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraud_by_false_representation) relating to misleading police in 2014.[^cite_note-\:32-300] In a statement, Haigh said that she had been [mugged](54849.md) on a night out in 2013 whilst working as a public policy manager for the insurer [Aviva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviva). She said that she had given the police a list of items that she thought were missing from her handbag, which wrongly included her mobile work phone supplied by Aviva.[^cite_note-301] She was issued with a new phone by her employer, but Haigh said she later discovered her old phone in a drawer which she switched on.[^cite_note-302] Haigh said that this signal was picked up on by Aviva and they alerted it to the police, who called in Haigh for police questioning to make a statement.[^cite_note-\:04-303] During the interview with the police, Haigh said that her solicitor had advised her "not to comment", and she did not respond to questions about the use of the phone when approached for comment.[^cite_note-304][^cite_note-\:17-305] A case file was sent to the [Crown Prosecution Service](383064.md) and she was charged with fraud by false representation. Six months before she was elected as an MP at the 2015 general election in November 2014, Haigh pleaded guilty when she appeared at the Camberwell Green magistrates' court, and received a [conditional discharge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_discharge).[^cite_note-\:43-306]

Starmer spoke to Haigh on the night of 28 November and said that it would be better for her and the government if she stood down; Haigh agreed and subsequently resigned as Transport Secretary that night.[^cite_note-\:43-306] The prime minister's spokesperson said in a briefing with reporters on 29 November that Starmer accepted Haigh's resignation following "further information emerging".[^cite_note-\:53-307] In a letter to Starmer, Haigh stated that whilst she was "totally committed to our political project," she believed it would be best served supporting him from "outside government".[^cite_note-308][^cite_note-309] Haigh also said that the issue would "inevitably be a distraction" from delivering on the work and policies of the government, but said she took "pride" in what they had done.[^cite_note-\:17-305] In response, Starmer said that Haigh had made "huge strides" as Transport Secretary, and that she still had a "huge contribution to make in the future".[^cite_note-310] She was succeeded as Transport Secretary by [Heidi Alexander](27306797.md).[^cite_note-311]

##### Farmers' protests

Main article: [2024–2025 United Kingdom farmers' protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024%E2%80%932025_United_Kingdom_farmers'_protests)



Tractors on the streets of London during a farmers' protest in November 2024

In response to changes to inheritance tax law for family farms announced in the October budget, mass protests organised by farmer organisations, against new [inheritance tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inheritance_tax_in_the_United_Kingdom) laws on agricultural assets, have taken place around the United Kingdom since November 2024. The new laws were proposed in the October 2024 budget, and have led to thousands of British farmers protesting, including in Parliament Square and addressing MPs directly in parliament.[^cite_note-312][^cite_note-313]

The protests were a response to proposed changes to [inheritance tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inheritance_tax_in_the_United_Kingdom) on agricultural assets, which media outlets dubbed as a "tractor tax". Previously, the intergenerational transfer of farms had been exempt from taxation as a result of 1992 [tax break](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_break) by the Conservative [Major ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Major_ministry) intended to protect food security.[^cite_note-\:03-314][^cite_note-\:33-315] In November 2024, the newly elected Labour Starmer ministry announced plans to remove the exemption from inheritance tax for farms valued over at least £1,000,000 in order to generate revenue for public services.[^cite_note-\:33-315] Set to take effect in April 2026, the new policy would see a 20% inheritance tax on farm valued over that threshold, half the standard rate of inheritance tax, and could be paid interest-free across ten years.[^cite_note-\:33-315]

Opposition to the change from farmers stemmed from their claim that farmers, while asset rich, are cash poor, which they said created a situation where heirs would have to sell farm land to meet tax obligations.[^cite_note-\:03-314] Supporters of the change claimed it would mitigate and prevent wealthy individuals from buying farmland to avoid inheritance tax.[^cite_note-\:122-316][^cite_note-317][^cite_note-318] Starmer responded to concerns by saying that the actual threshold for inheritance tax liability could reach up to as high as £3,000,000 once various exemptions were applied, including considerations for couples and specific agricultural property relief.[^cite_note-\:03-314][^cite_note-\:42-319] The [secretary of state for environment, food and rural affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_state_for_environment,_food_and_rural_affairs), [Steve Reed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Reed_\(politician\)), defended the tax changes as a measure to counter wealthy investors using agricultural land for tax avoidance, stating it had become "the most effective way for the super rich to avoid paying their inheritance tax."[^cite_note-\:122-316] Since 2024, television presenter, journalist, farmer, and author [Jeremy Clarkson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Clarkson) has been extremely critical of Starmer and the Labour Party.[^cite_note-320][^cite_note-321][^cite_note-322][^cite_note-323] Clarkson attended the England farmers' protests in London where he called tax hikes a "hammer blow to the back of the head" of British agriculture.[^cite_note-\:42-319] Clarkson later labelled Starmer "a nightmare for farmers".[^cite_note-324]

The legislation has been widely praised by campaigner groups and local leaders, including Mayor of Liverpool City Region [Steve Rotheram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Rotheram), who described it as a "landmark moment". ##### Proposed disability benefits reforms



[Liz Kendall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liz_Kendall), then [Secretary of State for Work and Pensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_State_for_Work_and_Pensions)

During her tenure as Work and Pensions Secretary, [Liz Kendall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liz_Kendall) initiated a series of reforms aimed at reshaping the UK's welfare system. One of her primary objectives was to shift the [Department for Work and Pensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_for_Work_and_Pensions) from merely administering benefits to actively promoting employment. In a speech in Barnsley, Kendall said the need to address factors such as health, skills, childcare, and transport, which significantly influence individuals ability to secure and maintain employment.[^cite_note-362]

Kendall proposed the "Youth Guarantee" for 18 to 21-year-olds, designed to ensure that young people are either earning or learning. This program offers opportunities for training or employment, with the stipulation that refusal to participate could result in benefit reductions. Kendall noted the importance of early career engagement, saying that unemployment during youth can have long-term detrimental effects on job prospects and earning potential.[^cite_note-363]

A significant aspect of Kendall's reform agenda involved tightening eligibility criteria for Personal Independence Payments (PIP) and incapacity benefits. These measures aimed to save approximately £5 billion annually by the end of the decade. The proposed changes were expected to affect around one million people, particularly those with mental health conditions and minor physical difficulties.[^cite_note-364]



Protest against disability welfare cuts at [Portsmouth](24207.md) Pride 2025

The proposed disability benefits reforms sparked considerable debate. Disability charities and opposition parties have criticized the cuts, labelling them as devastating and immoral. Organisations such as the Disability Benefits Consortium, Scope, and Mind argued that these changes could push disabled individuals further into poverty and exacerbate health issues, highlighting the potential social impact of the reforms.[^cite_note-365]

In response to the backlash, Kendall emphasised the need for a balanced approach that ensures fiscal responsibility while protecting vulnerable populations. She acknowledges the concerns raised by disability advocates and noted the importance of reinvesting savings into employment programs to support those on health-related benefits, aiming to create a more equitable system.[^cite_note-366]

Kendall's tenure was also marked by internal party challenges. Some Labour MPs expressed apprehension regarding the impact of welfare reforms on disadvantaged communities. The historical context of previous welfare cuts served as a cautionary backdrop, prompting calls for the party to uphold its commitment to social justice while pursuing necessary fiscal reforms.[^cite_note-367] On 26 June 2025, the government, following extensive pressure from over 120 backbench Labour MPs,[^cite_note-368] announced it would soften its plans for new welfare legislation by confirming that new qualification criteria for receiving [Personal Independence Payment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_Independence_Payment) (PIP) and the health aspect of [Universal Credit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Credit) (UC) would only apply to prospective new claimants rather than pre-existing claimants who can continue to claim under the pre-existing qualification system.[^cite_note-369]

##### Island of strangers speech

Main article: [Restoring Control over the Immigration System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restoring_Control_over_the_Immigration_System)



[Enoch Powell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enoch_Powell), whose [1968 speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rivers_of_Blood_speech) critics compared to Starmer's [immigration remarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restoring_Control_over_the_Immigration_System)

Starmer's speech on 12 May 2025 outlining comprehensive reforms to [UK immigration policy](1247786.md) generated intense criticism over its language and rhetoric. According to media coverage, Starmer opened his remarks by pledging to "take back control of our borders and close the book on a squalid chapter for our politics, our economy, and our country".[^cite_note-LBC-TakeControl-370] Media reports noted his deliberate use of "[take back control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Take_back_control)" from the outset, making clear the intended audience and message.[^cite_note-ConversationAnalysis-371] Political commentary highlighted Starmer's criticism of the previous Conservative government, with coverage noting he stated that between 2019 and 2023, "net migration quadrupled" and "reached nearly 1 million" in 2023.[^cite_note-NPR-Analysis-372] Media analysis noted he declared that "Taking back control is a Labour argument".[^cite_note-NPR-Analysis-372]

Most controversially, academic analysis by David Miller, professor of political theory at Nuffield College, Oxford, noted that Starmer warned that "Without them, we risk becoming an island of strangers, not a nation that walks forward together".[^cite_note-NewStatesman-Analysis-373] According to media reports, Starmer described the UK's immigration system as contributing to forces "slowly pulling our country apart" while stating that immigrants "make a massive contribution" to British society.[^cite_note-PMO_Remarks_Immigration-374] Political analysis noted that he characterised parts of the economy as "almost addicted to importing cheap labour".[^cite_note-PMO_Remarks_Immigration-374]

Media analysis noted that the speech was "widely seen as a reaction to the results of the recent local elections, in which the right-wing and anti-immigration party Reform UK won 30% of the vote".[^cite_note-TheNational2025-375] Political observers noted that "close your eyes and Starmer's speech could have been delivered almost in its entirety by Reform UK leader [Nigel Farage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Farage)".[^cite_note-WSWS-Analysis-376] Farage criticised the announcement, calling Starmer "a hypocrite who believes in open borders". Reform UK argued these reforms were merely a reaction to their own recent electoral gains and expressed doubt that the government would or could follow through with real results.[^cite_note-CNN_Reform-377] In Parliament, Farage told Starmer that Reform "very much enjoyed your speech on Monday, you seem to be learning a very great deal from us".[^cite_note-CNN_Reform-377] Academic researchers noted that Starmer's rhetoric "chimes with classic far-right narratives where migration is framed as the root of all societal ills".[^cite_note-ConversationAnalysis-371] Analysis suggested the speech contained elements reminiscent of far-right conspiracies about elites deliberately encouraging mass immigration, with Starmer's words potentially acting as a "dog whistle".[^cite_note-ConversationAnalysis-371]

The speech provoked intense divided reactions within Starmer's Labour Party.[^cite_note-378][^cite_note-BBC_Regrets-379] [Diane Abbott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diane_Abbott), the veteran Labour MP, branded the speech "fundamentally racist", stating: "I was very disturbed to hear Keir Starmer on the subject of immigration... I thought that was a fundamentally racist thing to say. I think Keir Starmer is quite wrong to say that the way that you beat Reform is to copy Reform."[^cite_note-Abbott_Racist-380] The MP [Nadia Whittome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nadia_Whittome) said that the speech "mimics the scaremongering of the far right," describing Starmer's words as "shameful and dangerous".[^cite_note-381] [Sarah Owen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Owen), Labour chairwoman of the Women and Equalities Select Committee, argued that the government should focus on "investing in communities to thrive – not pitting people against each other", warning that "chasing the tail of the right" could put the country on "a very dark path".[^cite_note-382] [Welsh Labour](6732798.md) leader and Welsh First Minister [Eluned Morgan](723676.md) stated that she did not want to be "drawn into a debate where people are using divisive language when it comes to immigration."[^cite_note-383]

Other Labour MPs who criticised the speech included [Zarah Sultana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zarah_Sultana), who described the speech as "sickening" and tweeted: "The Prime Minister imitating Enoch Powell's 'Rivers of Blood' speech is sickening. That speech fuelled decades of racism and division. Echoing it today is a disgrace. It adds to anti-migrant rhetoric that puts lives at risk. Shame on you, Keir Starmer." She also questioned whether Starmer's speech had actually been written by Nigel Farage, asking: "Did Nigel Farage write this speech? Dehumanising and divisive. We deserve better than this."[^cite_note-NPR2025-384][^cite_note-Sultana_Sickening2-385] Former shadow chancellor [John McDonnell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_McDonnell), who lost the Labour whip, compared the "island of strangers" reference to "the divisive language of [Enoch Powell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enoch_Powell)" and questioned in Parliament: "When the prime minister referred to... an island of strangers, reflecting the language of Enoch Powell, does he realise how shockingly divisive that could be?"[^cite_note-LBC_Regrets-386][^cite_note-HuffPost_McDonnell2-387] The MP [Joani Reid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joani_Reid) stated that "we are not closing the door on immigration that can boost growth and strengthen the economy. But employers who hope to get away with paying low wages or avoid investment in staff training have had 'time' called today".[^cite_note-388] The then [Parliamentary Under-Secretary of State for Migration and Citizenship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_Under-Secretary_of_State_for_Migration_and_Citizenship), [Seema Malhotra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seema_Malhotra), stated that Starmer's speech recognised "how much migration has been and remains a vital part of our identity," saying that unless immigration policy changed "we risk being communities that live side by side, rather than work and walk together."[^cite_note-389] The then [Home Secretary](149104.md), [Yvette Cooper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yvette_Cooper), stated that Starmer was "right to say we need to change", adding that "I know that everybody always gets caught up in focusing on different phrases and so on, but we do have to be talking about the policies".[^cite_note-390]

Media commentary described the phrase "island of strangers" as becoming "the locus for a vitriolic exchange of views," with critics reading it not as academic philosophy but as echoing Enoch Powell's [Rivers of Blood speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rivers_of_Blood_speech), in which Powell championed those who "found themselves made strangers in their own country".[^cite_note-NewStatesman-Analysis-373] Political analysis noted that critics accused Starmer of having "aped the infamous, racist 1968 'rivers of blood' speech of Powell".[^cite_note-WSWS-Analysis-376][^cite_note-391][^cite_note-BBC_Regrets-379][^cite_note-LBC_Regrets-386] Critics drew direct parallels between the language used in both speeches. In Powell's 1968 address, he stated that white Britons "found themselves made strangers in their own country" as a result of immigration policies, warning of social breakdown and violence. Starmer's phrase "we risk becoming an island of strangers, not a nation that walks forward together" was seen as echoing this sentiment.[^cite_note-BBC_Regrets-379] One analysis noted that "Powell's speech suggests the levels of immigration would outnumber the white population of Britain and cause them to become 'strangers in their own country'" while Starmer warned that without immigration controls, Britain risks "becoming an island of strangers".[^cite_note-Kent_Analysis-392] The comparison drew criticism from various political figures. One media commentator argued that the speech was "pitched so far to the right that he even aped the infamous, racist 1968 'rivers of blood' speech".[^cite_note-Owen_Jones-393]

Initially, the [Prime Minister's Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Minister's_Office_\(United_Kingdom\)) rejected comparisons to Powell and defended use of the phrase, saying that Starmer was "using his own words to rightly both recognise the contribution migrants have made over generations and to make the point that uncontrolled migration has been too high".[^cite_note-394] Starmer initially "completely rejected" the suggestion he echoed Powell.[^cite_note-LBC_Doubles_Down-395] Starmer later expressed regret about the phrase. In an interview with his biographer, he admitted: "I wouldn't have used those words if I had known they were, or even would be interpreted as an echo of Powell. I had no idea – and my speechwriters didn't know either. But that particular phrase – no – it wasn't right. I'll give you the honest truth: I deeply regret using it."[^cite_note-396] Starmer revealed that the speech came shortly after an alleged arson attack on his family home, stating: "It's fair to say I wasn't in the best state to make a big speech... I was really, really worried."[^cite_note-ITV_Regrets-397]

Analysis suggested the controversy had political consequences for Starmer. Research by the [New Statesman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Statesman) found that the speech "backfired", showing "a drop in Labour support" with "no modelling scenario where we find the speech has any positive effect". The study found the speech made immigration 3 percentage points more salient and reduced support for all left-of-centre parties by 1.5 percentage points.[^cite_note-NewStatesman_Backfire-398] The apology was described as "another climbdown from Number 10 amid backlash from MPs", with the controversy described as "yet another internal Labour dispute" over Starmer's political positioning on immigration.[^cite_note-GBNews_Regret-399]

##### Angela Rayner tax scandal

Main article: [Angela Rayner tax scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angela_Rayner_tax_scandal)



[Angela Rayner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angela_Rayner) resigned from government in response to the [tax scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angela_Rayner_tax_scandal) in September 2025

A scandal erupted in 2025 when it was reported that [Deputy Prime Minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deputy_Prime_Minister) [Angela Rayner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angela_Rayner) had underpaid [stamp duty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stamp_duty_in_the_United_Kingdom) on a £800,000 flat in [Hove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hove) purchased in May 2025. Rayner treated the property as her primary residence for stamp duty and related purposes. Her primary residence for the purposes of Council Tax remained her constituency home in Ashton-under-Lyne. This was reported in news media resulting in a payment of stamp duty, reported to £40,000 less than if it had not been her primary residence. Prior to the purchase, she had sold her 25% share in the Ashton-under-Lyne property to a trust for her disabled son, which already held a 50% share, and remains a trustee alongside her former husband and a solicitor; the remaining 25% is still owned by her former husband.[^cite_note-trust-article-400]

Following media scrutiny, on 3 September 2025 Rayner admitted to underpaying stamp duty and referred herself to the prime minister's independent adviser on ministerial standards. She stated she had acted on legal advice and believed she was liable only for the standard rate rather than the higher rate applied to additional properties.[^cite_note-rayner-article-401] Rayner resigned from her ministerial posts and the deputy leadership of the Labour Party on 5 September 2025.[^cite_note-402]

Rayner resigned in response to the scandal on 5 September 2025.[^cite_note-403] Her resignation was widely described in the press as damaging to the Starmer ministry.[^cite_note-404]

In April 2026, it was reported that Mandelson had been denied security clearance by [security vetting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Security_vetting_in_the_United_Kingdom) back in January 2025. A government spokesperson claimed "the decision to grant developed vetting to Peter Mandelson against the recommendation of UK Security Vetting was taken by officials in the FCDO".[^cite_note-412] Starmer said he was not told that he had failed security vetting, and denied claims that he had misled the [House of Commons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Commons). [Foreign Secretary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Secretary_\(United_Kingdom\)) [Yvette Cooper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yvette_Cooper)sacked top civil servant in the Foreign Office [Olly Robbins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olly_Robbins), who claimed he had been put under "serious pressure" to push through the appointment at a summons to the [Foreign Affairs Select Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Affairs_Select_Committee).[^cite_note-413][^cite_note-414] [Leader of the Opposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leader_of_the_Opposition_\(United_Kingdom\)) [Kemi Badenoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kemi_Badenoch) called Starmer's position "untenable",[^cite_note-415] while the scandal prompted other party leaders to call for the prime minister's resignation, including [Ed Davey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ed_Davey), leader of the [Liberal Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Democrats_\(UK\)); [Nigel Farage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Farage), leader of [Reform UK](59757202.md); [Zack Polanski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zack_Polanski), leader of the [Greens](79787.md); [Liz Saville Roberts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liz_Saville_Roberts), leader of [Plaid Cymru](53343.md) in Westminster; and [John Swinney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Swinney), the leader of the [Scottish National Party](28887.md) and [First Minister of Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Minister_of_Scotland).[^cite_note-416]

##### 2026 Gordon and Denton by-election

Main article: [2026 Gorton and Denton by-election](82184590.md)



After [Andy Burnham](414818.md) had his candidacy controversially blocked, the by-election was won by Green Party candidate [Hannah Spencer](82277289.md), whom Burnham previously defeated in the [2024 Greater Manchester mayoral election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Greater_Manchester_mayoral_election)

Following media and public speculation, [Andy Burnham](414818.md), the incumbent [Mayor of Greater Manchester](44287322.md) and former MP for [Leigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leigh_\(UK_Parliament_constituency\)), requested approval to stand as Labour's candidate in the [Gordon and Denton by-election](82184590.md) on 24 January.[^cite_note-guardian-taylor-24jan26-417] As a sitting [directly elected mayor](598892.md), the party's rules required him to seek the approval of the party's [National Executive Committee](343817.md) (NEC) before putting himself forward for selection as a parliamentary candidate. The NEC met on 25 January and voted 8–1 against Burnham's candidacy.[^cite_note-bbc-nevett-mason-418] In the vote, NEC Chair and [Home Secretary](149104.md) [Shabana Mahmood](27266407.md) abstained, Starmer voted against, and party Deputy Leader [Lucy Powell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucy_Powell) voted for allowing Burnham to stand.[^cite_note-bbc-nevett-mason-418] Burnham expressed his disappointment and concern about the impact of the ruling for Labour in the [2026 United Kingdom local elections](79139854.md), the [2026 Scottish Parliament election](65934215.md) and [2026 Senedd election](68245236.md).[^cite_note-bbc-nevett-mason-418] After the decision to block Burnham from standing as a Labour candidate, 50 Labour MPs and half a dozen Labour peers signed a letter objecting to the NEC's decision.[^cite_note-bbc-wheeler-seddon-419] This ultimately led to [Hannah Spencer](82277289.md) of the [Green Party](79787.md) winning the by-election.[^cite_note-auto3-420] After the by-election result, Burnham publicly congratulated Spencer on her "historic win," noting that he had been isolating with [COVID-19](63030231.md) during the final stages of the campaign. Spencer has acknowledged Burnham's local popularity, stating that if he had been the candidate, she would have faced a "harder fight".[^cite_note-auto3-420][^cite_note-auto4-421] Many commentators speculated that Burnham could have retained the seat for Labour, especially as he had previously beaten Spencer in the [2024 mayoral election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Greater_Manchester_mayoral_election).[^cite_note-Burnham-422][^cite_note-auto4-421]

The by-election had been framed by the media as a route for Burnham to return to Westminster and potentially challenge Starmer for the Labour leadership.[^cite_note-bbc-nevett-423][^cite_note-sky-coates-424] Labour figures were reported to be concerned that Burnham's candidacy would trigger a by-election for the position of mayor, which would be expensive and politically risky for the party: MP [Graham Stringer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_Stringer) expressed his reluctance to let [Reform UK](59757202.md) "have a go" at winning the mayoralty.[^cite_note-bbc-nevett-423] Burnham's application to stand in the by-election was supported by some senior Labour figures, including former deputy prime minister [Angela Rayner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angela_Rayner), Mayor of London [Sadiq Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sadiq_Khan), Powell, and Energy Secretary [Ed Miliband](1290018.md).[^cite_note-independent-devlin-425][^cite_note-bbc-whannel-24jan26-426] Labour sources also stated concerns to the BBC about the prospect of a "divisive campaign".[^cite_note-bbc-nevett-mason-418] [Andrea Egan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrea_Egan), the general secretary of the trade union [Unison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unison_\(trade_union\)), warned against any potential "stitch-up" in Labour's selection process.[^cite_note-guardian-mason-23jan26-427] [Mainstream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainstream_\(political_organisation\)), a group associated with Burnham,[^cite_note-newstatesman-eaton-428] launched a petition to Mahmood calling for a "fair, democratic and open selection". [Momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momentum_\(organisation\)) also expressed support for Burnham's candidacy.[^cite_note-labourlist-green-429]

#### 2026 leadership crisis and resignation

Main article: [2026 Labour Party leadership crisis](83157483.md)



[Keir Starmer](18681491.md) (left) resigned as Prime Minister and was succeeded by [Andy Burnham](414818.md) (right)

In early June 2026, Starmer faced a leadership and government crisis.[^cite_note-\:44-430][^cite_note-431] It was caused by a historically poor result for the party at the [2026 local elections](79139854.md).[^cite_note-432][^cite_note-433] By mid-May 2026, over 95 Labour MPs had called on Starmer to resign or set out a timetable for his departure, and one [cabinet minister](162790.md) (Health Secretary [Wes Streeting](17169295.md)), four junior ministers (including [Jess Phillips](46646379.md)), and four [ministerial aides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_private_secretary) resigned in protest.[^cite_note-\:22-434] In June 2026, the crisis deepened following disputes over the government's [planned defence spending](#Defence), which resulted in four resignations in the [Ministry of Defence](203793.md): one cabinet minister (Defence Secretary [John Healey](434625.md)), one junior minister ([Al Carns](77290491.md)), and two [Parliamentary private secretaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_private_secretary): [Rachel Hopkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rachel_Hopkins) and [Pamela Nash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pamela_Nash)).[^cite_note-guardian-20260611-160][^cite_note-standard-20260612-435] Two cabinet ministers were reported to be privately urging him to go.[^cite_note-436][^cite_note-\:18-437] 103 MPs signed a joint statement defending Starmer's premiership in response to the crisis.

Potential leadership candidates widely speculated in the media to succeed Starmer as prime minister and Labour leader include Greater Manchester mayor [Andy Burnham](414818.md), former [Health Secretary](356531.md) [Wes Streeting](17169295.md), former deputy prime minister and housing secretary [Angela Rayner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angela_Rayner), and former Labour leader and incumbent Energy Secretary [Ed Miliband](1290018.md). Although he was highly critical of Starmer in his resignation letter, Streeting did not formally start a leadership challenge despite widespread media speculation he would do so.[^cite_note-\:54-438] Later that day, Burnham announced his intentions to stand in the [Makerfield](1811225.md) by-election triggered by [Josh Simons](77295092.md)' resignation, having previously applied to be the Labour Party candidate in the 2026 Gorton and Denton by-election but having his candidacy blocked by the party's [National Executive Committee](343817.md), a decision that Rayner has since called a mistake. Burnham went on to win the by-election and rejoined Parliament.[^cite_note-439]

Starmer announced his resignation outside 10 Downing Street on 22 June 2026. Burnham was nominated by over 90% of Labour MPs in the resulting leadership election, and was elected unopposed. He was appointed [prime minister](24150.md) on 20 July[^cite_note-440]

## Local election results and opinion polling

Main articles: [2025 United Kingdom local elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_United_Kingdom_local_elections) and [Leadership approval opinion polling for the next United Kingdom general election § Keir Starmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leadership_approval_opinion_polling_for_the_next_United_Kingdom_general_election#Keir_Starmer)



Approval ratings of Keir Starmer as Prime Minister

Starmer has been viewed unfavourably by the British public during his tenure as prime minister.[^cite_note-441] Starmer's average approval rating fell from +5% after the election to -30% by January 2025 before levelling off until April 2025 when it began to decline further, reaching -46% by November that year. A poll by [Ipsos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ipsos) indicates that Starmer is the most unpopular prime minister since Ipsos's records began in 1977. It found 13% of the public were satisfied with Starmer's job performance, 79% dissatisfied, giving a net approval rating of minus 66.[^cite_note-442][^cite_note-443][^cite_note-IpsosSep20252-444] Starmer's average net approval remained higher than [Boris Johnson](19065069.md)'s during the [Partygate scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partygate_scandal) prior to his resignation as Conservative leader and prime minister, [Jeremy Corbyn](415036.md)'s when he resigned as Leader of the opposition and Labour leader, and [Liz Truss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liz_Truss)' when she resigned as Conservative leader and prime minister.[^cite_note-445] Starmer's unpopularity has been tied to poor results for Labour in the [2025 United Kingdom local elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_United_Kingdom_local_elections). Likewise, the party has garnered poor polling numbers ahead of the [2026 United Kingdom local elections](79139854.md), [2026 Scottish Parliament election](65934215.md) and [2026 Senedd election](68245236.md). An opinion-piece in *[Politico](9172097.md)* viewed this as a likely catalyst for a leadership challenge.[^cite_note-446]

In September 2025, *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)* reported that plans to replace Starmer had begun among groups of MPs.[^cite_note-447][^cite_note-448] The reports were instigated by briefings to the media from Starmer's allies, which stated that he would resist any attempted challenge to his leadership.[^cite_note-449] By November, further reports emerged that plans to replace Starmer with [Wes Streeting](17169295.md) could be enacted after the [November 2025 United Kingdom budget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/November_2025_United_Kingdom_budget); this ultimately did not occur.[^cite_note-450] Shortly thereafter, [Clive Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clive_Lewis_\(politician\)) became the first Labour MP to publicly call for Starmer's resignation.[^cite_note-451] A [YouGov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/YouGov) poll for *[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times)* in February 2026 indicates that 51% of respondents thought Starmer was either more sleazy or as sleazy as Johnson. The poll found that 43% of participants did not think Starmer had handled the Mandelson scandal well; 23% thought that he had; 14% thought Starmer had shown good judgment in his handling of the affair.[^cite_note-452]

On 9 February 2026, Scottish Labour Party leader [Anas Sarwar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anas_Sarwar) held a press conference in Glasgow to publicly call for Starmer to resign. Sarwar stated that "the distraction needs to end and the leadership in Downing Street has to change," citing a series of "mistakes" that he believed were undermining Labour's prospects in the upcoming elections. Despite describing Starmer as a "decent man" and a "friend," Sarwar argued that his primary loyalty was to Scotland and that the prime minister's continued leadership was sabotaging the party's future.[^cite_note-453] Sarwar revealed he had spoken to Starmer earlier that day to inform him of his decision, noting that the two had "disagreed" on the necessity of his resignation. In the immediate aftermath, senior Cabinet members including Lammy and Reeves rallied behind Starmer, emphasizing his mandate and urging party unity. Within Scottish Labour, the move caused significant friction; while [Monica Lennon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monica_Lennon) supported Sarwar's "leadership," others like former Scottish Secretary [Ian Murray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_Murray_\(Scottish_politician\)) branded the call a threat to party stability. Opposition leaders [John Swinney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Swinney) and [Russell Findlay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell_Findlay) characterised the situation as "opportunism" and a "meltdown."[^cite_note-auto2-454] Addressing the Parliamentary Labour Party later that evening, Starmer remained defiant, stating he had "won every fight I've ever been in" and refused to walk away.[^cite_note-\:9-455]

[Green Party](79787.md) candidate [Hannah Spencer](82277289.md) won the [Gordon and Denton by-election](82184590.md) by a majority of 4,402. Political scientist [John Curtice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Curtice) called the result "seismic" and compared it on BBC TV with the [1962 Orpington by-election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1962_Orpington_by-election) for its historic impact. It was triggered by the resignation of [Andrew Gwynne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Gwynne), who was sitting as an independent following his suspension from the Labour Party. Gwynne said his resignation was due to "significant ill health", and advice from his doctor that it was unsafe for him to return to work. It was the second parliamentary by-election during Starmer's tenure as prime minister, after the [2025 Runcorn and Helsby by-election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Runcorn_and_Helsby_by-election), which Reform UK candidate [Sarah Pochin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Pochin) won. Candidates representing 11 parties were standing, but numerous commentators described it as a three-way race between Labour, the Green Party and Reform UK. Political scientist [Robert Ford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Ford_\(academic\)) described the by-election as a "pollster's nightmare", with any of three parties being able to win. Turnout for the by-election was 47.62%, only fractionally down on the 2024 general election.[^cite_note-456][^cite_note-457] Spencer won the by-election with a majority of 4,402 votes, defeating the [Reform UK](59757202.md), with the Labour Party (which had held on in [the region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Gorton_\(UK_Parliament_constituency\)) uninterruptedly since [1935](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1935_United_Kingdom_general_election)) being reduced to a distant third position. She became the Green Party's fifth MP and first in the [North of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_England).[^cite_note-2026G\&DResults-458][^cite_note-459] This was also the first ever parliamentary by-election win for the Greens.[^cite_note-auto3-420]

## Ministry

Main article: [Starmer ministry](77280613.md)



Starmer attending his first [Cabinet](77280613.md) meeting, 6 July 2024

Starmer formed his government throughout 5–7 July, after his party won 411 seats in the [2024 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_Kingdom_general_election), with the new Cabinet first meeting on 6 July and the new Parliament being called to meet on 9 July.[^cite_note-\:0-460][^cite_note-461][^cite_note-462] It has been noted for its female political representation, appointing women to a record half of the [Cabinet](162790.md) (including [Rachel Reeves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rachel_Reeves) as the first female [Chancellor of the Exchequer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancellor_of_the_Exchequer) in British history) and two of the five top positions in the British government, formerly including [Angela Rayner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angela_Rayner) as [Deputy Prime Minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deputy_Prime_Minister_of_the_United_Kingdom) and [Secretary of State for Housing, Communities and Local Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_State_for_Housing,_Communities_and_Local_Government).[^cite_note-463][^cite_note-464]

Starmer also appointed three [politically independent](2172378.md) experts: scientist [Patrick Vallance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Vallance) as [Minister of State for Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_of_State_for_Science), rehabilitation campaigner [James Timpson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Timpson) as [Minister of State for Prisons, Parole and Probation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_of_State_for_Prisons,_Parole_and_Probation), and international law expert [Richard Hermer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Hermer) as [Attorney General for England and Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attorney_General_for_England_and_Wales).[^cite_note-465] The government includes a few ministers from the [New Labour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Labour) governments of Tony Blair and Gordon Brown, including [Hilary Benn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilary_Benn), [Yvette Cooper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yvette_Cooper), [David Lammy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Lammy) and [Ed Miliband](1290018.md) in the Cabinet, and [Jacqui Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacqui_Smith) and [Douglas Alexander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Alexander) as junior ministers.[^cite_note-466][^cite_note-467][^cite_note-468]
