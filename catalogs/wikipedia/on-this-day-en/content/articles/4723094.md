**Nouri Kamil Muhammad-Hasan al-Maliki** ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language): نوري كامل محمد حسن المالكي; born 20 June 1950), also known as **Jawad al-Maliki** (جواد المالكي) or simply Nouri al Maliki, is an Iraqi politician and leader of the [Islamic Dawa Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Dawa_Party) since 2007. He served as the [prime minister of Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_minister_of_Iraq) from 2006 to 2014 and as the [vice president](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vice_President_of_Iraq) from 2014 to 2015 and again from 2016 to 2018.

Al-Maliki began his political career as a [Shia dissident](22350732.md) opposed to former Iraqi president [Saddam Hussein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saddam_Hussein) in the late 1970s, and rose to prominence after he fled a death sentence and went into exile for 24 years. During his time abroad, he became a senior leader of the Islamic Dawa Party, coordinated the activities of anti-Saddam guerrillas, and built relationships with officials from the [Islamic Republic of Iran](14653.md) and [Ba'athist Syria](57134800.md), seeking their help in overthrowing Saddam's government. Both during and after the [American-led occupation of Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupation_of_Iraq_\(2003%E2%80%932011\)) (2003–2011), al-Maliki worked closely with the [Multi-National Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-National_Force_%E2%80%93_Iraq) (MNF–I), and continued to cooperate with the [United States](3434750.md) following the withdrawal from Iraq.

Three years after the [2003 invasion of Iraq](201936.md), al-Maliki became the country's first post-Saddam full-term prime minister. The [first-term al-Maliki administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Maliki_I_Government) succeeded the [Iraqi Transitional Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_Transitional_Government); his first cabinet was approved by the [Iraqi National Assembly](1445450.md) and formally sworn in on 20 May 2006. His second cabinet, in which he also held the positions of acting Interior Minister, acting Defense Minister, and acting National Security Minister, was approved on 21 December 2010. In the wake of a string of defeats to the [Islamic State](9087364.md) during their [Northern Iraq offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Iraq_offensive_\(June_2014\)), American officials said that al-Maliki should give up his premiership.[^cite_note-3] Two months later under pressure, on 14 August 2014, he announced his resignation as prime minister.[^cite_note-4]

During his eight years in power from 2006 to 2014, allegations of corruption were widespread, with hundreds of billions of dollars allegedly vanishing from government coffers.[^cite_note-5] He was criticized by American officials[^cite_note-6] and by local Iraqis for empowering Shia militias, for his close ties with Iranian government/military officials,[^cite_note-7] and for fuelling [Iraqi sectarian violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sectarian_violence_in_Iraq) by favouring Shia political/military figures over [Kurds](35423251.md) and [Sunni Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam_in_Iraq) as well as other [non-Shia minorities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minorities_in_Iraq).[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9] In September 2014, al-Maliki was elected as one of three of Iraq's vice presidents, an office he held despite attempts to abolish the post.[^cite_note-aawsat1-10]

## Early life and education

Nouri al-Maliki was born in the village of Janaja in Abu Gharaq, a central Iraqi town situated between [Karbala](210957.md) and [Al Hillah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Hillah). He is a member of the [Al-Ali Tribe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ali_tribe_\(Iraq\)), an offshoot of the [Bani Malik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bani_Malik_\(tribe\)) tribe. He attended school in Al Hindiyah ([Hindiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindiya)). Al-Maliki received his high school degree from Hindiya city and moved to Baghdad with his family. Al-Maliki lived for a time in [Al Hillah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Hillah), where he worked in the education department. His grandfather, [Muhammad Hasan Abi al-Mahasin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Hasan_Abi_al-Mahasin), was a poet and cleric who was the representative of the Revolutionary Council (Al-Majlis Al-Milli) of the Iraqi revolution against the British in 1920, and was Iraq's Minister of Education under King [Faisal I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faisal_I).[^cite_note-11]

## Exile and return to Iraq



Al-Maliki during his youth

On 16 July 1979, al-Maliki fled Iraq after he was discovered to be a member of the outlawed [Islamic Dawa Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Dawa_Party). According to a brief biography on the Islamic Dawa Party's website, he left Iraq via [Jordan](7515964.md) in October, and soon moved to [Syria](7515849.md), adopting the pseudonym "Jawad". He left Syria for [Iran](14653.md) in 1982, where he lived in [Tehran](57654.md) until 1990, before returning to [Damascus](8914.md) where he remained until [U.S.-led coalition forces invaded Iraq and toppled Saddam's regime in 2003](201936.md).[^cite_note-islamicdawaparty.com-12] While living in Syria, he worked as a political officer for Dawa, developing close ties with [Hezbollah](13919.md) and particularly with the [Iranian government](50913538.md), supporting Iran's effort to topple Saddam's regime.[^cite_note-13]

While living in Damascus, al-Maliki edited the party newspaper *Al-Mawqif* and rose to head the party's Damascus branch. In 1990, he joined the Joint Action Committee and served as one of its rotating chairman. The committee was a Damascus-based opposition coalition for a number of Hussein's opponents.[^cite_note-islamicdawaparty.com-12] The Dawa Party participated in the Iraqi National Congress between 1992 and 1995, withdrawing because of disagreements over who should head it.[^cite_note-14]

Upon his return to his native Iraq after the fall of Saddam in April 2003, al-Maliki became the deputy leader of the Supreme National Debaathification Commission of the [Iraqi Interim Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_Interim_Government), formed to purge former [Baath Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baath_Party) officials from the military and government. He was elected to the transitional [National Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Assembly_of_Iraq) in January 2005. He was a member of the committee that drafted the new [constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Iraq) that was passed in October 2005.

### Selection as prime minister



Prime Minister al-Maliki shakes hands with U.S. President [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama) in [Baghdad](4492.md), 7 April 2009

In the [December 2005 Iraqi parliamentary election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/December_2005_Iraqi_parliamentary_election), the [United Iraqi Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Iraqi_Alliance) won the plurality of seats, and nominated [Ibrahim al-Jaafari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibrahim_al-Jaafari) to be Iraq's first full-term post-war prime minister. In April 2006, amid mounting criticism of ineffective leadership and favoritism by [Kurdish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurdish_people) and [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni) Arab politicians in parliament, al-Jaafari was forced to resign from power. On 22 April 2006, following close U.S. involvement in the selection of a new prime minister, al-Maliki's name arose from the four that had been interviewed by the [CIA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CIA) on their connections to [Iran](14653.md) (the others including [Hussein al-Shahristani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hussein_al-Shahristani) and [Ali al-Adeeb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_al-Adeeb)).

[United States Ambassador to Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_United_States_ambassadors_to_Iraq) [Zalmay Khalilzad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zalmay_Khalilzad) said that "\[Maliki's] reputation is as someone who is independent of Iran." Khalilzad also maintained that Iran "pressured everyone for Jaafari to stay".[^cite_note-15] However, al-Maliki was the preferred candidate of [Qasem Soleimani](16805217.md), the commander of the [Quds Force](4028355.md), and it was [Soleimani](16805217.md) who brokered the deal between senior [Shiite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shiite) and [Kurdish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurdish_people) leaders that led to his election as prime minister.[^cite_note-16]

### Formation of Al Maliki I Government

See also: [Al Maliki I Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Maliki_I_Government) and [2006 Iraqi government formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_Iraqi_government_formation)

On 20 May 2006, al-Maliki presented his [Cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Ministers_of_Iraq) to Parliament, minus permanent ministers of Defense and of Interior. He announced that he would temporarily handle the Interior Ministry himself, and [Salam al-Zobaie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salam_al-Zobaie) would temporarily act as Defense Minister. "We pray to God almighty to give us strength so we can meet the ambitious goals of our people who have suffered a lot", al-Maliki told the members of the assembly.[^cite_note-17]

### June–December 2006

During his first term, al-Maliki vowed to crack down on insurgents who he called "organized armed groups who are acting outside the state and outside the law". He had been criticized for taking too long to name permanent interior and defense ministers, which he did on 8 June 2006,[^cite_note-cnnJune2-18] just as al-Maliki and the Americans announced the killing of [Al Qaeda in Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Qaeda_in_Iraq) leader [Abu Musab al-Zarqawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Musab_al-Zarqawi).[^cite_note-cnnJune8-19][^cite_note-apJune8-20] Meanwhile, al-Maliki criticized coalition armed forces as reports of allegedly deliberate killings of Iraqi civilians (at [Haditha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haditha) and elsewhere) became known. He has been quoted as saying, "\[t]his is a phenomenon that has become common among many of the multinational forces. No respect for citizens, smashing civilian cars and killing on a suspicion or a hunch. It's unacceptable." According to Ambassador Khalilzad, al-Maliki had been misquoted, but it was unclear in what way.[^cite_note-forbesjune2-21]

The international [Committee to Protect Journalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_to_Protect_Journalists) wrote to al-Maliki in June 2006, complaining of a "disturbing pattern of restrictions on the press" and of the "imprisonment, intimidation, and censorship of journalists".[^cite_note-22]

His relationship with the press was often contentious. On 24 August 2006, for example, he banned television channels from broadcasting images of bloodshed in the country and warned of legal action against those violating the order. Major General Rashid Flayah, head of a national police division added "...We are building the country with Kalashnikovs and you should help in building it with the use of your pen".[^cite_note-23] Early in his term, al-Maliki was criticized by some for alleged reluctance to tackle Shiite militias. In October 2006, he complained about an American raid against a Shiite militia leader because he said it had been conducted without his approval.[^cite_note-24] Al-Maliki's job was complicated by the balance of power within parliament, with his position relying on the support of two Shiite blocs, that of [Muqtada al-Sadr](351058.md) and the [Supreme Islamic Iraqi Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Islamic_Iraqi_Council) of [Abdul Aziz al-Hakim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdul_Aziz_al-Hakim), that his Dawa party has often been at odds with.[^cite_note-25] Progress was also frequently blocked by Sunni Arab politicians who alleged that the dominant Shiite parties were pursuing sectarian advantage. Al-Maliki had some success in finding compromise.

On 30 December 2006, al-Maliki signed the death warrant of [Saddam Hussein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saddam_Hussein) and declined a stay of execution, saying there would be "no review or delay" in the event. Citing the wishes of relatives of Hussein's victims, he said, "Our respect for human rights requires us to execute him."[^cite_note-26] Hussein's execution was carried out on 30 December 2006 (notably, the first [Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim) day of the feast of [Eid ul-Adha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eid_ul-Adha)).

### January 2007 – end 2009



The celebration ceremony of Iraq's national sovereignty was attended by Iraq's prime minister, Nouri al-Maliki, left, and the Minister of Defense, [Abd al-Qadir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdul_Qadir_Obeidi), right, Baghdad, 30 June 2009.

On 2 January 2007, the *[Wall Street Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wall_Street_Journal)* published an interview with al-Maliki in which he said he wished he could end his term before it expires in 2009.[^cite_note-27]

In 2007, unnamed U.S. military officers alleged al-Maliki was replacing Iraqi commanders who had cracked down on Shiite militias with party loyalists. An al-Maliki spokesman denied the allegation.[^cite_note-28]

In May 2007, the [Islamic Dawa Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Dawa_Party) removed Jaafari and elected al-Maliki as Secretary-General of the Dawa Party.[^cite_note-29]

In July 2008, al-Maliki, who earlier in the year fought off a recall effort in parliament, convinced Sunni politicians to end a year-long boycott \[possibly in relation to the [renewal, in December 2007, of UN mandate for U.S. operations in Iraq, without Iraqi parliament's approval](5043324.md#2007:_US_troops_surge)?] of the chamber and appointed some of them to cabinet positions. Analysts said the return of the Sunnis was made possible by the security gains under al-Maliki and by apparent progress in negotiations with the United States over American military withdrawal.[^cite_note-30]

By late 2008, al-Maliki started to stop transparency efforts by firing inspector generals.[^cite_note-31] He also started using sections of the armed forces against his political rivals.[^cite_note-32]

By October–November 2008, the al-Malki government had witnessed improvements in the security situation in many parts of the country. In Baghdad, a peace deal signed between [Muqtada al-Sadr](351058.md)'s [Mahdi Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahdi_Army) and the government had eased tensions, though sporadic sectarian incidents continued, as did occasional fighting between U.S. forces and Shiite militiamen, particularly in [Sadr City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sadr_City).[^cite_note-33]

Maliki in May 2009 talked about the need to make a secure and sustainable environment for investment in order for successful reconstruction and has enacted new investment laws to try to achieve this. He also acknowledged Iraq's unfortunate reliance on oil to finance reconstruction thus far, although the revenue began to be spent on other possible revenue sources including agriculture and energy.[^cite_note-34]

### Al Maliki II Government, 2010–2014

Main article: [Al Maliki II Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Maliki_II_Government)

See also: [2010 Iraqi government formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Iraqi_government_formation), [2010 Iraqi parliamentary election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Iraqi_parliamentary_election), and [History of Iraq (2003–2011)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Iraq_\(2003%E2%80%932011\))

On 22 December 2010, al-Maliki's second government, including all main blocs in the new parliament, was unanimously approved by parliament, 9 months after the [2010 parliamentary election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Iraqi_parliamentary_election). On 5 February 2011, a spokesperson for al-Maliki said he would not run for a third term in 2014, limiting himself in the name of democracy, in a nod to the [Arab Spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Spring).[^cite_note-35]

On 19 December 2011, the Vice President of Iraq, [Tariq al-Hashemi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tariq_al-Hashemi), was accused of orchestrating bombing attacks and a hit squad killing Shiite politicians, and his arrest was warranted. This led to his Sunni/Shia [Iraqiyya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_National_Movement) party (with 91 seats the largest party in parliament) boycotting parliament, which lasted until late January 2012. Hashemi was in September 2012 *in absentia* sentenced to death but had already fled to Turkey, which declared it will not extradite him to Iraq. This affair fueled Sunni Muslim and Kurdish resentment against Maliki who critics said was monopolizing power.[^cite_note-aljaz1012t-36]

Al-Maliki lead Iraq through an increase in anti-Sunni violence, including the bloody crack-down on the [2012–2013 Sunni protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012%E2%80%932013_Iraqi_protests), which has been interpreted as leading to the rise of [ISIS](9087364.md).[^cite_note-37] The military under the al-Maliki administration was known for its corruption and was plagued with [ghost soldiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost_soldiers), a corruption scheme with soldiers names on the pay rolls but not actually in service.[^cite_note-\:1-38] When ISIS increased its activity in the first part of the [2013–2017 War in Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_in_Iraq_\(2013%E2%80%932017\)), Maliki led Iraq through major defeats, including the [June 2014 northern Iraq offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Iraq_offensive_\(June_2014\)) which saw the catastrophic collapse of the Iraqi army in that region and the [fall of Mosul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Mosul), where an army of 1,500 ISIS militants won over 60,000 Iraqi soldiers.

A former commander of the Iraqi ground forces, Ali Ghaidan, accused al-Maliki of being the one who issued the order to withdraw from the city of Mosul.[^cite_note-39] By late June, the Iraqi government had lost control of its borders with both [Jordan](7515964.md) and [Syria](7515849.md).[^cite_note-40] al-Maliki called for a national [state of emergency](298608.md) on 10 June following the attack on Mosul, which had been seized overnight. However, despite the security crisis, [Iraq's parliament](1445450.md) did not allow Maliki to declare a state of emergency; many [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni) [Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab) and [Kurdish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurd) legislators boycotted the session because they opposed expanding the prime minister's powers.[^cite_note-autogenerated2-41]

By August 2014 al-Maliki was still holding on to power tenaciously despite Iraq's president [Fuad Masum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuad_Masum) nominating [Haidar al-Abadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haidar_al-Abadi) to take over. Al-Maliki referred the matter to the federal court claiming the president's nomination was a "constitutional violation". He said: "The insistence on this until the end is to protect the state."[^cite_note-NewPM-42] On 14 August 2014, however, in the face of growing calls from world leaders and members of his own party the embattled prime minister Al-Maliki announced he was stepping down.[^cite_note-BBC_News_2014-43]

### Sunni friction

See also: [Sectarian violence against Sunni Arabs in Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sectarian_violence_against_Sunni_Arabs_in_Iraq)

Maliki's critics assert that he did his utmost to limit the power of both Kurds and Sunnis between 2006 and 2014. Their view is that Maliki worked to further centralise governance and amass greater controls and power—from militarily to legislative—for his party. Instead of strengthening and securing Iraq, Maliki's actions have led to a rise in both [Kurdish nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurdish_nationalism) and Sunni insurgency, which has resulted in civil war and the effective failure of the Iraqi state.[^cite_note-44]

The reign of al-Maliki has been described as sectarian by both Sunni Iraqis and western analysts; something which helped fuel a Sunni uprising in the country in 2014.[^cite_note-45] During the [Northern Iraq offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Iraq_offensive_\(June_2014\)), beginning in June 2014, [ISIS](9087364.md) vowed to take power away from al-Maliki, who called upon Kurdish [Peshmerga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peshmerga) forces to help keep Iraq out of the hands of ISIS, as well as air support from American [drones](337755.md) in order to eliminate dangerous jihadist elements in the country, which was refused by the United States, as "administration spokesmen have insisted that the United States is not actively considering using warplanes or armed drones to strike \[jihadist havens]."

The announcement of al-Maliki's resignation on 14 August 2014 and the leadership transition to Haider al-Abadi caused a major realignment of Sunni Arab public opinion away from armed opposition groups and to the Iraqi government, since many Iraqi Sunni Arabs were optimistic that the new government would address their grievances and deliver more public goods and services to them than the government led by al-Maliki.[^cite_note-46]

### Relationship with the U.S.

See also: [Iraq–United States relations](17681177.md)



U.S. President [George W. Bush](3414021.md) and al-Maliki shake hands during [a press conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bush_shoeing_incident).

In an interview published by the German magazine *[Der Spiegel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Der_Spiegel)* in June 2008, al-Maliki said that a schedule for a withdrawal of U.S. troops from the country of "about 16 months ... would be the right time-frame for a withdrawal, with the possibility of slight changes". In the interview, he said the U.S. government has been reluctant to agree to a timetable "because they feel it would appear tantamount to an admission of defeat. But that isn't the case at all ... it is not evidence of a defeat, but of a victory, of a severe blow we have inflicted on [Al Qaeda](1921.md) and the militias." He said U.S. negotiators were coming around to his point of view.[^cite_note-47]

[Hillary Clinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hillary_Clinton) and [Carl Levin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Levin) were two of several U.S. politicians who called for him to be removed from office in 2007. Senator Clinton urged Iraq's parliament to select a "less divisive and more unifying figure" and implied she felt al-Maliki was too concerned about Iraq's Shiite majority and not enough with national reconciliation. "During his trip to Iraq last week, Senator Levin ... confirmed that the Iraqi government is nonfunctional and cannot produce a political settlement because it is too beholden to religious and sectarian leaders", she said.[^cite_note-48]

Al-Maliki said the [Democratic](5043544.md) [senators](24909346.md) were acting as if Iraq were "their property" and that they should "come to their senses" and "respect democracy".[^cite_note-49] After 17 Iraqis were shot and killed by [Blackwater USA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackwater_Baghdad_shootings) security guards al-Maliki called on the U.S. embassy to stop working with the company and said: "What happened was a crime. It has left a deep grudge and anger, both inside the government and among the Iraqi people."[^cite_note-50]

Maliki's friendly gestures towards Iran have sometimes created tension between his government and the United States but he has also been willing to consider steps opposed by Tehran, particularly while carrying out negotiations with the United States on a joint-security pact. A June 2008 news report noted that al-Maliki's visit to Tehran seemed to be "aimed at getting Iran to tone down its opposition and ease criticism within Iraq". Al-Maliki said an agreement reached with the U.S. won't preclude good relations with neighbors like Iran.[^cite_note-51]

In August 2007, [CNN](62028.md) reported that the firm of Barbour, Griffith & Rogers had "begun a public campaign to undermine the government of Iraqi Prime Minister Nuri al-Maliki". The network described BGR as a "powerhouse Republican [lobbying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lobbying) firm with close ties to the White House".[^cite_note-autogenerated1-52] CNN also mentioned that [Ayad Allawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayad_Allawi) is both al-Maliki's rival and BGR's client, although it did not assert that Allawi had hired BGR to undermine al-Maliki.[^cite_note-autogenerated1-52]

In late 2014, Vice President Al-Maliki accused the United States of using [ISIL](9087364.md) as a pretext to maintain its military presence in Iraq. He stated that "the Americans began this sedition in Syria and then expanded its dimensions into Iraq and it seems that they intend to further stretch this problem to other countries in their future plans."[^cite_note-53]

### Official visits



Nouri al-Maliki, inspecting a Guard of Honour formed by No 7 Company [Coldstream Guards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coldstream_Guards) at the [FCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_and_Commonwealth_Office), 30 April 2009.

On 13 June 2006, [U.S. President](24113.md) [George W. Bush](3414021.md) paid a visit to [Baghdad](4492.md) to meet with al-Maliki and [President of Iraq](209174.md) [Jalal Talabani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jalal_Talabani), as a token of support for the new government.[^cite_note-abramowitz-54] During this visit, they announced the Iraqi Leaders Initiative, in which students from Iraq would go to the United States to build a personal connection between the two countries.[^cite_note-55] On 25 June, al-Maliki presented a national reconciliation plan to the Iraqi parliament. The peace plan sets out to remove powerful militias from the streets, open a dialogue with rebels, and review the status of purged members of the once dominant Ba'ath party. Some viewed this as a bold step towards rebuilding Iraq and reaching out to Sunnis.[^cite_note-reconciliationplan-56]



Nouri al-Maliki with Speaker Pelosi in Baghdad 2009

By July 2006, when al-Maliki visited the United States, violence had continued and even escalated, leading many to conclude that the reconciliation plan was not working or was moving too slowly. On 26 July 2006, al-Maliki addressed a joint meeting of the [U.S. Congress](31756.md).[^cite_note-USCongress-57] Several New York Democrats boycotted the speech after Al-Maliki condemned Israel's [attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_Israel-Lebanon_conflict) on [Lebanon](17771.md). [Howard Dean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Dean), the DNC chairman, accused Al-Maliki of being an "anti-Semite" and said the United States shouldn't spend so much on Iraq and then hand it over to people like al-Maliki.[^cite_note-58]

In September 2006, Al-Maliki made his first official visit to neighbouring [Iran](14653.md), whose alleged influence on Iraq is a matter of concern for Washington, D.C. He discussed with Iranian officials, including president [Mahmoud Ahmadinejad](7907317.md), the "principle of no interference in internal affairs" during his visit on 11 and 12 September 2006, i.e., political and security issues. His visit closely followed an incident in which Iran detained Iraqi soldiers it accused of having illegally crossed the border.[^cite_note-Iraq_PM_will_visit_Iran_Monday-59]

Ibrahim Shaker, Iraqi defence ministry spokesman, said the five soldiers, one officer and one translator involved had simply been doing "their duty".[^cite_note-Iraq_PM_will_visit_Iran_Monday-59] During his visit al-Maliki called the Islamic Republic of Iran "a good friend and brother". A press conference given by al-Maliki and U.S. President George Bush on 14 December 2008, was disrupted when Iraqi journalist [Muntadhar al-Zaidi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muntadhar_al-Zaidi) [threw his shoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bush_shoeing_incident) at Bush.[^cite_note-60]

## Later duties

On 26 January 2013 al-Maliki's opponents passed a law[^cite_note-wordpress1-61] which prohibited al-Maliki from running for a third term but an Iraqi court later rejected it.[^cite_note-62] By August 2014, al-Maliki lost all his chances to win a third term in office.[^cite_note-63]

On 8 September 2014, during approval of the new government led by [Haider al-Abadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haider_al-Abadi), al-Maliki was named one of the three vice presidents, a prestigious albeit largely ceremonial post.[^cite_note-64] On 11 August 2015, the Parliament approved a reform package by Prime Minister al-Abadi that foresaw, among other measures, the elimination of the three vice president posts.[^cite_note-65] However, following a lawsuit opened by fellow Vice President [Usama al-Nujayfi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usama_al-Nujayfi), al-Maliki declared in September 2015 that he was still holding his office because the removal of the post was not in line with the Iraqi Constitution.[^cite_note-aawsat1-10] [Osama al-Nujaifi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osama_al-Nujaifi) filed a complaint against the decision in November 2015, considering it to be against the Constitution.[^cite_note-66] On 10 October 2016, the three posts of Vice Presidents were restored by the [Supreme Court of Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Iraq) which termed their abolition as unconstitutional.[^cite_note-restored-1]

In January 2026, following the [2025 Iraqi parliamentary election](71453388.md), he was nominated as the sole candidate for [Iraqi premiership](208879.md) by the [Coordination Framework](82383522.md).[^cite_note-67] United States President [Donald Trump](4848272.md) stated that the United States would end its support for Iraq if al-Maliki is elected.[^cite_note-68][^cite_note-69] Al-Maliki rejected Trump's comments, describing them as "blatant American interference in Iraq's internal affairs", and a "violation of its (Iraq's) sovereignty."[^cite_note-70] In March, the Coordination Framework reportedly withdrew his nomination.[^cite_note-71]

## Controversies

During al-Maliki's first term, 2006–2010, his anti-Sunni policies brought about the ethnic cleansing Sunnis;[^cite_note-Rached_39–53-72] when al-Maliki insisted on disarming Sunni groups before Shia militias, and armed Shia's entered neighborhoods where Sunnis had been disarmed.[^cite_note-73][^cite_note-74] In Baghdad, Sunni population plummeted from 45% in 2003 to 25% in 2007.[^cite_note-Rached_39–53-72]

During his second term, 2011–2014, al-Maliki's policies allowed the Shia militias to rise in power, despite the prohibition against the existence of non-state militias under article 9(b) of the [Iraqi constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Iraq).[^cite_note-75][^cite_note-washingtoninstitute.org-76] Executive orders issued by al-Maliki provided the militias with government support and allowed them to bear arms, eventually forming the [Popular Mobilization Forces](45671374.md) as a new branch of the Iraqi military.[^cite_note-washingtoninstitute.org-76] al-Maliki is blamed for the [sectarian tensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sectarian_violence_in_Iraq) that resulted in the welcoming of ISIS by the Sunni population in Iraq, and for the corruption and mismanagement that resulted in the weakening of the Iraqi military, making it unable to fight ISIS on the battlefield.[^cite_note-\:0-77] al-Maliki is seen as responsible for the fall of three provinces into the hands of ISIS, and the resulting 40,000 Iraqi deaths.[^cite_note-\:0-77]

In 2011, after [American forces left Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Withdrawal_of_United_States_troops_from_Iraq_\(2007%E2%80%932011\)),[^cite_note-\:2-78] al-Maliki reportedly allied himself with Iran, charging the Sunni vice president with fraudulent allegations of terrorism, initiating a "political Jihad" against the Sunni finance minister of Iraq, freeing a Hezbollah terrorist from prison despite objections from the US, and opening Iraq to Iranian supply chains in support of the Assad regime.[^cite_note-\:2-78] Early that year, protests against the government were violently suppressed by Al-Maliki forces. During demonstrations on February 25, among the largest in the Arab world at the time, 29 people were shot dead by security forces in a number of cities, among them Baghdad, Fallujah, Mosul and Tikrit.[^cite_note-\:3-79] In March, the offices of the [Kurdish ICP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_Communist_Party) and Iraqi Nation Party had been seized by authorities.[^cite_note-\:3-79] Despite widespread participation, Al-Maliki blamed the protests on conspiracies and foreign elements, as well as “enemies of the political process, the armed terrorist groups and the remnants of the former regime” within Iraq.[^cite_note-80][^cite_note-\:2-78] The incidents were seen as part of a continuous crackdown against government opposition in Iraq.[^cite_note-\:3-79]

In 2012, al-Maliki equipped the Shia militia [Asa’ib Ahl al-Haq](26106154.md) with bullet proof vehicles, released all their prisoners detained by US forces, and allowed them to conduct an anti-American parade celebrating the withdrawal of US forces.[^cite_note-81] This act was seen as the initiation of a political alliance between al-Maliki and Asa’ib Ahl al-Haq.[^cite_note-82]

In 2013, Sunni protesters in the demonstrations of [Falluja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falluja) and [Hawija](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawija) were declared terrorists by al-Maliki, who, using the military and helicopters, suppressed them violently.[^cite_note-83]

### Corruption and theft

According to spokesman of The Iraqi Commission of Integrity (CoI) Adil Nouri, during al-Maliki's 8-year term between 2006 and 2014, half of the funds meant for the reconstruction of Iraq, and an identical portion of the country's oil revenue were "stolen" and “vanished" from government coffers, amounting to approximately 500 billion dollars.[^cite_note-84] Another study reported that the funds lost through secret deals and smuggling may amount to one trillion dollars.[^cite_note-\:4-85] al-Maliki's government has been accused of fabricating crises and terrorism threats to conceal its corruption, leaving [Haider al-Abadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haider_al-Abadi), al-Maliki's successor a nearly empty treasury.[^cite_note-\:4-85]

## Personal life

Al-Maliki is married to Faleeha Khalil, with whom he has four daughters and one son. His son Ahmed was head of Al-Maliki's security, and two of his sons-in-law also worked in his office.[^cite_note-86]

On 26 April 2006, al-Maliki stopped using the pseudonym Jawad which he had used since moving to Syria in the early 1980s.[^cite_note-chitrib-87] However, the pseudo- or code name (*[Kunya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kunya_\(Arabic\))*) "Abu Esraa" (father of Esraa – his eldest daughter) is still occasionally heard on Iraqi satellite media, because it is very common in Arab culture (and in Iraqi culture in particular) to call someone by his eldest child's name, especially among his close friends and followers.
