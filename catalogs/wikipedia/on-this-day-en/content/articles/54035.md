***Jus soli*** (English: [/dʒʌs ˈsoʊlaɪ/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [*juss SOH-ly*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key)[^cite_note-1] or [/juːs ˈsoʊli/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [*yooss SOH-lee*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key),[^cite_note-2] Latin: [\[juːs ˈsɔliː\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Latin)), meaning 'right of soil', is the [right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right) of anyone born in the territory of a state to [nationality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationality) or [citizenship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizenship). *Jus soli* was part of the [English common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_common_law), in contrast to *[jus sanguinis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jus_sanguinis)* ('right of blood') associated with the [French Civil Code of 1804](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Code).[^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4]

*Jus soli* is the predominant rule in the [Americas](29833.md); explanations for this geographical phenomenon include the establishment of lenient laws by past European colonial powers to entice immigrants from the [Old World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_World) and displace [native populations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas) in the [New World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_World), along with the emergence of successful [wars of independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wars_of_national_liberation) movements that widened the definition and granting of citizenship, as a prerequisite to the abolishment of slavery since the 19th century.[^cite_note-5]

There are 35 countries that provide citizenship unconditionally to anyone born within their national borders.[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7] Some countries outside the Americas with mixed systems extend *jus soli* citizenship on a limited basis to children who are not otherwise eligible for any national citizenship, such as children born to women who are unwed or from countries that do not recognize maternal *jus sanguinis* citizenship.[^cite_note-Global_Citizenship_Studies_2014_p._213-8][^cite_note-Treisman-9] Others impose a residency requirement requiring parents to live in the country for a certain number of years before children born in the country become eligible for conditional *jus soli* citizenship.[^cite_note-10] These mixed systems were implemented to fulfill treaty obligations after the atrocities of [World War II](32927.md) increased awareness about the vulnerability of [stateless](789786.md) persons.[^cite_note-Citizenship-11] When the sovereignty over a territory is transferred from one state to another, individuals may be given the right of [option of nationality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Option_of_nationality) based on *jus soli* or other rules.[^cite_note-Vaillot-12]

## Background

Jus soli, sometimes called *lex soli*, is the principle of citizenship acquired by the place of birth. French jurist Charles Demolombe invented the term around 1860.[^cite_note-13] Children born to a parent in the diplomatic or consular service of another state are often not eligible for *jus soli* citizenship in a host State.[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15]

Unconditional *jus soli* is mostly found in the Americas. Some countries outside the Americas with mixed systems extend *jus soli* citizenship on a limited basis to children who are not otherwise eligible for any national citizenship, such as children born to women who are unwed or from countries that do not recognize maternal *jus sanguinis* citizenship.[^cite_note-Global_Citizenship_Studies_2014_p._213-8][^cite_note-Treisman-9] Others impose a residency requirement requiring parents to live in the country for a certain number of years before children born in the country become eligible for conditional *jus soli* citizenship.[^cite_note-16] [UNHCR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNHCR) gives ten reasons for why people become stateless including laws related to marriage, administrative practices, renunciation of citizenship and nationality laws that discriminate on the basis of gender.[^cite_note-Weissbrodt-17]

There is a trend in some countries toward restricting *jus soli* by requiring that at least one of the child's parents be a citizen, national or legal permanent resident of the state in question at time of the child's birth.[^cite_note-cmatters-18]

### Reduction of statelessness



A map of parties to the Convention on the Reduction of Statelessness. Parties to Convention in dark green; countries which have signed, but not ratified in light green; non-members in grey.

Countries that have acceded to the 1961 [Convention on the Reduction of Statelessness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convention_on_the_Reduction_of_Statelessness) are obligated to grant nationality to people born in their territory who would otherwise become [stateless persons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stateless_person).[^cite_note-19][^cite_note-21][^cite_note-Weissbrodt-17] These mixed systems were implemented to fulfill treaty obligations after the atrocities of [World War II](32927.md) increased awareness about the vulnerability of stateless persons.[^cite_note-Citizenship-11][^cite_note-22]

The [American Convention on Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Convention_on_Human_Rights) similarly provides that "Every person has the right to the nationality of the state in whose territory he was born if he does not have the right to any other nationality."[^cite_note-Chen223-20]

### Birthright citizenship

The *New Oxford American Dictionary* defines birthright citizenship as "a legal right to citizenship for all children born in a country's territory, regardless of parentage".[^cite_note-23] In the United States *jus sanguinis* is not a constitutional right or a birth right.[^cite_note-schacher-24] Citizenship by *jus sanguinis* is a legal status conferred by statute. The term birthright citizenship usually means *jus soli* citizenship.[^cite_note-25]

Birthright citizenship is rooted in colonial history when settlers born in the colonial United States were considered "natural born" subjects of the King of England. The idea of conferring citizenship based on being born within the borders of the United States comes from this history.[^cite_note-26][^cite_note-naturalborn-27] Allegiance based on [natural law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_law) principles was the core concept of citizenship in *[Calvin's Case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvin's_Case)* in which [Edward Coke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Coke) said that "they that are born under the obedience, power, faith, ligealty or ligeance of the King are natural subjects and no aliens".[^cite_note-28] The American concept of citizenship is derived from republican principles and may have been influenced by the French writer [Emer de Vattel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emer_de_Vattel).[^cite_note-naturalborn-27]

### Abolition

Countries have been restricting the right of *jus soli* in order to discourage [birth tourism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_tourism), [anchor babies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anchor_baby) and [illegal immigration](7124412.md), for example with the [British Nationality Act 1981](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Nationality_Act_1981).[^cite_note-Dangerfield-29] India has abolished jus soli largely in reaction to [illegal immigration](7124412.md) from [Bangladesh](3454.md).[^cite_note-30] A person who was born in India from 26 January 1950 until 1 July 1987 is a citizen by birth, regardless of the parents' nationality. It was restricted in 1987 to people with at least one parent who was a citizen. As of 2008, a person born in India is a citizen only if at least one parent is a citizen, and the other parent is a citizen or a legal migrant. New Zealand abolished unrestricted birthright citizenship in 2005.[^cite_note-31] Around 9% of all births in the United States in 2023 were to mothers who were [illegal immigrants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illegal_immigration_in_the_United_States) or who had legal temporary status in the U.S.[^cite_note-y152-32]

### Europe

-  [France](5843419.md): Children born in France (including [overseas territories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overseas_France)) to at least one parent who is either (i) a French national or (ii) born in France, are automatically granted French nationality at birth. -  [Malta](19137.md): A person born in Malta on or after 1 August 1989 is automatically a Maltese citizen if at least one of their parents is Maltese or was born in Malta. Anyone born in Malta before 1 August 1989, regardless of their parents' circumstances, is automatically a Maltese citizen, as the country conferred unconditional *jus soli* until this date (see [Maltese nationality law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maltese_nationality_law)).

-  [Netherlands](21148.md): After 31 December 1984, the following conditions apply: "The child's mother has Dutch nationality at the time of birth, or the child's parents both have Dutch nationality at the time of birth, or the father of the child has Dutch nationality at the time of birth and is married/registered partner to the non-Dutch mother or acknowledged the child, or the child and the child's mother have their principal residence in the Kingdom of the Netherlands at the time of birth. One of the mother's parents also had her main residence in the Kingdom on the day the mother was born, or the child and the child's father have their principal residence in the Kingdom of the Netherlands at the time of birth. One of the father's parents also had his main residence in The Kingdom on the day the father was born."

  [^cite_note-82]

  [^cite_note-83]

-  [Portugal](23033.md): A child born in Portuguese territory to parents who do not possess another nationality is a Portuguese citizen. Also, a person born to foreign parents who were not serving their respective states at the time of birth is a Portuguese citizen if the person declares that they want to be Portuguese and provided that one of the parents has resided in Portugal for at least one year at the time of birth.

  [^cite_note-84]

-  [Spain](26667.md): A child born in Spain to foreign parents may acquire Spanish citizenship *jus soli* under certain conditions, for example, if either one of the parents was also born in Spain or if neither of the parents can transmit their nationality to the child (such as stateless parents).

-  [Ukraine](31750.md): A child born on the territory of Ukraine may acquire Ukrainian citizenship *jus soli*, if they do not acquire foreign nationality by *jus sanguinis* from parents, or if their parents have been granted refugee or asylum status in Ukraine, or if the child is stateless or of unknown nationality (see [Ukrainian citizenship law, articles 6 and 7](https://zakon.rada.gov.ua/laws/show/2235-14#Text)).

-  [United Kingdom](31717.md): Unrestricted *jus soli* citizenship was abolished by the [British Nationality Act of 1981](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Nationality_Act_1981). Between 1 January 1983 and 1 July 2006, children born out of wedlock to foreign (non-British) women and British men were not eligible for citizenship unless the mother was legally "settled" in the country. Beginning in 2006, British fathers could automatically pass on their nationality to children born out of wedlock. In 2015, this change was made retroactive, granting citizenship to out-of-wedlock children born prior to 2006.

  [^cite_note-85]

  &#x20;Under the current law, if neither parent is British or settled, then a child born in the UK can apply for British citizenship if they have spent the first ten years of their life in the UK (see [British nationality law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_nationality_law)).
