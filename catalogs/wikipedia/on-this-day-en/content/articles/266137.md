A **protest** (also called a **demonstration**, **remonstration**, or **remonstrance**) is a public act of objection, disapproval or dissent against political advantage.[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-2] Protests can be thought of as acts of [cooperation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperation) in which numerous people cooperate by attending, and share the potential costs and risks of doing so.[^cite_note-Larson-3] Protests can take many different forms, from individual statements to mass [political demonstrations](6787314.md). Protesters may organize a protest as a way of publicly making their opinions heard in an attempt to influence [public opinion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_opinion) or government policy, or they may undertake [direct action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_action) in an attempt to enact desired changes themselves.[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5] When protests are part of a systematic and peaceful [nonviolent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonviolence) campaign to achieve a particular objective, and involve the use of pressure as well as persuasion, they go beyond mere protest and may be better described as [civil resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_resistance) or [nonviolent resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonviolent_resistance).[^cite_note-CivilResistance-6]

Various forms of self-expression and protest are sometimes restricted by governmental policy (such as the requirement of [protest permits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protest_permit)),[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8] economic circumstances, religious orthodoxy, social structures, or media monopoly. One state reaction to protests is the use of [riot police](1148542.md). Observers have noted an increased [militarization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Militarization_of_police) of [protest policing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protest_policing) in many countries, with police deploying [armored vehicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vehicle_armour) and snipers against protesters. When such restrictions occur, protests may assume the form of open [civil disobedience](37073.md), more subtle forms of resistance against the restrictions, or may spill over into other areas such as culture and emigration.

A protest itself may at times be the subject of a [counter-protest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counter-protest). In such cases, counter-protesters demonstrate their support for the person, policy, action, etc. that is the subject of the original protest. Protesters and counter-protesters can sometimes violently clash. One study found that nonviolent activism during the [civil rights movement](49001.md) in the United States tended to produce favorable media coverage and changes in public opinion focusing on the issues organizers were raising, but violent protests tended to generate unfavorable media coverage that generated public desire to restore law and order.[^cite_note-9]

## Forms

See also: [Repertoire of contention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repertoire_of_contention)

A protest can take many forms.[^cite_note-12][^cite_note-Pinckney-13] Willingness to participate is influenced by individuals' ties within [social networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_network). Social connections can affect both the spread of factual information about a protest and [social pressures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_pressure) on participants.[^cite_note-Larson-3] Willing to participate will also vary depending on the type of protest. Likelihood that someone will respond to a protest is also affected by group identification, and by the types of tactics involved.[^cite_note-Bugden-14]



TET passed candidates who are protesting over SSC scam in [West Bengal](34040.md), beneath the statue of [Mahatma Gandhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahatma_Gandhi) in [Kolkata](47905.md) Maidan.

The Dynamics of Collective Action project and the [Global Nonviolent Action Database](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Nonviolent_Action_Database)[^cite_note-15] are two of the leading data collection efforts attempting to capture information about protest events. The Dynamics of Collective Action project considers the repertoire of protest tactics (and their definitions) to include:[^cite_note-\:0-16]

- **Rally** or **Demonstration**: Demonstration, rally, or similar protest, without reference to marching or walking in a [picket line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picketing) or standing in a [vigil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vigil). Reference to speeches, speakers, singing, or preaching, often verified by the presence of [PA sound equipment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_address_system) and sometimes by a platform or stage. Ordinarily will include worship services, speeches, briefings.

- **March**: Reference to moving from one location to another; to distinguish from rotating or walking in a circle with picket signs (which is a picket).

- **Vigil**: Most vigils have banners, placards, or leaflets so that people passing by, despite silence from participants, can be informed about the purpose of the vigil.

- **Picket**: The modal (most common) activity is picketing; there may be references to a picket line, informational picketing, or holding signs; "carrying signs and walking around in a circle". Holding signs, placards, or banners is not the defining criteria; rather, it is holding or carrying those items and walking a circular route, a phrase sometimes surprisingly found in the permit application.

- **Civil Disobedience**: Explicit protest that involves deliberately breaking laws deemed unjust in order to protest them; crossing barricades, prohibited use of segregated facilities (such as [lunch-counters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunch_counter_protests) or restrooms), voter registration drives (to earn non-eligible people the right to vote), or tying up phone lines.

- **Ceremony**: These celebrate or protest status transitions ranging from birth and death dates of individuals, organizations or nations; seasons; re-enlistment or commissioning of military personnel; or to anniversaries of any of the above. These are sometimes referenced by presenting flowers or wreaths commemorating, dedicating, or celebrating status transitions or their anniversary; e.g., an annual [merchant marine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merchant_marine) memorial service, celebrating [Hanukkah](7388.md) or Easter, or celebrating the birthday of [Martin Luther King Jr.](20076.md)

- **[Dramaturgical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dramaturgical_action) Demonstration**

- **Motorcade**: Vehicular procession (electoral campaigns or other issues)

- **Protest camps** or [peace camps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_camps), a long-standing protest or occupation, a form of [intentional community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intentional_community)

  [^cite_note-17]

- **Information distribution**: Tabling/petition gathering, lobbying, letter-writing campaigns, or [teach-ins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teach-in).

- **Symbolic Display**: e.g., a [menorah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menorah_\(Hanukkah\)) or [creche scene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nativity_scene), graffiti, [cross burning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_burning), sign, or standing display.

- **Attack** by collective group (not-one-on-one [assault](1466.md), crime, rape): Motivation for attack is the "other group's identity"

  [^cite_note-\:0-16]

  , as in [gay-bashing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gay_bashing) or [lynching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynching). Can also include verbal attacks or threats. (See [hate crime](13547.md))

- **Riot, melee, mob violence**: Large-scale (50+), use of violence by instigators against persons, property, police, or buildings separately or in combination, lasting several hours.

- **Strike, slow down, sick-ins, and employee work protest of any kind**: Regular strike through failure of negotiations or wildcat air strike. (Make note if a [wildcat strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildcat_strike_action).)

- **Boycott**: Organized refusal to buy or use a product or service. Examples: [rent strikes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rent_strike), [Montgomery bus boycotts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montgomery_bus_boycott)

- **Press Conference**: Only if specifically named as such in report, and must be the predominant activity form. Could involve disclosure of information to "educate the public" or influence various decision-makers.

- **Organization formation announcement** or **meeting announcement**: Meeting or press conference to announce the formation of a new organization.

- **Conflict, attack or clash (no instigator)**: This includes any boundary conflict in which no instigator can be identified, i.e. Black/white conflicts, abortion/anti-abortion conflicts.

- **Prayer Walk:** A prayer walk is an activity that consists of walking and praying at the same time. It is done not for the physical benefit but for the spiritual exercise, either publicly functioning as a demonstration or rally.

- **Lawsuit**: Legal maneuver by social movement organization or group.

- **[Peopleless Protest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peopleless_protest):** Simultaneous online and offline protests involving physical representations of protesters in public spaces that are subsequently assembled online. Developed in Europe during the [COVID-19 pandemic](62750956.md). Holographic projections of protesters were used in Spain to highlight the use of anti-protest laws in 2015.

## Typology



March next to the [Benito Juárez Hemicycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benito_Ju%C3%A1rez_Hemicycle), 27 August 1968, [Mexico City](18987.md)



[Street protesters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_protester) with signs demonstrating in [Helsinki, Finland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helsinki), after [Russia invaded Ukraine](79400268.md) in February 2022

Thomas Ratliff and Lori Hall[^cite_note-19] have devised a typology of six broad activity categories of the protest activities described in the Dynamics of Collective Action project.

- **Literal, symbolic, aesthetic and sensory** – Artistic, dramaturgical, and symbolic displays (street theater, dancing, etc.) including use of images, objects, graphic art, musical performances, or vocal/auditory exhibitions (speech-making, chanting, etc.).

  [^cite_note-20]

  [^cite_note-21]

  &#x20;May also include tactile exchanges of information (petitions, leaflets, etc.) and the destruction of objects of symbolic or political value. Highly visible and most diverse category of activity; impacts on society (police response, media focus, impact on potential allies, etc.) often are underestimated.

- **Solemnity and the sacred** – Vigils, prayer, or rallies, in the form of religious service, [candlelight vigils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candlelight_vigil), cross or coffin bearing etc. All directly related to the [Durkheimian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durkheimian) "[sacred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacred)", or some form of religious or spiritual practice, belief, or ideology. Events where sacred activity is the primary focus are rarely responded to by police with force or presence. Solemnity usually provides a distinct quietness or stillness, changing the energy, description, and interpretation of such events.

- **Institutional and conventional** – Institutionalized activity or activity highly dependent on formal political processes and social institutions (press conferences, lawsuits, lobbying, etc.). Often conflated with non-confrontational and nonviolent activities in research as the *other* or reference category. More *acceptable* because it operates, to some degree, within the system. Historically contentious issue in regard to the practice of protest due to this integration within the system.

- **Movement in space** – Marches or parades (processional activities) from one spatio-temporal location to another, with beginning or ending places sometimes chosen for symbolic reasons. Picket lines often used in labor strikes but can be used by non-labor actors but the key differences between picket and processionals are the distance of movement. Events that take the form of a procession are logistically much more difficult to police (even if it is for the safety of protesters). Marches are some of the largest events in this period.

- **Civil disobedience** – Withholding obligations, [sit-ins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sit-in), blockades, [shop-ins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shop-in), occupations, bannering, "camping", etc., are all specific activities which constitute the tactical form of civil disobedience. In some way, these activities directly or technically break the law. Usually given most attention by researchers, media, and authorities. Often conflated with violence and threats because of direct action and confrontational nature, but should serve as a distinct category of action (both in the context of tactical and strategic planning and in the control of activity).

- **Collective violence and threats** – Collective violence such as pushing, shoving, hitting, punching, damaging property, throwing objects, verbal threats, etc., is usually committed by a relative few out of many protesters (even tens of thousands). It is rare in occurrence and rarely condoned by the public or onlookers (particularly the media). Usually met with equivalent or overwhelming force in response by authorities.

Some forms of [direct action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_action) listed in this article are also [public demonstrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demonstration_\(people\)) or rallies:

- [Protest march](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demonstration_\(people\)), a historically and geographically common form of [nonviolent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonviolent) action by groups of people.

- [Picketing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picketing_\(protest\)), a form of protest in which people congregate outside a place of work or location where an event is taking place. Often, this is done in an attempt to dissuade others from going in ("crossing the picket line"), but it can also be done to draw public attention to a cause.

- [Street protesters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_protester) demonstrate in areas with high visibility, often employing handmade [placards](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/placard) such as [sandwich boards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandwich_board) or picket signs in order to maximize exposure and interaction with the public.

- [Lockdowns](2530301.md) and [lock-ons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lock-on_\(protest_tactic\)) are a way to stop movement of an object like a structure or tree, and to thwart the removal of actual protesters from the location. Users employ various chains, locks and even the [sleeping dragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sleeping_dragon_\(manoeuver\)) for impairment of those trying to remove them with a matrix of composted materials.

- [Die-ins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Die-in) are a form of protest where participants simulate being dead (with varying degrees of realism). In the simplest form of a die-in, protesters simply lie down on the ground and pretend to be dead, sometimes covering themselves with signs or banners. Much of the effectiveness depends on the posture of the protesters, for when not properly executed, the protest might look more like a "sleep-in". For added realism, simulated wounds are sometimes painted on the bodies, or bandages, usually made to appear bloody, are used.

- [Protest song](23477.md) is a song which protests perceived problems in society. Every major movement in Western history has been accompanied by its own collection of protest songs, from slave [emancipation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_the_United_States) to women's [suffrage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suffrage), the [labor movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labor_movement), [civil rights](37071.md), the anti-war movement, the [feminist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feminist) movement, the environmental movement.

  [^cite_note-22]

  [^cite_note-23]

  &#x20;Over time, the songs have come to protest more abstract, moral issues, such as [injustice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injustice), [racial discrimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_discrimination), the morality of war in general (as opposed to purely protesting individual wars), [globalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globalization), inflation, [social inequalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_inequalities), and [incarceration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incarceration).

- [Radical cheerleading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_cheerleading). The idea is to ironically re-appropriate the aesthetics of [cheerleading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheerleading), for example by changing the [chants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chant) to promote feminism and left-wing causes. Many radical cheerleaders (some of whom are male, [transgender](19904525.md) or non-gender identified) are in appearance far from the stereotypical image of a cheerleader.

- [Critical Mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_Mass_\(cycling\)) bike rides have been perceived as protest activities. A 2006 *[New Yorker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Yorker)* article described Critical Mass' activity in New York City as "monthly political-protest rides", and characterized Critical Mass as a part of a [social movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_movement);

  [^cite_note-24]

  &#x20;the U.K. [e-zine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E-zine) [Urban75](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban75), which advertises as well as publishes photographs of the Critical Mass event in London, describes this as "the monthly protest by cyclists reclaiming the streets of London".

  [^cite_note-25]

  &#x20;However, Critical Mass participants have insisted that these events should be viewed as "celebrations" and spontaneous gatherings, not as protests or organized demonstrations.

  [^cite_note-26]

  [^cite_note-27]

  &#x20;This stance allows Critical Mass to argue a legal position that its events can occur without advance notification of local police.

  [^cite_note-28]

  [^cite_note-29]

- [Toyi-toyi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toyi-toyi) is a Southern African dance originally from Zimbabwe that became famous for its use in political protests in the apartheid-era South Africa. See [Protest in South Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protest_in_South_Africa).

## Protest policing

This section is an excerpt from [Protest policing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protest_policing).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Protest_policing\&action=edit)]



[Seattle Police Department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seattle_Police_Department) officers at the [George Floyd protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Floyd_protests), 2020

[Protest policing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protest_policing) or public order policing is part of a state’s response to [political dissent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_dissent) and [social movements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_movement). [Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police) maintenance of public order during protest is an essential component of [liberal democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_democracy), with [military response to protest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_response_to_protest) being more common under [authoritarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authoritarian) regimes.[^cite_note-Protest_policing_\:0-34]

Australasian, European, and North American democratic states have all experienced increased surveillance of protest movements and more militarized protest policing since 1995 and through the first decades of the 21st century.[^cite_note-Protest_policing_\:1-35][^cite_note-Protest_policing_\:3-36]

[Criminalization of dissent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocratic_legalism) is legislation or law enforcement that penalizes political dissent. It may also be accomplished through media that controls public discourse to delegitimize critics of the state. Study of protest criminalization places protest policing in a broader framework of criminology and sociology of law.[^cite_note-Protest_policing_\:1-35]

In the UK, in a four day period in 2023, policing data showed that 67 protests occurred, requiring 622 police officers to attend. The estimated financial cost of this was £140,346. Protests impact the communities they occur within and policing these costs a considerable amount.[^cite_note-37]

## Economic effects against companies



Protest march in [Palmerston North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palmerston_North), [New Zealand](4913064.md)



Protesters outside the [Oireachtas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oireachtas) in [Dublin](8504.md), [Republic of Ireland](14560.md)

A study of 342 US protests covered by *[The New York Times](30680.md)* newspaper from 1962 to 1990 showed that such public activities usually affected the company's [publicly traded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publicly_traded) [stock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock) price. The most intriguing aspect of the study's findings revealed that the amount of media coverage the event received was of the most importance to this study. Stock prices fell an average of one-tenth of a percent for every paragraph printed about the event.[^cite_note-38]
