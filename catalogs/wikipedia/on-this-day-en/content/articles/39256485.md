The **Legislative Assembly of Minas Gerais** ([Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_language): *Assembleia Legislativa de Minas Gerais*) is the [state legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislative_Assemblies_of_Brazilian_states) of [Brazil](3383.md)'s [Minas Gerais](222651.md) state.[^cite_note-tufts-1] It consists of 77 [state deputies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_deputy) elected by [proportional representation](23298.md)[^cite_note-2] and is based in [Belo Horizonte](82198.md), the state capital.[^cite_note-tufts-1] The Assembly has been based at the [Palácio da Inconfidência](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pal%C3%A1cio_da_Inconfid%C3%AAncia?action=edit\&redlink=1) since the building's 1972 opening; it was made a [national heritage site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_heritage_site) in 2009.[^cite_note-3]

Deputies elected in the [2018 Brazilian state elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Brazilian_state_elections?action=edit\&redlink=1) took office in February 2019 as part of the 19th Legislature.[^cite_note-globo-4] Their terms will end in February 2023.[^cite_note-globo-4] [Agostinho Patrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agostinho_Patrus) of Brazil's [Green Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Party_\(Brazil\)) is currently the Assembly president.[^cite_note-5]

## History

Brazil's [1824 Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazilian_Constitution_of_1824), the first since claiming independence from Portugal in 1822, began establishing governmental and legislative powers; [Ouro Preto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouro_Preto), then the capital of Minas Gerais, was chosen as the province's main administrative hub.[^cite_note-papadia-6] It was written to the benefit of wealthy landowners and put native Brazilians, many of them [Indigenous](55510.md) or [Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afro-Brazilians), at a disadvantage, particularly because early laws allowed for [slavery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_Brazil).[^cite_note-papadia-6][^cite_note-ucla-7] An 1834 Amendment allowed its provinces, including Minas Gerais, to establish provincial legislation.[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9][^cite_note-papadia-6] At the time of the provincial legislature's establishment, Minas Gerais had more enslaved people than any other province in Brazil.[^cite_note-ucla-7]

In 1889, [Emperor Pedro II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedro_II_of_Brazil), the final monarch of the [Empire of Brazil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_of_Brazil), was [quietly overthrown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proclamation_of_the_Republic_\(Brazil\)) by the military, who established the [First Brazilian Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Brazilian_Republic), formally known as the United States of Brazil.[^cite_note-loc-10][^cite_note-11] Wealthy men who had previously owned slaves, as well Minas Gerais and [São Paulo](229422.md), Brazil's largest and richest provinces, headed the coup d'état and the subsequent creation of a republic.[^cite_note-12][^cite_note-loc-10][^cite_note-cmrv-13] Emperor Pedro II was replaced by [Marshal Deodoro da Fonseca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deodoro_da_Fonseca), the first president of the [new oligarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronelism).[^cite_note-loc-10][^cite_note-escola-14][^cite_note-cpdoc-15]

The Empire's constitution was nullified and the Republic's [federalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federalism) constitution replaced it in 1891.[^cite_note-escola-14] Provinces became states and, due to the decentralization of the government and increased state power, began local elections of their own political representatives and legislative bodies.[^cite_note-escola-14][^cite_note-cpdoc-15][^cite_note-loc-10][^cite_note-cmrv-13] Minas Gerais' first state constitution was also established in 1891 and was used, with amendments added later, until 1989.[^cite_note-assembly89-16] The constitution created the [bicameral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicameralism) Minas Gerais Congress and consisted of a Chamber of Deputies and a Senate.[^cite_note-assembly89-16] Oligarchs from [São Paulo](229422.md) and Minas Gerais took turns as president of the Republic during this period, as they held a significant amount of power in Brazil.[^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18]

Following the [Revolution of 1930](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolution_of_1930), failed presidential candidate [Getúlio Vargas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Get%C3%BAlio_Vargas) was able to take control of the government and shift the country into the [New Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vargas_Era#Rise_of_Get%C3%BAlio_Vargas) era.[^cite_note-bethell-19][^cite_note-20][^cite_note-rosenn-21] This meant the dissolution of state legislatures and state power in the National Congress declined.[^cite_note-assembly89-16] The constitution did, however, introduce [proportional representation](23298.md), which still exists in Brazilian politics today.[^cite_note-22] The 1935 Minas Gerais Constitution revived the state's [unicameral](204304.md), 48-person Assembly, albeit with diminished power.[^cite_note-assembly89-16][^cite_note-ufmg-23] In 1937, Vargas seized absolute power and brought the country into [Estado Novo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estado_Novo_\(Brazil\)), Brazil's first dictatorship.[^cite_note-24] Estado Novo's first constitution was put in place immediately and was inspired by Poland's [1935 authoritarian constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/April_Constitution_of_Poland).[^cite_note-25] This constitution did away with political parties, further decreased state legislative power by making them into "administrative divisions of a unitary state,"[^cite_note-rosenn-21] and ended free elections.[^cite_note-26]

In 1945, Vargas was pushed aside and Brazil returned briefly to a democracy.[^cite_note-ufmg-23] A democratic constitution was introduced and promised full political freedom, reduction of centralized power, and state rights to elect local political representatives.[^cite_note-plan-27][^cite_note-rep4-28] In 1947, the Constituent Assembly of Minas Gerais was revived, this time with 72 deputies.[^cite_note-ufmg-23]

The [1964 military coup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_Brazilian_coup_d'%C3%A9tat) plunged the country into another dictatorship, this one lasting for more than 20 years.[^cite_note-brown-29][^cite_note-info-30] [Institutional Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutional_Acts) were introduced and viewed as superior to the constitution.[^cite_note-info-30] Again, political rights were oppressed, elections were skewed, state autonomy was limited, and political parties were quashed, which created a heavily controlled [bipartisan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bipartisanship) government.[^cite_note-31] In 1967, Minas Gerais' weakened Assembly majoritatively consisted of members of [ARENA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Renewal_Alliance), one of the two political parties allowed under the Acts.[^cite_note-ufmg-23] In 1984, [civil unrest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diretas_J%C3%A1) demanding a return to democracy began cropping up around the country.[^cite_note-1988const-32] While dissent from Brazilians continued, [José Sarney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Sarney) stepped in as president when his platform partner [Tancredo Neves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tancredo_Neves) died.[^cite_note-1988const-32] Sarney reestablished elections in 1986 to repopulate the National Assembly.[^cite_note-1988const-32] A new constitution was passed in 1988 and was the most strongly "democratic Constitution in the history of \[the] country."[^cite_note-1988const-32] The new constitution restored power to the states, democratic elections, and other freedoms oppressed during the military dictatorship.[^cite_note-1988c-33] States could have their own symbols, including flags, and legislative bodies, who could pass state laws.[^cite_note-1988c-33] In 1989, the Legislative Assembly was again revived, this time with 77 deputies.[^cite_note-ufmg-23]

### Location

The Assembly was located in [Ouro Preto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouro_Preto) for the first century or so of its existence in a free Brazil. The Assembly's library was established in 1892 and resided for 5 years in the building now hosting the [Federal University of Ouro Preto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_University_of_Ouro_Preto)'s pharmacy school.[^cite_note-encontro-34] In 1897, as a symbol of progress, the state capital moved from Ouro Preto to [Cidade de Minas](82198.md), now Belo Horizonte.[^cite_note-35] The Assembly moved into a building on [Avenida Afonso Pena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avenida_Afonso_Pena?action=edit\&redlink=1) and its library settled at Praça da República, now known as the [Praça Afonso Arinos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pra%C3%A7a_Afonso_Arinos?action=edit\&redlink=1).[^cite_note-encontro-34] They later moved to a building on [Rua Tamoios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rua_Tamoios?action=edit\&redlink=1) following a 1959 fire before settling in the newly built [Palácio da Inconfidência](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pal%C3%A1cio_da_Inconfid%C3%AAncia?action=edit\&redlink=1) in 1972.[^cite_note-encontro-34]

## Organization

The Legislative Assembly of Minas Gerais (ALMG) is made up of 77 deputies.[^cite_note-ufmg-23] The number of members is established "correspond\[ing] to triple the representation of the State in the Chamber of Deputies and, when the number 36 is reached, it will be increased by as many Federal Deputies \[minus] twelve."[^cite_note-36] Deputies are elected for four-year terms, called legislatures.[^cite_note-37] Each year of the legislature has a single legislative session.[^cite_note-38] The [Plenary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plenary_session) is the "deliberative body," where state deputies meet to discuss and vote on proposed bills.[^cite_note-39] The plenary is the highest court within the Assembly, with administrative authority over committees, the assembly table, party leaders, and the [cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_\(government\)).[^cite_note-40] Some plenary events are open discussions with the public.[^cite_note-41]

### Committees

Committees are small groups of deputies working together on a single project or focus; committee foci include public administration, financial and budget inspection, and transportation.[^cite_note-committees-42] Propositions currently under consideration are debated in these sessions.[^cite_note-committees-42] Opinions formed by committee members are powerful; at times, authorities and specialists from the general public are invited to weigh in and offer a different point of view.[^cite_note-43]

### Blocks

Each political party that has at least five deputies forms a group called a bench.[^cite_note-blocks-48] A block is a group made up of at least 16 deputies.[^cite_note-blocks-48] Blocks must be in place for legislative work to begin.[^cite_note-49] Benches and blocks both have a leader and vice-leader, though party representatives with less than 5 members cannot be either.[^cite_note-blocks-48] Parties with large numbers of members can form a block but the limit is one per party.[^cite_note-blocks-48]
