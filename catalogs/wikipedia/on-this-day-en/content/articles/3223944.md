**David Howell Petraeus** ([/pɪˈtreɪ.əs/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English); born 7 November 1952) is a retired [United States Army](32087.md) [general](2965165.md) who served as the fourth [director of the Central Intelligence Agency](1777424.md) (CIA) from September 2011[^cite_note-3] until his resignation in November 2012.[^cite_note-4] Prior to his assuming the directorship of the CIA, Petraeus served 37 years in the [United States Army](32087.md). His last assignments in the Army were as commander of the [International Security Assistance Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Security_Assistance_Force) (ISAF) and commander, U.S. Forces – Afghanistan (USFOR-A) from July 2010 to July 2011. His other [four-star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4_star_rank) assignments include serving as the 10th commander, [U.S. Central Command](338482.md) (USCENTCOM) from October 2008 to June 2010, and as commanding general, [Multi-National Force – Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multinational_Force_Iraq) (MNF-I) from February 2007 to September 2008.[^cite_note-Change_in_command-5] As commander of MNF-I, Petraeus oversaw all [coalition forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-National_Force_%E2%80%93_Iraq) in Iraq.[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7]

Petraeus was the [General George C. Marshall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Marshall) Award winner as the top graduate of the [U.S. Army Command and General Staff College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Army_Command_and_General_Staff_College) class of 1983.[^cite_note-8] He later served as assistant professor of international relations at the [United States Military Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Military_Academy) and also completed a fellowship at [Georgetown University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgetown_University).[^cite_note-9] Since 2022, he has taught courses in [international relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_relations) at [Yale University](34273.md) as a Kissinger Senior Fellow of the university's [Jackson Institute for Global Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackson_Institute_for_Global_Affairs).[^cite_note-10]

Petraeus has repeatedly stated that he has no plans to run for elected political office.[^cite_note-timesonline.co.uk-11][^cite_note-nytimes.com-12][^cite_note-MSNBC-13] On 23 June 2010, president [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama) nominated Petraeus to succeed General [Stanley McChrystal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_McChrystal) as commanding general of the [International Security Assistance Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Security_Assistance_Force) in [Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_in_Afghanistan_\(2001%E2%80%93present\)), technically a step down from his position as Commander of [United States Central Command](338482.md), which oversees the military efforts in Afghanistan, Pakistan, [Central Asia](6742.md), the [Arabian Peninsula](47858.md), and [Egypt](8087628.md).[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-nytimes1-15][^cite_note-16]

On 30 June 2011, Petraeus was unanimously confirmed as the [director of the CIA](1777424.md) by the U.S. Senate 94–0.[^cite_note-17] Petraeus relinquished command of U.S. and [NATO](21133.md) forces in Afghanistan on 18 July 2011, and retired from the U.S. Army on 31 August 2011.[^cite_note-18] On 9 November 2012, he resigned from his position as director of the CIA, citing his extramarital affair with his biographer [Paula Broadwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paula_Broadwell), which was discovered in the course of an [Federal Bureau of Investigation](11127.md) (FBI) investigation.[^cite_note-19][^cite_note-20] In January 2015, officials reported that the FBI and the [Department of Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Department_of_Justice) (DOJ) prosecutors had recommended bringing felony charges against Petraeus for providing [classified information](3539962.md) to Broadwell while serving as director of the CIA.[^cite_note-https-21] Eventually, Petraeus pleaded guilty to one misdemeanor charge of [mishandling classified information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mishandling_of_US_classified_information).[^cite_note-Mar_3_2015-22] He was later sentenced to two years of probation and fined US\$100,000 for the unauthorized removal and retention of classified material he gave to Broadwell.[^cite_note-23]

## Early life and education

Petraeus was born in [Cornwall-on-Hudson, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornwall-on-Hudson,_New_York), the son of Miriam Sweet (née Howell), a librarian, and Sixtus Petraeus, a sea captain. His father was a [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_people) merchant mariner who immigrated to the United States at the start of World War II,[^cite_note-24] from [Franeker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franeker), the [Netherlands](21148.md),[^cite_note-obit1-25][^cite_note-26] and his mother was American, a resident of Brooklyn, New York.[^cite_note-wapo4-27] They met at the [Seamen's Church Institute of New York and New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seamen's_Church_Institute_of_New_York_and_New_Jersey) and married. Sixtus Petraeus commanded a [Liberty ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberty_ship) for the U.S. for the duration of World War II.[^cite_note-wapo4-27] The family moved after the war, settling in Cornwall-on-Hudson, where Petraeus grew up and graduated from [Cornwall Central High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornwall_Central_High_School) in 1970.



Petraeus with his son Stephen in [Afghanistan](737.md), 2010

Petraeus went on to the [United States Military Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Military_Academy) at [West Point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Point,_New_York). Petraeus was on the intercollegiate soccer and ski teams, was a cadet captain on the brigade staff, and was a "distinguished cadet" academically, graduating in the top 5% of the Class of 1974 (ranked 40th overall). In the class yearbook, Petraeus was remembered as "always going for it in sports, academics, leadership, and even his social life".[^cite_note-early_bio-28]

While a cadet, Petraeus started dating the daughter of Army General [William A. Knowlton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_A._Knowlton) (the West Point superintendent at the time), [Holly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holly_Petraeus).[^cite_note-abcnews.go.com-29][^cite_note-30] Two months after Petraeus graduated, they married.[^cite_note-31] Holly, who is multi-lingual, was a [National Merit Scholar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Merit_Scholarship_Program) in high school, and graduated *[summa cum laude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summa_cum_laude)* from [Dickinson College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dickinson_College). They have a daughter and son, Anne and Stephen. Petraeus administered the [oath of office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Uniformed_Services_Oath_of_Office) at his son's 2009 commissioning into the Army after his son's graduation from the [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](18879.md).[^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33] His son went on to serve as an officer in [Afghanistan](737.md) as a member of 3rd Platoon, Alpha Company, 1st Battalion, [503rd Infantry Regiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/503rd_Infantry_Regiment), [173rd Airborne Brigade Combat Team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/173rd_Airborne_Brigade_Combat_Team).[^cite_note-34]

Petraeus's residence is a small property in the [Springfield, New Hampshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Springfield,_New_Hampshire), which his wife inherited from her family.[^cite_note-35] Petraeus once told a friend that he was a [Rockefeller Republican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockefeller_Republican).[^cite_note-newyorker.com-1]

Petraeus graduated from [West Point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Point) in 1974, receiving a [B.S.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Science) degree in [Military Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Science). He earned the General [George C. Marshall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_C._Marshall) Award as the top graduate of the [U.S. Army Command and General Staff College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Command_and_General_Staff_College) Class of 1983 at [Fort Leavenworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Leavenworth), [Kansas](16716.md). He subsequently earned an [M.P.A.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Public_Administration) degree in 1985 and a [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy) in [international relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_relations) and [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics) in 1987 from [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University)'s [Woodrow Wilson School of Public and International Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodrow_Wilson_School_of_Public_and_International_Affairs), where he was mentored by Richard H. Ullman, an international relations scholar and progressive commentator.[^cite_note-36]

At that time, he also served as an assistant professor of international relations at the [U.S. Military Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Military_Academy) from 1985 to 1987. His doctoral dissertation was titled "The American Military and the Lessons of [Vietnam](202354.md): A Study of Military Influence and the Use of Force in the Post-Vietnam Era".[^cite_note-37] He completed a military fellowship at [Georgetown University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgetown_University)'s [Edmund A. Walsh School of Foreign Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_A._Walsh_School_of_Foreign_Service) in 1994–1995,[^cite_note-38] although he was called away early to serve in Haiti as the Chief of Operations for NATO there in early 1995.

From late 2005 through February 2007,[^cite_note-39] Petraeus served as commanding general of Fort Leavenworth, Kansas, and the [U.S. Army Combined Arms Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Army_Combined_Arms_Center) (CAC) located there. As commander of CAC, Petraeus was responsible for oversight of the [Command and General Staff College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Command_and_General_Staff_College) and seventeen other schools, centers, and training programs as well as for developing the Army's doctrinal manuals, training the Army's officers, and supervising the Army's center for the collection and dissemination of lessons learned. During his time at CAC, Petraeus and Marine [Lt. Gen. James F. Amos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_F._Amos) jointly oversaw the publication of [Field Manual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Army_Field_Manuals) 3–24, *Counterinsurgency*, the body of which was written by an unusually diverse group of military officers, academics, human rights advocates, and journalists who had been assembled by Petraeus and Amos.[^cite_note-40][^cite_note-41]

At both Fort Leavenworth and throughout the military's schools and training programs, Petraeus integrated the study of counterinsurgency into lesson plans and training exercises. In recognition of the fact that soldiers in Iraq often performed duties far different from those for which they trained, Petraeus stressed the importance of teaching soldiers how to think and how to fight, and the need to foster flexibility and adaptability in leaders.[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43]

Petraeus called this change the most significant part of [The Surge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq_War_troop_surge_of_2007), saying in 2016, "the surge that mattered most was the surge of ideas. It was the change of strategy, and in many respects, this represented quite a significant change to what it was we were doing before the surge."[^cite_note-44] Petraeus has at times been labeled "the world's leading expert in counter-insurgency warfare".[^cite_note-45] Later, having refined his ideas on counterinsurgency based on the implementation of the new [counterinsurgency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterinsurgency) doctrine in Iraq, he published both in Iraq as well as in the Sep/Oct 2008 edition of *[Military Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Review)* his "Commander's Counterinsurgency Guidance" to help guide leaders and units in the Multi-National Force-Iraq.[^cite_note-46]

## U.S. Army Officer (1974 – 2011)



U.S. Army Gen. David H. Petraeus, during his time in the Army

### 1970s

Upon his graduation from West Point in 1974, Petraeus was commissioned an infantry officer. After completing [Ranger School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranger_School) (Distinguished Honor Graduate and other honors), Petraeus was assigned to the [509th Airborne Battalion Combat Team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/509th_Infantry_Regiment_\(United_States\)), a light infantry unit stationed in [Vicenza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vicenza), Italy.[^cite_note-47] Ever since, [light infantry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_infantry) has been at the core of his career, punctuated by assignments to [mechanized units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armoured_warfare), unit commands, staff assignments, and educational institutions. After leaving the 509th as a [first lieutenant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._first_lieutenant),[^cite_note-48] Petraeus began a brief association with mechanized units when he became assistant operations officer on the staff of the 2nd Brigade, [24th Infantry Division (Mechanized)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/24th_Infantry_Division_\(United_States\)), at [Fort Stewart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Stewart), [Georgia](48830.md). In 1979, he assumed command of a [company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Company_\(military_unit\)) in the same division: A Company, 2nd Battalion, 19th Infantry Regiment (Mechanized), and then served as that battalion's operations officer, a major's position that he held as a junior captain.

### 1980s

In 1981, Petraeus became [aide-de-camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aide-de-camp) to [General John Galvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Galvin_\(general\)), then commanding general of the [24th Infantry Division (Mechanized)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/24th_Infantry_Division_\(United_States\)).[^cite_note-NPR_20070123-49] He spent the next few years furthering his military and civilian education, including spending 1982–83 at Fort Leavenworth, Kansas, attending the Command and General Staff College. At graduation in 1983, he was the General George C. Marshall Award winner as the top graduate of the U.S. Army Command and General Staff College. From 1983 to 1985, he was at Princeton; and 1985–87 at West Point.

After earning his Ph.D. and teaching at West Point, Petraeus continued up the command ladder, serving as military assistant to Gen. John Galvin, the [Supreme Allied Commander in Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Headquarters_Allied_Powers_Europe). From there, he moved to the [3rd Infantry Division (Mechanized)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3rd_Infantry_Division_\(United_States\)). During 1988–1989, he served as operations officer to the 3rd Infantry Division (Mechanized)'s [30th Infantry Regiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/30th_Infantry_Regiment_\(United_States\)). He was then posted as an aide and assistant executive officer to the U.S. Army Chief of Staff, General [Carl Vuono](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Vuono), in Washington, D.C.

### 1990s

Upon promotion to [lieutenant colonel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lieutenant_colonel_\(United_States\)), Petraeus moved from the office of the chief of staff to [Fort Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Campbell), [Kentucky](16846.md), where he commanded the [101st Airborne Division (Air Assault)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/101st_Airborne_Division)'s 3rd Battalion [187th Infantry Regiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/187th_Infantry_Regiment_\(United_States\)), known as the "Iron Rakkasans", from 1991 to 1993. In 1991 he was accidentally shot in the chest with an [M-16](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M16_rifle) rifle during a [live-fire exercise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live-fire_exercise) when a soldier tripped and his rifle discharged.[^cite_note-50] He was taken to [Vanderbilt University Medical Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanderbilt_University_Medical_Center), [Nashville](22018.md), [Tennessee](30395.md), where he was operated on by future [U.S. Senator](24909346.md) [Bill Frist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Frist). The hospital released him early after he did fifty [push-ups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Push-up) without resting, just a few days after the accident.[^cite_note-51][^cite_note-52]

During 1993–94, Petraeus continued his long association with the 101st Airborne Division (Air Assault) as the division's assistant chief of staff, G-3 (plans, operations, and training) and installation director of plans, training, and mobilization (DPTM). In 1995, he was assigned to the [United Nations](31769.md) Mission in [Haiti](13373.md) Military Staff as its chief operations officer during [Operation Uphold Democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Uphold_Democracy). His next command, from 1995 to 1997, was the 1st Brigade, [82nd Airborne Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/82nd_Airborne_Division), centered on the [504th Parachute Infantry Regiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/504th_Infantry_Regiment_\(United_States\)). At that post, his brigade's training cycle at [Fort Polk's Joint Readiness Training Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Polk) for low-intensity warfare was chronicled by novelist and military enthusiast [Tom Clancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Clancy) in his book *Airborne*.[^cite_note-An_Open_Mind_for_a_New_Army-53]

From 1997 to 1999, Petraeus served in [the Pentagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pentagon) as executive assistant to the director of the Joint Staff and then to the [Chairman of the Joint Chiefs](364458.md), Gen. [Henry Shelton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Shelton), who described Petraeus as "a high-energy individual who likes to lead from the front, in any field, he is going into".[^cite_note-An_Open_Mind_for_a_New_Army-53] In 1999, as a [brigadier general](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brigadier_general_\(United_States\)), Petraeus returned to the 82nd, serving as the assistant division commander for operations and then, briefly, as acting commanding general. During his time with the 82nd, he deployed to [Kuwait](7515890.md) as part of [Operation Desert Spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Desert_Spring), the continuous rotation of combat forces through Kuwait during the decade after the [Gulf War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_War).

### 2000s

From the 82nd, he moved on to serve as chief of staff of [XVIII Airborne Corps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XVIII_Airborne_Corps) at [Fort Bragg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Bragg,_North_Carolina) during 2000–2001. In 2000, Petraeus suffered his second major injury, when, during a civilian [skydiving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skydiving) jump, his parachute collapsed at low altitude due to a hook turn, resulting in a hard landing that broke his pelvis. He was selected for promotion to [major general](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_general_\(United_States\)) in 2001.[^cite_note-54] During 2001–2002, as a brigadier general, Petraeus served a ten-month tour in [Bosnia and Herzegovina](3463.md) as part of [Operation Joint Forge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Joint_Forge). In Bosnia, he was the [NATO](21133.md) [Stabilization Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SFOR) assistant chief of staff for operations as well as the deputy commander of the U.S. Joint Interagency Counter-Terrorism Task Force, a command created after the [9/11 attacks](5058690.md) to add counterterrorism capability to the U.S. forces attached to the NATO command in Bosnia. In 2004, he was promoted to lieutenant general.[^cite_note-55]

In 2007, he was promoted to General.[^cite_note-56] On 23 April 2008, Secretary of Defense Gates announced that President Bush was nominating General Petraeus to command [U.S. Central Command](338482.md) (USCENTCOM), headquartered in [Tampa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tampa), Florida. In 2010, Petraeus was nominated to command the [International Security Assistance Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Security_Assistance_Force) in [Afghanistan](737.md), which required [Senate confirmation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_positions_filled_by_presidential_appointment_with_Senate_confirmation).[^cite_note-57] He was confirmed on 30 June 2010,[^cite_note-petraeus_confirmed_IT-58] and took over command from temporary commander [Lieutenant-General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lieutenant-General_\(United_Kingdom\)) [Sir Nick Parker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Nick_Parker) on 4 July 2010.[^cite_note-assume_command-59]

### Involvement in the Iraq War

#### 101st Airborne Division



Maj. Gen. David H. Petraeus (right), commanding general, 101st Airborne Division (Air Assault), looks on as Lt. Gen. [William S. Wallace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_S._Wallace), V Corps commanding general speaks to soldiers, 21 March 2003, Kuwait

In 2003, Petraeus, then a major general, saw combat for the first time when he commanded the 101st Airborne Division during [V Corps's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V_Corps_\(United_States\)) drive to [Baghdad](4492.md). In a campaign chronicled in detail by [Pulitzer Prize](24230.md)-winning author [Rick Atkinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rick_Atkinson) of *[The Washington Post](102226.md)* in the book *In the Company of Soldiers*, Petraeus led his division through fierce fighting south of [Baghdad](4492.md), in [Karbala](210957.md), [Hilla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Hillah) and [Najaf](200512.md). Following the [fall of Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_invasion_of_Baghdad), the division conducted the longest [heliborne assault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_helicopters) on record in order to reach [Nineveh Governorate](200568.md), where it would spend much of 2003. The 1st Brigade was responsible for the area [south of Mosul](200568.md), the 2nd Brigade for the city itself, and the 3rd Brigade for the region stretching toward the [Syrian](7515849.md) border. An often-repeated story of Petraeus's time with the 101st is his asking of [embedded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embedded_journalist) *[The Washington Post](102226.md)* [reporter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reporter) [Rick Atkinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rick_Atkinson) to "Tell me how this ends",[^cite_note-60] an anecdote he and other journalists have used to portray Petraeus as an early recognizer of the difficulties that would follow the fall of Baghdad.[^cite_note-NPR_20070123-49][^cite_note-61][^cite_note-62][^cite_note-63][^cite_note-64][^cite_note-65]

In [Mosul](200475.md), a city of nearly two million people, Petraeus and the 101st employed classic counterinsurgency methods to build security and stability, including conducting targeted [kinetic operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_operation) and using force judiciously, jump-starting the economy, building local security forces, staging elections for the city council within weeks of their arrival, overseeing a program of [public works](323158.md), reinvigorating the political process,[^cite_note-autogenerated1-66][^cite_note-67][^cite_note-68] and launching 4,500 [reconstruction projects in Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconstruction_of_Iraq).[^cite_note-69]

This approach can be attributed to Petraeus, who had been steeped in nation-building during his previous tours in nations such as Bosnia and Haiti and thus approached nation-building as a central military mission and who was "prepared to act while the civilian authority in Baghdad was still getting organized", according to Michael Gordon of *[The New York Times](30680.md)*.[^cite_note-70] Some Iraqis gave Petraeus the nickname '[King David](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_David)',[^cite_note-autogenerated1-66][^cite_note-71] which was later adopted by some of his colleagues.[^cite_note-72][^cite_note-73][^cite_note-74] In 2004, *[Newsweek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newsweek)* stated that "It's widely accepted that no force worked harder to win Iraqi hearts and minds than the 101st Airborne Division led by Petraeus."[^cite_note-75]



Petraeus on patrol in [Mosul](200475.md) with Gen. [Peter J. Schoomaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Schoomaker), 2003

One of the General's major [public works](323158.md) was the restoration and re-opening of the [University of Mosul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Mosul).[^cite_note-76][^cite_note-Learning_Counterinsurgency-77] Petraeus strongly supported the use of commanders' discretionary funds for public works, telling [Coalition Provisional Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coalition_Provisional_Authority) [director](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civilian_Administrator_of_Iraq) [L. Paul Bremer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L._Paul_Bremer) "Money is ammunition" during the director's first visit to [Mosul](200475.md).[^cite_note-78][^cite_note-79] Petraeus's often repeated[^cite_note-80] [catchphrase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catchphrase) was later incorporated into official military briefings[^cite_note-81][^cite_note-82] and was also eventually incorporated into the U.S. Army Counterinsurgency Field Manual drafted with Petraeus's oversight.[^cite_note-83]

Award of the Bronze Star Medal with V Device awarded to Petraeus for actions in combat leading the 101st Airborne (Air Assault) Division during Iraqi Freedom, awarded May 2003.

Petraeus's Bronze Star Medal with V Device for actions in combat leading the 101st Airborne (Air Assault) Division during Operation Iraqi Freedom, May 2003

In February 2004, the 101st was replaced in Mosul by a portion of I Corps headquarters, but operational forces consisted solely of a unit roughly one quarter its size—a [Stryker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stryker) brigade. The following summer, the governor of Nineveh Province, [Osama Youssef Kashmoula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osama_Youssef_Kashmoula),[^cite_note-iraqbodycount-84] was assassinated, and most of the Sunni Arab Provincial Council members walked out in the ensuing selection of the new governor, leaving Kurdish members in charge of a predominantly Sunni Arab province. Later that year, the local police commander defected to the Kurdish Minister of Interior in Irbil after repeated assassination attempts against him, attacks on his house, and the kidnapping of his sister. The largely [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni) [Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab) police collapsed under insurgent attacks launched at the same time Coalition Forces attacked [Fallujah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallujah) in November 2004.



Orders awarding the Combat Action Badge to then LTG David H. Petraeus for actions in combat during Iraqi Freedom

There are differing explanations for the apparent collapse of the police force in Mosul. *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)* quoted an anonymous U.S. diplomat saying, "Mosul basically collapsed after he \[Petraeus] left." Former diplomat [Peter Galbraith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Galbraith) criticized Petraeus's command of the 101st, saying his achievements had been exaggerated and his reputation inflated. He wrote for *[The New York Review of Books](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Review_of_Books)* that "Petraeus ignored warnings from America's Kurdish allies that he was appointing the wrong people to key positions in Mosul's local government and police."[^cite_note-85]

On the other hand, in the book *Fiasco*, *[The Washington Post](102226.md)* reporter Tom Ricks wrote that "Mosul was quiet while he (Petraeus) was there, and likely would have remained so had his successor had as many troops as he had—and as much understanding of counterinsurgency techniques." Ricks went on to say that "the population-oriented approach Petraeus took in Mosul in 2003 would be the one the entire U.S. Army in Iraq was trying to adopt in 2006".[^cite_note-86]

*[Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_\(magazine\))* columnist [Joe Klein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Klein_\(journalist\)) largely agreed with Ricks, writing that the [Stryker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stryker) brigade that replaced the 101st "didn't do any of the local governance that Petraeus had done". Moving away from counterinsurgency principles, "they were occupiers, not builders".[^cite_note-87] *[The New York Times](30680.md)* reporter Michael Gordon and retired General Bernard Trainor echoed Ricks and Klein, including in their book *Cobra II* a quote that Petraeus "did it right and won over Mosul."[^cite_note-88]

#### Multi-National Security Transition Command – Iraq

In June 2004, less than six months after the 101st returned to the U.S., Petraeus was promoted to lieutenant general and became the first commander of the [Multi-National Security Transition Command - Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-National_Security_Transition_Command_-_Iraq). This newly created command had responsibility for training, equipping, and mentoring Iraq's growing army, police, and other security forces, as well as developing Iraq's security institutions and building associated infrastructure, such as training bases, police stations, and border forts.[^cite_note-Ajami,_Faoud_2006_pp._295-89]

During Petraeus's fifteen months at the helm of MNSTC-I, he stood up a three-star command virtually from scratch and in the midst of serious fighting in places like Fallujah, Mosul, and Najaf. By the end of his command, some 100,000 Iraqi Security Forces had been trained; Iraqi Army and Police were being employed in combat; countless reconstruction projects had been executed; and hundreds of thousands of weapons, body armor, and other equipment had been distributed in what was described as the "largest military procurement and distribution effort since World War II", at a cost of over \$11 billion.[^cite_note-Ajami,_Faoud_2006_pp._295-89]

In September 2004, Petraeus wrote an article for *[The Washington Post](102226.md)* in which he described the tangible progress being made in building Iraq's security forces from the ground up while also noting the many challenges associated with doing so. "Although there have been reverses – not to mention horrific terrorist attacks," Petraeus wrote, "there has been progress in the effort to enable Iraqis to shoulder more of the load for their own security, something they are keen to do."[^cite_note-90]

Some of the challenges involved in building security forces had to do with accomplishing this task in the midst of a tough insurgency—or, as Petraeus wrote, "making the mission akin to repairing an aircraft while in flight—and while being shot at". Other challenges included allegations of corruption as well as efforts to improve Iraq's supply accountability procedures. For example, according to former [Interim Iraq Governing Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interim_Iraq_Governing_Council) member [Ali A. Allawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_A._Allawi) in *The Occupation of Iraq: Winning the War, Losing the Peace*, "under the very noses of the security transition command, officials both inside and outside the ministry of defense were planning to embezzle most, if not all, of the procurement budget of the army."[^cite_note-91] *[The Washington Post](102226.md)* stated in August 2007 that [the Pentagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pentagon) had lost track of approximately 30% of weapons supplied to the Iraqi security forces. The [General Accounting Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Accounting_Office) said that the weapons distribution was haphazard, rushed, and did not follow established procedures—particularly from 2004 to 2005, when security training was led by Petraeus and Iraq's security forces began to see combat in places like Najaf and Samarra.[^cite_note-92]

Over a hundred thousand [AK-47](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AK-47) [assault rifles](18950865.md) and [pistols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pistol) were delivered to Iraqi forces without full documentation, and some of the missing weapons may have been abducted by [Iraqi insurgents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_insurgency_\(Iraq_War\)).[^cite_note-93][^cite_note-94] Thousands of [body armour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_armour) pieces have also been lost.[^cite_note-95] *[The Independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Independent)* stated that the military believed "the situation on the ground was so urgent, and the agency responsible for recording the transfers of arms so short-staffed, that field commanders had little choice in the matter."[^cite_note-96] The Pentagon conducted its own investigation, and accountability was subsequently regained for many of the weapons.[^cite_note-97]

Following his second tour in Iraq, Petraeus authored a widely read article in *Military Review*, listing fourteen observations he had made during two tours in Iraq, including: do not do too much with your own hands, money is ammunition, increasing the number of stakeholders is critical to success, success in a counterinsurgency requires more than just military operations, ultimate success depends on local leaders, there is no substitute for flexible and adaptable leaders, and, finally, a leader's most important task is to set the right tone.[^cite_note-98]

#### Multi-National Force – Iraq (spring 2007)



Petraeus walking through a market in Baghdad, March 2007

The intervening time between the Iraq commands was spent at Fort Leavenworth, where Petraeus further developed his military doctrine and pursued an important White House contact in [Meghan O'Sullivan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meghan_O'Sullivan) who was the principal adviser to the president on the war.[^cite_note-99] In January 2007, as part of his overhauled Iraq strategy, President [George W. Bush](3414021.md) announced that Petraeus would succeed Gen. [George Casey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_William_Casey_Jr.) as commanding general of MNF-I to lead all U.S. troops in Iraq. In his memoirs, President Bush likened his selection of Petraeus to the elevations of other great generals of American history, writing, "Lincoln discovered Generals Grant and Sherman. Roosevelt had Eisenhower and Bradley. I found David Petraeus and Ray Odierno."[^cite_note-100][^cite_note-101]

On 23 January, the [Senate Armed Services Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senate_Armed_Services_Committee) held Petraeus's nomination hearing, during which he testified on his ideas for Iraq, particularly the strategy underpinning the "[surge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq_War_troop_surge_of_2007)" of forces. During his opening statement, Petraeus stated that "security of the population, especially in Baghdad, and in partnership with the Iraqi Security Forces, will be the focus of the military effort." He went on to state that security would require establishing a persistent presence, especially in Iraq's most threatened neighborhoods. He also noted the critical importance of helping Iraq increase its governmental capacity, develop employment programs, and improve daily life for its citizens.[^cite_note-102]

Throughout Petraeus's tenure in Iraq, [Multi-National Force-Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-National_Force-Iraq) endeavored to work with the [Government of Iraq](7515928.md) to carry out this strategy that focused on securing the population. Doing so required establishing—and maintaining—persistent presence by living among the population, separating reconcilable Iraqis from irreconcilable enemies, relentlessly pursuing the enemy, taking back sanctuaries and then holding areas that had been cleared, and continuing to develop Iraq's security forces and to support local security forces, often called [Sons of Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sons_of_Iraq), and integrate them into the Iraqi Army and Police and other employment programs.[^cite_note-103][^cite_note-104]

The strategy underpinning the "surge" of forces, as well as the ideas Petraeus included in U.S. Army Field Manual 3–24, *Counterinsurgency*, have been referred to by some journalists and politicians as the "Petraeus Doctrine", although the surge itself was proposed a few months before Petraeus took command. Despite the misgivings of most Democratic and a few Republican senators over the proposed implementation of the "Petraeus Doctrine" in Iraq, specifically regarding the troop surge, Petraeus was unanimously confirmed as a four-star general and MNF-I commander on 27 January.[^cite_note-105][^cite_note-106]

Before leaving for Iraq, Petraeus recruited a number of highly educated military officers, nicknamed "Petraeus guys" or "designated thinkers", to advise him as commander, including Col. Mike Meese, head of the Social Sciences Department at West Point and Col. [H.R. McMaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H.R._McMaster), famous for his leadership at the [Battle of 73 Easting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_73_Easting) in the Gulf War and in the pacification of [Tal Afar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tal_Afar) more recently, as well as for his doctoral dissertation on Vietnam-era civil-military relations titled [*Dereliction of Duty*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dereliction_of_Duty_\(1998_book\)). While most of Petraeus's closest advisers were American military officers, he also hired Lt. Col. [David Kilcullen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Kilcullen) of the [Australian Army](2795.md), who was working for the [U.S. State Department](31975.md).[^cite_note-107] Kilcullen upon his return from Iraq published *The Accidental Guerrilla*,[^cite_note-108] and has discussed the central front of the war and lessons learned in Iraq in *The Washington Post*.[^cite_note-109]



U.S. Army Gen. David H. Petraeus, the commander of [Multi-National Force – Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-National_Force_%E2%80%93_Iraq), briefs reporters at the Pentagon 26 April 2007, on his view of the current military situation in Iraq.

After taking command of MNF-I on 10 February 2007, Petraeus inspected U.S. and Iraqi units all over Iraq, visiting outposts in greater [Baghdad](4492.md), [Tikrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tikrit), [Baquba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baqubah), [Ramadi](245655.md), [Mosul](200475.md), [Kirkuk](199292.md), [Bayji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayji), [Samarra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samarra), [Basrah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basrah) and as far west as al-Hit and Al Qaim. In April 2007, Petraeus made his first visit to Washington as MNF-I Commander, reporting to President Bush and Congress on the progress of the "surge" and the overall situation in Iraq. During this visit he met privately with members of Congress and reportedly argued against setting a timetable for U.S. troop withdrawal from Iraq.[^cite_note-CNN_Senate_Iraq-110]

By late May 2007, Congress had not imposed any timetables in war funding legislation for troop withdrawal.[^cite_note-111] The enacted legislation did mandate that Petraeus and the U.S. Ambassador to Iraq, [Ryan Crocker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ryan_Crocker), deliver a report to Congress by 15 September 2007, detailing their assessment of the military, economic and political situation of Iraq.

In June 2007, Petraeus stated in an interview that there were "astonishing signs of normalcy" in Baghdad, and this comment drew criticism from Senate majority leader [Harry Reid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Reid). In the same interview, however, Petraeus stated that "many problems remain" and he noted the need to help the Iraqis "stitch back together the fabric of society that was torn during the height of sectarian violence" in late 2006.[^cite_note-112] Petraeus also warned that he expected that the situation in Iraq would require the continued deployment of the elevated troop level of more than 150,000 beyond September 2007; he also stated that U.S. involvement in Iraq could last years afterward.[^cite_note-113]

These statements are representative of the fact that throughout their time in Iraq, Petraeus and Crocker remained circumspect and refused to classify themselves as optimists or pessimists, noting, instead, that they were realists and that the reality in Iraq was very hard. They also repeatedly emphasized the importance of forthright reports and an unvarnished approach.[^cite_note-114][^cite_note-115] Indeed, Petraeus's realistic approach and assessments were lauded during the McLaughlin Group's 2008 Year-End Awards, when [Monica Crowley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monica_Crowley) nominated Petraeus for the most honest person of the year, stating, "\[H]e spoke about the great successes of the surge in Iraq, but he always tempered it, never sugar-coated it."[^cite_note-116]

#### Multi-National Force – Iraq (summer and fall 2007)

In July 2007, the [White House](33057.md) submitted to Congress the [interim report on Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_Benchmark_Assessment_Report), which stated that coalition forces had made satisfactory progress on 6 of 18 benchmarks set by Congress. On 7 September 2007, in a letter addressed to the troops he was commanding, Petraeus wrote that much military progress had been made, but that the national-level political progress that was hoped for had not been achieved.[^cite_note-117] Petraeus's [Report to Congress on the Situation in Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Report_to_Congress_on_the_Situation_in_Iraq) was delivered to Congress on 10 September 2007.

On 15 August 2007, the *[Los Angeles Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Angeles_Times)* had stated that, according to [unnamed administration officials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journalism_sourcing), the report "would actually be written by the [White House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Bush_Administration), with inputs from officials throughout the government."[^cite_note-118] However, Petraeus declared in his testimony to Congress that "I wrote this testimony myself." He further elaborated that his testimony to Congress "has not been cleared by, nor shared with, anyone in the Pentagon, the White House, or Congress."[^cite_note-auto-119]



GEN Petraeus with LTG [Odierno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_Odierno) (left), President [Bush](3414021.md) (center), SecDef [Gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Gates), and SecState [Rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condoleezza_Rice) (right) at [Al Asad Airbase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Asad_Airbase) in September 2007

In his September Congressional testimony, Petraeus stated, "As a bottom line up front, the military objectives of the surge are, in large measure, being met." He cited numerous factors for this progress, to include the fact that Coalition and Iraqi Forces had dealt significant blows to Al-Qaeda Iraq and had disrupted Shia militias, that ethno-sectarian violence had been reduced, and that the tribal rejection of Al-Qaeda had spread from Anbar Province to numerous other locations across Iraq. Based on this progress and additional progress expected to be achieved, Petraeus recommended drawing down the surge forces from Iraq and gradually transitioning increased responsibilities to Iraqi Forces, as their capabilities and conditions on the ground permitted.[^cite_note-120]

[Democratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Democratic_Party) [Senate Majority Leader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Party_leaders_of_the_United_States_Senate) [Harry Reid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Reid) of [Nevada](21216.md) argued Petraeus's "plan is just more of the same" and "is neither a drawdown or a change in mission that we need." Democratic [Representative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_House) [Robert Wexler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Wexler) of [Florida](18933066.md) accused Petraeus of "[cherry-picking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherry_picking_\(fallacy\)) statistics" and "massaging information".[^cite_note-121] Chairman of the [House Foreign Affairs Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_Foreign_Affairs_Committee) [Tom Lantos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Lantos) of [California](5407.md) called Petraeus and [U.S. Ambassador to Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Ambassador_to_Iraq) [Ryan Crocker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ryan_Crocker) "two of our nation's most capable [public servants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_servants)" and said Democrats feel "esteem for their [professionalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professionalism)". But he also said that "We can no longer take their assertions on Iraq at [face value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_value)"; concluding, "We need to get out of Iraq, for that country's sake as well as our own."[^cite_note-122]

[Republican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Republican_Party) [Presidential candidate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Republican_presidential_candidates,_2008) [Duncan Hunter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duncan_L._Hunter) called the report "a candid, independent assessment given with integrity".[^cite_note-123] Republican senator [Jon Kyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jon_Kyl) of [Arizona](21883824.md) stated, "I commend General Petraeus for his honest and forthright assessment of the situation in Iraq."[^cite_note-124] [Anti-war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opposition_to_the_Iraq_War#Opposition_in_the_United_States) Republican senator [Chuck Hagel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuck_Hagel) of [Nebraska](21647.md) criticized the report while praising Petraeus, saying "It's not your fault, general. ... It's not [Ambassador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambassador_to_Iraq) [Crocker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ryan_Crocker)'s fault. It's this administration's fault."[^cite_note-125] A *[USA Today](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USA_Today)*/[Gallup poll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallup_poll) taken after Petraeus's report to Congress showed virtually no change in negative public opinion toward the war.[^cite_note-126] A [Pew Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pew_Research_Center) survey, however, found that most Americans who had heard about the report approved of Petraeus's recommendations.[^cite_note-127]

On 20 September, the Senate passed an amendment by Republican [John Cornyn III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cornyn_III) of [Texas](29810.md) designed to "strongly condemn personal attacks on the honor and integrity of General Petraeus". Cornyn drafted the amendment in response to a [controversial full-page ad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MoveOn.org_ad_controversy) by the liberal group [Moveon.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moveon.org) in the 10 September 2007, edition of *[The New York Times](30680.md)*. All forty-nine Republican senators and twenty-two Democratic senators voted in support.[^cite_note-128] The House passed a similar resolution by a 341–79 vote on 26 September.

In December 2007, *[The Washington Post](102226.md)*'s "Fact Checker" stated that "While some of Petraeus's statistics are open to challenge, his claims about a general reduction in violence have been borne out over subsequent months. It now looks as if Petraeus was broadly right on this issue at least."[^cite_note-129]

Based on the conditions on the ground, in October 2007, Petraeus and [U.S. Ambassador to Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Ambassador_to_Iraq) [Ryan Crocker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ryan_Crocker) revised their campaign plan for Iraq. In recognition of the progress made against Al Qaeda Iraq, one of the major points would be "shifting the U.S. military effort to focus more on countering [Shiite militias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_militias_in_Iraq)."[^cite_note-130]

#### Multi-National Force – Iraq (spring 2008)

On 18 February 2008, *[USA Today](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USA_Today)* stated that "the U.S. effort has shown more success" and that, after the number of troops reached its peak in fall 2007, "U.S. deaths were at their lowest levels since the 2003 invasion, civilian casualties were down, and street life was resuming in Baghdad."[^cite_note-USA-131] In light of the significant reduction in violence and as the surge brigades began to redeploy without replacement, Petraeus characterized the progress as tenuous, fragile, and reversible and repeatedly reminded all involved that much work remained to be done.[^cite_note-132][^cite_note-133] During an early February trip to Iraq, Defense Secretary [Robert Gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Gates) endorsed the idea of a period of consolidation and evaluation upon completion of the withdrawal of surge brigades from Iraq.[^cite_note-134]

Petraeus and Crocker continued these themes at their two full days of testimony before Congress on 8–9 April. During his opening statement, Petraeus stated that "there has been significant but uneven security progress in Iraq", while also noting that "the situation in certain areas is still unsatisfactory and that innumerable challenges remain" and that "the progress made since last spring is fragile and reversible." He also recommended a continuation of the drawdown of surge forces as well as a 45-day period of consolidation and evaluation after the final surge brigade had redeployed in late July.[^cite_note-auto-119] Analysts for *[USA Today](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USA_Today)* and *[The New York Times](30680.md)* stated that the hearings "lacked the suspense of last September's debate," but they did include sharp questioning as well as both skepticism and praise from various Congressional leaders.[^cite_note-135][^cite_note-136]

In late May 2008, the [Senate Armed Services Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senate_Armed_Services_Committee) held nomination hearings for Petraeus and [Lieutenant General Ray Odierno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_T._Odierno) to lead [United States Central Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CENTCOM) and [Multi-National Force-Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multinational_Force_Iraq), respectively. During the hearings, Committee Chairman [Carl Levin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Levin) praised these two men, stating that "we owe Gen. Petraeus and Gen. Odierno a debt of gratitude for the commitment, determination and strength that they brought to their areas of responsibility. And regardless of how long the administration may choose to remain engaged in the strife in that country, our troops are better off with the leadership these two distinguished soldiers provide."[^cite_note-137]

During his opening statement, Petraeus discussed four principles that would guide his efforts if confirmed as [CENTCOM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CENTCOM) Commander: seeking to strengthen international partnerships; taking a "whole of government" approach; pursuing comprehensive efforts and solutions; and, finally, both supporting efforts in Iraq and Afghanistan and ensuring readiness for possible contingency operations in the future. Petraeus also noted that during the week before his testimony, the number of security incidents in Iraq was the lowest in over four years.[^cite_note-138] After Petraeus's return to Baghdad, and despite the continued drawdown of surge forces as well as recent Iraqi-led operations in places like Basrah, Mosul, and Baghdad, the number of security incidents in Iraq remained at their lowest level in over four years.[^cite_note-139]

#### Multi-National Force – Iraq (summer and fall 2008)



Petraeus explains security improvements in [Sadr City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sadr_City) while giving an aerial tour of Baghdad to Senator [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama), July 2008

In September 2008, Petraeus gave an interview to [BBC News](1139893.md) stating that he did not think using the term "victory" in describing the Iraq war was appropriate, saying "This is not the sort of struggle where you take a hill, plant the flag and go home to a victory parade ... it's not war with a simple slogan."[^cite_note-140]

Petraeus had discussed the term 'victory' before in March 2008, saying to [NPR News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NPR_News) that "an Iraq that is at peace with itself, at peace with its neighbors, that has a government that is representative of—and responsive to—its citizenry and is a contributing member of the global community" could arguably be called 'victory'.[^cite_note-141] On the eve of his change of command, in September 2008, Petraeus stated that "I don't use terms like victory or defeat ... I'm a realist, not an optimist or a pessimist. And the reality is that there has been significant progress but there are still serious challenges."[^cite_note-praise-142]

#### Change of command



Iraq Defense Minister [Abdul Qadir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qadir_Obeidi) presents a gift to Petraeus during a farewell ceremony in Baghdad in September 2008

On 16 September 2008, Petraeus formally gave over his command in Iraq to General [Raymond T. Odierno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_T._Odierno) in a government ceremony presided by Defense Secretary [Robert Gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Gates).[^cite_note-praise-142] During the ceremony, Gates stated that Petraeus "played a historic role" and created the "translation of a great strategy into a great success in very difficult circumstances". Gates also told Petraeus he believed "history will regard you as one of our nation's greatest battle captains."[^cite_note-praise-142] He presented Petraeus with the [Defense Distinguished Service Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defense_Distinguished_Service_Medal).[^cite_note-praise-142] At the event, Petraeus mentioned the difficulty in getting the [Sons of Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sons_of_Iraq) absorbed into the central [Government of Iraq](11752991.md) and warned about future consequences if the effort stalled.[^cite_note-praise-142]

When speaking of these and other challenges, Petraeus noted that "the gains \[achieved in Iraq] are tenuous and unlikely to survive without an American effort that outlasts his tenure." Even so, as Petraeus departed Iraq, it appeared to many that he was leaving a much different Iraq than the one that existed when he took command in February 2007. As described by Dexter Filkins, "violence has plummeted from its apocalyptic peaks, Iraqi leaders are asserting themselves, and streets that once seemed dead are flourishing with life."[^cite_note-143] The 3 January 2009, Iraq Trends (a chart produced weekly by the MNF-I) seemed to illustrate an increase in incidents followed by the sharp decline described by Dexter Filkens and others.

Petraeus' command of coalition forces during the Surge in Iraq was lauded by a number of observers. In his book *The Savior Generals* historian [Victor Davis Hanson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Davis_Hanson) wrote, "that without David Petraeus, the American effort in Iraq—along with the reputation of the U.S. military in the Middle East—would have been lost long ago."[^cite_note-144] In her introduction of Petraeus at the Baccalaureate ceremony for the Class of 2009 at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University), President Shirley Tilghman described his accomplishments. While acknowledging that much remained to be accomplished in Iraq, Tilghman paid tribute to Petraeus's "leadership in rethinking American military strategy through his principles of counterinsurgency," which are, she said, "eliminating 'simplistic definitions of victory and defeat in favor of incremental and nuanced progress'."[^cite_note-145]

### U.S. Central Command (fall 2008 to summer 2010)



Gen. David H. Petraeus speaking at the [New Hampshire Institute of Politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Hampshire_Institute_of_Politics) at [Saint Anselm College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Anselm_College)

On 31 October 2008, Petraeus assumed command of the [United States Central Command](338482.md) (USCENTCOM) headquartered in Tampa, Florida. Petraeus was responsible for U.S. operations in 20 countries spreading from Egypt to Pakistan—including Operations Iraqi Freedom and Enduring Freedom. During his time at CENTCOM, Petraeus advocated that countering the terrorist threats in the CENTCOM region required more than just counter-terrorism forces, demanding instead whole-of-government, comprehensive approaches akin to those of counterinsurgency.[^cite_note-146] One of his closest colleagues said that Petraeus knew that defeating an insurgency required living among the people, convincing them that we were better than the insurgents. "\[Y]ou can't kill 'em all ... \[Y]ou can't kill your way out of an insurgency. ... You have to find other kinds of ammunition, and it's not always a bullet."[^cite_note-147]

Petraeus reiterated this view in a 2009 interview published in *Parade* magazine.[^cite_note-148] In an interview for *Newsweek* magazine's "Interview Issue: The View From People Who Make a Difference," Petraeus expressed his support for President Obama's announced Afghanistan strategy and discussed his view that reconciliation efforts in Afghanistan should for the time being occur "at the lower and midlevels".[^cite_note-149]

In mid-August 2009, Petraeus established the [Afghanistan-Pakistan Center of Excellence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghanistan-Pakistan_Center_of_Excellence) within the USCENTCOM Directorate of [Intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence) to provide leadership to coordinate, integrate and focus analysis efforts in support of operations in Afghanistan and Pakistan.[^cite_note-150]

During a February 2010 World Affairs Council event in Philadelphia, General Petraeus discussed the ways in which diplomacy, history, and culture impact overall military strategy, then explained how these issues informed the U.S. approach to counterinsurgencies in Iraq and Afghanistan.[^cite_note-151]

On 16 March 2010, in testimony to the [Senate Armed Services Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senate_Armed_Services_Committee), Petraeus described the continuing [Israeli–Palestinian conflict](46216.md) as a challenge to U.S. interests in the region. According to the testimony, the conflict was "fomenting anti-American sentiment" due to "a [perception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perception) of U.S. favoritism for [Israel](9282173.md)". This was widely commented on in the media.[^cite_note-152][^cite_note-153][^cite_note-154][^cite_note-155] When questioned by journalist Philip Klein, Petraeus said the original reporter "picked apart" and "spun" his speech. He said he believed there were many important factors standing in the way of peace, including "a whole bunch of extremist organizations, some of which by the way deny Israel's right to exist. There's a country that has a nuclear program who denies that the [Holocaust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocaust) took place. So again we have all these factors in there. This \[Israel] is just one."[^cite_note-156][^cite_note-157]

In March 2010, Petraeus visited the [New Hampshire Institute of Politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Hampshire_Institute_of_Politics) at [Saint Anselm College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Anselm_College) to speak about Iraq and Afghanistan.[^cite_note-158] Petraeus spoke a few days after the seventh anniversary of the U.S. invasion of Iraq, noting the successful changes in Iraq since the U.S. [troop surge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troop_surge). The visit to Saint Anselm created rumors that Petraeus was contemplating a run for the presidency; however, he denied the speculation, saying that he was not aware that the college had been the site of numerous presidential debates.[^cite_note-159]

Toward the close of his tenure as CENTCOM Commander, including in his interview published in *[Vanity Fair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanity_Fair_\(magazine\))*, Petraeus discussed the effort to determine and send to Afghanistan the right "inputs" for success there; these inputs included several structures and organizations that proved important in Iraq, including "an engagement cell to support reconciliation ... a finance cell to go after financing of the enemy ... \[a] really robust detainee-operations task force, a rule-of-law task force, an energy-fusion cell—all these other sort of nonstandard missions that are very important."[^cite_note-160]

On 5 May 2010, *The New York Times* published an article suggesting there was mounting evidence of a Taliban role in the Times Square bombing plot.[^cite_note-161] On 7 May 2010, Petraeus announced that Times Square bombing suspect [Faisal Shahzad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faisal_Shahzad) was a "lone wolf" terrorist who did not work with others.[^cite_note-162] On 10 May 2010, Attorney General Eric Holder said that the evidence showed the Pakistani Taliban directed this plot.[^cite_note-163]

#### Health

General Petraeus was diagnosed with early-stage [prostate cancer](88078.md) in February 2009 and underwent two months of successful [radiation treatment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_treatment) at [Walter Reed Army Medical Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Reed_Army_Medical_Center).[^cite_note-164] The diagnosis and treatment were not publicly disclosed until October 2009 because Petraeus and his family regarded his illness as a personal matter that did not interfere with the performance of his duties.[^cite_note-165]

On 15 June 2010, Petraeus momentarily fainted while being questioned by the Senate Armed Services Committee. He quickly recovered and was able to walk and exit the room without assistance.[^cite_note-166] He attributed the episode to possible [dehydration](155056.md).

### Commander of U.S. and ISAF forces in Afghanistan (2010–2011)



Petraeus having tea with the [Afghan Border Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghan_Border_Police) Commander at the border with Uzbekistan

On 23 June 2010, Obama announced that he would nominate Petraeus to succeed General [Stanley A. McChrystal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_A._McChrystal) as the commander of U.S. Forces in Afghanistan. The change of command was prompted by McChrystal's comments about the [Obama administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidency_of_Barack_Obama) and its policies in Afghanistan during an interview with *[Rolling Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolling_Stone)* magazine.[^cite_note-nytimes1-15] The nomination was technically a positional step down from his position as commander of Central Command; however, the President said that he believed that he was the best man for the job. After being confirmed by the Senate on 30 June,[^cite_note-petraeus_confirmed_IT-58] Petraeus formally assumed command on 4 July.[^cite_note-CBC-2010/07/03/petraeus-afghanistan-167]

During the assumption-of-command remarks,[^cite_note-168] Petraeus provided his vision and goals to NATO, the members of his command, and his Afghan partners. As he was known to do while the commander in Iraq, Petraeus delivered his first Letter to the Troops[^cite_note-169] on the same day he assumed command.[^cite_note-170]

In December 2009, then President Obama announced that there would be a 30,000 troop surge in Afghanistan, but that in 18 months there would be a troop drawdown to take these forces out of the country again. Petraeus later shared his surprise at this decision, noting that he didn't agree with the choice to announce a drawdown given that it limited bargaining power with the enemy.[^cite_note-171]

On 1 August 2010, shortly after the disclosure of the [Afghan war logs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghan_War_Diary) on [WikiLeaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WikiLeaks), which included reports of high civilian deaths and increased Taliban attacks, Petraeus issued his updated Tactical Directive for the prevention of civilian casualties, providing guidance and intent for the use of force by the U.S. military units operating in Afghanistan (replacing the 1 July 2009, version). This directive reinforced the concept of "disciplined use of force in partnership with Afghan Security Forces" in the fight against insurgent forces.

> We must never forget that the center of gravity in this struggle is the Afghan people; it is they who will ultimately determine the future of Afghanistan ... Prior to the use of fires, the commander approving the strike must determine that no civilians are present. If unable to assess the risk of civilian presence, fires are prohibited, except under of the following two conditions (specific conditions deleted due to operational security; however, they have to do with the risk to ISAF and Afghan forces).[^cite_note-172]

In the October 2010 issue of *Army Magazine*, Petraeus discussed changes that had taken place over the previous 18 months, including sections discussing "setting the conditions for progress", "capitalizing on the conditions for progress", "improving security", "supporting governance expansion", "promoting economic development", "reducing corruption", and "our troopers: carrying out a difficult mission".[^cite_note-173]



Petraeus talks with U.S. soldiers at Combat Outpost Monti in eastern Afghanistan in August 2010



Petraeus visits Regional Command West in Afghanistan, May 2011

Sources interviewed by [Steve Coll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Coll) were critical of aspects of Petraeus's tenure as ISAF commander. Some described Petraeus as being physically exhausted from his previous tours abroad (as well as his recovery from prostate cancer), having a proclivity to see Afghanistan through the prism of his experience in Iraq—to the point of occasionally referring to Afghanistan as "Iraq"—and being slow to react to "[green-on-blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green-on-blue_attacks)" or "insider attacks" by Afghan soldiers against their American partners. Petraeus sought to recreate the "[Sunni Awakening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sons_of_Iraq#Anbar_Awakening)" in Iraq by offering financial inducements to former Taliban insurgents to integrate into [Hamid Karzai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamid_Karzai)'s government, but the results were not impressive.[^cite_note-174]

As commander in Afghanistan, Petraeus joined President Obama as a primary target for Osama bin Laden. After his death, documents recovered from bin Laden's compound unveiled a plot to assassinate the two men as they traveled by plane. Bin Laden's communications with a top deputy stated, "The reason for concentrating on them is that Obama is the head of infidelity and killing him automatically will make \[Vice President] Biden take over the presidency. Biden is totally unprepared for that post, which will lead the U.S. into a crisis. As for Petraeus, he is the man of the hour ... and killing him would alter the war's path" in Afghanistan.[^cite_note-175][^cite_note-176]

In early March 2011, Petraeus made a "rare apology" following a NATO helicopter airstrike under his command that resulted in the deaths of nine Afghan boys and the wounding of a 10th, as they gathered firewood in Eastern Afghanistan. In a statement, Petraeus apologized to the members of the Afghan government, the people of Afghanistan, and the surviving family members, and said, "These deaths should have never happened." Several journalists and observers noted the humanitarian candor in Petraeus's open regrets.[^cite_note-177][^cite_note-178] Petraeus relinquished command of U.S. and NATO forces in Afghanistan on 18 July 2011.[^cite_note-179] He received the Defense Distinguished Service Medal and the NATO Meritorious Service Medal for his service.

### Retirement from the U.S. Army (2011)

Petraeus retired from the U.S. Army on 31 August 2011. His retirement ceremony was held at [Joint Base Myer–Henderson Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_Base_Myer%E2%80%93Henderson_Hall).[^cite_note-180][^cite_note-181] During this ceremony, he was awarded the [Army Distinguished Service Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distinguished_Service_Medal_\(U.S._Army\)) by [Deputy Secretary of Defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Deputy_Secretary_of_Defense) [William J. Lynn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_J._Lynn).[^cite_note-182] During the ceremony, Lynn noted that Petraeus had played an important role as both a combat leader and strategist in the post-9/11 world. Lynn also cited General Petraeus's efforts in current counter insurgency strategy.[^cite_note-183]

Admiral [Michael Mullen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Mullen), [Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff](364458.md) in his remarks compared General Petraeus to [Ulysses S. Grant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulysses_S._Grant), [John J. Pershing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_J._Pershing), [George Marshall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Marshall) and [Dwight D. Eisenhower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwight_D._Eisenhower) as one of the great battle captains of American history.[^cite_note-184] With his four-star rank, Petraeus receives an annual pension of about \$220,000.[^cite_note-185]

## CIA director (2011–2012)



Petraeus sworn in at the CIA Headquarters as his wife, [Holly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holly_Petraeus), looks on

On 28 April 2011, President [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama) announced that he had nominated Petraeus to become the new [Director of the Central Intelligence Agency](1777424.md).[^cite_note-187] The nomination was confirmed by the United States Senate 94–0 on 30 June 2011.[^cite_note-188] Petraeus was sworn in at the White House on 6 September[^cite_note-189] and then ceremonially sworn in by Vice President [Joe Biden](145422.md) at CIA Headquarters in Langley, Virginia on 11 October 2011.[^cite_note-190]

Petraeus's tenure at the CIA was more low profile than that of his predecessor, [Leon Panetta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_Panetta), declining to give media interviews while director and speaking to Congress in [closed sessions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_session_of_the_United_States_Congress). He also differed from Panetta in management style, as an article in *[The New York Times](30680.md)* published just days before his resignation said; Panetta "wooed the work force and often did not question operational details, \[while] Petraeus is a demanding boss who does not hesitate to order substandard work redone or details of plans adjusted".[^cite_note-NYTnov2-191] Petraeus's philosophy on leadership at the time was summarized in a twelve-point article published by Newsweek on 5 November 2012.[^cite_note-192]

Although Petraeus was given good marks by most observers for his work heading the CIA,[^cite_note-NYTnov2-191] during October 2012 some critics took issue with the availability of accurate information from the CIA concerning an [attack in Benghazi, Libya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attack_on_the_U.S._diplomatic_mission_in_Benghazi), the month prior. By the 11th of September, four Americans had been killed, including the ambassador, and more than thirty evacuated. Only seven of those evacuated did not work for the CIA. According to a *[Wall Street Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wall_Street_Journal)* story, other government agencies complained about being left "largely in the dark about the CIA's role," with Secretary of State [Hillary Clinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hillary_Clinton) telephoning Petraeus directly the night of the attacks seeking assistance. Although the "State Department believed it had a formal agreement with the CIA to provide backup security," "the CIA didn't have the same understanding about its security responsibilities," reported *The Wall Street Journal*.[^cite_note-193]

### Petraeus scandal

Main article: [Petraeus scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petraeus_scandal)



Petraeus with [Paula Broadwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paula_Broadwell) in July 2011

Petraeus reportedly began an affair with [Paula Broadwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paula_Broadwell), principal author of his biography, *[All In: The Education of General David Petraeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_In:_The_Education_of_General_David_Petraeus)*, after Petraeus left his ISAF command on 18 July 2011, to become CIA director. Petraeus reportedly ended the affair in the summer of 2012, around the time that he learned that Broadwell had been sending harassing emails to a longstanding family friend of the Petraeuses, [Jill Kelley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jill_Kelley).[^cite_note-WaPoNov12-194]

Kelley, a Florida socialite who frequently entertained senior military personnel at her and her husband's [Tampa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tampa,_Florida) mansion,[^cite_note-195] had approached an acquaintance who worked for the [FBI Tampa Field Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI_Tampa_Field_Office) in the late spring with regard to anonymous emails she considered threatening.[^cite_note-WaPoNov12-194] The Bureau traced the emails to Broadwell, and noted that Broadwell appeared to be exchanging intimate messages with an email account belonging to Petraeus, which instigated an investigation into whether that account had been hacked into or was someone posing as Petraeus.[^cite_note-196][^cite_note-197][^cite_note-198] According to an [Associated Press](18935732.md) report, rather than transmit emails to each other's inbox, which would have left a more obvious email trail, Petraeus and Broadwell left messages in a draft folder and the draft messages were then [read by the other person](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foldering) when they logged into the same account.[^cite_note-199]

Although [U.S. Attorney General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Attorney_General) [Eric Holder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Holder) was aware that the FBI had discovered the affair,[^cite_note-200] it was not until 6 November 2012, that Petraeus's nominal superior, Director of National Intelligence [James R. Clapper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_R._Clapper), was advised. That same evening Clapper called Petraeus and urged him to resign. Clapper notified the [White House](33057.md) the next day, 7 November. After being briefed the following day, Obama summoned Petraeus to the White House where Petraeus offered his resignation.[^cite_note-201] Obama accepted his resignation on 9 November,[^cite_note-202] and Petraeus cited his affair when announcing that same day that he would resign as CIA Director.[^cite_note-203] Eventually, Petraeus pleaded guilty to a misdemeanor charge of mishandling the classified information that he provided to his mistress and biographer.[^cite_note-Mar_3_2015-22]

#### Criticism after 2012 scandal

On 13 November 2012, Reagan administration Assistant Secretary of Defense [Lawrence Korb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Korb), CIA analyst and [Veteran Intelligence Professionals for Sanity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veteran_Intelligence_Professionals_for_Sanity) co-founder [Ray McGovern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_McGovern), and [investigative journalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Investigative_journalist) [Gareth Porter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gareth_Porter) appeared on [Al Jazeera English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Jazeera_English). Together they assessed the general's extensive military-media strategy, linking his writings on counterguerrilla operations and subsequent military media efforts to his downfall with his female biographer. Critics said that the Petraeus media strategy would prove damaging for American policy in the future because of omissions and distorted interpretations that Washington policymakers, other experts, and the American public accepted from Petraeus's media contacts.[^cite_note-204]

Military historians have noted the absence of field records for the Iraq and Afghanistan military campaigns, but have not been personally critical of the commanders in theater.[^cite_note-205]

#### Criminal charges and probation

In January 2015, *[The New York Times](30680.md)* reported that the FBI and the DOJ had recommended bringing felony charges against Petraeus for providing classified information to [Broadwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paula_Broadwell). Petraeus denied the allegations and was reported to have had no interest in a plea deal.[^cite_note-https-21] However, on 3 March 2015, the DOJ announced that Petraeus agreed to plead guilty in federal court in [Charlotte, North Carolina](57447.md), to a charge of unauthorized removal and retention of classified information.[^cite_note-206]

In the 15-page statement of facts filed by the government along with the plea agreement, the government stated that Petraeus had provided Broadwell access to documents containing top secret [sensitive compartmented information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensitive_compartmented_information), had later moved those documents to his personal residence and stored them in an unsecured drawer, and had deliberately and intentionally lied to federal investigators about both providing Broadwell access to the documents and their improper storage. These facts were acknowledged to be true by Petraeus as part of his plea agreement.[^cite_note-207]

On 23 April 2015, a federal judge sentenced Petraeus to two years' probation plus a fine of \$100,000. The fine was more than double the amount the Justice Department had requested.[^cite_note-208]

Press accounts in January 2016 indicated that Department of Defense staff were reviewing Department of Justice documents from the Petraeus prosecution and considering whether to recommend to the Secretary of Defense that Petraeus be demoted on the Army's retired list. Laws and regulations indicate that members of the military are retired at the last rank in which they are deemed to have served successfully; Petraeus's admission of an extramarital affair and guilty plea with regard to removing and retaining classified information while serving in the grade of general could be grounds for reduction in rank to lieutenant general. The matter was reviewed by then-Secretary of the Army [John M. McHugh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_M._McHugh) before he left office in October 2015; he recommended no further action.[^cite_note-209] On 29 January, press accounts indicated that Stephen C. Hedger, Assistant Secretary of Defense for Legislative Affairs, had written to the U.S. Senate [Armed Services Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Senate_Committee_on_Armed_Services). In his letter, Hedger informed the committee that Secretary of Defense [Ashton Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashton_Carter) had concurred with the Army's recommendation, and would not impose any further punishment on Petraeus.[^cite_note-210]

## Later life

Petraeus was named a [visiting professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visiting_professor) at [Macaulay Honors College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macaulay_Honors_College) at the [City University of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_University_of_New_York) in July 2013. According to a statement from Petraeus, "I look forward to leading a seminar at Macaulay that examines the developments that could position the United States—and our North American partners—to lead the world out of the current global economic slowdown."[^cite_note-211] After his anticipated \$200,000 salary for the academic year drew fire from critics, Petraeus agreed to take on the teaching position [for just \$1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-dollar_salary) in order to keep the focus on the students and away from any monetary controversy.[^cite_note-212] In September 2013 Petraeus was harassed by students at CUNY while walking on campus.[^cite_note-213]

On 1 May 2013, the [University of Southern California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Southern_California) named Petraeus as a Judge Widney Professor, "a title reserved for eminent individuals from the arts, sciences, professions, business and community and national leadership".[^cite_note-214] The president of the Currahee board of trustees announced 6 May, that Petraeus agreed to serve on the board of trustees that preserves [Camp Toccoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camp_Toccoa). During WWII, four of the main parachute infantry regiments of the Army trained at Camp Toccoa prior to their deployment.[^cite_note-215]

[Kohlberg Kravis Roberts & Co. L.P.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kohlberg_Kravis_Roberts), a New York [investment firm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Investment_firm), hired Petraeus as chairman of the firm's newly created KKR Global Institute in May 2013. Part of his work would support KKR's investment teams and portfolio companies when studying new investments, especially in new locations.[^cite_note-216]

In October 2013, Petraeus joined Harvard's [John F. Kennedy School of Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Kennedy_School_of_Government) as a non-resident senior fellow at the [Belfer Center for Science and International Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belfer_Center_for_Science_and_International_Affairs). According to the school, Petraeus joined to lead a new project focusing on the technological, scientific and economic dynamics that are spurring renewed North American competitiveness. "The Coming North America Decades" project would analyze how potential policy choices could effect this ongoing transformation.[^cite_note-217] In 2016, the center announced a new project involving Petraeus and focusing on strategic leadership.[^cite_note-218]

General Petraeus was one of the "11 legendary generals" profiled in the 2014 National Geographic Channel feature "American War Generals".[^cite_note-219] In 2015, Petraeus suggested the U.S. should arm members of the terror group [Al-Nusra Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Nusra_Front) (the Syrian branch of [Al-Qaeda](1921.md)) in Syria to fight [ISIS](9087364.md).[^cite_note-220]

In June 2016, Petraeus and [Mark Kelly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Kelly), a retired NASA astronaut and later senator from Arizona, announced the creation of the now defunct gun control group Veterans Coalition for Common Sense.[^cite_note-221]

Petraeus delivered the inaugural lecture in a series dedicated to his mentor and the former dean of [The Fletcher School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fletcher_School_of_Law_and_Diplomacy) at [Tufts University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tufts_University), [General Jack Galvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Galvin_\(general\)).[^cite_note-222] He also delivered the inaugural lecture in a series dedicated to Admiral [Stansfield Turner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stansfield_Turner) at the U.S. [Naval War College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_War_College) on 7 August 2018. The lecture series honors the achievements of Turner, who served as the college's president from 1972 to 1974.[^cite_note-223]

As a member of a [panel discussion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panel_discussion) in October 2018 concerning a film documenting the *[Stars and Stripes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stars_and_Stripes_\(newspaper\))* newspaper, Petraeus shared his personal experiences with the newspaper with the audience.[^cite_note-224]

Petraeus spoke at the [WORLD.MINDS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World.minds) meeting in June 2025 in Washington DC about China, AI and the transatlantic relationship.[^cite_note-225] In September 2025 at the Concordia Annual Summit, Petraeus interviewed the President of Syria, [Ahmed al-Sharaa](44007699.md).[^cite_note-226] During the discussion, Petraeus described himself as one of al-Sharaa's "fans".[^cite_note-227]

On 15 April 2026, Petraeus was named a founding member of the ARES Military Expert Council (**A**llied **R**eform and **E**xpert **S**upport), an international advisory body established by the [General Staff of the Armed Forces of Ukraine](17313362.md) under the [Commander-in-Chief of the Armed Forces of Ukraine](63525946.md) [Oleksandr Syrskyi](69446333.md). The council, chaired by former [Deputy Supreme Allied Commander Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deputy_Supreme_Allied_Commander_Europe) [General Sir Richard Shirreff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Shirreff), was created to facilitate the exchange of advanced Ukrainian and international military experience and to support institutional reforms within the [Armed Forces of Ukraine](42799055.md).[^cite_note-ZSU-ARES-Petraeus-228][^cite_note-ZN-ARES-Petraeus-229]

On September 13, 2026, Petraeus was Ukraine attending the [Yalta European Strategy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yalta_European_Strategy) conference when a Russian drone struck a passenger train at [Yahodyn](79499133.md) railway station, approximately 2 kilometers from the Polish border. Petraeus was traveling on a separate train that remained at the station at the time of the attack. The diplomatic train carrying former British prime minister [Boris Johnson](19065069.md) and other European officials had departed the station earlier. [Ukrainian Railways](3149341.md) said it was "quite likely" that the diplomatic train had been the intended target.[^cite_note-230]

### Secretary of State consideration

On 18 November 2016, *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)* cited "diplomatic sources" as having said that Petraeus had entered the race for [U.S. Secretary of State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Secretary_of_State) in the [Trump administration](52231341.md).[^cite_note-231] Petraeus confirmed his interest in the position during a [BBC Radio 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_4) interview, stating that he would serve if asked.[^cite_note-232]

Petraeus met with then President-elect Donald Trump at [Trump Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trump_Tower) on 28 November to discuss the position and world affairs. Both Petraeus and Trump expressed favorable views of the meeting, with Trump taking to [Twitter](9988187.md) to announce, "Just met with General Petraeus—was very impressed!" Petraeus joined a short list of potential candidates for the position, including [Mitt Romney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitt_Romney) and [Rudy Giuliani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolph_Giuliani).[^cite_note-233]

There was public speculation that his nomination could hurt Trump's administration, but Republican senators [John McCain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_McCain) and [Lindsey Graham](363279.md) advocated for Petraeus, calling him "an extraordinary pick". Petraeus also received support from Democratic senator [Dianne Feinstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dianne_Feinstein), suggesting that Democrats would keep an open mind concerning his confirmation.[^cite_note-234]

On 13 December 2016, Trump officially selected [Rex Tillerson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rex_Tillerson) for the role of Secretary of State. Petraeus expressed his gratitude for Trump's consideration and deferred to former Secretary of Defense [Robert Gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Gates)' endorsement when asked his opinion of Tillerson.[^cite_note-235]

### Business activities

Petraeus has worked for [KKR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kohlberg_Kravis_Roberts) since 2013, where he is a partner and chairman of the KKR Global Institute.[^cite_note-236] On 14 April 2025, Petraeus was appointed the chair of US private equity firm KKR's Middle East division. KKR has had a presence in the Persian Gulf since 2009, with offices in both the financial hub of Dubai and Saudi Arabia's capital Riyadh. Petraeus commented, following the announcement, that "the Middle East is emerging as a leading investment powerhouse," and that the firm saw opportunities to invest in or lend to "domestic businesses" in the region.[^cite_note-237]

## Political positions

### Reaction to the decision to withdraw U.S. forces from Afghanistan

On 14 April 2021, President Joe Biden announced that [all U.S. troops in Afghanistan would be withdrawn from the country](66883576.md) by 11 September 2021, against the advice of many of his top generals and many diplomats.[^cite_note-238][^cite_note-239]

Petraeus has been an outspoken opponent of the U.S.'s strategy for withdrawal from Afghanistan, and the agreements made with the Taliban in preparation thereof. In a Fox News interview on 17 August 2021, Petraeus called former president Donald Trump's decision to release 5,000 detainees of the Taliban held by the Afghanistan government in 2020 flawed and disastrous, with a tragic outcome.[^cite_note-Fox_News-240] In another interview with the *Wall Street Journal* on 20 August 2021, he noted that the U.S. negotiated with the Taliban expecting to get something in return for withdrawing, which, he said, "didn't work out".[^cite_note-241]

During an interview with Fox News on 12 May 2021, Petraeus discussed his opposition to the withdrawal of U.S. forces in Afghanistan, stating, "I fear that we will come to regret this decision."[^cite_note-242] He warned of the possible psychological effects on Afghan forces following removal of U.S. support, the erosion of Afghan Air Force maintenance, and the risk of a "brutal civil war".[^cite_note-243][^cite_note-244]

Following [a military blitz by the Taliban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Taliban_offensive) that ended in the group [capturing the country's capital, Kabul](68481047.md), Petraeus again questioned the poor planning of troop withdrawal from the region and reiterated the dangers the world would face from the potential establishment of Al Qaeda or the Islamic State in the [Hindu Kush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_Kush). In an email interview with *[Die Weltwoche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Die_Weltwoche)*, Petraeus stated "I do not believe that either terrorist group will pose a near-term threat to the U.S. homeland or our NATO allies. The longer-term threat will depend on our ability to identify, disrupt, and degrade any such sanctuaries."[^cite_note-245]

Petraeus suggested that the removal of 18,000 contractors who maintained aircraft contributed to the surrender of Afghan government forces who ended up lacking air support against the Taliban.[^cite_note-Fox_News-240] In an interview with [Brian Kilmeade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Kilmeade) Petraeus drew parallels between the U.S. withdrawal from Afghanistan, the [Dunkirk evacuation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunkirk_evacuation), and the [Fall of Saigon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Saigon). He advised that American forces, drones, and close air support be used to secure Kabul Airport while the U.S. communicates with the Taliban to prevent them from impeding the movement of individuals that are trying to evacuate.[^cite_note-246]

During a discussion with CNN's Peter Bergen, Petraeus predicted that the Taliban would struggle to fund their new government following the withdrawal of U.S. and other donor nation financial support. He suggested that the resultant degradation of basic services and salaries for Afghan workers could ultimately force the Taliban to change their approach with the international community, but that it was too early to tell if the Taliban were reformed.[^cite_note-247]

Discussing the topic with NBC's Lester Holt, he expressed his belief that the Taliban's actions on the ground showed that they were "the same old Taliban", and that he was fairly confident that Al-Qaeda and the Islamic State would be able to re-establish sanctuaries in the region with the Taliban in power.[^cite_note-248] He expressed doubt that the U.S. would become involved in the country while the Taliban was in power, outside of military and intelligence actions to protect U.S. interests, and suggested that even the establishment of an embassy for diplomatic relations would not happen "any time soon".

### Views on the Russian invasion of Ukraine

Petraeus has been vocally critical of [Russia's invasion of Ukraine](79400268.md), joining three other retired military generals to form the Strategic Advisory Council for the Defense of Ukraine in July 2022 to provide strategic expertise and operational assistance to Ukrainian forces.[^cite_note-249]

Early in 2022, as tensions mounted between Russia and Ukraine, Petraeus attended a tabletop panel with Admiral [Michael Mullen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Mullen), [Michèle Flournoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mich%C3%A8le_Flournoy), and [Thomas Donilon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Donilon) to discuss economic sanctions, cyber defenses, and anti-Russian insurgency in Ukraine.[^cite_note-250]

In public appearances and interviews, Petraeus has criticized not only Russia's motives for the invasion, but also the tactics and standards of the country's military effort. In the weeks following the invasion of Ukraine, Petraeus spoke with [Jake Tapper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jake_Tapper) and [Anderson Cooper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anderson_Cooper), examining Russia's strategy in attempts to seize [Kyiv](585629.md).[^cite_note-251][^cite_note-252] On 12 March 2022, Petraeus spoke on One Nation with Brian Kilmeade, criticizing the Russian military's failed attempt to achieve a [combined arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combined_arms) effect, which, according to Petraeus, wasn't up to the normal standards anticipated by the United States or other NATO countries' militaries.[^cite_note-253] In May 2022, Petraeus commented that, in an attempt to create a stronger Russia, President Putin had instead strengthened the alliance between NATO countries.[^cite_note-254] In a later interview, Petraeus said that the Russian military was nowhere near as good as NATO had thought it would be.[^cite_note-255] He described the Russian military's preferred tactic as that of overwhelming artillery strikes, leading to the question of whether Russia's military valued volume over accuracy.[^cite_note-256] In October 2022, he called Russia's military mobilization in Ukraine "irreversible and disastrous".[^cite_note-257]

In addition to criticisms of Russia's war effort, Petraeus also discussed the negative implications that Russian sanctions have for worldwide financial markets, food supplies, and other commodities, and he commended the Ukrainian military for its impressive counter-attacks, specifically a raid on Russian tanks, the killing of five Russian generals, and the destruction of the Russian warship, Saratov.[^cite_note-258][^cite_note-259][^cite_note-260]

In mid- to late 2022, Petraeus spoke further on the Western response to Russia's aggression and the impacts U.S. involvement had on Ukraine's defense. In an interview with CNN's [Jim Sciutto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Sciutto) regarding Russia's plans to annex more regions in Ukraine, he said that US- and UK-supplied high-mobility artillery rocket systems had prompted Russian forces to relocate their headquarters and fuel supplies, which had significantly slowed Russia's advances in Ukraine.[^cite_note-261] In another interview, he said that Western support had improved counteroffensive strategies, resulting in retaking Crimea and Donbas territories from Russia.[^cite_note-262]

Petraeus has been a strong proponent of continued aid to Ukraine and increased NATO readiness for potential escalations by Russia and its allies.[^cite_note-263][^cite_note-264][^cite_note-265] He spoke of the need for the United States to prioritize military readiness to counter China and to increase aid for Ukraine in the annual defense policy bill.[^cite_note-266] He also discussed the importance of deploying Patriot missile systems in Ukraine as an anti-ballistic missile system and discussed measures that the US and NATO would employ if Russia were to carry out a nuclear attack in Ukraine.[^cite_note-267][^cite_note-268][^cite_note-269] In an Atlantic Council discussion concerning additional aid to Ukraine he emphasized the need for the deployment of Western aircraft and support staff, a stronger defense guarantee, and diplomatic and economic pressures on Russia to hasten peace talks.[^cite_note-270]

Petraeus expressed the belief that President Putin would eventually realize that the war is not sustainable on the battlefield and would agree to a negotiated resolution with Ukraine.[^cite_note-271][^cite_note-272] In an article published by *[Foreign Policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Policy)* magazine, Petraeus joined other experts to derive lessons from the Russian-Ukraine war that would prevent, deter, and combat forthcoming world conflicts.[^cite_note-273]

During a trip to Kyiv, Petraeus joined a group from the Atlantic Council in presenting the council's Global Citizen Award to Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, recognizing his exceptional leadership in defending Ukraine against Russian aggression.[^cite_note-274] While in Kyiv, Petraeus visited the American University of Kyiv, discussing his confidence in well-equipped Ukrainian troops and the challenges faced by Russian forces. He emphasized the effectiveness of Ukraine's newly formed brigades equipped with Western tanks and fighting vehicles, offering valuable insights and support to Ukraine's efforts in countering the Russian forces.[^cite_note-275][^cite_note-276]

In an interview with CNN, Petraeus shared his view that alleged Ukrainian [drone attacks](38212231.md) on Russian soil were a way to not only attack valid military targets but also to acquaint the Russian people with the fact that a war was happening. He also described a shift in the Ukrainian strategy in their summer offensive as "starve, stretch and strike," a tactic used to create greater opportunity for success later in the conflict.[^cite_note-277]

Speaking with CNN's Christiane Amanpour in response to the slow pace of Ukraine's counteroffensive in the summer of 2023, Petraeus noted that while the Russian military hadn't distinguished itself in many ways, the depth of their defenses in territory seized in Ukraine had proven formidable. In the same interview, he stated his opinion that Ukraine's forces had adapted well to Russian entrenchment, taking their time to pick through mine fields without the aid of air support. Petraeus once again argued for more military aid for Ukraine, and increased sanctions against Russia, in an effort to force a diplomatic resolution to the conflict.[^cite_note-278] In another conversation with Amanpour, he warned that if Putin succeeds in achieving his substantial objectives in Ukraine, then Moldova or one of the Baltic states could be invaded next. Petraeus expressed his strong agreement with Henry Kissinger's statement that "NATO's defense now begins at the border between Ukraine and Russia."[^cite_note-279]

### Views on Israel's 2023–present Invasion of Gaza

In June 2024, Petraeus co-wrote an opinion article with Meghan O'Sullivan and Richard Fontaine, comparing Israel's [invasion of Gaza](74998519.md) to American-led regime change in Iraq.[^cite_note-280] Petraeus describes the Israeli campaign as an "understandable response" to the 7 Oct. [Hamas-led attack on Israel](74998317.md), in contrast to the typical scholarly view and pro-Palestine criticism calling it a [genocide](75668427.md); he then proceeds to advise Israel in overcoming what he sees as America's past mistakes. Petraeus was accused by other commentators of "sanitizing history" on Iraq and puffing up his own legacy by writing this article.[^cite_note-281][^cite_note-282]

General Petraeus reviewed a book on the war in Gaza written from the Israeli military's perspective, which was published in July 2024. Petraeus praised what he considered "a gripping, exhaustively researched account of the brutal October 7th attack" presented in the book.[^cite_note-283]

## Personal life

According to Petraeus, he does not vote in elections, having stopped following his promotion to [Major General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_general_\(United_States\)) in 2002 as part of a desire to be seen as apolitical. He stated that he did not vote in the [2016 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_United_States_presidential_election).[^cite_note-284][^cite_note-285]

### Additional recognition

In 2005, Petraeus was identified as one of America's top leaders by *[U.S. News & World Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._News_&_World_Report)*.[^cite_note-usnews.com-354]

In 2007, *[Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_\(magazine\))* named Petraeus one of the 100 most influential leaders and revolutionaries of the year as well as one of its four runners up for Time Person of the Year.[^cite_note-time.com-355][^cite_note-Person_of_the_Year_2007-356] He was also named the second most influential American conservative by *[The Daily Telegraph](23797577.md)*[^cite_note-telegraph.co.uk-357] as well as *The Daily Telegraph*'s 2007 Man of the Year.[^cite_note-http-358][^cite_note-spiegel.de-359] His Ph.D. dissertation, "The American Military and the Lessons of Vietnam: A Study of Military Influence and the Use of Force in the Post-Vietnam Era", published by [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University) in 1987, was number two on the list of best-selling dissertations in 2007.[^cite_note-360]

In 2008, a poll conducted by *[Foreign Policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Policy_\(magazine\))* and *[Prospect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prospect_\(magazine\))* magazines selected Petraeus as one of the world's top 100 public intellectuals.[^cite_note-foreignpolicy.com-361] Also in 2008, the Static Line Association named Petraeus as its 2008 Airborne Man of the Year, and *[Der Spiegel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Der_Spiegel)* named him "America's most respected soldier".[^cite_note-red-devils.org-362] As 2008 came to a close, *Newsweek* named him the 16th most powerful person in the world in its 20 December 2008 edition,[^cite_note-newsweek.com-363] and *Prospect* magazine named him the "Public Intellectual of the Year".[^cite_note-prospect-magazine.co.uk-364] He was also named as one of the "75 Best People in the World" in the October 2009 issue of *[Esquire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esquire_magazine)*.[^cite_note-esquire.com-365]

On 7 March 2009, Petraeus received the Distinguished Citizen Award from the Congressional Medal of Honor Society at the [Ronald Reagan Presidential Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Reagan_Presidential_Library).[^cite_note-366][^cite_note-367]

Gen. David H. Petraeus, the top U.S. commander in the war against terrorism in the Middle East and Central Asia, was the spring commencement convocation speaker at Texas A\&M University on 13 May, a relatively new practice at Texas A & M.[^cite_note-368]

On 9 December 2010, [Barbara Walters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbara_Walters) picked Petraeus for the Most Fascinating Person of 2010. Walters called the top commander in Afghanistan "an American hero".[^cite_note-Barbara_Walters-369] Petraeus was chosen as "one of Time magazine's 50 "People Who Mattered" in December 2010.[^cite_note-370] The same year he was named number 12 of 50 people who mattered in 2010 by the *New Statesmen* magazine,[^cite_note-newstatesman.com-371] and Petraeus was listed as number 8 of 100 *[Foreign Policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Policy)* Top 100 Global Thinkers for 2011.[^cite_note-372]

The *[New Statesman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Statesman)* annual survey presents the most influential people from pop stars and dissident activists to tech gurus and heads of state, the people doing most to shape our world keep changing. On 26 September 2011, Petraeus was listed as number 2 of the 50 for 2011.[^cite_note-373] The Association of Special Operations Professionals named Petraeus as its 2011 Man of the Year for 2011, and was presented the award at Ft. Bragg on 2 November 2011, at its annual Special Operations Exposition.[^cite_note-374]

In January 2012, Petraeus was named one of "The 50 Most Powerful People in Washington" by *[GQ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GQ)* magazine.[^cite_note-375] Petraeus was inducted 29 January 2012, into the Reserve Officers Association's (ROA) Minuteman Hall of Fame as the 2011 Inductee during the 2012 ROA National Security Symposium.[^cite_note-roa-376] The German Order of Merit was presented to Petraeus on 14 February, by the German Secretary of Defense [Thomas de Maizière](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_de_Maizi%C3%A8re). According to de Maizière, he is an "outstanding strategist and a true friend of the German people".[^cite_note-377]

On 16 March 2012, the Dutch minister of defense Hans Hillen knighted Petraeus at the Hague with the [Knight Grand Cross of the Order of Orange-Nassau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Orange-Nassau) with swords. The Minister thanked Petraeus in his speech for his, "unconditional support to the Dutch troops and for being a driving force behind a successful mission. Through his personal efforts for cooperation between the Netherlands and America, the Netherlands could achieve significant operational successes with the Task Force Uruzgan."[^cite_note-378]

In 2012, Petraeus received the Golden Plate Award of the [American Academy of Achievement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Achievement).[^cite_note-379] The following year, in 2013, the [New-York Historical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New-York_Historical_Society) established the Petraeus-Hertog Lecture Leadership Library series.[^cite_note-380][^cite_note-381] Petraeus was featured alongside 18 other graduates of the [United States Military Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Military_Academy) in *West Point Leadership: Profiles of Courage*, a collection of leadership biographies published in 2013.[^cite_note-382]

Captured correspondence from [Osama bin Laden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osama_bin_Laden) "Letters from Abbottabad"[^cite_note-383] revealed that in May 2010, Bin Laden wanted to target President [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama) and General Petraeus. Bin Laden wrote: "The reason for concentrating on them is that Obama is the head of infidelity and killing him automatically will make Biden take over the presidency for the remainder of the term, as it is the norm over there. Biden is totally unprepared for that post, which will lead the U.S. into a crisis." It further went on to say, "As for Petraeus, he is the man of the hour in this last year of the war, and killing him would alter the war's path."[^cite_note-384]

Petraeus and Dr. [Henry Kissinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Kissinger) were the inaugural recipients of the Recanati-Kaplan Award for Civic Excellence and Cultural Engagement, presented by the [92nd Street Y](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/92nd_Street_Y) on 19 May 2019.[^cite_note-385][^cite_note-386] 92Y later established an Online Master Class in Leadership featuring a library series of lectures by Petraeus.[^cite_note-387][^cite_note-388]

On 19 November 2020, General Petraeus became the first American military professional to deliver the annual Lee Knowles Lecture at Cambridge University's Trinity College.[^cite_note-389][^cite_note-390]

On 7 December 2020, the [Institute for the Study of War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_the_Study_of_War) launched "The General David H. Petraeus Center for Emerging Leaders" to offer new educational and professional development programs to its students. Petraeus has been on the board of ISW since November 2013.[^cite_note-391][^cite_note-392]

In August 2026, Petraeus was named to Forbes's inaugural list of America's 250 most successful living veterans.[^cite_note-393]
