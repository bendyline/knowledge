A **hung parliament** is a term used mainly in the [United Kingdom](31717.md) and [Australia](4689264.md) to describe a situation in which no single [political party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_party) or pre-existing [coalition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coalition) has an [absolute majority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_majority) of [legislators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislator) (commonly known as [members](98066.md) or [representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representative_democracy)) in a [parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament) or other [legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislature). The term is most often applied to legislatures operating under the [Westminster system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_system) and typically employing [majoritarian electoral systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majoritarian_representation).

This situation is also known as a **balanced parliament**,[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-2] or—for [local government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_government_in_the_United_Kingdom) in the [United Kingdom](31717.md)—a parliament under [no overall control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_overall_control) (NOC).[^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5] A hung parliament may result in a [coalition government](6036.md), a [minority government](271434.md), or a [snap election](462915.md) if a government cannot be formed.

In [multi-party systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-party_system), particularly where [proportional representation](23298.md) is employed, it is rare for a single party to hold a majority of the seats, and likewise rare for one party to form government on its own (i.e. [coalition government](6036.md) is the norm). Consequently, the term is generally either unused in these systems, as a legislature without a single-party majority is the norm and thus every parliament is "hung", or reinterpreted to refer to the case of no coalition bloc holding a majority of the seats (however, this reinterpretation itself requires the existence of stable coalition blocs in the first place).

In the Westminster system, in the absence of a clear majority, no party or coalition has an automatic constitutional entitlement to [form government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_formation). This can result in the formation of a [coalition government](6036.md) of parties which can together command a majority, or the formation of a [minority government](271434.md), where the ruling party receives [confidence and supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence_and_supply) from smaller parties or independent legislators. If none of these solutions prove workable, the head of state may dissolve parliament (typically on the advice of the head of government), triggering a [snap election](462915.md).

In [Canada](5042916.md), the term is generally not used, as it is typical for the party that wins a plurality (but not a majority) of seats to form a [minority government](271434.md) on its own. These situations are typically called a "[minority government](271434.md)" or "minority parliament" by the Canadian media. The ruling party then seeks to work with other parties on a case-by-case basis.

## Overview

A normal objective of [parliamentary systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_system) – especially those requiring [responsible government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Responsible_government) such as the [Westminster system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_system) – is the formation of a stable government (i.e. ideally one that lasts a full parliamentary term, until the next election would normally be due). This requires a government to be able to muster sufficient votes in parliament to pass motions of [confidence and supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence_and_supply), especially [motions of no-confidence](208101.md) and [budget bills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budget_bill). If such motions fail, they normally result in the [dissolution of parliament](652293.md) and a fresh election. In some parliamentary systems, however, a new government may be formed without recourse to an election – if, for example, a minor party holds the [balance of power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balance_of_power_\(parliament\)), it may publicly express for the opposition, thereby creating a new majority.

The term "hung parliament" is most often used of parliaments dominated by [two major parties or coalitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-party_system). [General elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_election) in such systems usually result in one party having an absolute majority and thus quickly forming a new government. In most parliamentary systems, a hung parliament is considered exceptional and is often seen as undesirable. In other contexts, a hung parliament may be seen as ideal – for example, if opinions among the voting public are polarised regarding one or more issues, a hung parliament may lead to the emergence of a compromise or consensus.

If a legislature is [bicameral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicameral), the term "hung parliament" is usually used only with respect to the [lower house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_house).

In a [multi-party system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-party_system) with legislators elected by [proportional representation](23298.md) or a similar systems, it is usually exceptionally rare and difficult for any party to have an absolute majority. Thus, under such situations, every parliament is "hung" and coalition governments are normal. However, the term may be used to describe an election in which no established coalition wins an outright majority (such as the [German federal election of 2005](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_German_federal_election) or the [2018 Italian general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Italian_general_election)).

## History

The term apparently emerged in the United Kingdom, around the time of the 1974 election, by analogy with a [hung jury](621236.md), that is, one unable to reach a verdict. <https://wordhistories.net/2017/06/07/origin-of-hung-parliament/> However, whereas a hung jury results in a [mistrial](3276812.md), requiring a new trial, there is no general rule under which the absence of a clear majority requires a fresh election. In recent years, most "hung parliaments" have served their full term.

### Australia



The House of Representatives elected in 2010, with a 72–72 tie between the Labor Party and the Opposition Coalition.

Australian parliaments are modelled on the [Westminster system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_system), with a hung parliament typically defined as a lack of a lower house parliamentary majority from either the [Australian Labor Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Labor_Party) or [Liberal](18453.md)/[National](21927.md) [Coalition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coalition_\(Australia\)).

Hung parliaments are rare at the federal level in Australia, as a de facto [two-party system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-party_system), in which the Australian Labor Party competes against a permanent Liberal-National Coalition of the [conservative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative) parties, has existed with only brief interruptions since the early 20th century. Prior to 1909, no party had had a majority in the [House of Representatives](61565.md). As a result, there were frequent changes of government, several of which took place during parliamentary terms.

Since 1909, when the two-party system was cemented, hung parliaments have occurred as a result of the [1919](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1919_Australian_federal_election), [1922](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1922_Australian_federal_election), [1934](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1934_Australian_federal_election), [1940](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1940_Australian_federal_election), and [2010](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Australian_federal_election) elections, although that terminology was not in use until the late 20th century. Each of those elections resulted in the formation of [minority governments](271434.md), although the 1922 and 1934 elections resulted in the formation of coalition governments within a few months of the election (in 1922, before the new parliament had opened). After the 1940 election, there was an inter-election change of government as a result of independents switching support, resulting in the defeat of the [Fadden government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fadden_government) and the formation of the [Curtin government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtin_government).

Declining support for the major parties in recent times is leading to more non-majoritarian outcomes at elections.[^cite_note-pgia-6] At the [2010 federal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Australian_federal_election), which resulted in an exact 72–72 seat tie between Labor and the Liberal-National Coalition, incumbent Prime Minister [Julia Gillard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_Gillard) secured the support of four out of six Independent and Green Party crossbenchers and continued to govern until [2013](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2013_Australian_federal_election). In [the 2016 federal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Australian_federal_election) the Liberal-National Coalition won 76 seats, the bare minimum required to form a majority government. The Liberal-National Coalition government lost its majority government status after a [by-election in 2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Wentworth_by-election), but regained its majority in [2019](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Australian_federal_election).

Hung parliaments are rather more common at a state level. The [Tasmanian House of Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tasmanian_House_of_Assembly) and the unicameral [Australian Capital Territory Legislative Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Capital_Territory_Legislative_Assembly) are both elected by [Hare-Clark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hare-Clark) proportional representation, thus, elections commonly return hung parliaments. In other states and territories, candidates contest single-member seats. With far fewer seats than federal parliament, hung parliaments are more likely to be elected. Recent examples include [New South Wales in 1991](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1991_New_South_Wales_state_election) and [2023](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_New_South_Wales_state_election), Queensland in [1998](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1998_Queensland_state_election) and [2015](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_Queensland_state_election), [Victoria in 1999](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1999_Victorian_state_election), [South Australia in 1997](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997_South_Australian_state_election) and [2002](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2002_South_Australian_state_election), [Western Australia in 2008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Western_Australian_state_election), the [Australian Capital Territory in 2008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Australian_Capital_Territory_election) and [2012](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Australian_Capital_Territory_election) and [Tasmania in 2010](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Tasmanian_state_election).

In the lead up to 2025 election, polling results by the Australia Institute showed that more than twice as many Australians support a power-sharing arrangement in the next term of parliament as oppose one (41.7% vs 19.7%).[^cite_note-7] An analysis of 25 power-sharing parliaments in Australia shows crossbenchers negotiate a wide range of concessions for confidence and supply. Negotiations include parliamentary and policy reforms, extra staff and resources, and presiding officer  positions for crossbenchers.[^cite_note-8] At the [2025 Australian federal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Australian_federal_election), the incumbent Labor government was returned in 94 of 150 seat landslide victory.

### Canada

Further information: [Federal minority governments in Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_minority_governments_in_Canada)

In [Canada](5042916.md), the term is generally not used, as it is typical for the party that wins a plurality (but not a majority) of seats to form a [minority government](271434.md) on its own. These situations are typically called a "[minority government](271434.md)" or "minority parliament" by the media. The ruling party then seeks to work with other parties on a case-by-case basis. The average lifespan of a minority government in Canada is two years.



The [45th Canadian Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/45th_Canadian_Parliament) elected in the [2025 Canadian federal election](68483222.md) resulted in Canada's most recent (and formerly) minority parliament.

Minority parliaments at either the federal and provincial level are an infrequent but not unusual occurrence in Canada.[^cite_note-9] Six of the previous eight recent federal elections have resulted in hung parliaments ([the 38th](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/38th_Canadian_Parliament), [the 39th](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/39th_Canadian_Parliament), [the 40th](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/40th_Canadian_Parliament), [the 43rd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/43rd_Canadian_Parliament), [the 44th](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/44th_Canadian_Parliament), and [the 45th](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/45th_Canadian_Parliament)). Following all six elections the largest party ruled as a "[minority government](271434.md)". Although Canadian minority governments have tended to be short-lived, the two successive minorities under Prime Minister [Stephen Harper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Harper) managed to hold on to power from February 2006 until a [no confidence vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_confidence_vote) in March 2011. The subsequent election saw a majority parliament elected with Harper's [Conservative Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Party_of_Canada) obtaining a 24-seat majority.

While most Canadian minority governments end in dissolution via non-confidence or a snap election call, there have been a few attempts to transition to a new government without returning to the ballot box. Most notably, the [2008 Canadian Federal Election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Canadian_Federal_Election) resulted in the [2008–09 Canadian parliamentary dispute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008%E2%80%9309_Canadian_parliamentary_dispute). While the Conservative Party had a plurality of seats, the [Liberal Party](149536.md) and [New Democratic Party (NDP)](19283982.md), supported by The [Bloc Québécois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloc_Qu%C3%A9b%C3%A9cois), agreed to defeat the Conservatives in favour of a Liberal/NDP [coalition government](6036.md). On 4 December 2008, [Governor General](38152.md) [Michaëlle Jean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micha%C3%ABlle_Jean) granted [Prime Minister](24135.md) [Stephen Harper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Harper)'s request for a [prorogation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prorogation_in_Canada) of Parliament on the condition that parliament reconvene early in the new year. The first session of the 40th parliament thus ended, delaying and ultimately avoiding a vote of non-confidence.[^cite_note-10]

At the territorial level, a unique situation happened in the [2021 Yukon general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Yukon_general_election), in which the electoral district of [Vuntut Gwitchin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vuntut_Gwitchin_\(electoral_district\)) resulted in a tie. A judicial recount was held and the tie remained. A draw was held between the two candidates which ultimately named [NDP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yukon_New_Democratic_Party) challenger [Annie Blake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annie_Blake) the winner against incumbent [Liberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yukon_Liberal_Party) cabinet minister and MLA [Pauline Frost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauline_Frost). This victory ultimately resulted in a hung parliament in the [Yukon legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yukon_legislature) with the [NDP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yukon_New_Democratic_Party) holding the balance of power.

### Fiji

### France

Since the establishment of the [two-round system](25932.md) for parliamentary elections in 1958, hung parliaments are unusual under the [Fifth Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Fifth_Republic). Still, 2 general elections out of 16 resulted in such a parliamentary configuration since 1958:

- [June 1988 parliamentary elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_French_legislative_election): after being reelected as President of France in May 1988, [François Mitterrand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_Mitterrand) dissolved the [National Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_National_Assembly) and called for a snap legislative election on 5 and 12 June 1988 to regain the majority he lost to a centre-right to right-wing coalition in 1986. The snap election resulted in France's first hung parliament since the fall of the [Fourth Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Fourth_Republic) in 1958, with Mitterrand's [PS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_Party_\(France\)) as the largest party (275 seats) but 14 short of an overall majority. The [Communists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Communist_Party) and the independent Centrists ended up as potential kingmakers in the newly elected Assembly.



9th National Assembly of France, elected in 1988, was France's first hung parliament since 1958.

- [June 2022 parliamentary elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_French_legislative_election): less than two months after being reelected as president, [Emmanuel Macron](43671127.md) and his government, now led by Prime Minister [Élisabeth Borne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89lisabeth_Borne), fought the 2022 legislative election taking place on the 12 and 19 June. In the second round, Macron's [centrist coalition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ensemble_Citoyens) unexpectedly lost its majority in the National Assembly and was reduced to being the largest bloc (with 251 seats) in a hung parliament. Going into the election with a 115-seat majority, Macron's government now fell 38 short of an absolute majority in the [lower house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_house) of Parliament, the widest margin for any French cabinet since 1958. While both the left-wing [NUPES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NUPES) and the far-right [RN](67119.md) achieved significant gains, the centre-right to right-wing [Republicans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Republicans_\(France\)) were left holding the [balance of power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balance_of_power_\(parliament\)) in this hung parliament, despite suffering seizable losses.



16th National Assembly of France, elected in 2022, is France's second hung parliament since 1958.

- [June–July 2024 parliamentary elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_French_legislative_election): on the evening of the [2024 European parliament election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_European_Parliament_election_in_France), with the preliminary results showing that the National Rally had significantly outperformed his coalition, [Emmanuel Macron](43671127.md) dissolved parliament and called an election on 30 June and 7 July 2024. The snap election resulted in the left-wing [NUPES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NUPES) alliance, [Macron's centrist coalition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ensemble_\(political_coalition\)), and the National Rally each receiving similar numbers of seats, with NUPES in the lead and the centre-right-to-right-wing Republicans no longer holding enough seats to hold the balance of power. This situation led to a [prolonged political crisis](77741666.md) that is still ongoing, with several [prime ministers](218751.md) being appointed and forced to resign in rapid succession: the [Republican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Republicans_\(France\)) [Michel Barnier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Barnier) (who led the government from 5 September [until 4 December 2024](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collapse_of_the_Barnier_government)); the [Democrat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Movement_\(France\)) [François Bayrou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_Bayrou) (who led the government [from 13 December 2024](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayrou_government) until 9 September 2025); and [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_\(French_political_party\)) politician [Sébastien Lecornu](54364868.md) (who led the government [from 9 September](81029757.md) until his resignation on October 6, a period of just 26 days; Lecornu remains prime minister in a [caretaker capacity](240683.md)).



The 17th National Assembly of France, the incumbent national assembly, is a hung parliament.

### Ireland

Because Ireland uses [PR-STV](23298.md#Single_transferable_vote), it is rare for any one party to have a majority on its own. The last such occasion was in 1977. However, one or other coalitions are known to be possible before and during the election. Therefore, a "hung Dáil" (Dáil Éireann being the lower and most dominant chamber of the Oireachtas/Parliament) in Ireland refers more to the inability of a coalition of parties who traditionally enter government together or would be expected to govern together, from doing so.

The President has no direct role in the formation of governments in the case of a hung parliament. However, he retains the power to convene a meeting of either or both the Dáil and Senate which could become important if there was a government trying to use parliamentary recess to prevent confidence votes and hold onto power. The President may also refuse to dissolve Dáil Eireann and call an election if the Taoiseach loses a vote of confidence, instead giving the other parties a chance to see if they can put together a government without proceeding to another election.

In 2016, Fine Gael and Labour, who had been in government the previous five years, were unable, due to Labour's collapse, to enter government again. Fianna Fáil had enough seats to put together a rainbow government with the other centre-left, hard left parties and independents but negotiations broke down. Fianna Fáil had also promised not to enter coalition with Sinn Féin.

The press began to speculate about a Germany style "Grand Coalition" similar to the Christian Democrats and Social Democrats there. Many members of FF considered FG too [right wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_wing) to enter coalition with and threatened to leave the party this came to pass. As talks continued on without a new government (the old government, constitutionally, which had just been voted out, remaining in power including ministers who had lost their seats) FF agreed to allow a government to form by abstention. The parliamentary arithmetic fell in such a way that if FF TD's abstained on confidence and supply matters, a FG minority government could, with the support of a group of independents, form a new government. This was agreed in exchange for a number of policy concessions. Once the deal with FF was signed, Taoiseach Enda Kenny conducted talks with the independents and entered government for a second term.

### New Zealand

See also: [Electoral reform in New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_reform_in_New_Zealand)

Hung parliaments were relatively uncommon in New Zealand prior to the introduction of proportional representation in 1993. On only four occasions since the beginnings of party politics in [1890](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1890_New_Zealand_general_election) had a hung parliament occurred under the [first-past-the-post](261709.md) system: in [1911](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1911_New_Zealand_general_election), [1922](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1922_New_Zealand_general_election), [1928](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1928_New_Zealand_general_election) and [1931](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1931_New_Zealand_general_election). The rarity between 1936 and 1996 was due to the regression into a two-party system, alternating between the long dominating [Labour Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Labour_Party) and [National Party](333149.md).[^cite_note-26] From the first [MMP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed-member_proportional_representation) election in [1996](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_New_Zealand_general_election) until the [2020 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_New_Zealand_general_election) no single party gained an outright majority in parliament. The 2020 election was the first to return a majority (for the Labour Party) since 1993.[^cite_note-27] Under the MMP system, the term "hung parliament" has been reappropriated to refer to the case of no [bloc of parties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_bloc) being able to secure a majority of seats.[^cite_note-28]

### United Kingdom

Further information: [Parliament of the United Kingdom](13964.md)



The House of Commons following the 2017 general election. No party had a majority.

In the [United Kingdom](31717.md), before World War I, a largely stable two-party system existed for generations; traditionally, only the [Tories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tories) and [Whigs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whigs_\(British_political_party\)), or from the mid-19th century the [Conservative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Party_\(UK\)) and [Liberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Party_\(UK\)) parties, managed to deliver Members of Parliament in significant numbers. Hung parliaments were thus rare, especially during the 19th century. The possibility of change arose when, in the aftermath of the [Act of Union, 1800](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Act_of_Union,_1800), a number of Irish MPs took seats in the House, though initially [these](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Ireland_1801%E2%80%931923#Home_Rule_movement_\(1870%E2%80%931914\)) followed the traditional alignments. However, two Reform Acts ([in 1867](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reform_Act_1867) and [in 1884](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representation_of_the_People_Act_1884)) significantly extended the franchise and redrew the constituencies, and coincided with [a change in Irish politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_emancipation). Following the [1885 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1885_United_Kingdom_general_election), neither party had an overall majority. The [Irish Parliamentary Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Parliamentary_Party) held the balance of power and made [Irish Home Rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Home_Rule) a condition of their support. However, the Liberal Party split on the issue of Irish Home Rule, leading to [another general election in 1886](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1886_United_Kingdom_general_election), in which the Conservatives won the most seats and governed with the support of the fragment of Liberalism opposed to Home Rule, the [Liberal Unionist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Unionist_Party).

Both the [election of January 1910](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/January_1910_United_Kingdom_general_election), and that of [December 1910](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/December_1910_United_Kingdom_general_election) produced a hung parliament with an almost identical number of seats won by the governing Liberal Party and the Conservative Party. This was due both to the constitutional crisis and to the rise of the [Labour Party](19279158.md). The [elections of 1929](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1929_United_Kingdom_general_election) resulted in the last hung parliament for many years; in the meantime, Labour had replaced the Liberals as one of the two dominating parties.

Since the elections of 1929, three general elections have resulted in hung parliaments in the UK. The first was the [election in February 1974](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/February_1974_United_Kingdom_general_election), and the ensuing parliament lasted only until [October](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/October_1974_United_Kingdom_general_election). The second was the [May 2010 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_United_Kingdom_general_election), the result of which was a hung parliament with the Conservative party as the largest single party. The results for the three main parties were: Conservatives 306, Labour 258, Liberal Democrats 57.[^cite_note-29] The third one resulted from the [snap election](462915.md) held in [June 2017](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_United_Kingdom_general_election) that had been called for by [Theresa May](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theresa_May) in order to strengthen her majority heading into [Brexit negotiations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brexit_negotiations) later in 2017. However, this election backfired on May and her [Conservative Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Party_\(UK\)), resulting in a hung parliament after the snap election.[^cite_note-30]

The formation of the coalition resulting from the 2010 election led to the [Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed-term_Parliaments_Act_2011), which instituted fixed five-year Parliaments and transferred the power to call [early elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_election) from the Monarch on the advice of Prime Minister to Parliament itself. This was the idea of the [Deputy Prime Minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deputy_Prime_Minister) [Nick Clegg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_Clegg), then the leader of the [Liberal Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Democrats_\(UK\)), who said that this would stop the Prime Minister and leader of the Conservative Party, [David Cameron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Cameron), from calling a snap election to end the hung parliament, as many other Conservatives had requested. This act was revoked in 2022 through the [Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_and_Calling_of_Parliament_Act_2022) to return the powers of dissolution to the Monarch.

Hung parliaments can also arise when slim government majorities are eroded by [by-election](155434.md) defeats and [defection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crossing_the_floor) of [Members of Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Members_of_Parliament) to opposition parties, as well as [resignations of MPs](192296.md) from the [House of Commons](13828.md). This happened in December 1996 to the Conservative government of [John Major](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Major) (1990–97) and in mid-1978 to the Labour government of [James Callaghan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Callaghan) (1976–79); this latter period covers the era known as the [Winter of Discontent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_of_Discontent). The minority government of Jim Callaghan came when Labour ended their 15-month [Lib–Lab pact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lib%E2%80%93Lab_pact) with the Liberals, having lost their majority in early 1977.

According to researchers Andrew Blick and Stuart Wilks-Heeg, the phrase "hung parliament" did not enter into common usage in the UK until the mid-1970s. It was first used in the press by journalist [Simon Hoggart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Hoggart) in *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)* in 1974.[^cite_note-31]

Academic treatments of hung parliaments include [David Butler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Butler_\(psephologist\))'s *Governing Without a Majority: Dilemmas for Hung Parliaments in Britain* (Sheridan House, 1986) and [Vernon Bogdanor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vernon_Bogdanor)'s 'Multi-Party Politics and the Constitution' (Cambridge University Press, 1983).

## Consequences

In countries where parliaments under majority control are the norm, a hung parliament is often viewed as an unusual and undesirable election result, leading to relatively weak and unstable government. A period of uncertainty after the election is common, as major party leaders negotiate with independents and minor parties to establish a working majority.

An aspiring [head of government](37570.md) may seek to build a [coalition government](6036.md); in [Westminster systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_system), this typically involves agreement on a joint legislative programme and a number of [ministerial](739237.md) posts going to the minor coalition partners, in return for a stable majority. Alternatively, a minority government may be formed, establishing [confidence and supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence_and_supply) agreements in return for policy concessions agreed in advance, or relying on case by case support.

### Australia

In the [Western Australian state election of 2008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Western_Australian_state_election) the [Australian Labor Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Labor_Party) won more seats than the [Liberal Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Party_of_Australia_\(Western_Australian_Division\)) at 28 to 24. The [National Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Party_of_Australia_\(WA\)) along with three independents had the seats needed to give either party a majority. To help the Liberal Party form government, the Nationals supported the party on the condition that the [Royalties for Regions policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royalties_for_Regions_policy) was implemented.

In the [1999 Victorian state election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1999_Victorian_state_election), the Labor Party won 42 seats, while the incumbent Liberal National Coalition retained 43, with three seats falling to independents. The Labor Party formed a minority government with the three independents.

The [2010 Tasmanian state election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Tasmanian_state_election) resulted in a hung parliament. After a period of negotiation, the incumbent Labor government led by [David Bartlett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bartlett) was recommissioned, but containing the Leader of the Tasmanian Greens, [Nick McKim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_McKim), as a minister, and the Greens' [Cassy O'Connor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassy_O'Connor) as Cabinet Secretary.

In the [2010 federal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Australian_federal_election), neither Labor nor the Liberal coalition secured the majority of seats required to form a Government in their own right. In order to counter the potential instability of minority government involved groups may negotiate written agreements defining their terms of support. Such measures were undertaken by the [Gillard Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gillard_government) in 2010.[^cite_note-gpia-32]

### France

In the [1988 French legislative elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_French_legislative_elections), a hung parliament occurred with the [Socialists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_Party_\(France\)) as the largest party. Following talks with parliamentary leaders, Prime Minister [Michel Rocard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Rocard) formed a new minority government, incorporating centrist ministers in a sort of unofficial coalition with the pivotal independent centrist group in the Assembly, ensuring a somewhat stable government until 1991. His direct successors, Prime Ministers [Édith Cresson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89dith_Cresson) and [Pierre Bérégovoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_B%C3%A9r%C3%A9govoy), both formed minority governments, relying alternately on the Communists' or the Centrists' support in Parliament (depending on the issue).

In the [2022 French legislative elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_French_legislative_elections), a hung parliament occurred again with President Macron's [Ensemble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ensemble_Citoyens) coalition as the largest bloc in the National Assembly. Both the President and the Prime Minister held talks with opposition leaders in order to try forming a coalition government with the centre-right (LR) and the centre-left (PS and the Greens), or at least reaching some sort of confidence-and-supply deal with them. Talks rapidly failed since no opposition party showed interest in propelling Macron's administration. In July 2022, Prime Minister [Borne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89lisabeth_Borne) reshuffled her Cabinet and officially formed a [minority government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borne_government). As of June 2023, it is still the current government of France.

### India

In [India](14533.md), if an election results in a 'hung assembly' in one of the state [Legislative Assemblies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vidhan_Sabha) and no party is capable of gaining confidence, then fresh elections are announced to be held as soon as possible. Until this occurs [President's Rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President's_Rule) is applied. In [India](14533.md) there have been many situations of hung assemblies in the state legislatures. However, invariably, the President of India in the case of Lok Sabha elections and the Governor of the state concerned, in the case of state elections, would attempt to give opportunities to the parties, starting with the one that got the maximum number of seats in the elections, to explore possibilities of forming a coalition government, before bringing in President's Rule.

### New Zealand

The first such occasion was in [1911](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1911_New_Zealand_general_election) when the [Liberal Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Liberal_Party) won fewer seats than the opposition [Reform Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Reform_Party) despite tallying the most votes. A vote of no confidence was placed by Reform and the Liberals survived by just one vote. This prompted Prime Minister Sir [Joseph Ward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Ward) to resign, his replacement [Thomas Mackenzie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Mackenzie) was later defeated in July 1912 in a vote with several MPs and Labour crossing the floor to vote with the opposition, the last time in New Zealand history a government has changed on a confidence vote. This broke 23 years of Liberal governance and [William Massey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Massey) formed a new Reform Party government. Massey governed through to his death in 1925, though in [1922](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1922_New_Zealand_general_election) the Reform Party suffered major losses and Massey was forced negotiate with several Independent MPs to retain power.

In [1928](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1928_New_Zealand_general_election), Reform were ousted from governance and Joseph Ward once again won back power. However, the Reform and United (Liberal) parties were tied on seats with Labour holding the balance of power. Labour chose to back Ward rather than let Reform leader [Gordon Coates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Coates) remain in office. In the next election in [1931](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1931_New_Zealand_general_election), there was again a three-way deadlock. On this occasion the Reform and United parties became a coalition government out of mutual fear of Labour's ever-increasing appeal as the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression) worsened.

[1993](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1993_New_Zealand_general_election) was the last time a hung parliament occurred in New Zealand. Governor-General Dame [Catherine Tizard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catherine_Tizard) asked Sir [David Beattie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Beattie) to form a committee, along with three retired appeal court judges, to decide whom to appoint as Prime Minister.[^cite_note-33] However, National won an extra seat after special votes were counted, giving National 50 seats and Labour 45 seats (4 were won by third-party candidates). Labour's Sir [Peter Tapsell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Tapsell_\(New_Zealand_politician\)) agreed to become [Speaker of the New Zealand House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speaker_of_the_New_Zealand_House_of_Representatives). As a result, National did not lose a vote in the house and maintained a dubious majority for three years.

### United Kingdom

In the [February 1974 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/February_1974_United_Kingdom_general_election), no party gained an overall parliamentary majority. Labour won the most seats (301, which was 17 seats short of an overall majority) with the Conservatives on 297 seats, although the Conservatives had a larger share of the popular vote. As the incumbent [Prime Minister](24150.md), [Edward Heath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Heath) remained in office attempting to build a coalition with the [Liberals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Party_\(UK\)). When these negotiations were unsuccessful Heath resigned and Labour led by [Harold Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Wilson) took over in a minority government.

In the [2010 UK general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_UK_general_election), another hung parliament occurred with the [Conservatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Party_\(UK\)) as the largest party, and discussions followed to help create a stable government. This resulted in agreement on a coalition government, which was also a [majority government](465640.md), between the [Conservative Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Party_\(UK\)), which won the most votes and seats in the election, and the [Liberal Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Democrats_\(UK\)).

In the [2017 UK general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_UK_general_election), a hung parliament occurred for the second time in seven years with the [Conservatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Party_\(UK\)) again being the largest party. The Conservatives led by [Theresa May](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theresa_May) formed a minority government, supported by a [confidence-and-supply agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative%E2%80%93DUP_agreement) with the [Northern Ireland](21265.md)'s [Democratic Unionist Party](221428.md).

## Working majority

There have been occasions when, although a parliament or assembly is technically hung, the party in power has a [working majority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Working_majority). For example, in the United Kingdom, the tradition is that the [Speaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speaker_\(politics\)) and Deputy Speakers do not vote and [Sinn Féin](28175.md) MPs never take their seats per their policy of [abstentionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstentionism#In_Northern_Ireland), so these members can be discounted from the opposition numbers.

### United Kingdom

In 2005, this was the case in the 60-seat [National Assembly for Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Assembly_for_Wales), where Labour lost their majority when [Peter Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Law) was expelled for standing against the official candidate in the [2005 Westminster election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_United_Kingdom_general_election) in the [Blaenau Gwent constituency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blaenau_Gwent_\(UK_Parliament_constituency\)). When the Assembly was [first elected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_National_Assembly_for_Wales_election) on 1 May 2003, [Labour](6732798.md) won 30 seats, [Plaid Cymru](53343.md) won 12, the [Conservatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Conservatives) won 11, [Liberal Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Liberal_Democrats) won 6, and the [John Marek Independent Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Marek_Independent_Party) won a seat.

When [Dafydd Elis-Thomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dafydd_Elis-Thomas) (Plaid Cymru) was reelected as the presiding officer, this reduced the number of opposition AMs who could vote to 29, as the presiding officer votes only in the event of a tie and, even then, not on party political lines but according to [Speaker Denison's rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speaker_Denison's_rule). Thus, Labour had a working majority of one seat until Law ran in Blaenau Gwent.[^cite_note-34]
