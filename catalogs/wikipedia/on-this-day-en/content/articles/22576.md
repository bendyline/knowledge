The modern **Olympic Games** (**Olympics**; [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language): *Jeux olympiques*)[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-2] are a series of international [multi-sport events](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-sport_event). Considered among the biggest sporting events in the world, the Olympics are organised into distinct [Summer](27566.md) and [Winter Games](34069.md), each featuring different sports. The Olympic Games, [open](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_\(sport\)) to both amateur and professional athletes, involve thousands of athletes and [more than 200 teams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_National_Olympic_Committees), each team representing a [sovereign state](23604120.md) or territory. The Games often, but not always, substitute for any [world championships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_championship) during the year in which they take place.[^cite_note-EB-3] The Summer and Winter Olympics are each held every four years, and themselves occur two years apart within the four-year [Olympiad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympiad). This arrangement has been in place since the [1994 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1994_Winter_Olympics), prior to which the Summer and Winter Games were held in the same year.[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5]

Their creation was inspired by the [ancient Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Olympic_Games), held in [Olympia, Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympia,_Greece), from the 8th century BC to the 4th century AD. [Baron Pierre de Coubertin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baron_Pierre_de_Coubertin) founded the [International Olympic Committee](15147.md) (IOC) in 1894, leading to the first modern Games in [Athens](1216.md) in [1896](48644.md). The IOC is the governing body of the [Olympic Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Movement), which encompasses all [entities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entity) and individuals involved in the Olympic Games. The [Olympic Charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Charter) defines their structure and authority.

The evolution of the Olympic Movement during the 20th and 21st centuries has resulted in numerous changes to the Olympic Games. Some of these adjustments include the creation of the Winter Olympic Games for snow and ice sports, the [Paralympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paralympic_Games) for athletes with disabilities, the [Youth Olympic Games](11315968.md) for athletes aged 14 to 18, the five Continental Games ([Pan American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_American_Games), [African](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Games), [Asian](39205.md), [European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Games), and [Pacific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Games)), and the [World Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Games) for sports that are not contested in the Olympic Games. The IOC also endorses the [Deaflympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deaflympics) and the [Special Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Olympics). The IOC has adapted to a variety of economic, political, and technological advancements. The [abuse of amateur rules](#Amateurism_and_professionalism) prompted the IOC to shift away from pure [amateurism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amateur_sports), as envisioned by Coubertin, to the acceptance of [professional athletes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professional_athletes) participating at the Games. The growing importance of mass media has created the issue of [corporate sponsorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_sponsorship) and general commercialisation of the Games. World Wars [I](4764461.md) and [II](32927.md) led to the cancellation of the [1916](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1916_Summer_Olympics), [1940](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1940_Summer_Olympics), and [1944](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1944_Summer_Olympics) Olympics; large-scale boycotts during the [Cold War](325329.md) limited participation in the [1980](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980_Summer_Olympics) and [1984](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1984_Summer_Olympics) Olympics;[^cite_note-olympic.org-6] and the [2020](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Summer_Olympics) Olympics were postponed until 2021 because of the [COVID-19 restrictions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_restrictions).

The Olympic Movement consists of [international sports federations](398336.md) (IFs), [National Olympic Committees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Olympic_Committee) (NOCs), and organising committees for each specific Olympic Games. As the decision-making body, the IOC is responsible for choosing the host city for each Games, and organises and funds the Games according to the [Olympic Charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Charter). The IOC also determines the Olympic programme, consisting of the sports to be contested at the Games. There are several Olympic rituals and symbols, such as the [Olympic flag](731192.md), the [Olympic flame](74217.md) and [torch relay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Olympic_torch_relays), and the [opening and closing ceremonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Games_ceremony). Over 14,000 athletes competed at the [2020 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Summer_Olympics) and [2022 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Winter_Olympics) combined, in 40 different sports and 448 events. The first-, second-, and third-place finishers in each event receive [Olympic medals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_medal): gold, silver, and bronze, respectively.[^cite_note-7]

Since the [1996 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_Summer_Olympics),[^cite_note-8] The Games have grown to the point that nearly every nation is now represented; colonies and overseas territories are often allowed to field their own teams. This growth has created numerous challenges and [controversies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Olympic_Games_scandals,_controversies_and_incidents), including [boycotts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Olympic_Games_boycotts), [doping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doping_at_the_Olympic_Games), [match fixing](678924.md), bribery, and terrorism. Every two years, the Olympics and its media exposure provide athletes with the chance to attain national and international fame. The Games also provide an opportunity for the host city and country to showcase themselves to the world.

The Olympic Games have become a significant global event, fostering international cooperation and cultural exchange.[^cite_note-9] At the same time, hosting the Olympic Games can also bring significant economic benefits and challenges to the host city, affecting infrastructure, tourism and local communities.[^cite_note-10]

## Ancient Olympics

Main article: [Ancient Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Olympic_Games)



The [Ancient Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Olympic_Games) stadium in [Olympia, Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympia,_Greece)

The "Ancient Olympic Games" ([Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language): τὰ Ὀλύμπια, *ta Olympia*)[^cite_note-perseus.tuft.edu-11] were religious and athletic festivals held every four years at the sanctuary of [Zeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeus) in [Olympia, Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympia,_Greece). The date of the festival was determined according to a complicated formula whereby the midpoint of the festival would occur during the second full moon after the [summer solstice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summer_solstice)—usually late August or early September.[^cite_note-FinleyPleket-12]<sup>: 26 </sup>[^cite_note-Perrottet-13]<sup>: 38 </sup> This "came after the annual harvest but before the picking of the olives."[^cite_note-Perrottet-13] Heralds were sent from Elis to announce the dates.[^cite_note-FinleyPleket-12]<sup>: 26 </sup>[^cite_note-Perrottet-13]<sup>: 38 </sup> Competition at first was among only "legitimate sons of free-born Greek parents."[^cite_note-Perrottet-13]<sup>: 49 </sup> However, after Macedonia, and then Rome, conquered Greece, the ten Olympic judges loosened the earlier standard and permitted anyone who spoke Greek to participate.[^cite_note-Perrottet-13]<sup>: 50 </sup> Tens of thousands of Greeks would make the difficult journey to attend the festival.[^cite_note-FinleyPleket-12]<sup>: 55 </sup> Some sources say as many as 40,000 attended.[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15] These Games featured mainly athletic but also combat sports such as wrestling and the [pankration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pankration), horse and chariot racing events. It has been widely written that during the Games, all conflicts among the participating city-states were postponed until the Games were finished. This cessation of hostilities was known as the Olympic peace or truce.[^cite_note-Swaddling_2000_p54-16] This idea is a modern myth because the Greeks never suspended their wars. The truce only allowed those religious pilgrims who were travelling to [Olympia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympia,_Greece) to pass through warring territories unmolested because they were protected by Zeus.[^cite_note-FinleyPleket-12]<sup>: 98–99 </sup>[^cite_note-17]

The origin of the Olympics is shrouded in mystery and legend;[^cite_note-Young2004-18]<sup>: 12 </sup> one of the most popular myths identifies [Heracles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heracles) and his father [Zeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeus) as the progenitors of the Games.[^cite_note-Young2004-18]<sup>: 12–13 </sup>[^cite_note-19][^cite_note-Pindar-20][^cite_note-FOOTNOTERichardson1992227-21] According to legend, it was Heracles who first called the Games "Olympic" and established the custom of holding them every four years.[^cite_note-Pindar-20] The myth continues that after Heracles completed his [twelve labours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labours_of_Hercules), he built the [Olympic Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Stadium) as an honour to Zeus. Following its completion, he walked in a straight line for 200 steps and called this distance a "[stadion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stadion_\(unit\))" (Ancient Greek: στάδιον, [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin): *stadium*, "stage"), which later became a unit of distance. The most widely accepted inception date for the Ancient Olympics is 776 BC; this is based on inscriptions, found at [Olympia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympia,_Greece), listing the winners of a footrace held every four years starting in 776 BC.[^cite_note-EB-3] For the first thirteen Olympics, the *stadion* footrace was the only event contested,[^cite_note-Perrottet-13]<sup>: 145 </sup>[^cite_note-22] and victory in that sprint was so valued that the next [Olympiad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympiad) was named after the winner, e.g. "the third year of the eighteenth Olympiad when Ladas of Argos won the stadion."[^cite_note-Perrottet-13]<sup>: 145 </sup> The Ancient Games varied over time, but they came to feature running events, a pentathlon (consisting of a jumping event, [discus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discus_throw) and [javelin throws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Javelin_throw), a foot race, and wrestling), boxing, wrestling, pankration, and [equestrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equestrianism) events.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECrowther200759–61-23][^cite_note-24] Tradition has it that [Coroebus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coroebus_of_Elis), a cook from the city of [Elis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elis_\(city\)), was the first Olympic champion,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGolden200924-25] which indicates that the competition was not limited to the aristocracy.[^cite_note-Perrottet-13]<sup>: 50–51 </sup>

The Olympics were of fundamental religious importance, and the sporting events, which were held alongside ritual sacrifices honouring both Zeus (whose [famous statue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statue_of_Zeus_at_Olympia) by [Phidias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phidias) stood in his temple at [Olympia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympia,_Greece)) and [Pelops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelops) (divine hero and mythical king of Olympia), did not start until the festival's second day.[^cite_note-FinleyPleket-12]<sup>: 15 </sup> (Pelops was famous for his chariot race with King [Oenomaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oenomaus) of [Pisatis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisatis).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBurkert198395-26]) The winners of the events were admired and immortalised in poems and statues.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESwaddling199990–93-27] Although a wreath made from the sacred olive tree in the precinct of Zeus was the only official prize at the Olympic Games, winners' fame brought them economic wealth, too, in the form of subsidies from their hometowns and wealthy sponsors. See, e.g., Finley and Pleket,[^cite_note-FinleyPleket-12]<sup>: 24, 76–82 </sup> and Perrottet[^cite_note-Perrottet-13]<sup>: 13–14 </sup> The Games were held every four years, and this period, known as an [Olympiad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympiad), was used by Greeks as one of their units of time measurement. The Games were part of a cycle known as the [Panhellenic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panhellenic_Games), which included the [Pythian Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythian_Games), the [Nemean Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nemean_Games), and the [Isthmian Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isthmian_Games).[^cite_note-28]

The Olympic Games reached the height of their success in the 6th and 5th centuries BC, but then gradually declined in importance as the [Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome) gained power and influence in Greece. While there is no scholarly consensus as to when the Games officially ended, the most commonly held date is 393 AD, when the emperor [Theodosius I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodosius_I) decreed that all pagan cults and practices be eliminated.[^cite_note-30] Another date commonly cited is 426 AD, when his successor, [Theodosius II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodosius_II), ordered the destruction of all Greek temples.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECrowther200754-29]

### Forerunners



[Pierre de Coubertin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_de_Coubertin), co-founder of the [International Olympic Committee](15147.md) and its second president

Various uses of the term "Olympic" to describe athletic events in the modern era have been documented since the 17th century. The first such event was the [Cotswold Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotswold_Games) or "Cotswold Olimpick Games", an annual meeting near [Chipping Campden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chipping_Campden), England, involving various sports. It was first organised by the lawyer [Robert Dover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Dover_\(Cotswold_Games\)) between 1612 and 1642, with several later celebrations leading up to the present day. The [British Olympic Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Olympic_Association), in its bid for the [2012 Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Olympic_Games) in London, mentioned these games as "the first stirrings of Britain's Olympic beginnings".[^cite_note-BOA-31]

*L'Olympiade de la République*, a national Olympic festival held annually from 1796 to 1798 in [Revolutionary France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutionary_France) also attempted to emulate the ancient Olympic Games.[^cite_note-frencholympics-32] The competition included several disciplines from the ancient Greek Olympics. The 1796 Games also marked the introduction of the [metric system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_system) into sport.[^cite_note-frencholympics-32]



The 1834 handbill, written in phonetic vernacular, advertising "Ho-limpyc Gaymes" in [Oswestry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswestry), Shropshire, England

In 1834 and 1836, the Olympic Games were held in Ramlösa, [Sweden](5058739.md), and in [Stockholm](26741.md) in 1843, organised by Gustaf Johan Schartau and others. The Games were attended by 25,000 spectators at most.[^cite_note-FindlingPelle-33]

In 1850, an Olympian Class was started by [William Penny Brookes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Penny_Brookes) at [Much Wenlock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Much_Wenlock), in [Shropshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shropshire), England. In 1859, Brookes changed the name to the [Wenlock Olympian Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wenlock_Olympian_Games). This annual sports festival continues to this day.[^cite_note-Young2004.13-34]<sup>: 144 </sup> The Wenlock Olympian Society was founded by Brookes on 15 November 1860.[^cite_note-Young1996-35]<sup>: 28 </sup>

Between 1862 and 1867, [Liverpool](18081.md) held an annual Grand Olympic Festival. Devised by [John Hulley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hulley) and [Charles Pierre Melly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Pierre_Melly), these games were the first to be wholly amateur in nature and international in outlook, although only 'gentlemen amateurs' could compete.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMatthews200553–54-36][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWeiler2004-37] The programme of the first modern Olympiad in Athens in 1896 was almost identical to that of the Liverpool Olympics.[^cite_note-Girginov_Parry_2005_p38-38] In 1865 Hulley, Brookes and E. G. Ravenstein founded the National Olympian Association in Liverpool, a forerunner of the [British Olympic Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Olympic_Association). Its articles of foundation provided the framework for the [International Olympic Charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Charter).[^cite_note-Young1996-35]<sup>: 24 </sup> In 1866, a national Olympic Games in Great Britain was organised at London's [Crystal Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Crystal_Palace).[^cite_note-39]

### Revival



[Evangelos Zappas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelos_Zappas), co-founder of the [International Olympic Committee](15147.md)

Greek interest in reviving the Olympic Games began with the [Greek War of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_War_of_Independence) from the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md) in 1821. It was first proposed by poet and newspaper editor [Panagiotis Soutsos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panagiotis_Soutsos) in his poem "Dialogue of the Dead", published in 1833.[^cite_note-Young1996-35]<sup>: 1 </sup> [Evangelos Zappas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelos_Zappas), a wealthy Greek-Romanian philanthropist, first wrote to [King Otto of Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Otto_of_Greece), in 1856, offering to fund a permanent revival of the Olympic Games.[^cite_note-Young1996-35]<sup>: 14 </sup> Zappas sponsored the first [Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zappas_Olympics) in 1859, which was held in an [Athens](1216.md) city square. Athletes participated from Greece and the Ottoman Empire. Zappas funded the restoration of the ancient [Panathenaic Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panathenaic_Stadium) so that it could host all future Olympic Games.[^cite_note-Young1996-35]<sup>: 14 </sup>

The stadium hosted the Olympics in 1870 and 1875.[^cite_note-Young1996-35]<sup>: 2, 13–23, 81 </sup> Thirty thousand spectators attended that Games in 1870, though no official attendance records are available for the 1875 Games.[^cite_note-Young1996-35]<sup>: 44 </sup> In 1890, after attending the Olympian Games of the [Wenlock Olympian Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wenlock_Olympian_Society), [Baron Pierre de Coubertin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baron_Pierre_de_Coubertin), who aimed to promote international peace and friendship through sports,[^cite_note-EB-3] was inspired to found the [International Olympic Committee](15147.md) (IOC).[^cite_note-wenlock-40] Coubertin built on the ideas and work of Brookes and Zappas with the aim of establishing internationally rotating Olympic Games that would occur every four years.[^cite_note-wenlock-40] He presented these ideas during the first [Olympic Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Congress) of the newly created International Olympic Committee. This meeting was held from 16 to 23 June 1894, at the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris). On the last day of the Congress, it was decided that the first Olympic Games to come under the auspices of the IOC would take place in Athens in 1896.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECoubertinPhilemonPolitisAnninos18978Part_2-41] The IOC elected the Greek writer [Demetrius Vikelas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demetrius_Vikelas) as its first president.[^cite_note-Young1996-35]<sup>: 100–105 </sup>

### 1896 Games

Main article: [1896 Summer Olympics](48644.md)



The opening ceremony of the [1896 Summer Olympics](48644.md) in [Panathinaiko Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panathinaiko_Stadium) in [Athens](1216.md)

The first Games held under the auspices of the IOC were hosted in the Panathenaic Stadium in [Athens](1216.md) in 1896. The Games brought together 14 nations and 241 athletes who competed in 43 events.[^cite_note-42] Zappas and his cousin [Konstantinos Zappas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konstantinos_Zappas) had left the Greek government a trust to fund future Olympic Games. This trust was used to help finance the 1896 Games.[^cite_note-Young1996-35]<sup>: 117 </sup>[^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44] [George Averoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Averoff) contributed generously for the refurbishment of the stadium in preparation for the Games.[^cite_note-Young1996-35]<sup>: 128 </sup> The Greek government also provided funding, which was expected to be recouped through the sale of tickets and from the sale of the first Olympic commemorative stamp set.[^cite_note-Young1996-35]<sup>: 128 </sup>

Greek officials and the public were enthusiastic about the experience of hosting an Olympic Games.[^cite_note-45] This feeling was shared by many of the athletes, who even demanded that Athens be the permanent Olympic host city. The IOC intended for subsequent Games to be rotated to various host cities around the world. The [second Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1900_Summer_Olympics) was held in Paris.[^cite_note-46]

### Changes and adaptations

Main article: [Summer Olympic Games](27566.md)



[Francis Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Olympic_Field) at [Washington University in St. Louis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_University_in_St._Louis) during the [1904 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1904_Summer_Olympics), the first held outside Europe

After the success of the [1896 Games](48644.md), the Olympics entered a period of stagnation which threatened its survival. The Olympic Games held at the [Paris Exposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exposition_Universelle_\(1900\)) in [1900](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1900_Summer_Olympics) and the [Louisiana Purchase Exposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_Purchase_Exposition) at [St. Louis](27687.md) in [1904](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1904_Summer_Olympics) failed to attract much participation or notice.[^cite_note-47] The Games rebounded with the [1906 Intercalated Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1906_Intercalated_Games) (so-called because they were the second Olympics to take place within the third Olympiad), which were held in Athens. These Games attracted a broad international field of participants and generated a great deal of public interest, marking the beginning of a rise in both the popularity and the size of the Olympics. The 1906 Games were officially recognised by the IOC at the time (although not any longer), and no Intercalated Games have been held since.[^cite_note-48]

#### Winter Games

Main article: [Winter Olympic Games](34069.md)



An [ice hockey](14790.md) game during the [1928 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1928_Winter_Olympics) at [St. Moritz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Moritz)

The Winter Olympics was created to feature snow and ice sports that were logistically impossible to hold during the Summer Games. Figure skating (in 1908 and 1920) and ice hockey (in 1920) were featured as Olympic events at the Summer Olympics.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHines2011xxii-49] The IOC desired to expand this list of sports to encompass other winter activities. At the [1921 Olympic Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_IOC_meetings) in [Lausanne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lausanne), it was decided to hold a winter version of the Olympic Games. A winter sports week (it was actually 11 days) was held in [1924](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1924_Winter_Olympics) in [Chamonix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamonix), France, in connection with the [Paris Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1924_Summer_Olympics) held three months later; this event became the first [Winter Olympic Games](34069.md).[^cite_note-chamonix-50] Although, it was intended that the *same* country host both the Winter and Summer Games in a given year, this idea was quickly abandoned. The IOC mandated that the Winter Games be celebrated every four years in the same year as their summer counterpart.[^cite_note-51] This tradition was upheld until the [1992 Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1992_Winter_Olympics) in [Albertville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albertville), France; after that, beginning with the [1994 Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1994_Winter_Olympics), the Winter Olympics were held every four years, two years after each Summer Olympics.[^cite_note-FindlingPelle-33]<sup>: 405 </sup> This allowed the IOC to space out the logistical and financial costs of the two events from one another, and generate advertising revenue from the Games every two years instead of four.[^cite_note-52]

#### Paralympics

Main article: [Paralympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paralympic_Games)



[1964 Summer Paralympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_Summer_Paralympics) in [Tokyo](30057.md)

In 1948, [Sir Ludwig Guttmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Ludwig_Guttmann), determined to promote the rehabilitation of soldiers after [World War II](32927.md), organised a multi-sport event between several hospitals to coincide with the [1948 London Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1948_London_Olympics). Originally known as the [Stoke Mandeville Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoke_Mandeville_Games), Guttmann's event became an annual sports festival. Over the next 12 years, Guttmann and others continued their efforts to use sports as an avenue to healing.

In 1960, Guttmann brought 400 athletes to Rome to compete in the "Parallel Olympics", which ran in parallel with the [Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1960_Summer_Olympics) and came to be known as the first [Paralympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1960_Summer_Paralympics). Since then, the Paralympics have been held in every Olympic year and, starting with the [1988 Summer Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_Summer_Olympics) in Seoul, the host city for the Olympics has also played host to the Paralympics.[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-55] The [International Olympic Committee](15147.md) (IOC) and the [International Paralympic Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Paralympic_Committee) (IPC) signed an agreement in 2001 which guaranteed that host cities would be contracted to manage both the Olympic and Paralympic Games.[^cite_note-Parahist-56][^cite_note-57] The agreement came into effect at the [2008 Summer Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Summer_Paralympics) in Beijing, and at the [2010 Winter Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Winter_Paralympics) in Vancouver.

Two years before the 2012 Games, the [LOCOG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LOCOG) chairman [Lord Coe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Coe) made the following statement about the [Paralympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Summer_Paralympics) and [Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Summer_Olympics) in London:[^cite_note-58]

> We want to change public attitudes towards disability, celebrate the excellence of Paralympic sport and to enshrine from the very outset that the two Games are an integrated whole.

#### Youth Games

Main article: [Youth Olympic Games](11315968.md)

In 2010, the Olympic Games were complemented by the Youth Games, which give athletes between the ages of 14 and 18 the chance to compete. The Youth Olympic Games were conceived by IOC president [Jacques Rogge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Rogge) in 2001 and approved during the 119th Congress of the IOC.[^cite_note-Rogge_introduces-59][^cite_note-60] The [first Summer Youth Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Summer_Youth_Olympics) were held in Singapore from 14 to 26 August 2010, while the [inaugural Winter Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Winter_Youth_Olympics) were hosted in [Innsbruck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Innsbruck), Austria, two years later.[^cite_note-61] These Games will be shorter than the senior Games; the summer version will last twelve days, while the winter version will last nine days.[^cite_note-CRIonYOG-62] The IOC allows 3,500 athletes and 875 officials to participate at the Summer Youth Games, and 970 athletes and 580 officials at the Winter Youth Games.[^cite_note-YOGisGo-63][^cite_note-No_kidding-64] The sports to be contested will coincide with those scheduled for the senior Games, however there will be variations on the sports including mixed NOC and mixed gender teams as well as a reduced number of disciplines and events.[^cite_note-65] In 2026, IOC president [Kirsty Coventry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirsty_Coventry) announced a restructuring of the Olympic movement and a pause on awarding any future editions of the Youth Olympic Games.[^cite_note-66]

### 21st-century Games

Over 14,000 athletes competed at the [2020 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Summer_Olympics) and [2022 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Winter_Olympics) combined, in 40 different sports and 448 events.[^cite_note-67][^cite_note-68] The Summer Olympics have grown from 241 participants representing 14 nations in 1896, to more than 11,300 competitors representing 206 nations in [2020](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Summer_Olympics).[^cite_note-69] The scope and scale of the Winter Olympics is smaller; for example, [Beijing](18603746.md) hosted 2,971 athletes from 91 nations in [2022](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Winter_Olympics). Most of the athletes and officials are housed in the [Olympic Village](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Village) for the duration of the Games. This accommodation centre is designed to be a self-contained home for all Olympic participants, and is furnished with cafeterias, health clinics, and locations for religious expression.[^cite_note-70] The [2024 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Summer_Olympics) were the first Olympics in history to reach full gender parity on the field of play, with equal numbers of male and female athletes.[^cite_note-71]

The IOC has allowed the formation of [National Olympic Committees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Olympic_Committee) (NOCs) to represent individual nations. These do not meet the strict requirements for political sovereignty that other international organisations demand. As a result, [colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonies) and [dependencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dependent_territory) are permitted to compete at Olympic Games, examples being territories such as [Puerto Rico](23041.md), [Bermuda](3460.md), and [Hong Kong](13404.md), all of which compete as separate nations despite being legally a part of another country.[^cite_note-72] The current version of the Olympic Charter allows for the establishment of new NOCs to represent nations that qualify as "an independent State recognised by the international community".[^cite_note-73] Consequently, the IOC did not allow the formation of NOCs for [Sint Maarten](9529005.md) and [Curaçao](65205.md) when they gained the same constitutional status as [Aruba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aruba) in 2010, although the IOC had recognised the [Aruban Olympic Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aruban_Olympic_Committee) in 1986.[^cite_note-74][^cite_note-75] Since 2012, athletes from the former [Netherlands Antilles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netherlands_Antilles) have had the option to represent either the [Netherlands](21148.md) or Aruba.[^cite_note-76]

### Cost of the Games

See also: [Cost of the Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost_of_the_Olympic_Games)

The Oxford Olympics Study 2016 found that, since 1960, sports-related costs for the Summer Games were on average US\$5.2 billion and for the Winter Games \$3.1 billion. These figures do not include wider infrastructure costs like roads, urban rail, and airports, which often cost as much as or more than the sports-related costs. The most expensive Summer Games were Beijing 2008 at US\$40‍–‍44 billion,[^cite_note-77] and the most expensive Winter Games were Sochi 2014 at US\$51 billion.[^cite_note-The_Guardian-78][^cite_note-study-79] As of 2016, costs per athlete were, on average, US\$599,000 for the Summer Games and \$1.3 million for the Winter Games; for London 2012, the cost per athlete was \$1.4 million, and the figure was \$7.9 million for Sochi 2014.[^cite_note-study-79]

Where ambitious construction for the [1976 Summer Games in Montreal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1976_Summer_Olympics) and the 1980 Summer Games in Moscow had burdened organisers with expenses greatly in excess of revenues, Los Angeles strictly controlled expenses for the 1984 Summer Games by using existing facilities and only two new that were paid for by corporate sponsors. The organising committee, led by [Peter Ueberroth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Ueberroth), used some of the profits to endow the [LA84 Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LA84_Foundation) to promote youth sports in [Southern California](62520.md), educate coaches and maintain a sports library. The 1984 Summer Olympics are often considered until that date, the most financially successful modern Olympics and a model for future Games.[^cite_note-financiallySuccessful-80]

Budget overruns are common for the Games. Average overrun for Games since 1960 is 156% in real terms,[^cite_note-81] which means that actual costs turned out to be on average 2.56 times the budget that was estimated at the time of winning the bid to host the Games. Montreal 1976 had the highest cost overrun for Summer Games, and for any Games, at 720%; Lake Placid 1980 had the highest cost overrun for Winter Games, at 324%. London 2012 had a cost overrun of 76%, Sochi 2014 of 289%.[^cite_note-study-79]

It has been documented that cost and cost overrun for the Games follow a [power-law distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power-law_distribution), meaning that the Games are prone to large [cost overruns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost_overrun), and it is only a matter of time before an overrun occurs that exceeds the largest to date. In short, hosting the Games carries extreme economical and financial risks.[^cite_note-\:0-82]

The final cost for the Tokyo 2020 Olympics was reported to be JPY 1,423.8 billion (US\$13 billion). This was achieved by balancing revenues and expenditures through various efforts to increase revenue and continuously review expenditures. The primary sources of revenue included the International Olympic Committee (IOC) contribution of JPY 86.8 billion (US\$0.8 billion), TOP sponsorship of JPY 56.9 billion (US\$0.5 billion), local sponsorship of JPY 376.1 billion (US\$3.4 billion), an insurance payout of JPY 50 billion (US\$0.5 billion) for the postponement of the Games, and other sources including licensing. The expenditures included JPY 195.5 billion (US\$1.8 billion) for venue-related costs and JPY 444.9 billion (US\$4 billion) for service expenditures. The total cost also accounted for COVID-19 countermeasures amounting to JPY 35.3 billion (US\$0.3 billion). Despite initial estimates, the total costs were reduced by JPY 220.2 billion (US\$2 billion) from the budget announced in December 2020, and JPY 29.2 billion (US\$0.3 billion) from the estimated budget in December 2021. This successful financial management resulted in a balanced budget for the Tokyo 2020 Games.[^cite_note-83][^cite_note-84]

### Economic and social impact on host cities and countries

Seeking a scholarly institution to independently research the Games, [Bob Barney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Barney) led efforts to establish the International Centre for Olympic Studies in 1989, endeavouring to write about [sociocultural impacts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_environment) of the Olympic Games.[^cite_note-Cengel-2008-85][^cite_note-86] He felt that the Olympics "is worthy of study because it is one of the biggest meetings in a global context and has many political, economic, and other problems associated with it".[^cite_note-Cengel-2008-85] He began *Olympika* in 1992, the first peer-reviewed academic journal focused on the Olympic Games.[^cite_note-CSSOR-87][^cite_note-CSSOR-2022-88] Founded in 1991, the [International Society of Olympic Historians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Society_of_Olympic_Historians) publishes the *Journal of Olympic History*.[^cite_note-89]

Some economists are sceptical about the economic benefits of hosting the Olympic Games, emphasising that such "mega-events" often have large costs while yielding relatively few tangible benefits in the long run.[^cite_note-90] Hosting (or even bidding for) the Olympics appears to increase the host country's exports, as the host or candidate country sends a signal about trade openness when bidding to host the Games.[^cite_note-91] Research suggests that hosting the Summer Olympics has a positive effect on the [philanthropic contributions of corporations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_giving) headquartered in the host city, which seems to benefit the local nonprofit sector. This effect begins in the years leading up to the Games and might persist for several years afterwards, though it is not permanent.[^cite_note-92]

The Games have had significant negative effects on host communities; for example, the [Centre on Housing Rights and Evictions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre_on_Housing_Rights_and_Evictions) reports that the Olympics displaced more than two million people over two decades, often disproportionately affecting disadvantaged groups.[^cite_note-93] The [2014 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Winter_Olympics) in [Sochi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sochi) were the most expensive Olympic Games in history, costing in excess of US\$50 billion. According to a report by the [European Bank for Reconstruction and Development](37963.md) released at the time of the Games, the cost would not boost Russia's national economy but could attract business to Sochi and the southern [Krasnodar region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krasnodar_region) of Russia as a result of improved services. By December 2014, eight months after the Games, *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)* stated that Sochi "now feels like a ghost town", citing the spread-out nature of the stadiums and arenas and the still-unfinished infrastructure.[^cite_note-94] At least four cities withdrew their bids for the [2022 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Winter_Olympics), citing the high costs or lack of local support,[^cite_note-95] resulting in only [a two-city race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bids_for_the_2022_Winter_Olympics) between [Almaty, Kazakhstan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almaty,_Kazakhstan), and Beijing, China (which hosted the [2008 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Summer_Olympics)). *The Guardian* stated that the biggest threat to the future of the Olympics is that few cities or countries want to host them.[^cite_note-96] Bidding for the [2024 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Summer_Olympics) became a two-city race between Paris and Los Angeles, so the IOC took the unusual step of [simultaneously awarding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bids_for_the_2024_and_2028_Summer_Olympics) both the 2024 Games to Paris and the [2028 Games](12695048.md) to Los Angeles. Both of the bids were praised for their [high-tech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-tech) plans and innovative ways of using a record-breaking number of existing and temporary facilities.[^cite_note-97]

## International Olympic Committee

See also: [International Olympic Committee](15147.md)

The Olympic Movement encompasses a large number of national and international sporting organisations and federations, recognised media partners, as well as athletes, officials, judges, and every other person and institution that agrees to abide by the rules of the Olympic Charter.[^cite_note-movement-98] As the umbrella organisation of the Olympic Movement, the [International Olympic Committee](15147.md) (IOC) is responsible for selecting the host city, overseeing the planning of the Olympic Games, updating and approving the Olympic sports programme, and negotiating sponsorship and broadcasting rights.[^cite_note-movement_roles-99]

The Olympic Movement is made of three main elements:

- [International Federations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_international_sport_federations) (IFs) are the [governing bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sports_governing_body) that supervise a sport at an international level. For example, the International Federation of Association Football ([FIFA](11049.md)) is the international governing body for association football, and the [Fédération Internationale de Volleyball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F%C3%A9d%C3%A9ration_Internationale_de_Volleyball) is the international governing body for volleyball. There are currently 35 IFs in the Olympic Movement, representing each of the Olympic sports.

  [^cite_note-100]

- [National Olympic Committees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Olympic_Committee) (NOCs) represent and regulate the Olympic Movement within each country. For example, the [Hellenic Olympic Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenic_Olympic_Committee) (HOC) is the NOC of [Greece](12108.md), officially the Hellenic Republic. There are currently 206 NOCs recognised by the IOC.

  [^cite_note-101]

  [^cite_note-102]

- Organising Committees for the Olympic Games (OCOGs) are temporary committees responsible for the organisation of each Olympic Games. OCOGs are dissolved after each Games once the final report is delivered to the IOC.

  [^cite_note-103]

French and English are the official languages of the Olympic Movement. The other language used at each Olympic Games is the language of the host country (or languages, if a country has more than one official language apart from French or English). Every proclamation (such as the announcement of each country during the parade of nations in the opening ceremony) is spoken in these three (or more) languages, or the main two depending on whether the host country is an English or French speaking country. These languages were spoken in the traditional order: French is always spoken first, followed by English, and then the dominant language of the host nation (when this is not English or French).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEOlympic_Charter200753Rule_24-104]

In March 2025, [Kirsty Coventry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirsty_Coventry) made history by becoming the first woman and the first African to be elected as President of the IOC. Coventry embodies high aspirations, envisioning a future where the Olympics serve as a symbol of global unity, weaving together diverse cultures and nations through athletic excellence. Coventry's leadership is set to ensure that every nation has an equal chance to shine on the Olympic stage, separating sports from politics. Coventry's plans align with the IOC's broader efforts to strengthen global partnerships, particularly with [BRICS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BRICS) nations, while reinforcing its role as a global unifier, promoting peace and development through sports.[^cite_note-105][^cite_note-106]

### Allegations of bribery and corruption

The IOC has often been accused of being an intractable organisation, with several life members on the committee. The presidential terms of [Avery Brundage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avery_Brundage) and [Juan Antonio Samaranch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Antonio_Samaranch) were especially controversial. Brundage fought strongly for amateurism and against the commercialisation of the Olympic Games, even as these attitudes came to be seen as incongruous with the realities of modern sports. The advent of state-sponsored athletes from the [Eastern Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Bloc) countries further eroded the ideology of the pure [amateur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amateur_sports), as it placed self-financed amateurs of Western countries at a disadvantage.[^cite_note-Maraniss-107]<sup>: 52–60 </sup> Brundage was accused of [antisemitism](1078.md) and of racism in [resisting the exclusion of South Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apartheid-era_South_Africa_and_the_Olympics).[^cite_note-Maraniss-107]<sup>: 60–69 </sup> Under the Samaranch presidency, the office was accused of both [nepotism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nepotism) and corruption.[^cite_note-108] Samaranch's ties with the [Franco regime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franco_regime) in Spain were also a source of criticism.[^cite_note-109]

In 1998, it was reported that several IOC members had [taken gifts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2002_Winter_Olympic_bid_scandal) from members of the [Salt Lake City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_Lake_City) bid committee for the hosting of the [2002 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2002_Winter_Olympics). There were soon four independent investigations underway: by the IOC, the [United States Olympic Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Olympic_Committee) (USOC), the [Salt Lake Organizing Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_Lake_Organizing_Committee) (SLOC), and the [United States Department of Justice](52563.md) (DOJ). Although nothing strictly illegal had occurred, it was felt that the acceptance of the gifts was morally dubious. As a result of the investigation, ten members of the IOC were expelled and a further ten sanctioned.[^cite_note-110] Stricter rules were adopted for future bids, and caps were introduced to define how much IOC members could accept from bid cities. Additionally, new [term](505825.md) and age limits were put into place for IOC membership, and fifteen former Olympic athletes were added to the committee. Nevertheless, from sporting and business standpoints, the 2002 Olympics were one of the most successful Winter Games in history; records were set in both the broadcasting and marketing programs. Over two billion viewers watched more than 13 billion viewer-hours.[^cite_note-Marketing_Matters_6_2002-111] The 2002 Games were also a financial success, raising more money with fewer sponsors than any prior Olympic Games, leaving SLOC with a surplus of [\$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USD)40 million. This excess revenue was used to create the Utah Athletic Foundation (also known as the Utah Olympic Legacy Foundation), which maintains and operates many of the surviving Olympic venues.[^cite_note-Marketing_Matters_6_2002-111]

It was reported in 1999 that the [Nagano Olympic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1998_Winter_Olympics) bid committee had spent approximately \$14 million on entertaining the 62 IOC members and many of their associates. The precise figures are unknown since [Nagano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagano_Olympic_Organizing_Committee) destroyed the financial records after the IOC requested that the entertainment expenditures should not be made public.[^cite_note-112][^cite_note-113]

In July 2000, when the *[Los Angeles Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Angeles_Times)* reported on the tangled nature of how the IOC redistributes profits from sponsorships and [broadcasting rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcasting_rights), Olympic historian [Bob Barney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Barney) stated that he had "yet to see matters of corruption in the IOC" but noted that there were "matters of unaccountability".[^cite_note-114] He later noted that when the spotlight is on the athletes, it has "the power to eclipse impressions of scandal or corruption", with respect to the Olympic bid process.[^cite_note-115]

In August 2004, a [BBC](19344654.md) documentary, *[Panorama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panorama_\(British_TV_programme\)): Buying the Games*, reported the results of an investigation into bribes allegedly used in the bidding process for the [2012 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Summer_Olympics).[^cite_note-Buying_the_Games-116] The documentary claimed that it was possible to bribe IOC members into voting for a particular candidate city. After being narrowly defeated in their bid for the 2012 Games,[^cite_note-117] Parisian mayor [Bertrand Delanoë](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertrand_Delano%C3%AB) specifically accused the British prime minister [Tony Blair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Blair) and the [London bid committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_bid_for_the_2012_Summer_Olympics), headed by former Olympic champion [Sebastian Coe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sebastian_Coe), of breaking the bid rules. He cited French president [Jacques Chirac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Chirac) as a witness; Chirac gave guarded interviews concerning his involvement[^cite_note-118] but the allegation was never fully explored. [Turin's bid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turin_bid_for_the_2006_Winter_Olympics) to host the [2006 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_Winter_Olympics) was also clouded by controversy; a prominent IOC member, [Marc Hodler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marc_Hodler), closely connected to the rival [bid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sion_bid_for_the_2006_Winter_Olympics) of [Sion, Switzerland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sion,_Switzerland), alleged bribery of IOC officials by members of the Turin Organising Committee. These accusations led to a wide-ranging investigation, and also served to sour many IOC members against Sion's bid which potentially helped Turin to capture the host city nomination.[^cite_note-119]

## Commercialisation

### Under national organising committees

The Olympic Games have been commercialised to various degrees since the inaugural [1896 Summer Olympics](48644.md) in Athens, when a number of companies paid for advertising,[^cite_note-IOC2011Marketing-120] including [Kodak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kodak).[^cite_note-121][^cite_note-122] In 1908, [Oxo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxo_\(food\)), [Odol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odol) mouthwash, and Indian Foot Powder became official sponsors of the [London Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1908_Summer_Olympics).[^cite_note-123][^cite_note-124] [Coca-Cola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coca-Cola) first sponsored the Summer Olympics in [1928](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1928_Summer_Olympics), and has remained an Olympic sponsor ever since.[^cite_note-IOC2011Marketing-120] Before the IOC took control of sponsorship, the NOCs had responsibility for negotiating their own contracts for sponsorship and use of the Olympic symbols.[^cite_note-CooperChen-125]<sup>: 231 </sup>

### Under IOC control

The IOC originally resisted funding by corporate sponsors. It was not until the retirement of IOC President [Avery Brundage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avery_Brundage), in 1972, that the IOC began to explore the potential of the television medium and the lucrative advertising markets available to them.[^cite_note-CooperChen-125]<sup>: 231 </sup> Under the leadership of [Juan Antonio Samaranch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Antonio_Samaranch), the Games began to shift toward international sponsors who sought to link their products to the Olympic brand.[^cite_note-television-126]

### Budget

During the first half of the 20th century, the IOC ran on a small budget.[^cite_note-television-126][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBuchanonMallon2006ci-127] As president of the IOC from 1952 to 1972, Avery Brundage rejected all attempts to link the Olympics with commercial interest.[^cite_note-CooperChen-125]<sup>: 231 </sup> He believed that the lobby of corporate interests would unduly impact the IOC's decision-making. His resistance to this revenue stream meant the IOC left organising committees to negotiate their own sponsorship contracts and use the Olympic symbols.[^cite_note-CooperChen-125]<sup>: 231 </sup> When Brundage retired, the IOC had US\$2 million in assets; eight years later the IOC coffers had swelled to US\$45 million. This was primarily caused by a shift in ideology toward expansion of the Games through corporate sponsorship and the sale of television rights.[^cite_note-CooperChen-125]<sup>: 231 </sup> When Juan Antonio Samaranch was elected IOC president in 1980, his desire was to make the IOC financially independent.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBuchanonMallon2006ci-127]

The [1984 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1984_Summer_Olympics) became a watershed moment in Olympic history. The Los Angeles-based organising committee, led by [Peter Ueberroth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Ueberroth), was able to generate a surplus of US\$225 million—an unprecedented amount at that time.[^cite_note-FindlingPelle-33]<sup>: 209 </sup> The organising committee had been able to create such a surplus in part by selling exclusive sponsorship rights to select companies.[^cite_note-FindlingPelle-33]<sup>: 209 </sup> The IOC sought to gain control of these sponsorship rights. Samaranch helped to establish The Olympic Programme (TOP) in 1985, in order to create an Olympic brand.[^cite_note-television-126] Membership in TOP was, and is, very exclusive and expensive. Fees cost US\$50 million for a four-year membership.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBuchanonMallon2006ci-127] Members of TOP received exclusive global advertising rights for their product category, and use of the Olympic emblem—the [five interlocking rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_rings)—in their publications and advertisements.[^cite_note-Slack-128]<sup>: 194 </sup>

### Effect of television



A *[Berliner Illustrirte Zeitung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berliner_Illustrirte_Zeitung)* cartoon published during the [1936 Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1936_Summer_Olympics) in [Berlin](3354.md) envisions the Olympics in 2000, when spectators would be replaced by television and radio, with the cheers of spectators broadcast through loudspeakers. Almost a century after the prediction, spectators still watch the Games in person and by globally-broadcast television coverage.

The [1936 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1936_Summer_Olympics) in [Berlin](3354.md) were the first Games to be broadcast on television, though only to local audiences.[^cite_note-129] The [1956 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1956_Winter_Olympics) in Italy were the first internationally televised Olympic Games,[^cite_note-130] and the broadcasting rights for the [following Winter Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1960_Winter_Olympics) in California were sold for the first time to specialised television broadcasting networks—[CBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CBS) paid US\$394,000 for the American rights.[^cite_note-Slack-128]<sup>: 192 </sup>[^cite_note-television-126] In the following decades, the Olympics became one of the ideological fronts of the Cold War, and the [IOC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IOC) wanted to take advantage of this heightened interest via the broadcast medium.[^cite_note-Slack-128]<sup>: 192 </sup> The sale of broadcast rights enabled the IOC to increase the exposure of the Olympic Games, thereby generating more interest, which in turn enhanced the appeal of TV air time to the advertisers. This cycle allowed the IOC to charge ever-increasing fees for those rights.[^cite_note-Slack-128]<sup>: 192 </sup> For example, CBS paid US\$375 million for the American broadcast rights for the [1998 Nagano Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1998_Winter_Olympics),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGershon200017-131] while [NBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NBC) spent US\$3.5 billion for the American rights to air every Olympic Games from 2000 to 2012.[^cite_note-television-126]

In 2011, NBC agreed to a \$4.38 billion contract with the IOC to broadcast the Olympics through to the [2020 Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Summer_Olympics), the most expensive television rights deal in Olympic history.[^cite_note-adweek-132] NBC then agreed to a \$7.75 billion contract extension on 7 May 2014, to air the Olympics up to and including the 2032 Games.[^cite_note-133] NBC also acquired the American television rights to the [Youth Olympic Games](11315968.md), beginning in [2014](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Summer_Youth_Olympics),[^cite_note-134] and the [Paralympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paralympic_Games).[^cite_note-135] More than half of the Olympic Committee's global sponsors are American companies,[^cite_note-auto-136] and NBC is one of the major sources of revenue for the IOC.[^cite_note-auto-136]

On 13 March 2025, it was announced that [Comcast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comcast) would pay the IOC \$3 billion to air the Olympics through 2036, and that Comcast would transition its role in the Games from Rights Holder to Strategic Partner, furthering their partnership. The announcement comes after the [2024 Paris Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Summer_Olympics) received strong ratings for NBC and a boost in subscribers for [Peacock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peacock_\(streaming_service\)). This deal gave NBC the rights to air the [2026 Milano-Cortina Winter Olympics](38416091.md) (which occurred from 6 February to 22 February 2026), and gives the network the rights to air the [2028 Los Angeles Olympics](12695048.md), the [2030 French Alps Winter Olympics](57658858.md), the [2032 Brisbane Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2032_Summer_Olympics), the [2034 Utah Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2034_Winter_Olympics) and the [2036 Olympic Games](63724811.md), with the latter marking the 100th anniversary of the first televised Games held in Berlin.[^cite_note-137]

Viewership increased exponentially from the 1960s until the end of the 20th century. This was due to the advent of satellites for broadcasting live television worldwide starting in 1964, and the introduction of [colour television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colour_television) in 1968.[^cite_note-138] The global audience for the [1968 Mexico City Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1968_Summer_Olympics) was estimated to be 600 million, whereas the audience numbers at the [Los Angeles Games of 1984](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1984_Summer_Olympics) had increased to 900 million; this number had swelled to 3.5 billion by the [1992 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1992_Summer_Olympics) in Barcelona.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETomlinson200514-139][^cite_note-140][^cite_note-141][^cite_note-ratings-142][^cite_note-Slack-128]<sup>: 16–18 </sup> With such high costs charged to broadcast the Games, the added pressure of the internet, and increased competition from cable, the television lobby demanded concessions from the IOC to boost ratings. The IOC responded by making a number of changes to the Olympic programme; at the Summer Games, the gymnastics competition was expanded from seven to nine nights, and a Champions Gala was added to attract greater interest;[^cite_note-Slack-128]<sup>: 17 </sup> the events programmes were also expanded for swimming and diving, both popular sports with a broad base of television viewers.[^cite_note-Slack-128]<sup>: 17 </sup> Due to the substantial fees paid by NBC for rights to the Olympics, the IOC has allowed the network to influence the event scheduling to maximise U.S. television ratings when possible.[^cite_note-CooperChen-125]<sup>: 230 </sup>[^cite_note-ratings-142][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWoods2007146-143][^cite_note-144] Notable examples of maximising U.S. television viewership include scheduling the finals of the swimming events only during the mornings of the host cities Beijing (during the [2008 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Summer_Olympics)) and Tokyo (during the [2020 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Summer_Olympics)), which coincide with the evening [prime time](24973.md#North_America) broadcast slots of the United States.[^cite_note-145][^cite_note-146][^cite_note-147][^cite_note-148][^cite_note-149]

### Olympic marketing

The funds from licensing programs support everything that goes into the Games, alongside other revenue streams from ticket sales and broadcasting rights, which represent more than 60 percent.[^cite_note-150][^cite_note-151]

The sale of the Olympic brand has been controversial. The argument is that the Games have become indistinguishable from any other commercialised sporting spectacle.[^cite_note-Slack-128]<sup>: 194 </sup>[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBuchanonMallon2006cii-152] Another criticism is that the Games are funded by host cities and national governments; the IOC incurs none of the cost, yet controls all the rights and profits from the Olympic symbols. The IOC also takes a percentage of all sponsorship and broadcast income.[^cite_note-Slack-128]<sup>: 194 </sup> Host cities continue to compete ardently for the right to host the Games, even though there is no certainty that they will earn back their investments.[^cite_note-Slack-128]<sup>: 194–195 </sup> Research has shown that trade is around 30 percent higher for countries that have hosted the Olympics.[^cite_note-153]

## Symbols

Main article: [Olympic symbols](731192.md)



The [Olympic flag](731192.md)

The Olympic Movement uses symbols to represent the ideals embodied in the Olympic Charter. The main emblem of the Games is the [Olympic rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_rings)—five intertwined rings symbolising the unity of the five inhabited continents ([Africa](5334607.md), [the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Americas) (considered as one continent), [Asia](689.md), [Europe](9239.md), and [Oceania](22621.md)). The coloured version of the rings—blue, yellow, black, green, and red—over a white field forms the Olympic flag. These colours were chosen because every nation had at least one of them on its national flag. The flag was adopted in 1914 but flown for the first time only at the [1920 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1920_Summer_Olympics) in Antwerp, Belgium. It has since been hoisted during each celebration of the Games.[^cite_note-olympicsymbols-154][^cite_note-155] The IOC maintains exclusive ownership and control[^cite_note-156] of the use of Olympic symbols through the [Nairobi Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nairobi_Treaty) on the Protection of the Olympic Symbol.

The [Olympic motto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_motto), *Citius, Altius, Fortius*, a [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) expression meaning "Faster, Higher, Stronger" was proposed by [Pierre de Coubertin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_de_Coubertin) in 1894 and has been official since 1924. The motto was coined by Coubertin's friend, the [Dominican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominican_Order) priest [Henri Didon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Didon) OP, for a Paris youth gathering of 1891.[^cite_note-157]

Coubertin's Olympic ideals are expressed in the [Olympic creed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_creed):

> The most important thing in the Olympic Games is not to win but to take part, just as the most important thing in life is not the triumph but the struggle. The essential thing is not to have conquered but to have fought well.[^cite_note-olympicsymbols-154]

Months before each Games, the [Olympic flame](74217.md) is lit at the [Temple of Hera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Hera,_Olympia) in [Olympia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympia,_Greece) in a ceremony that reflects ancient Greek rituals. A female performer, acting as a priestess joined by ten female performers as [Vestal Virgins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vestal_Virgins), ignites a torch by placing it inside a [parabolic mirror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabolic_mirror) which focuses the sun's rays; she then lights the torch of the first relay bearer (who also is a Greek athlete), thus initiating the Olympic torch relay that will carry the flame to the host city's Olympic stadium, where it plays an important role in the opening ceremony.[^cite_note-torch-158] Though the flame has been an Olympic symbol since [1928](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1928_Summer_Olympics), the torch relay was only introduced at the [1936 Summer Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1936_Summer_Olympics) to promote the [Third Reich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Reich).[^cite_note-olympicsymbols-154][^cite_note-159]

The [Olympic mascot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_mascot), an animal, a human or an [anthropomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropomorphic) figure representing the cultural heritage of the host country, was introduced in [1968](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1968_Summer_Olympics). It has played an important part of the identity and promotion of the Games since the [1980 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980_Summer_Olympics), when the Soviet bear cub [Misha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misha) reached international stardom. The mascot of the Summer Olympics in London was named Wenlock after the town of [Much Wenlock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Much_Wenlock) in [Shropshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shropshire). Much Wenlock still hosts the Wenlock Olympian Games, which were an inspiration to Pierre de Coubertin for the Olympic Games.[^cite_note-160] The mascot for the [Paris 2024](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Summer_Olympics) Olympic Games is an anthropomorphic [Phrygian cap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phrygian_cap) based on the one [Marianne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marianne) wore at the time of the [French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution).[^cite_note-\:4-161]

## Ceremonies

Main article: [Olympic Games ceremony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Games_ceremony)

### Opening ceremony



Opening ceremony of the [2020 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Summer_Olympics) in [Tokyo](30057.md)

As mandated by the [Olympic Charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Charter), various elements frame the opening ceremony of the Olympic Games. This ceremony takes place on a Friday and is held prior to the commencement of the sporting events (apart from some group-stage football matches, softball games, and rowing heats).[^cite_note-OCFactSheetS-162][^cite_note-OCFactSheetW-163] Most of the rituals for the opening ceremony were established at the 1920 Summer Olympics in Antwerp.[^cite_note-OCprotocol1-164] The ceremony typically starts with the entrance of the president of the International Olympic Committee and a representative of the host country followed by the hoisting of the host country's flag and a performance of its national anthem.[^cite_note-OCFactSheetS-162][^cite_note-OCFactSheetW-163] The host nation then presents artistic displays of music, singing, dance, and theatre representative of its culture.[^cite_note-OCprotocol1-164] The artistic presentations have grown in scale and complexity as successive hosts attempt to provide a ceremony that outlasts its predecessor's in terms of memorability. The opening ceremony of the Beijing Games reportedly cost \$100 million, with much of the cost incurred in the artistic segment.[^cite_note-165]

After the artistic portion of the ceremony, the athletes parade into the stadium grouped by nation. Greece is traditionally the first nation to enter and leads the parade in order to honour the origins of the Olympics. Nations then enter the stadium alphabetically according to the host country's chosen language, with the host country's athletes being the last to enter. During the [2004 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Summer_Olympics), which was hosted in [Athens](1216.md), the Greek flag entered the stadium first, while the Greek delegation entered last. Beginning with the [2020 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Summer_Olympics), the succeeding hosts of the respective Olympic Games (summer or winter) will enter immediately before the current host in descending order. Speeches are given by the [President of the Organising Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_Organising_Committee_for_the_Olympic_Games), the IOC president, and the head of state/representative of the host country, formally opening the Games. Finally, the Olympic torch is brought into the stadium and passed on until it reaches the final torch carrier, often a successful Olympic athlete from the host nation, who lights the Olympic flame in the stadium's cauldron.[^cite_note-OCFactSheetS-162][^cite_note-OCFactSheetW-163]

### Closing ceremony



Athletes gather in the stadium during the closing ceremony of the [2008 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Summer_Olympics) in [Beijing](18603746.md)

The closing ceremony of the Olympic Games takes place on a Sunday and after all sporting events have concluded. Flag-bearers from each participating country enter the stadium, followed by the athletes who enter together, without any national distinction.[^cite_note-IOCclosingProtocol-166] Three national flags are hoisted while the corresponding national anthems are played: the flag of the current host country; the flag of Greece, to honour the birthplace of the Olympic Games; and the flag of the country hosting the next Summer or Winter Olympic Games.[^cite_note-IOCclosingProtocol-166] The president of the organising committee and the IOC president make their closing speeches, the Games are officially closed, and the Olympic flame is extinguished.[^cite_note-167] In what is known as the Antwerp Ceremony, the current mayor of the city that organised the Games transfers a special Olympic flag to the president of the IOC, who then passes it on to the current mayor of the city hosting the next Olympic Games.[^cite_note-168] The next host nation then also briefly introduces itself with artistic displays of dance and theatre representative of its culture.[^cite_note-IOCclosingProtocol-166]

As is customary, the last medal presentation of the Games is held as part of the closing ceremony. Typically, the marathon medals are presented at the Summer Olympics,[^cite_note-IOCclosingProtocol-166][^cite_note-169] while the cross-country skiing mass start medals are awarded at the Winter Olympics.[^cite_note-170]

### Medal presentation



A medal ceremony at the [2008 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Summer_Olympics) with (from left to right): the [Danish flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_flag), the [Union Flag of the UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_Jack), and the [New Zealand flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_flag)

A medal ceremony is held after the conclusion of each Olympic event. The winner, and the second- and third-place competitors or teams, stand on top of a three-tiered [rostrum](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/rostrum) to be awarded their respective medals by a member of the IOC.[^cite_note-171] After the medals have been received, the national flags of the three medallists are raised while the [national anthem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_anthem) of the gold medallist's country is played.[^cite_note-otraditions-172] Volunteering citizens of the host country also act as hosts during the medal ceremonies, assisting the officials who present the medals and acting as flag-bearers.[^cite_note-173] In the Summer Olympics, each medal ceremony is held at the venue where the event has taken place,[^cite_note-174] but the ceremonies at the Winter Olympics are usually held in a special "plaza".[^cite_note-175]

The practice of awarding Olympic medals at [podium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Podium) ceremonies was established at the 1932 Winter Olympics, based on pedestals used at the [1930 British Empire Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1930_British_Empire_Games), as proposed by [Melville Marks Robinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melville_Marks_Robinson).[^cite_note-176]

## Sports

Main article: [Olympic sports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_sports)

The [Olympic Games programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_sports) consists of 35 sports, 30 disciplines and 408 events. For example, [wrestling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrestling_at_the_Summer_Olympics) is a Summer Olympic sport, comprising two disciplines: [Greco-Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Roman_wrestling) and [Freestyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freestyle_wrestling). It is further broken down into fourteen events for men and four events for women, each representing a different weight class.[^cite_note-177] The Summer Olympics programme includes 26 sports, while the Winter Olympics programme features 15 sports.[^cite_note-Sports-178] [Athletics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athletics_at_the_Summer_Olympics), [swimming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swimming_at_the_Summer_Olympics), [fencing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fencing_at_the_Summer_Olympics), and [artistic gymnastics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnastics_at_the_Summer_Olympics) are the only summer sports that have never been absent from the Olympic programme. [Cross-country skiing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-country_skiing_at_the_Winter_Olympics), [figure skating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Figure_skating_at_the_Olympic_Games), [ice hockey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_hockey_at_the_Olympic_Games), [Nordic combined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nordic_combined_at_the_Winter_Olympics), [ski jumping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ski_jumping_at_the_Winter_Olympics), and [speed skating](3682366.md) have been featured at every Winter Olympics programme since its inception in [1924](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1924_Winter_Olympics). Current Olympic sports, like [badminton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Badminton_at_the_Summer_Olympics), [basketball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basketball_at_the_Summer_Olympics), and [volleyball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volleyball_at_the_Summer_Olympics), first appeared on the programme as [demonstration sports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demonstration_sport) and were later promoted to full Olympic sports. Some sports that were featured in earlier Games were later dropped from the programme.[^cite_note-179]

Olympic sports are governed by [international sports federations](398336.md) (IFs) recognised by the IOC as the global supervisors of those sports. There are 35 federations represented at the IOC.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEOlympic_Charter200788–90-180] There are sports recognised by the IOC that are not included in the Olympic programme. These sports are not considered Olympic sports, but they can be promoted to this status during a programme revision that occurs in the first IOC session following a celebration of the Olympic Games.[^cite_note-181][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEOlympic_Charter200787-182] During such revisions, sports can be excluded or included in the programme on the basis of a two-thirds majority vote of the members of the IOC.[^cite_note-183] There are recognised sports that have never been on an Olympic programme in any capacity, for example, orienteering.[^cite_note-recognizedsports-184]

In October and November 2004, the IOC established an Olympic Programme Commission, tasked with reviewing the sports on the Olympic programme and all non-Olympic recognised sports. The goal was to apply a systematic approach to establishing the Olympic programme for each celebration of the Games.[^cite_note-factsheetsports-185] The commission formulated seven criteria to judge whether a sport should be included on the Olympic programme.[^cite_note-factsheetsports-185] These criteria are history and tradition of the sport, universality, popularity of the sport, image, athletes' health, development of the International Federation that governs the sport, and costs of holding the sport.[^cite_note-factsheetsports-185] From this study five recognised sports emerged as candidates for inclusion at the 2012 Summer Olympics: golf, karate, rugby sevens, roller sports and [squash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squash_\(sport\)).[^cite_note-factsheetsports-185] These sports were reviewed by the IOC executive board and then referred to the General Session in Singapore in July 2005. Of the five sports recommended for inclusion only two were selected as finalists: karate and squash.[^cite_note-factsheetsports-185] Neither sport attained the required two-thirds vote and consequently they were not promoted to the Olympic programme.[^cite_note-factsheetsports-185] In October 2009 the IOC voted to instate golf and rugby sevens as Olympic sports for the [2016](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Summer_Olympics) and [2020 Summer Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Summer_Olympic_Games).[^cite_note-newosports-186]

The 114th [IOC Session](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IOC_Session), in 2002, limited the Summer Games programme to a maximum of 28 sports, 301 events, and 10,500 athletes.[^cite_note-factsheetsports-185] Three years later, at the [117th IOC Session](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/117th_IOC_Session), the first major programme revision was performed, resulting in the exclusion of [baseball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baseball_at_the_Summer_Olympics) and [softball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Softball_at_the_Summer_Olympics) from the official programme of the [2012 London Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Summer_Olympics). Since there was no agreement in the promotion of two other sports, the 2012 programme featured just 26 sports.[^cite_note-factsheetsports-185] The 2016 and 2020 Games returned to the maximum of 28 sports given the addition of rugby and golf.[^cite_note-newosports-186]

### Amateurism and professionalism

Further information: [Amateur sports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amateur_sports)



The Gold medal game between [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia_men's_national_ice_hockey_team) and [Czech Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czech_Republic_men's_national_ice_hockey_team) at the [1998 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1998_Winter_Olympics); professional [National Hockey League](21809.md) players have been eligible to participate in [ice hockey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_hockey_at_the_Olympic_Games) since then.

The [ethos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethos) of the aristocracy as exemplified in the [English public school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_public_school) greatly influenced [Pierre de Coubertin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_de_Coubertin).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEassom1994120–123-187] The public schools subscribed to the belief that sport formed an important part of education, an attitude summed up in the saying *[mens sana in corpore sano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mens_sana_in_corpore_sano)*, a healthy mind in a healthy body. In this ethos, a gentleman was one who became an all-rounder, not the best at one specific thing. There was also a prevailing concept of fairness, in which practising or training was considered tantamount to cheating.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEassom1994120–123-187] Those who practised a sport professionally were considered to have an unfair advantage over those who practised it merely as a hobby.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEassom1994120–123-187]

The exclusion of professionals caused several controversies throughout the history of the modern Olympics. The [1912 Olympic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1912_Summer_Olympics) pentathlon and [decathlon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decathlon) champion [Jim Thorpe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Thorpe) was stripped of his medals when it was discovered that he had taken expense money playing semi-professional baseball before the Olympics. His medals were posthumously restored by the IOC in 1983 after a ruling found that the decision to strip him of his medals fell [outside of the required 30-day period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athletics_at_the_1912_Summer_Olympics_%E2%80%93_Men's_decathlon).[^cite_note-188] Swiss and Austrian skiers boycotted the [1936 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1936_Winter_Olympics) in support of their skiing teachers, who were not allowed to compete because they earned money with their sport and were thus considered professionals.[^cite_note-189]

The advent of the state-sponsored "full-time amateur athlete" of the [Eastern Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Bloc) countries eroded the ideology of the pure [amateur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amateur_sports#Olympics), as it put the self-financed amateurs of the Western countries at a disadvantage. The [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union_at_the_Olympics) entered teams of athletes who were all nominally students, soldiers, or working in a profession, but in reality they were all paid by the state to train on a full-time basis.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEassom1994120–123-187][^cite_note-190][^cite_note-cia.gov-191] The situation greatly disadvantaged American and Western European athletes, and this was a significant factor in the decline of American medal hauls in the 1970s and 1980s.[^cite_note-192] As a result, the Olympics shifted away from amateurism, as envisioned by Pierre de Coubertin, to allowing participation of [professional athletes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professional_athletes),[^cite_note-Amateurism-193] but only in the 1990s, after the [collapse of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collapse_of_the_Soviet_Union) and its influence within the [IOC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IOC).[^cite_note-194][^cite_note-195][^cite_note-196]

#### Team Canada ice hockey dispute

Near the end of the 1960s, the [Canadian Amateur Hockey Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Amateur_Hockey_Association) (CAHA) felt their amateur players could no longer be competitive against the Soviet team's full-time athletes and the other constantly improving European teams. They pushed for the ability to use players from professional leagues but met opposition from the [International Ice Hockey Federation](16760449.md) (IIHF) and IOC. At the IIHF Congress in 1969, the IIHF decided to allow Canada to use nine non-NHL professional hockey players[^cite_note-Num17-197] at the 1970 World Championships in [Montreal](7954681.md) and [Winnipeg](100730.md), Canada.[^cite_note-198] The decision was reversed in January 1970 after Brundage said that ice hockey's status as an Olympic sport would be in jeopardy if the change was made.[^cite_note-Num17-197] In response, Canada withdrew from international ice hockey competition and officials stated that they would not return until "[open competition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_\(sport\))" was instituted.[^cite_note-Num17-197][^cite_note-199] [Günther Sabetzki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%BCnther_Sabetzki) became president of the IIHF in 1975 and helped to resolve the dispute with the CAHA. In 1976, the IIHF agreed to allow "open competition" between all players in the World Championships. However, NHL players were still not allowed to play in the Olympics until 1988, because of the IOC's amateur-only policy.[^cite_note-200]

## Controversies

### Boycotts

[Australia](4689264.md), [France](5843419.md), [Greece](12108.md), [Switzerland](26748.md) and the [United Kingdom](31717.md) are the only countries to be represented at every Olympic Games since their inception in 1896. While countries sometimes miss an Olympics due to a lack of qualified athletes, some choose to boycott a celebration of the Games for various reasons. The [Olympic Council of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Council_of_Ireland) withdrew from the [1936 Berlin Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1936_Summer_Olympics), because the IOC insisted its team needed to be restricted to the [Irish Free State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Free_State) rather than representing the entire island of Ireland.[^cite_note-Krüger_Murray_2003_p230-201]

There were three boycotts of the [1956 Melbourne Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1956_Summer_Olympics): the [Netherlands](21148.md), [Spain](26667.md), and [Switzerland](26748.md) refused to attend because of the repression of the [Hungarian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_Revolution_of_1956) by the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) but did send an equestrian delegation to Stockholm; [Cambodia](334751.md), [Egypt](8087628.md), [Iraq](7515928.md), and [Lebanon](17771.md) boycotted the Games because of the [Suez Crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_Crisis); and the [People's Republic of China](5405.md) boycotted the Games due to the participation of the [Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_China), composed of athletes coming from [Taiwan](25734.md).[^cite_note-Boycott_1-202]

In [1972](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1972_Summer_Olympics) and [1976](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1976_Summer_Olympics) a large number of African countries threatened the IOC with a boycott to force them to ban [South Africa](17416221.md) and [Rhodesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodesia), because of their [segregationist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Segregationist) rule. [New Zealand](4913064.md) was also one of the African boycott targets, because [its national rugby union team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_national_rugby_union_team) had toured [apartheid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apartheid)-ruled South Africa. The IOC conceded in the first two cases but refused to ban New Zealand on the grounds that rugby was not an Olympic sport.[^cite_note-CBC_boycott-203] Fulfilling their threat, twenty African countries were joined by [Guyana](17238691.md) and Iraq in a withdrawal from the Montreal Games, after a few of their athletes had already competed.[^cite_note-CBC_boycott-203][^cite_note-204]

The Republic of China (Taiwan) was excluded from the [1976 Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1976_Summer_Olympics) by order of [Pierre Trudeau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Trudeau), the prime minister of Canada. Trudeau's action was widely condemned as having brought shame on Canada for having succumbed to political pressure to keep the Chinese delegation from competing under its name.[^cite_note-205] The ROC refused a proposed compromise that would have still allowed them to use the [ROC flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_Taiwan) and [anthem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Anthem_of_the_Republic_of_China) as long as the name was changed.[^cite_note-206] Athletes from Taiwan did not participate again until the [1984 Los Angeles Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1984_Summer_Olympics), when they returned under the name of Chinese Taipei and with a special flag and anthem.[^cite_note-corbit-207]

In 1980 and 1984, the [Cold War](325329.md) opponents boycotted each other's Games. The [United States](3434750.md) and sixty-five other countries boycotted the [Moscow Olympics in 1980](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980_Summer_Olympics) because of the [Soviet invasion of Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet%E2%80%93Afghan_War). This boycott reduced the number of nations participating to 80, the lowest number since 1956.[^cite_note-208] The Soviet Union and 15 other nations countered by boycotting the Los Angeles Olympics of 1984. Although a boycott led by the Soviet Union depleted the field in certain sports, 140 National Olympic Committees took part, which was a record at the time.[^cite_note-olympic.org-6] The fact that Romania, a Warsaw Pact country, opted to compete despite Soviet demands led to a warm reception of the Romanian team by the United States. When the Romanian athletes entered during the opening ceremonies, they received a standing ovation from the spectators, who comprised mostly U.S. citizens. The boycotting nations of the Eastern Bloc staged their own alternate event, the [Friendship Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friendship_Games), in July and August.[^cite_note-1980_Boycott-209][^cite_note-1980_Boycott_2-210]

There had been growing calls for boycotts of Chinese goods and the 2008 Olympics in Beijing in protest of China's [human rights record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights_in_China), and in response to [Tibetan disturbances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Tibetan_unrest). Ultimately, no nation supported a boycott.[^cite_note-211][^cite_note-EUTibet-212] In August 2008, the government of [Georgia](48768.md) called for a boycott of the [2014 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Winter_Olympics), set to be held in [Sochi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sochi), Russia, in response to Russia's participation in the [2008 South Ossetia war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_South_Ossetia_war).[^cite_note-213][^cite_note-214] Continuing human rights violations in China have led to "[diplomatic boycotts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concerns_and_controversies_at_the_2022_Winter_Olympics#Diplomatic_boycotts_or_non-attendance)", where athletes still compete at the Games but diplomats do not attend, of the [2022 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Winter_Olympics) in Beijing by several countries, most notably the United States and United Kingdom.[^cite_note-215][^cite_note-216]

### Politics



[Jesse Owens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesse_Owens) on the podium after winning the long jump at the [1936 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1936_Summer_Olympics), where he won four gold medals; in his 1980 obituary, *[The New York Times](30680.md)* called him "perhaps the greatest and most famous athlete in [track and field](194904.md) history".

[^cite_note-217]



Gold medallist [Tommie Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tommie_Smith) (centre) and bronze medallist [John Carlos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Carlos) (right) showing the [raised fist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raised_fist) on the podium after the 200 m race at the [1968 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1968_Summer_Olympics); both are wearing [Olympic Project for Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Project_for_Human_Rights) badges. [Peter Norman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Norman) (silver medallist, left) from Australia is also wearing an OPHR badge, in solidarity with Smith and Carlos.

The Olympic Games have been used as a platform to promote political ideologies almost from its inception. Nazi Germany wished to portray the [National Socialist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Party) as benevolent and peace-loving when they hosted the [1936 Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1936_Summer_Olympics), though they used the Games to display [Aryan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryan) superiority.[^cite_note-FindlingPelle-33]<sup>: 107 </sup> Germany was the most successful nation at the Games, adding support to their allegations of Aryan supremacy, but the message was somewhat blunted by the notable victories of African American [Jesse Owens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesse_Owens), who won four gold medals, and [Hungarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungary_at_the_1936_Summer_Olympics) Jew [Ibolya Csák](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibolya_Cs%C3%A1k).[^cite_note-FindlingPelle-33]<sup>: 111–112 </sup> The [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union_at_the_Olympics) did not participate until the [1952 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1952_Summer_Olympics) in Helsinki. Instead, starting in 1928, the Soviets organised an international sports event called [Spartakiads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spartakiad). During the interwar period of the 1920s and 1930s, communist and socialist organisations in several countries, including the United States, attempted to counter what they called the "bourgeois" Olympics with the [Workers Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_Workers'_Sport_International).[^cite_note-GSE-218][^cite_note-FOOTNOTERoche2000106-219] It was not until the [1956 Summer Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union_at_the_1956_Summer_Olympics) that the Soviets emerged as a sporting superpower and, in doing so, took full advantage of the publicity that came with winning at the Olympics.[^cite_note-220] Soviet Union's success might be attributed to a heavy state's investment in sports to fulfill its political agenda on an international stage.[^cite_note-221][^cite_note-cia.gov-191]

Individual athletes have also used the Olympic stage to promote their own political agenda. At the [1968 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1968_Summer_Olympics) in Mexico City, two American track and field athletes, [Tommie Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tommie_Smith) and [John Carlos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Carlos), who finished first and third in the 200 metres, performed the [Black Power salute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1968_Olympics_Black_Power_salute) on the victory stand. The second-place finisher, [Peter Norman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Norman) of Australia, wore an [Olympic Project for Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Project_for_Human_Rights) badge in support of Smith and Carlos. In response to the protest, IOC president [Avery Brundage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avery_Brundage) ordered Smith and Carlos suspended from the US team and banned from the Olympic Village. When the US Olympic Committee refused, Brundage threatened to ban the entire US track team. This threat led to the expulsion of the two athletes from the Games.[^cite_note-Black_Salute-222] In another notable incident in the gymnastics competition, while standing on the medal podium after the balance beam event final, in which [Natalia Kuchinskaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natalia_Kuchinskaya) of the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) had controversially taken the gold, [Czechoslovak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czechoslovakia) gymnast [Věra Čáslavská](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C4%9Bra_%C4%8C%C3%A1slavsk%C3%A1) quietly turned her head down and away during the playing of the Soviet national anthem. The action was Čáslavská's silent protest against the recent [Soviet invasion of Czechoslovakia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warsaw_Pact_invasion_of_Czechoslovakia). Her protest was repeated when she accepted her medal for her floor exercise routine when the judges changed the preliminary scores of the Soviet [Larisa Petrik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larisa_Petrik) to allow her to tie with Čáslavská for the gold. While Čáslavská's countrymen supported her actions and her outspoken opposition to communism (she had publicly signed and supported [Ludvik Vaculik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludvik_Vaculik)'s "[Two Thousand Words](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Two_Thousand_Words)" manifesto), the new regime responded by banning her from both sporting events and international travel for many years and made her an outcast from society until the fall of communism.

Currently, the government of Iran has taken steps to avoid any competition between its athletes and those from Israel. [Arash Miresmaeili](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arash_Miresmaeili), an Iranian [judoka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judoka), did not compete in a match against an Israeli during the [2004 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Summer_Olympics) in Athens. Although he was officially disqualified for being overweight, Miresmaeli was awarded US\$125,000 in prize money by the Iranian government, an amount paid to all Iranian gold medal winners. He was officially cleared of intentionally avoiding the bout, but his receipt of the prize money raised suspicion.[^cite_note-223]

After the [2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine](79400268.md), the IOC Executive Board recommended "no participation of Russian and Belarusian athletes and officials, urges International Sports Federations and organisers of sports events worldwide to do everything in their power to ensure that no athlete or sports official from Russia or Belarus be allowed to take part under the name of Russia or Belarus".[^cite_note-224] In 2023, the IOC announced that Russian and Belarusian athletes could participate in the Olympics under certain conditions: they must not represent their country or any associated organisation, and those actively supporting the [2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine](79400268.md) would be barred from competing. This decision aimed to allow athletes to compete and enhance their stature. While 32 athletes accepted the invitation, 28 qualified athletes declined. Competing under a neutral flag and uniform, these athletes had a neutral song played if they won any medals, instead of their national anthems. Additionally, the audience was prohibited from waving Russian and Belarusian flags.[^cite_note-225] The ban was lifted in July 2026, allowing Russian athletes to compete at the [2028 Summer Olympics](12695048.md).[^cite_note-226] The ban on Belarusian athletes was previously lifted in May.

### Use of performance-enhancing drugs

Main article: [Doping at the Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doping_at_the_Olympic_Games)



[Thomas Hicks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hicks_\(athlete\)) running the [marathon](49132.md) at the [1904 Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1904_Summer_Olympics) in [St. Louis](27687.md)

In the early 20th century, many Olympic athletes began using drugs to improve their athletic abilities. For example, in 1904, [Thomas Hicks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hicks_\(athlete\)), a gold medallist in the marathon, was given [strychnine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strychnine) by his coach (at the time, taking different substances was allowed, as there was no data regarding the effect of these substances on a body of an athlete).[^cite_note-227] The only Olympic death linked to performance enhancing occurred at the [1960 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1960_Summer_Olympics) in [Rome](25458.md). A Danish cyclist, [Knud Enemark Jensen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knud_Enemark_Jensen), fell from his bicycle and later died. A coroner's inquiry found that he was under the influence of [amphetamines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphetamines).[^cite_note-wadahistory-228] By the mid-1960s, sports federations started to ban the use of performance-enhancing drugs; in 1967, the IOC followed suit.[^cite_note-229]

According to British journalist [Andrew Jennings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Jennings), a [KGB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KGB) colonel stated that the agency's officers had posed as anti-doping authorities from the [International Olympic Committed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Olympic_Committed?action=edit\&redlink=1) to undermine [doping tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doping_test) and that Soviet athletes were "rescued with \[these] tremendous efforts".[^cite_note-DG2011-230] On the topic of the [1980 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980_Summer_Olympics), a 1989 Australian study said "There is hardly a medal winner at the Moscow Games, certainly not a gold medal winner, who is not on one sort of drug or another: usually several kinds. The Moscow Games might as well have been called the Chemists' Games."[^cite_note-DG2011-230]

In 2016, documents obtained revealed the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union)'s plans for a statewide doping system in [track and field](194904.md) in preparation for the [1984 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1984_Summer_Olympics) in [Los Angeles](18110.md). Dated prior to the country's decision to boycott the Games, the document detailed the existing steroids operations of the program, along with suggestions for further enhancements.[^cite_note-NYT160813-231] The communication, directed to the Soviet Union's head of track and field, was prepared by Sergei Portugalov of the Institute for Physical Culture. Portugalov was also one of the main figures involved in the implementation of the Russian doping programme prior to the 2016 Summer Olympics.[^cite_note-NYT160813-231]

The first Olympic athlete to test positive for the use of performance-enhancing drugs was [Hans-Gunnar Liljenwall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans-Gunnar_Liljenwall), a Swedish [pentathlete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_pentathlon) at the [1968 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1968_Summer_Olympics), who lost his bronze medal for alcohol use.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPorterfield200815-232] One of the most publicised doping-related disqualifications occurred after the [1988 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_Summer_Olympics) where Canadian sprinter [Ben Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Johnson_\(Canadian_sprinter\)) (who won the [100-metre dash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athletics_at_the_1988_Summer_Olympics_%E2%80%93_Men's_100_metres)) tested positive for [stanozolol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanozolol).[^cite_note-233]

In 1999, the IOC formed the [World Anti-Doping Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Anti-Doping_Agency) (WADA) in an effort to systematise the research and detection of performance-enhancing drugs. There was a sharp increase in positive drug tests at the [2000 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2000_Summer_Olympics) and [2002 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2002_Winter_Olympics) due to improved testing conditions. Several medallists in weightlifting and cross-country skiing from post-Soviet states were disqualified because of doping offences. The IOC-established drug testing regimen (now known as the Olympic Standard) has set the worldwide benchmark that other sporting federations attempt to emulate.[^cite_note-234] During the Beijing games, 3,667 athletes were tested by the IOC under the auspices of the World Anti-Doping Agency. Both urine and blood tests were used to detect banned substances.[^cite_note-wadahistory-228][^cite_note-235] In London over 6,000 Olympic and Paralympic athletes were tested. Prior to the Games, 107 athletes tested positive for banned substances and were not allowed to compete.[^cite_note-236][^cite_note-237][^cite_note-238]

In 2024, rumors about China's doping spread as many of the athletes, especially in swimming, were accused of doping before Tokyo 2020. 23 Chinese swimmers were tested positive for prohibited substances, with both the World Anti-Doping Agency and World Aquatics accepting Chinese claims that positive tests were caused by tainted food.[^cite_note-239] After the FBI and Justice Department started investigating, the IOC threatened to revoke the 2034 Olympics in Salt Lake City, unless they dropped the investigation.[^cite_note-240]

#### Russian doping scandal

Further information: [Doping in Russia](55980489.md), [McLaren Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McLaren_Report), [Russia at the 2012 Summer Olympics § Russian doping scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia_at_the_2012_Summer_Olympics#Russian_doping_scandal), [Russia at the 2014 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia_at_the_2014_Winter_Olympics), [Russia at the 2016 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia_at_the_2016_Summer_Olympics), [Olympic Athletes from Russia at the 2018 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Athletes_from_Russia_at_the_2018_Winter_Olympics), and [Oswald Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_Commission)

[Doping](860860.md) in [Russian sports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_sports) has a systemic nature. [Russia](3787642.md) has had 44 [Olympic medals stripped](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_stripped_Olympic_medals) for doping violations—the most of any country, and more than a quarter of the global total. From 2011 to 2015, more than a thousand Russian competitors in various sports, including summer, winter, and Paralympic sports, benefited from a [cover-up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cover-up).[^cite_note-EDP-241][^cite_note-McLarenII-242][^cite_note-NYT161209-243][^cite_note-Guardian161209-244] Russia was partially banned from the [2016 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Summer_Olympics) and was banned from the [2018 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Winter_Olympics) (while still being allowed to participate as the "[Olympic Athletes from Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Athletes_from_Russia)") due to the state-sponsored doping programme.[^cite_note-245][^cite_note-246]

In December 2019, Russia was banned for four years from all major sporting events for systematic doping and lying to WADA.[^cite_note-247] The ban was issued by WADA on 9 December 2019, and the Russian anti-doping agency RUSADA had 21 days to make an appeal to the [Court of Arbitration for Sport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_Arbitration_for_Sport) (CAS). The ban meant that Russian athletes would only be allowed to compete under the Olympic flag after passing anti-doping tests.[^cite_note-248] Russia appealed the decision to the CAS.[^cite_note-249] On review of Russia's appeal of its case from WADA, CAS ruled on 17 December 2020 to reduce the penalty that WADA had placed. Instead of banning Russia from sporting events, the ruling allowed Russia to participate at the Olympics and other international events, but for a period of two years the team cannot use the Russian name, flag, or anthem and must present themselves as "Neutral Athlete" or "Neutral Team". The ruling does allow for team uniforms to display "Russia" on the uniform as well as the use of the Russian flag colours within the uniform's design, although the name should be up to equal predominance as the "Neutral Athlete/Team" designation.[^cite_note-250]

In February 2022, during the Beijing Olympics, the international news media reported that the issue of doping was again raised over a positive test for [trimetazidine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trimetazidine) by the ROC's [Kamila Valieva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamila_Valieva),[^cite_note-251][^cite_note-252] which was officially confirmed on 11 February.[^cite_note-253] Valieva's sample in question was taken by the [Russian Anti-Doping Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Anti-Doping_Agency) (RUSADA) at the [2022 Russian Figure Skating Championships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Russian_Figure_Skating_Championships) on 25 December, but the sample was not analyzed at the [World Anti-Doping Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Anti-Doping_Agency) (WADA) laboratory where it was sent for testing until 8 February, one day after the team event concluded.[^cite_note-254] CAS was expected to hear the case on 13 February with a decision scheduled for announcement the next day, ahead of her scheduled appearance in the women's singles event beginning on 15 February.[^cite_note-255][^cite_note-256] Due to Valieva being a minor at the time, as well as being classified as a "protected person" under WADA guidelines, RUSADA and the IOC announced on 12 February that they would broaden the scope of their respective investigations to include members of her entourage (e.g. coaches, team doctors, etc.).[^cite_note-257] By the end of the Beijing Olympics, a total five athletes were reported for doping violations.[^cite_note-258] A decision by RUSADA, issued in mid-October and endorsed by WADA, stated that the details of the Valieva hearing and its scheduled dates would be placed under international guidelines for the protection of minors (Valieva was 15 years old when the positive test results were disclosed) and would not be disclosed publicly.[^cite_note-Evan_Bates-259] Although Russia as a country is currently banned from participating in international skating events due to the [2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine](79400268.md), Valieva has continued to compete within Russian borders without being hindered by RUSADA as recently as the Russian Grand Prix held in October 2022.[^cite_note-260] On 28 September 2023, CAS "ordered the production of further documentation" and announced that her hearing had been adjourned until 9‍–‍10 November. It appeared that one of the parties in the case requested a file that had not previously been a part of the proceedings.[^cite_note-USA_TODAY_2023_z927-261] On 10 November, CAS announced that a final decision was expected by the end of January 2024.[^cite_note-Anon._r079-262][^cite_note-USA_TODAY_2023_q217-263] On 29 January 2024, the CAS disqualified Valieva for four years retroactive to 25 December 2021 for an anti-doping rule violation.[^cite_note-264][^cite_note-265]

### Sex discrimination

Main article: [Participation of women in the Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Participation_of_women_in_the_Olympics)



[Charlotte Cooper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlotte_Cooper_\(tennis\)) of the [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Britain_at_the_Olympics), the first female Olympic champion, in the [1900 Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1900_Summer_Olympics)

Women were first allowed to compete at the [1900 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1900_Summer_Olympics) in Paris, but at the [1992 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1992_Summer_Olympics) 35 countries were still only fielding all-male delegations.[^cite_note-nytimes.com-266] This number dropped rapidly over the following years. In 2000, [Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain_at_the_Olympics) sent two women competitors for the first time: [Fatema Hameed Gerashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatema_Hameed_Gerashi) and [Mariam Mohamed Hadi Al Hilli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariam_Mohamed_Hadi_Al_Hilli).[^cite_note-267] In 2004, [Robina Muqimyar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robina_Muqimyar) and [Fariba Rezayee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fariba_Rezayee) became the first women to compete for [Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghanistan_at_the_Olympics).[^cite_note-268] In 2008, the [United Arab Emirates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Arab_Emirates_at_the_Olympics) sent female athletes for the first time; [Maitha Al Maktoum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maitha_Al_Maktoum) competed in taekwondo, and [Latifa Al Maktoum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latifa_bint_Ahmed_Al_Maktoum) in equestrian. Both athletes were from [Dubai's ruling family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Maktoum).[^cite_note-269]

By 2010, only three countries had never sent female athletes to the Games: [Brunei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brunei_at_the_Olympics), [Saudi Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saudi_Arabia_at_the_Olympics), and [Qatar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qatar_at_the_Olympics). Brunei had taken part in only three celebrations of the Games, sending a single athlete on each occasion, but Saudi Arabia and Qatar had been competing regularly with all-male teams. In 2010, the International Olympic Committee announced it would "press" these countries to enable and facilitate the participation of women for the [2012 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Summer_Olympics) in London. [Anita DeFrantz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anita_DeFrantz), chair of the IOC's Women and Sports Commission, suggested that countries be barred if they prevented women from competing. Shortly thereafter, the [Qatar Olympic Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qatar_Olympic_Committee) announced that it "hoped to send up to four female athletes in [shooting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shooting_at_the_2012_Summer_Olympics) and [fencing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fencing_at_the_2012_Summer_Olympics)" to the 2012 Summer Games.[^cite_note-270]

In 2008, [Ali al-Ahmed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_al-Ahmed), director of the [Institute for Gulf Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Gulf_Affairs), likewise called for Saudi Arabia to be barred from the Games, describing its ban on women athletes as a violation of the International Olympic Committee charter. He noted: "For the last 15 years, many international nongovernmental organisations worldwide have been trying to lobby the IOC for better enforcement of its own laws banning gender discrimination. While their efforts did result in increasing numbers of women Olympians, the IOC has been reluctant to take a strong position and threaten the discriminating countries with suspension or expulsion."[^cite_note-nytimes.com-266] In July 2010, *[The Independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Independent)* reported: "Pressure is growing on the International Olympic Committee to kick out Saudi Arabia, who are likely to be the only major nation not to include women in their Olympic team for 2012. ... Should Saudi Arabia ... send a male-only team to London, we understand they will face protests from equal rights and women's groups which threaten to disrupt the Games".[^cite_note-independent.co.uk-271]

At the 2012 Summer Olympics, every participating nation included female athletes for the first time in Olympic history.[^cite_note-272] Saudi Arabia included two female athletes in its delegation; Qatar, four; and Brunei, one ([Maziah Mahusin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maziah_Mahusin), in the 400 m hurdles). Qatar made one of its first female Olympians, [Bahiya al-Hamad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahiya_al-Hamad) (shooting), its flagbearer at the 2012 Games,[^cite_note-273] and runner [Maryam Yusuf Jamal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryam_Yusuf_Jamal) of [Bahrain](18933277.md) became the first Persian Gulf female athlete to win a medal when she won a bronze for her showing in the [1500 m race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athletics_at_the_2012_Summer_Olympics_%E2%80%93_Women's_1500_metres).[^cite_note-274]

The only sports on the Olympic programme that features men and women individually competing against one another are the equestrian disciplines, as there is no "Women's Eventing", or "Men's Dressage". As of 2008, there were still more medal events for men than women. With the addition of women's boxing to the programme in the 2012 Summer Olympics, however, women athletes were able to compete in all the sports open to men.[^cite_note-275] In the winter Olympics, women are still unable to compete in the [Nordic combined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nordic_combined).[^cite_note-276] After men were included in artistic swimming at the Paris 2024 games,[^cite_note-277] the only remaining Olympic event in which male athletes may not compete is [rhythmic gymnastics](189053.md).[^cite_note-278] Despite being eligible to qualify for the 2024 Paris games, no men were included on any artistic swimming team.[^cite_note-279]

A recent addition to the Games has been the inclusion of mixed events, whereby men and women of the same nation compete together against other teams. Beginning in 2018, the sport of [curling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curling) introduced a mixed event where teams of one man and one woman competed in their own tournament for a medal. As of the 2024 Paris games, there are now 13 mixed medal events across 11 disciplines at the summer games.[^cite_note-280]

### War and terrorism

The [world wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_war) caused three Olympiads to pass without a celebration of the Games: the [1916 Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1916_Summer_Olympics) were cancelled because of [World War I](4764461.md), and the summer and winter games of 1940 and 1944 were cancelled because of World War II.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHines2011xxii-49] The [Russo-Georgian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Georgian_War) between Georgia and Russia erupted on the opening day of the [2008 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Summer_Olympics) in Beijing. Both American President [George W. Bush](3414021.md) and Russian Prime Minister [Vladimir Putin](32817.md) were attending the Olympics at that time and spoke together about the conflict at a luncheon hosted by the Chinese president [Hu Jintao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hu_Jintao).[^cite_note-MSNBC_2009-01-30-281][^cite_note-282]

Terrorism most directly affected the Olympic Games in 1972. When the [Summer Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1972_Summer_Olympics) were held in [Munich](19058.md), Germany, eleven members of the [Israeli Olympic team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel_at_the_1972_Summer_Olympics) were taken hostage by the [Palestinian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestine_Liberation_Organization) terrorist group [Black September](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_September_Organization) in what is now known as the [Munich massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munich_massacre). The terrorists killed two of the athletes soon after taking them hostage and killed the other nine during a failed liberation attempt. A German police officer and five of the terrorists also died.[^cite_note-283] Following the selection of [Barcelona](4443.md), Spain, to host the [1992 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1992_Summer_Olympics), the separatist [ETA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETA_\(separatist_group\)) terrorist organisation launched attacks in the region, including the [1991 bombing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vic_bombing) in the Catalonian city of [Vic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vic,_Catalonia) that killed ten people.[^cite_note-284][^cite_note-285]

Terrorism affected two Olympic Games held in the United States. During the [1996 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_Summer_Olympics) in Atlanta, [a bomb was detonated at the Centennial Olympic Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centennial_Olympic_Park_bombing), killing two people and injuring 111 others. The bomb was set by [Eric Rudolph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Rudolph), who is serving a life sentence for the bombing.[^cite_note-286] The [2002 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2002_Winter_Olympics) in Salt Lake City took place just five months after the [September 11 attacks](5058690.md), which meant a higher level of security than ever before provided for an Olympic Games. The opening ceremonies of the Games featured symbols relating to 9/11, including the flag that flew at [Ground Zero](67173.md) and honour guards of [NYPD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NYPD) and [FDNY](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FDNY) members.[^cite_note-287]

## Citizenship

### IOC rules for citizenship

The Olympic Charter requires that an athlete be a [legal national](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationality) of the country for which they compete. Dual nationals may compete for either country, as long as three years have passed since the competitor competed for the former country. However, if the NOCs and IF involved agree, then the IOC executive board may reduce or cancel this period.[^cite_note-Olympic_Charter_2011-288] This waiting period exists only for athletes who previously competed for one nation and want to compete for another. If an athlete gains a new or second nationality, then they do not need to wait any designated amount of time before participating for the new or second nation. The IOC is only concerned with issues of citizenship and nationality after individual nations have granted citizenship to athletes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEShachar20112114–2116-289]

In October 2023, the IOC suspended the [Russian Olympic Committee](25045856.md) for incorporating the [four annexed Ukrainian regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_annexation_of_Donetsk,_Kherson,_Luhansk_and_Zaporizhzhia_oblasts) into its charter, and the suspension was upheld in February 2024 by [CAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_Arbitration_for_Sport).[^cite_note-290] Without any NOC from Russia to approve a change of citizenship in under three years from Russia to another country, the IOC executive board approved changes of nationality in November 2023 and March 2024 for a total of five athletes from Russia to another country, including [Georgi Tiblov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgii_Tibilov_\(wrestler,_born_2000\)) and [Aleksandr Komarov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleksandr_Komarov_\(wrestler\)) in wrestling (from Russia to Serbia), [Mikhail Yakovlev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Iakovlev) in cycling (from Russia to Israel), [Valeriia Liubimova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valeriia_Liubimova?action=edit\&redlink=1) in cycling, and [Anastasiia Kirpichnikova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anastasiia_Kirpichnikova) in swimming (both from Russia to France).[^cite_note-291][^cite_note-292][^cite_note-293][^cite_note-294]

### Reasons for changing citizenship

Occasionally, an athlete will become a citizen of a different country to enable them to compete in the Olympics. This is often because they are attracted to sponsorship deals or training facilities in the other country, or the athlete might be unable to qualify from within their country of birth. In preparation for the 2014 Winter Olympics in Sochi, the Russian Olympic Committee naturalised a South Korean-born short-track speed-skater, [Ahn Hyun-soo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahn_Hyun-soo), and an American-born snowboarder, [Vic Wild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vic_Wild). The two athletes won five gold medals and one bronze medal between them at the 2014 Games.[^cite_note-autogenerated1-295]

## Champions and medallists

Further information: [Lists of Olympic medallists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lists_of_Olympic_medallists) and [List of multiple Olympic gold medallists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_multiple_Olympic_gold_medallists)



[Paavo Nurmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paavo_Nurmi), legendary [Finnish](10577.md) [middle-distance](89280.md) and [long-distance runner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long-distance_runner), nicknamed the "[Flying Finn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flying_Finn)", set 22 [world records](3443107.md) at distances between [1500 m](2707254.md) and 20 km, winning nine [gold](526488.md) and three [silver](526647.md) medals in the 1920 through 1928 Olympic Games.

Medals are awarded to the athletes or teams who place first, second, and third in each event. The winners receive gold medals, which were solid gold until 1912, later made of gilded silver, and now gold-plated silver. Every gold medal must contain at least six grams of pure gold.[^cite_note-BeijingOlymp-1-296] The runners-up are awarded silver medals and the third-place athletes receive bronze medals. In events contested by a [single-elimination tournament](939680.md) (most notably boxing), third place might not be determined and the losers of both semi-finals each receive a bronze medal.

At the [1896 Olympics](48644.md), only the winner and runner-up of each event received medals—silver for first and bronze for second, with no gold medals awarded. The current three-medal format was introduced at the [1904 Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1904_Summer_Olympics).[^cite_note-297] From 1948 onward, athletes placing fourth, fifth, and sixth have received certificates, which came to be known officially as [Olympic diplomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_diploma); from 1984, these have also been awarded to the seventh- and eighth-place finishers. At the [2004 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Summer_Olympics) in Athens, the gold, silver, and bronze medal winners were also presented with [olive wreaths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olive_wreath).[^cite_note-298] The [IOC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IOC) does not keep statistics of medals won on a national level (except for team sports), but the NOCs and the media record medal statistics and use them as a measure of each nation's success.[^cite_note-299]

## Nations

### Participants

Main articles: [List of participating nations at the Summer Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_participating_nations_at_the_Summer_Olympic_Games) and [List of participating nations at the Winter Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_participating_nations_at_the_Winter_Olympic_Games)

As of the [2020 Summer Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Summer_Games) in Tokyo, all of the current 207 NOCs (and 19 obsolete NOCs) have participated in at least one edition of the [Summer Olympics](27566.md). Competitors from five nations—[Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australia_at_the_Olympics), [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France_at_the_Olympics),[^cite_note-302] [Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Britain_at_the_Olympics),[^cite_note-304] [Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greece_at_the_Olympics), and [Switzerland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Switzerland_at_the_Olympics)[^cite_note-307]—have competed in all 28 Summer Olympics. Athletes competing under the Olympic flag, Mixed Teams and the [Refugee Team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refugee_Olympic_Team_at_the_2016_Summer_Olympics) have competed at six Summer Olympics Games.

A total of 119 NOCs (110 of the current 207 NOCs, and nine obsolete NOCs) have participated in at least one edition of the [Winter Olympics](34069.md). Competitors from 12 nations—[Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria_at_the_Olympics), [Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canada_at_the_Olympics), [Finland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finland_at_the_Olympics), France, Great Britain, [Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungary_at_the_Olympics), [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italy_at_the_Olympics), [Norway](3630909.md), [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland_at_the_Olympics), [Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweden_at_the_Olympics), Switzerland, and the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_at_the_Olympics)—have participated in all 23 Winter Games to date.

### Host nations and cities

Main article: [List of Olympic Games host cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Olympic_Games_host_cities)

See also: [List of Olympic medals by host nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Olympic_medals_by_host_nation)



Map of [Summer Olympics](27566.md) locations with countries that have hosted one Summer Olympics shaded in green and countries that have hosted two or more shaded in blue



Map of [Winter Olympics](34069.md) locations with countries that have hosted one Winter Olympics shaded in green and countries that have hosted two or more shaded in blue

The host city for an Olympic Games had historically been chosen seven to eight years ahead of their celebration. Beginning with the 2024 and 2028 Olympics selection process in 2017, the IOC has proceeded to announce the winning bid with a longer lead-in time in order to provide time for the winning cities/regions to prepare.[^cite_note-bidpro-308][^cite_note-309] The process of selection is carried out in two phases that span a two-year period. The prospective host city applies to its country's National Olympic Committee; if more than one city from the same country submits a proposal to its NOC, the national committee typically holds an internal selection, since only one city per NOC can be presented to the International Olympic Committee for consideration. Once the deadline for submission of proposals by the NOCs is reached, the first phase (Application) begins with the applicant cities asked to complete a questionnaire regarding several key criteria related to the organisation of the Olympic Games.[^cite_note-hostcity-310] In this form, the applicants must give assurances that they will comply with the Olympic Charter and with any other regulations established by the IOC Executive Committee.[^cite_note-bidpro-308] The evaluation of the filled questionnaires by a specialised group provides the IOC with an overview of each applicant's project and their potential to host the Games. On the basis of this technical evaluation, the IOC executive board selects the applicants that will proceed to the candidature stage.[^cite_note-hostcity-310]

Once the candidate cities are selected, they must submit to the IOC a bigger and more detailed presentation of their project as part of a candidature file. Each city is thoroughly analysed by an evaluation commission. This commission will also visit the candidate cities, interviewing local officials and inspecting prospective venue sites, before submitting a report on its findings one month prior to the IOC's final decision. During the interview process the candidate city must also guarantee that it will be able to fund the Games.[^cite_note-bidpro-308] After the work of the evaluation commission, a list of candidates is presented to the General Session of the IOC, which must assemble in a country that does not have a candidate city in the running. The IOC members gathered in the Session have the final vote on the host city. Once elected, the host city bid committee (together with the NOC of the respective country) signs a Host City Contract with the IOC, officially becoming an Olympic host nation and host city.[^cite_note-bidpro-308]

By 2032, the Olympic Games will have been hosted by 47 cities in 23 countries. As of 2021, since the 1988 Summer Olympics in Seoul, South Korea, the Olympics have been held in [Asia](689.md) or [Oceania](22621.md) four times, a sharp increase compared to the previous 92 years of modern Olympic history. The 2016 Games in [Rio de Janeiro](25936.md) were the first Olympics for a South American country. No bids from countries in Africa have succeeded, although Egypt (thrice) and South Africa (in 2004) have tried.[^cite_note-311]
