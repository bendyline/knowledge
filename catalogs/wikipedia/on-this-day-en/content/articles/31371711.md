The **Belarusian opposition** consists of groups and individuals in [Belarus](3457.md) seeking to challenge, from 1988 to 1991, the authorities of [Soviet Belarus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byelorussian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic), and since 1995, the leader of the country [Alexander Lukashenko](156589.md), whom supporters of the movement often consider to be a [dictator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictator).[^cite_note-3] Supporters of the movement tend to call for a [parliamentary democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_democracy) based on a [Western model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_democracy), with [freedom of speech](21401843.md) and [political](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pluralist_democracy) and [religious pluralism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_pluralism). The opposition movement vary in political ideology, ranging from [liberals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberalism), [socialists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism), [anarchists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anarchism_in_Belarus), and [Polish minority activists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poles_in_Belarus) to [Belarusian nationalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belarusian_nationalism).

## Background

See also: [Human rights in Belarus](21401671.md) and [Censorship in Belarus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship_in_Belarus)

The modern Belarusian democracy movement originated in the late 1980s when [Mikhail Gorbachev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Gorbachev)'s [Perestroika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perestroika) and the [Chernobyl disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernobyl_disaster) exposed the serious shortcomings of the [Soviet system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics_of_the_Soviet_Union) and galvanized a significant section of Belarusians around the issues of environment, [de-Stalinization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De-Stalinization), national revival and democratic change.[^cite_note-4]

The [dissolution of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union) brought about a brief period of democracy from 1991 to 1994. However, since his election in 1994, [Alexander Lukashenko](156589.md) established an authoritarian rule by 1996, creating a [political system](68948.md) which the [United Nations Human Rights Council](635790.md) (UNHRC) stated is "incompatible with the concept of human rights".[^cite_note-5]

## History

### 1988 anti-Soviet protests

On 3 June 1988, the [Minsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minsk)-based weekly "Litaratura i mastatstva" ("Literature And Art") published an article by archeologists [Zianon Pazniak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zianon_Pazniak) and Yauhen Shmyhalyou about the unearthing of 500 mass graves of [Stalinist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stalinism) victims in [Kurapaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurapaty) on the outskirts of the Belarusian capital. The article was the first publication in Belarus about crimes of the Soviet-era authorities. This was followed in October that year by the establishment of the [Martyrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martyrology) of Belarus to commemorate the victims of communism, and an organizational committee for the creation of the [Belarusian Popular Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belarusian_Popular_Front), which subsequently became an ardent advocate of Belarus's independence from the Soviet Union.[^cite_note-\:0-6]

On 30 October 1988, riot police in Minsk violently dispersed a mass demonstration to commemorate the victims of Stalinism at Kurapaty – the first of many such clashes in modern Belarusian history.[^cite_note-\:0-6]

### Establishment of the Belarusian Popular Front













Anti-communist demonstration in the Supreme Soviet of Belarus, 7 November 1990

On 24 and 25 June 1989, the Belarusian Popular Front “Revival” ([Belarusian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belarusian_language): Адраджэньне, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Belarusian): *Adradžeńnie*) was formally established with Pazniak as chairman.[^cite_note-\:0-6]

This group carried out an anti-communist manifestation on 7 November 1990, the anniversary of the October Revolution, under the Lenin Monument in [Minsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minsk) in front of the [Supreme Soviet of the Byelorussian SSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Soviet_of_the_Byelorussian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic), in memory of the regime's victims.

### 1991 Belarusian Strikes

Main article: [1991 Belarusian strikes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1991_Belarusian_strikes)

The 1991 Belarusian Strikes were a series of nationwide strikes and pro-independence rallies against the Soviet authorities and their policies. Falling living standards and unemployment along with [Glasnost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glasnost) and Perestroika policies also sparked massive demonstrations and unrest by mostly young people, demanding democracy and leading labour protests across Belarus.

&#x20;In 1990, Belarus held its [first competitive parliamentary election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1990_Byelorussian_Supreme_Soviet_election) to the [Supreme Soviet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Soviet_of_the_Byelorussian_SSR), which upon the [dissolution of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union) declared Belarus an independent nation.[^cite_note-\:0-6][^cite_note-\:1-7]



[Zianon Pazniak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zianon_Pazniak), leader of the [Belarusian Popular Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belarusian_Popular_Front), the main anti-communist and anti-Lukashenko movement from 1988 to 1996.

### Election of Lukashenko

On 23 June and 10 July 1994, Belarus held its [first presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1994_Belarusian_presidential_election), won by [Lukashenko](156589.md). From 1995, he began to consolidate his power at the expense of the [Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_Belarus) and other institutions.[^cite_note-\:0-6][^cite_note-\:1-7]

### 1995–1996 Belarusian constitutional crisis

Main article: [1995–1996 Belarusian constitutional crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1995%E2%80%931996_Belarusian_constitutional_crisis)

Lukashenko engaged in a visible and brutal confrontation with the [Supreme Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Council_of_Belarus) and [Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Belarus), using violence and bomb hoax to disperse the hunger strike of opposition-aligned parliamentarians that stayed in a Supreme Council building for a nighth from 11 to 12 April 1995, harassing them and forcing to accept a referendum that liquidated national [white-red white flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White-red-white_flag) and replaced it with a one similar to the [Byelorussian Soviet Socialist Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byelorussian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic).

Lukashenko was threatened from summer to November 1996, when opposition voted 73 from 199 votes to [impeach him](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impeachment_of_Alexander_Lukashenko?action=edit\&redlink=1), with support of the Supreme Court. However, [Russia](25391.md) intervened with [Prime Minister](339344.md) [Viktor Chernomyrdin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viktor_Chernomyrdin) acted like a mediatiator, which de facto supported and saved Lukashenko and helped him to ultimately dissolve the Supreme Council via [referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_Belarusian_referendum).

### Minsk Spring (1996–97)

Main article: [Minsk Spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minsk_Spring)

A series of mass street protests called “Minsk Spring” or “Belarusian Spring” took place in the springs of 1996–97 triggered by a [constitutional referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_Belarusian_referendum) on amendments to the 1994 [Constitution of Belarus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Belarus). The Belarusian political system became increasingly authoritarian with the government seeking to curtail all political freedoms.[^cite_note-8]

### Charter 97

Main article: [Charter 97](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charter_97)

[Charter 97](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charter_97) is a [human rights](13831.md) group taking its inspiration from the 1997 declaration calling for democracy in [Belarus](3457.md). The document – whose title deliberately echoes the [Czechoslovak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czechoslovakia) human rights declaration [Charter 77](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charter_77) twenty years earlier – was created on the anniversary of the referendum held in 1996, and which, in the words of the organization, declares "devotion to the principles of independence, freedom and democracy, respect to the human rights, solidarity with everybody, who stands for elimination of dictatorial regime and restoration of democracy in Belarus."

### Freedom March (1999)

Main article: [Freedom March (Belarus)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_March_\(Belarus\))

On 17 October 1999, dozens of Belarusians took to the streets to rally against the impending implementation of the [Union State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_State) of Belarus with [Russia](25391.md), forming a [confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederation) and ending Belarus' independence. In response to the march, the Belarusian government suspended further integration between the two states.

### Jeans Revolution (2006)

Main article: [Jeans Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeans_Revolution)



Protests at October Square in Minsk, 19 March 2006

The [Jeans Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeans_Revolution) was a term used by the opposition in [Belarus](3457.md) and its supporters to describe their effort and aspirations[^cite_note-9] on democratic changes in Belarus, in the period leading up to the [2006 presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_Belarusian_presidential_election).

### 2010 presidential election

Main article: [2010 Belarusian presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Belarusian_presidential_election)

After the [2010 Belarusian presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Belarusian_presidential_election), up to 40,000 people[^cite_note-10] protested against Lukashenko. Up to 700 opposition activists, including 7 presidential candidates, were arrested in the post-election crackdown.[^cite_note-11]

Several websites of the opposition and opposition candidates were also blocked or hacked.[^cite_note-12] [Facebook](7529378.md), [Twitter](9988187.md), [YouTube](3524766.md), [Google Talk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Talk), many [email](9738.md) services and [LiveJournal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LiveJournal) were also blocked.[^cite_note-13] The headquarters of Charter 97, the opposition group and website, was stormed by Lukashenko's security forces and all of its staff were arrested.[^cite_note-14]

According to [The Independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Independent), Lukashenko's security forces went after his opponents "with a ferocity that would not have looked out of place in Soviet times".[^cite_note-Independent_8_March_2011-15]

### 2011 protests

Main article: [2011 Belarusian protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Belarusian_protests)

A series of protests influenced by [a serious economic crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Belarus#Crisis_of_2011) took place in 2011. As a result of these protests, on 29 July, the government banned assemblies and gatherings.[^cite_note-16]

### 2017 protests

Main article: [2017 Belarusian protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_Belarusian_protests)

Due to an ongoing [economic recession](25382.md), continuing since the last series of protests in 2015, due to falling gasoline prices, that year a law was passed taxing the unemployed.[^cite_note-parasite-17] Roughly 470,000 Belarusians are obliged to pay the tax but only about 10% have since it was issued.[^cite_note-parasite-17]

Approximately 2,500 protesters[^cite_note-spate-18] filled the streets in the capital of Belarus, [Minsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minsk), on 17 February to protest a policy that required those who work for less than 183 days[^cite_note-19] per year to pay [USD\$](18717338.md)250 for "lost taxes" to help fund welfare policies.[^cite_note-spread-20] This converts to approximately [Rbls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belarusian_ruble) 5 million—a half-month's wages.[^cite_note-parasite-17] The law has proven unpopular and has been mocked in the public as the "law against social parasites".[^cite_note-parasite-17] On 19 February, another 2,000 demonstrated in the second city of [Homieĺ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gomel).[^cite_note-spread-20] Both gatherings were peaceful. Smaller demonstrations were held in other cities.[^cite_note-spate-18]

On 25 March, opposition leader [Vladimir Nekliayev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uladzimir_Nyaklyayew), who was set to speak at the main protest, was allegedly stopped in the morning on his way to Minsk.[^cite_note-21]

The government defended the mass arrests and beatings against citizens by alleging that the police had found "petrol bombs and arms-laden cars" near a protest in Minsk.[^cite_note-arrests-22]

### 2019 protests

Main article: [2019 Belarusian anti-Russian protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Belarusian_anti-Russian_protests)

Another [protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Belarusian_anti-Russian_protests) erupted on 7 December 2019 and their goal was to stop the integration with Russia into a [Union State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_State). These demonstrations were led by [Paval Sieviaryniec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paval_Sieviaryniec) and a new opposition figure [Sergei Tikhanovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergei_Tikhanovsky). They demanded Belarusian sovereignty, resignation of Alexander Lukashenko and [democratization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratization). Belarusian government again used repression against protesters which were quelled on 29 December.[^cite_note-23]

### 2020 presidential election, protests, and partisan movement

Main articles: [2020–2021 Belarusian protests](64399515.md), [2020 Belarusian presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Belarusian_presidential_election), and [Belarusian partisan movement (2020–present)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belarusian_partisan_movement_\(2020%E2%80%93present\))

An image of Belarusian leader of the opposition government-in-exile, Sviatlana Tsikhanouskaya.

Belarusian opposition [government-in-exile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Transitional_Cabinet_of_Belarus) leader, [Sviatlana Tsikhanouskaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sviatlana_Tsikhanouskaya), in 2024

In May 2020, a lowered approval of [Lukashenko](156589.md) amid [his handling of the COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_Belarus) led to street protests and the blogger [Sergei Tikhanovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siarhei_Tsikhanouski) labeling Lukashenko as a cockroach as in the children's poem "[The Monster Cockroach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Monster_Cockroach)", referring to a slipper signifying stamping the insect. Many opposition candidates registered for the [next election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Belarusian_presidential_election) as a result of the movement, but many of them were arrested.[^cite_note-24]

[Mass protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Belarusian_protests) erupted across Belarus following the [2020 Belarusian presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Belarusian_presidential_election) which was marred by allegations of widespread [electoral fraud](382165.md).[^cite_note-Belarus_election_notdemo-25][^cite_note-atlanticcouncil_about_elections-26] Subsequently, opposition presidential candidate [Sviatlana Tsikhanouskaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sviatlana_Tsikhanouskaya), Tikhanovsky's wife, claimed she had won the presidential election with between 60 and 70% of the vote[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28] and formed a [Coordination Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordination_Council_\(Belarus\)) to facilitate the peaceful and orderly transfer of power in Belarus.[^cite_note-29][^cite_note-30] Online groups on [Telegram](41501305.md) such as [Nexta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nexta), as well as smaller decentralised groups, played an important role in the spread of information and coordination of opposition activities.[^cite_note-31] The 2020 presidential campaign attracted an unprecedented number of new actors who entered politics from fields such as business, IT, and marketing.[^cite_note-32]

The suppression of the protests resulted in the intensification of an [ongoing partisan movement within the country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belarusian_partisan_movement_\(2020%E2%80%93present\)), which is backed by the Coordination Council. Leading partisan formations include the [Supraciǔ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supraci%C7%94?action=edit\&redlink=1) (an alliance between the [Cyber Partisans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyber_Partisans), [People's Self Defense Brigades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People's_Self_Defense_Brigades?action=edit\&redlink=1), and [Busly liaciać](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Busly_liacia%C4%87)),[^cite_note-33][^cite_note-34] the [Community of Railway Workers of Belarus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community_of_Railway_Workers_of_Belarus), and [BYPOL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BYPOL), among others.

### Freedom Day

**[Freedom Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_Day_\(Belarus\))** (Belarusian: Дзень Волі, romanized: *Dzień Voli*) is an unofficial holiday in [Belarus](3457.md) celebrated on 25 March to commemorate the declaration of independence by the [Belarusian Democratic Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belarusian_Democratic_Republic) by the [Third Constituent Charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Constituent_Charter) on that date in 1918. The day has been used annually by the Belarusian democracy movement to protest against Alexander Lukashenko since his election.

### Russian invasion of Ukraine

Main article: [Belarusian involvement in the Russian invasion of Ukraine § Opposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belarusian_involvement_in_the_Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine#Opposition)

Protests emerged from the Belarusian opposition condemning Lukashenko's support and involvement with the [Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine) on 27 February 2022, shortly after the war's beginning. According to the Ministry of Internal Affairs, over 800 people had been detained for involvement within these protests.[^cite_note-35][^cite_note-36] Activists from the opposition later engaged in activity to support Ukraine in the conflict, including attacks on the [Belarusian Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belarusian_Railway), a state railroad which had been utilized by Russia to support the [Kyiv offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyiv_offensive_\(2022\)).[^cite_note-c97.3-37][^cite_note-38]
