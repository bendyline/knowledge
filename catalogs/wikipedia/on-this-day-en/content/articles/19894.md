A **mosque** ([/mɒsk/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [*MOSK*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key)), also called a **masjid** ([/ˈmæsdʒɪd, ˈmʌs-/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [*MASS-jid, MUSS-*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key)),[^cite_note-1] is a [place of worship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Place_of_worship) for [Muslims](19541.md).[^cite_note-ODI-2] The term usually refers to a covered building, but can be any place where [Islamic prayers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salah) are performed, such as an outdoor courtyard.[^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4]&#x20;

Originally, mosques were simple places of prayer for the [early Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Muslims), and may have been open spaces rather than elaborate buildings.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGrabar196934-5] In the first stage of [Islamic architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_architecture) (650–750 CE), early mosques comprised open and closed covered spaces enclosed by walls, often with [minarets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minaret), from which the [Islamic call to prayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adhan) was issued on a daily basis.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGrabar196934–35-6] It is typical of mosque buildings to have a special ornamental niche (a *[mihrab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mihrab)*) set into the wall in the direction of the city of [Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecca) (the *[qibla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qibla)*), which Muslims must face during prayer,[^cite_note-ODI-2] as well as a facility for ritual cleansing (*[wudu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wudu)*).[^cite_note-ODI-2][^cite_note-campo-7] The pulpit (*[minbar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minbar)*), from which public sermons (*[khutbah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khutbah)*) are delivered on the event of [Friday prayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friday_prayer), was, in earlier times, characteristic of the central city mosque, but has since become common in smaller mosques.[^cite_note-EIMW-8][^cite_note-ODI-2] To varying degrees, mosque buildings are designed so that there are [segregated spaces for men and women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_and_gender_segregation).[^cite_note-ODI-2] This basic pattern of organization has assumed different forms depending on the region, period, and [Islamic denomination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_schools_and_branches).[^cite_note-campo-7]&#x20;

In addition to being places of worship in [Islam](6037917.md), mosques also serve as locations for [funeral services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_funeral) and [funeral prayers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funeral_prayer_\(Islam\)), marriages ([nikah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marriage_in_Islam)), vigils during [Ramadan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramadan), disputes, collection and distribution of [alms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alms_in_Islam), and [homeless shelters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeless_shelter).[^cite_note-ODI-2][^cite_note-EIMW-8] To this end, mosques have historically been multi-purpose buildings functioning as [community centres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community_centre), [courts of law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahkama), and [religious schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madrasa). In modern times, they have also preserved their role as places of religious instruction and debate.[^cite_note-ODI-2][^cite_note-EIMW-8] Special importance is accorded to, in descending order of importance: [al-Masjid al-Haram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masjid_al-Haram) in the city of Mecca, where [Hajj](23487999.md) and [Umrah](1591421.md) are performed; the [Prophet's Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophet%27s_Mosque) in the city of [Medina](36636.md), where [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad) is buried; and [al-Aqsa Mosque](65759.md) in the city of [Jerusalem](16043.md), where Muslims believe that [Muhammad ascended to heaven to meet God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isra%27_and_Mi%27raj) around 621 CE.[^cite_note-ODI-2]&#x20;

During and after the [early Muslim conquests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Muslim_conquests), mosques were established outside of [Arabia](47858.md) in the hundreds; many [synagogues](48559.md), [churches](6325.md), and [temples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple) were [converted into mosques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversion_of_non-Islamic_places_of_worship_into_mosques) and thus influenced Islamic architectural styles over the centuries.[^cite_note-EIMW-8] While most pre-modern mosques were funded by charitable endowments (*[waqf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waqf)*),[^cite_note-ODI-2] the modern-day trend of government regulation of large mosques has been countered by the rise of privately funded mosques, many of which serve as bases for different streams of [Islamic revivalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_revival) and social activism.[^cite_note-EIMW-8]&#x20;

## Etymology

The word *mosque* entered the [English language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language) from the [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language) *mosquée*, probably derived from [Italian](14708.md) *moschea* (a variant of Italian *moscheta*), from either [Middle Armenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Armenian_language) [մզկիթ](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%D5%B4%D5%A6%D5%AF%D5%AB%D5%A9) (*mzkit*), [Medieval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Greek) [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language): μασγίδιον (*masgídion*), or Spanish *mezquita*, from [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language): مسجد, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Arabic): *masjid* ("site of prostration in prayer" and hence a place of worship), either from [Nabataean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabataean_Aramaic) *masg<sup>ĕ</sup>dhā́* or from [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language): سَجَدَ, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Arabic): *[sajada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sujud)* (meaning "to [prostrate](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/prostrate)"), probably ultimately from [Nabataean Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabataean_Arabic) *masg<sup>ĕ</sup>dhā́* or [Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaic_language) *s<sup>ĕ</sup>ghēdh*.[^cite_note-9]&#x20;

## History

### Origins

See also: [List of the oldest mosques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_the_oldest_mosques)

Islam was established in Arabia during the lifetime of [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad) in the 7th century CE.[^cite_note-Watt2003-10] The [first mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_the_oldest_mosques) in history could be either the sanctuary built around the *[Ka'bah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaaba)* in [Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecca), known today as *[Al-Masjid al-Haram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Masjid_al-Haram)* ('The Sacred Mosque'), or the [Quba Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quba_Mosque) in [Medina](36636.md), the first structure built by Muhammad upon his [emigration from Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hijra_\(Islam\)) in 622 [CE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Era),[^cite_note-11] both located in the Hejaz region in present-day Saudi Arabia.[^cite_note-Palmer2016-12]&#x20;

Other scholars reference Islamic tradition[^cite_note-Esposito1998-13][^cite_note-Esposito2002b-14][^cite_note-Peters2003-15] and passages of the Quran,[^cite_note-16][^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18] according to which Islam as a religion precedes Muhammad, and includes previous prophets such as Abraham.[^cite_note-Alli2013-19] In Islamic tradition, [Abraham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_in_Islam) is credited with having built the *Ka'bah* in Mecca, and consequently its sanctuary, *Al-Masjid al-Haram*, which is seen by Muslims as the first mosque that existed.[^cite_note-20][^cite_note-Michigan_C_1986-21][^cite_note-Abu_Sway_2011-22][^cite_note-Dyrness2013-23] A [hadith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadith) in [Sahih al-Bukhari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahih_al-Bukhari) states that the sanctuary of the *Ka'bah* was the first mosque on Earth, with the second mosque being [Al-Aqsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Aqsa) in [Jerusalem](16043.md),[^cite_note-24] which is also associated with Abraham.[^cite_note-Michigan_C_1986-21] Since as early as 638 CE, the Sacred Mosque of Mecca has been expanded on several occasions to accommodate the increasing number of Muslims who either live in the area or make the annual pilgrimage known as *[Hajj](23487999.md)* to the city.[^cite_note-25]&#x20;

&#x20;Either way, after the Quba Mosque, [Muhammad went on to establish another mosque in Medina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_in_Medina), which is now known as *[Al-Masjid an-Nabawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Masjid_an-Nabawi)* ('The Prophet's Mosque'). Built on the site of his home, Muhammad participated in the construction of the mosque himself and helped pioneer the concept of the mosque as the focal point of the Islamic city.[^cite_note-chiu678-26] The Prophet's Mosque is considered by some scholars of [Islamic architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_architecture) to be the first mosque.[^cite_note-Petersen_p.195–6-27][^cite_note-\:24-28] The mosque had a roof supported by columns made of palm tree trunks[^cite_note-\:0522-29] and it included a large courtyard, a motif common among mosques built since then.[^cite_note-chiu678-26] Rebuilt and expanded over time,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPetersen1996182–184-30] it soon became a larger [hypostyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypostyle) structure.[^cite_note-\:24-28] It probably served as a model for the construction of early mosques elsewhere.[^cite_note-Petersen_p.195–6-27][^cite_note-\:24-28][^cite_note-\:0522-29] It introduced some of the features still common in today's mosques, including the niche at the front of the prayer space known as the *[mihrab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mihrab)* (first added in the [Umayyad period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_period))[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPetersen1996182–184-30] and the tiered pulpit called the *[minbar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minbar)*.[^cite_note-31]

The [Umayyad Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Mosque) in Damascus (715 CE) followed the plan of the [Prophet's Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophet%27s_Mosque) in Medina, and became the earliest example of a grand mosque that served as standard for future mosques

[^cite_note-\:3-32]

The [Umayyad Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Caliphate) was particularly instrumental in spreading Islam and establishing mosques within the [Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant), as the Umayyads constructed among the most revered mosques in the region: the [Qibli Mosque](65759.md) and [Dome of the Rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dome_of_the_Rock) both part of [Al-Aqsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Aqsa) in [Jerusalem](16043.md), and the [Umayyad Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Mosque) in [Damascus](8914.md),[^cite_note-33] and also built other important mosques in the region including the Great Mosques of [Aleppo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Mosque_of_Aleppo) and [Hama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Mosque_of_Hama).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKuban197416-34][^cite_note-\:02-35] They also renovated and expanded other mosques like the [Prophet's Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophet%27s_Mosque) in [Medina](36636.md) (which saw the introduction of the first [mihrab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mihrab))[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAli199933-36] the [Masjid al-Haram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masjid_al-Haram) in Mecca,[^cite_note-\:22-37] the [Great Mosque of Sanaa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Mosque_of_Sanaa),[^cite_note-\:1-38] and the mosques of [Kufa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Mosque_of_Kufa), [Basra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imam_Ali_Mosque_\(Basra\)) and [Amr ibn Al-As](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amr_ibn_al-As_Mosque) (in [Cairo](6293.md)).[^cite_note-\:3-32][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKuban197412-39] The designs of the Dome of the Rock and the Umayyad Mosque were influenced by [Byzantine architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_architecture), a trend that was revived much later with the rise of the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md).[^cite_note-40]&#x20;



The [Great Mosque of Kairouan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Mosque_of_Kairouan) (est. in 670, main current structure dates to 836), in [Tunisia](30188.md), with its minaret being one of the oldest standing minarets in the world&#x20;

The [Great Mosque of Kairouan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Mosque_of_Kairouan) in present-day [Tunisia](30188.md) was the first mosque built in the [Maghreb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maghreb) (northwest Africa), with its present form (dating from the ninth century) serving as a model for other Islamic places of worship in the Maghreb. It was the first in the region to incorporate a square [minaret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minaret), which was characteristic of later Maghrebi mosques, and includes [naves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nave) akin to a [basilica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica).[^cite_note-41][^cite_note-elleh114-42] Those features can also be found in [Andalusi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Andalus) mosques, including the [Great Mosque of Cordoba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Mosque_of_Cordoba), as they tended to reflect the architecture of the [Moors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moors) instead of their [Visigoth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visigoths) predecessors.[^cite_note-elleh114-42] Still, some elements of [Visigothic architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visigothic_architecture), like [horseshoe arches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horseshoe_arch), were infused into the mosque architecture of Spain and the Maghreb.[^cite_note-43]&#x20;

Muslim empires were instrumental in the evolution and spread of mosques. Although mosques were first established in India during the seventh century, they were not commonplace across [the subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent) until the arrival of the [Mughals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire) in the 16th and 17th centuries. Reflecting their [Timurid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timurid_dynasty) origins, [Mughal-style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_architecture) mosques included [onion domes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onion_dome), [pointed arches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogee), and elaborate circular minarets, features common in the [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_architecture) and [Central Asian styles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture_of_Central_Asia).[^cite_note-44] The [Jama Masjid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jama_Masjid,_Delhi) in [Delhi](37756.md) and the [Badshahi Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Badshahi_Mosque) in [Lahore](125315.md), built in a similar manner in the mid-17th century,[^cite_note-45] remain two of the largest mosques on the Indian subcontinent.[^cite_note-46]&#x20;



[Great Mosque of Xi'an](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Mosque_of_Xi%27an) in China established in 742

One of first grand mosques in [East Asia](19605700.md) was established in the eighth century in [Xi'an](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xi%27an) (at the time it was called [Chang'an](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang%27an) and it was the world's largest city).[^cite_note-47] The [Great Mosque of Xi'an](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Mosque_of_Xi%27an), whose current building dates from the 18th century, does not replicate the features often associated with mosques elsewhere.[^cite_note-cow30-48] Minarets were initially prohibited by the state.[^cite_note-ahm109-49] Following traditional [Chinese architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_architecture), the Great Mosque of Xi'an, like many other mosques in eastern China, resembles a [pagoda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pagoda), with a green roof instead of the yellow roof common on imperial structures in China. Mosques in western China were more likely to incorporate elements, like domes and minarets, traditionally seen in mosques elsewhere.[^cite_note-cow30-48]&#x20;



[Kampung Laut Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kampung_Laut_Mosque), built in the 15th century, reflecting the [southeast Asian](28741.md) local architecture.

A similar integration of foreign and local influences could be seen on the [Indonesian](14579.md) islands of [Sumatra](28679.md) and [Java](69336.md), where mosques, including the [Ampel Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampel_Mosque), were first established in the 15th century.[^cite_note-blobla439-50] Early Javanese mosques took design cues from [Hindu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_temple_architecture), [Buddhist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_architecture), and Chinese architectural influences, with tall timber, multi-level roofs similar to the pagodas of [Balinese Hindu temples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balinese_temple); the ubiquitous Islamic dome did not appear in Indonesia until the 19th century.[^cite_note-ahm109-49][^cite_note-51] In turn, the Javanese style influenced the styles of mosques in Indonesia's [Austronesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austronesia) neighbors: [Malaysia](3607937.md), [Brunei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brunei), and the Philippines.[^cite_note-blobla439-50] The Kampung Laut Mosque is considered to be the oldest mosque in Malaysia, its design includes a three-tier pyramidal roofs, the mosque and its minaret are made of timber, while the floor of the whole mosque is raised one meter above ground,[^cite_note-52] which is made for flood protection and natural coolin&#x67;**.**&#x20;



[Great Mosque of Djenné](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Mosque_of_Djenn%C3%A9) built around the 13th century in [Sudano-Sahelian style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudano-Sahelian_architecture)&#x20;

Another indigenous style to West Africa is the [Sudano-Sahelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudano-Sahelian_architecture) with its use of [mudbricks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mudbrick) and [adobe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adobe) plaster, examples of this style include the [Great Mosque of Djenné](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Mosque_of_Djenn%C3%A9) in [Mali](19127.md), the largest mud-brick building in [sub-Saharan Africa](27067.md),[^cite_note-\:242322-53] and the [Agadez Grand Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agadez_Grand_Mosque), in Niger.&#x20;

The Ottomans developed [an architectural style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_architecture) characterized by large central domes (sometimes surrounded by multiple smaller domes), pencil-shaped minarets, and open façades.[^cite_note-54] Mosques from the Ottoman period are still scattered across [Eastern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Europe), but the most rapid growth in the number of mosques in Europe has occurred within the past century as more Muslims have migrated to the continent. Many major European cities are home to mosques, like the [Grand Mosque of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Mosque_of_Paris), that incorporate domes, minarets, and other features often found with mosques in Muslim-majority countries.[^cite_note-blobla193-55] The first mosque in North America was founded by [Albanian Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albanian_American) in 1915, but the continent's oldest surviving mosque, the [Mother Mosque of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mother_Mosque_of_America), was built in 1934.[^cite_note-56] As in Europe, the number of American mosques has rapidly increased in recent decades as Muslim immigrants, particularly from [South Asia](21566765.md), have come in the United States. Greater than forty percent of mosques in the United States were constructed after 2000.[^cite_note-57]&#x20;

## Religious functions

See also: [Congregational mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congregational_mosque)

### Prayers



Eid prayers at the [Badshahi Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Badshahi_Mosque) in Lahore, Pakistan

There are two holidays (*[Eids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_holidays)*) in the [Islamic calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_calendar): *[ʿĪd al-Fiṭr](154944.md)* and *[ʿĪd al-Aḍḥā](358226.md)*, during which there are special prayers held at mosques in the morning. These [Eid prayers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salat_al_Eid) are supposed to be offered in large groups, and so, in the absence of an outdoor *[Eidgah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eidgah)*, a large mosque will normally host them for their congregants as well as the congregants of smaller local mosques. Some mosques will even rent [convention centers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convention_center) or other large public buildings to hold the large number of Muslims who attend. Mosques, especially those in countries where Muslims are the majority, will also host Eid prayers outside in courtyards, [town squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Town_square) or on the outskirts of town in an *Eidgah*.[^cite_note-58][^cite_note-59]&#x20;

### Ramadan



[Iftar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iftar) at [Taipei Grand Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taipei_Grand_Mosque), Taiwan during [Ramadan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramadan)

Islam's holiest month, *[Ramaḍān](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramadan)*, is observed through many events. As Muslims must [fast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sawm) during the day during Ramadan, mosques will host *[Ifṭār](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iftar)* dinners after sunset and the fourth required prayer of the day, that is *[Maghrib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maghrib_\(prayer\))*. Food is provided, at least in part, by members of the community, thereby creating daily [potluck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potluck) dinners. Because of the community contribution necessary to serve *iftar* dinners, mosques with smaller congregations may not be able to host the *iftar* dinners daily. Some mosques will also hold *[Suḥūr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suhoor)* meals before [dawn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dawn) to congregants attending the first required prayer of the day, *[Fajr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fajr)*. As with iftar dinners, congregants usually provide the food for suhoor, although able mosques may provide food instead. Mosques will often invite poorer members of the Muslim community to share in beginning and breaking the fasts, as providing [charity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alms) during Ramadan is regarded in Islam as especially honorable.[^cite_note-60]&#x20;

Following the last obligatory daily prayer (*[ʿIshāʾ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isha%27)*) special, optional *[Tarāwīḥ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarawih)* prayers are offered in larger mosques. During each night of prayers, which can last for up to two hours each night, usually one member of the community who has memorized the entire Quran (a [Hafiz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafiz_\(Quran\))) will recite a segment of the book.[^cite_note-teach-islam-61] Sometimes, several such people (not necessarily of the local community) take turns to do this. During the last ten days of Ramadan, larger mosques will host all-night programs to observe *[Laylat al-Qadr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laylat_al-Qadr)*, the night Muslims believe that Muhammad first received Quranic revelations.[^cite_note-teach-islam-61] On that night, between [sunset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunset) and [sunrise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunrise), mosques employ speakers to educate congregants in attendance about Islam. Mosques or the community usually provide meals periodically throughout the night&#x20;

During the last ten days of [Ramadan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramadan_\(calendar_month\)), larger mosques within the Muslim community will host *[Iʿtikāf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I%CA%BFtik%C4%81f)*, a practice in which at least one Muslim man from the community must participate. Muslims performing itikaf are required to stay within the mosque for ten consecutive days, often in worship or learning about Islam. As a result, the rest of the Muslim community is responsible for providing the participants with food, drinks, and whatever else they need during their stay.[^cite_note-teach-islam-61]&#x20;

### Charity

The third of the [Five Pillars of Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Pillars_of_Islam) states that Muslims are required to give approximately one-fortieth of their wealth to charity as *[Zakat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zakat)*.[^cite_note-62] Since mosques form the center of Muslim communities, they are where Muslims go to both give *zakat* and, if necessary, collect it. Before the holiday of *Eid ul-Fitr*, mosques also collect a special *zakat* that is supposed to assist in helping poor Muslims attend the prayers and celebrations associated with the holiday.&#x20;

## Architecture

Further information: [Islamic architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_architecture)

### Styles



[Mosque of Ibn Tulun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosque_of_Ibn_Tulun) in Cairo (built in 879), is an example of a [hypostyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypostyle) or "Arab-plan" mosque with a courtyard (*[sahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahn)*) surrounded by arcades ([*riwaq*s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riwaq_\(arcade\))) and a colonnaded main prayer hall

*Arab-plan* or [hypostyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypostyle) mosques are the earliest type of mosques, pioneered under the Umayyad Dynasty, and based on the plan of the early [Prophet's mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophet%27s_Mosque) in Medina.[^cite_note-63] These mosques have square or rectangular plans with an enclosed courtyard (*[sahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahn)*) and covered prayer hall. Historically, in the warm Middle Eastern and [Mediterranean climates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_climate), the courtyard served to accommodate the large number of worshippers during Friday prayers. Most early hypostyle mosques had flat roofs on prayer halls, which required the use of numerous [columns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Column) and [supports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Support_\(structure\)).[^cite_note-Masdjid1-64] Frequently, hypostyle mosques have outer [arcades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcade_\(architecture\)) ([*riwaq*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riwaq_\(arcade\))) so that visitors can enjoy the shade. Arab-plan mosques were constructed mostly under the Umayyad and Abbasid dynasties. The simplicity of the Arab plan limited the opportunities for further development, the mosques consequently losing popularity.[^cite_note-Masdjid1-64] One of the most notable hypostyle mosques is the [Great Mosque of Cordoba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosque%E2%80%93Cathedral_of_C%C3%B3rdoba) in Spain, the building being supported by over 850 columns,[^cite_note-mit-handout-65] other notable examples include the Umayyad Mosques of [Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Mosque) and [Aleppo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Mosque_of_Aleppo) in Syria, the Great Mosques of [Samarra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samarra) ([Al-Malwiya Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Mosque_of_Samarra) and [Abu Dulaf Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Dulaf_Mosque)) in Iraq and the [Qibli Mosque](65759.md) (the main mosque within the [Al-Aqsa Mosque Compound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Aqsa)) in Jerusalem,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBloomBlair2009550-66] in addition to the [Great Mosque of Kairouan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Mosque_of_Kairouan), and [Al-Zaytuna Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Zaytuna_Mosque) in Tunisia and [Al-Qarawiyyin Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_al-Qarawiyyin) in Fez.[^cite_note-67]&#x20;



Main courtyard of the [Jameh Mosque of Isfahan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jameh_Mosque_of_Isfahan), with a [four-iwan layout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-iwan_layout) typical of mosques in [Iran](14653.md) and [Central Asia](6742.md)

The first departure within mosque design started in Persia (Iran). The [Persians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_people) had inherited a rich [architectural legacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_architecture) from the earlier Persian dynasties, and they began incorporating elements from earlier [Parthian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthian_Empire) and [Sassanid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sassanid_Empire) designs into their mosques, influenced by buildings such as the [Palace of Ardashir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_Ardashir) and the [Sarvestan Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarvestan_Palace).[^cite_note-autogenerated1-68] Thus, [Islamic architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_architecture) witnessed the introduction of such structures as domes and large, arched entrances, referred to as *[iwans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iwan)*. During [Seljuq rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seljuq_dynasty), as [Islamic mysticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism) was on the rise, the [four-iwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shah_Mosque_\(Isfahan\)#Design_%E2%80%93_the_four-iwan_style) arrangement took form. The four-iwan format, finalized by the Seljuqs, and later inherited by the [Safavids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_dynasty), firmly established the courtyard façade of such mosques, with the towering gateways at every side, as more important than the actual buildings themselves.[^cite_note-autogenerated1-68] They typically took the form of a square-shaped central courtyard with large entrances at each side, giving the impression of gateways to the spiritual world.[^cite_note-69] The Persians also introduced [Persian gardens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_gardens) into mosque designs. Soon, a distinctly [Persian style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_architecture) of mosques started appearing that would significantly influence the designs of later [Timurid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timurid_dynasty), and also [Mughal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_architecture), mosque designs. A prominent example of the four-Iwan layout mosque outside of Central Asia is the [Mosque of Sultan Hasan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosque-Madrasa_of_Sultan_Hasan) in Cairo, an anomalous in Mamluk architecture.[^cite_note-70]&#x20;



The [Jama Masjid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jama_Masjid,_Delhi) in Delhi is an example of a three-dome mosque found in India, Pakistan and Bangladesh.&#x20;

The three-dome layout is a prominent plan in South Asia, it was developed as a result of the fusion between Islamic architecture and local South Asian traditions during the [Mughal era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire), which created a three-dome mosque style with an extensive walled courtyard.[^cite_note-71] the most notable examples of that are influenced by this plan are the [Jama Masjid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jama_Masjid,_Delhi) and [Moti Masjid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moti_Masjid_\(Red_Fort\)) in India, the [Badshahi Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Badshahi_Mosque), [Dai Anga Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dai_Anga_Mosque), and [Sunehri Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunehri_Mosque,_Lahore) in Pakistan, and the [One Row Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Row_Mosque), [Sat Gambuj Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sat_Gambuj_Mosque), and [Nayabad Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nayabad_Mosque) in Bangladesh.&#x20;



[Süleymaniye Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%BCleymaniye_Mosque) in Istanbul, with a central dome layout typical of mosques in Turkey and the Balkans

The Ottomans introduced central dome mosques in the 15th century. These mosques combine the courtyard (sahn) characteristic of the early Islamic mosques with a large dome centered over the prayer hall. In addition to having a large central dome, a common feature is smaller domes that exist off-center over the prayer hall or throughout the rest of the mosque, where prayer is not performed.[^cite_note-mit-vocab-72] This style was heavily influenced by Byzantine architecture with its use of large central domes.[^cite_note-Masdjid1-64] One of the most notable imperial Ottoman mosques is the [Süleymaniye Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%BCleymaniye_Mosque) in Istanbul, the mosque has foundations that go 12 m into the ground, and it is covered by a large central dome that measures 26 meters in diameter, that is contained within 2 [semi-domes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-dome), and small domes on either side that measure between 5 and 10 m in diameter. On the other hand, the latter bigger dome of the [Selimiye Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selimiye_Mosque,_Edirne) in Edrine, which measures 31.1 m in diameter, is held by giant [squinches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squinch) instead of the semi-domes. Both mosques are considered to be the best works of [Mimar Sinan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mimar_Sinan).[^cite_note-73]&#x20;

#### Decoration

Further information: [Islamic ornament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_ornament)



*[Muqarnas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muqarnas)* vault in the [Nasir al-Mulk Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasir_al-Mulk_Mosque) in [Shiraz](39622.md), Iran

Islam forbids [figurative art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Figurative_art), on the grounds that the artist must not imitate God's creation. Mosques are, therefore, decorated with [abstract patterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_art) and beautiful inscriptions. Decoration is often concentrated around doorways and the *[miḥrāb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mihrab)*. Tiles are used widely in mosques. They lend themselves to pattern-making, can be made with beautiful subtle colors, and can create a cool atmosphere, an advantage in the hot Arab countries. Quotations from the [Quran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quran) often adorn mosque interiors. These texts are meant to inspire people by their beauty, while also reminding them of the words of Allah.[^cite_note-74]&#x20;

### Prayer hall

The prayer hall, also known as the *muṣallá* ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language): مُصَلَّى), rarely has furniture; chairs and pews are generally absent from the prayer hall so as to allow as many worshipers as possible to line the room.[^cite_note-unitulsa-75] Some mosques have [Islamic calligraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_calligraphy) and Quranic verses on the walls to create a more religious atmosphere for worshippers.[^cite_note-teach-islam-61]&#x20;

Often, a limited part of the prayer hall is sanctified formally as a *masjid* in the *[sharīʿah](28840.md)* sense (although the term *masjid* is also used for the larger mosque complex as well). Once designated, there are onerous limitations on the use of this formally designated *masjid*, and it may not be used for any purpose other than worship; restrictions that do not necessarily apply to the rest of the prayer area, and to the rest of the mosque complex (although such uses may be restricted by the conditions of the *[waqf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waqf)* that owns the mosque).[^cite_note-qaSunniPathFiqhMasjid-76]&#x20;

In many mosques, especially the early congregational mosques, the prayer hall is built in the [hypostyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypostyle) form (the roof held up by a multitude of columns).[^cite_note-77] One of the finest examples of the hypostyle-plan mosques is the [Great Mosque of Kairouan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Mosque_of_Kairouan) in [Tunisia](30188.md).[^cite_note-78]&#x20;

Usually opposite the entrance to the prayer hall is the *[qibla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qibla)* wall (the direction of [Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecca), and thus the direction towards which [Muslims](19541.md) should face for prayer), the visually emphasized area inside the prayer hall. The *qibla* wall should, in a properly oriented mosque, be set perpendicular to a line leading to [Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecca), where the [Kaaba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaaba) is located.[^cite_note-79] Congregants pray in rows parallel to the qiblah wall and thus arrange themselves so they face Mecca. In the *qibla* wall, usually at its center, is the *[miḥrāb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mihrab)*, a niche or depression indicating the direction of Mecca. Usually the *mihrab* is not occupied by furniture either. A raised *[minbar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minbar)* (pulpit) is located to the right side of the *mihrab* for a *[khaṭīb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khatib)* (preacher), or some other speaker, to offer a *[khuṭbah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khutbah)* (sermon) during the ritual Friday prayers.&#x20;

&#x20;The *mihrab* serves as the location where the [imam](15240.md) or [mullah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mullah) leads the five daily prayers on a regular basis.[^cite_note-80] Left to the *mihrab*, in the front left corner of the mosque, sometimes there is a *kursu* (Turkish: *[kürsü](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/tr\:k%C3%BCrs%C3%BC#T%C3%BCrk%C3%A7e)*, Bosnian: *ćurs/ћурс*), a small elevated plateau (rarely with a chair or other type of seat) used for less formal preaching and speeches.

#### Mihrab and minbar



The Uthmani Mihrab, the principal mihrab of the [Prophet's Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Masjid_an-Nabawi), [Medina](36636.md), Saudi Arabia

A *[miḥrāb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mihrab)*, also spelled as *mehrab* is a semicircular [niche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niche_\(architecture\)) in the wall of a mosque that faces the *[qiblah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qibla)* (i.e. the "front" of the mosque); the imam stands in this niche and leads prayer. Given that the imam typically stands alone in the frontmost row, this niche's practical effect is to save unused space.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKuban19744-81] The *[minbar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minbar)* is a [pulpit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulpit) from which the Friday sermon is delivered. While the *minbar* of Muhammad was a simple chair, later it became larger and attracted artistic attention. Some remained made of wood, albeit exquisitely carved, while others were made of marble and featured [friezes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frieze).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKuban19745–6-82]&#x20;

### Minarets

Main article: [Minaret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minaret)



The spiral "Malwiya" minaret of the [Abbasid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_architecture) [Great Mosque of Samarra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Mosque_of_Samarra), in Iraq (built in 850) was the largest mosque at the time.

A common feature in mosques is the minaret, the tall, slender tower that usually is situated at one of the corners of the mosque structure. The top of the minaret is always the highest point in mosques that have one, and often the highest point in the immediate area.&#x20;



Two minarets made of clay with twenty layers of horizontal protruding wooden sticks from the [Great Mosque of Bobo-Dioulasso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Mosque_of_Bobo-Dioulasso) in Burkina Faso

The origin of the minaret and its initial functions are not clearly known and have long been a topic of scholarly discussion.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHillenbrand1994129–137-83][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBloom2013Chapter_1:_The_History_of_Scholarship_and_the_Nature_of_the_Problem-84] The earliest mosques lacked minarets, and the call to prayer was often performed from smaller structures or elevated platforms.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBloom201329–46-85][^cite_note-Creswell_12-86][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHillenbrand1994136–137-87] The early Muslim community of Medina gave the call to prayer from the doorway or the roof of the house of [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad), which doubled as a place for prayer.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBloom201323–30,_46-88] The first confirmed minarets in the form of towers date from the early 9th century under Abbasid rule and they did not become a standard feature of mosques until the 11th century.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBloom2013xvii,_64,_72-89][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPetersen1996187–188-90] These first minaret towers were placed in the middle of the wall opposite the qibla wall.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBloom201373–82-91] Among them, the minaret of the Great Mosque of Kairouan in Tunisia, dating from 836, is well-preserved and is one of the oldest surviving minarets in the world today.[^cite_note-\:2432-92][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPetersen1996187–188-90][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBloom201373–75-93]&#x20;

Before the five required daily prayers, a *[Mu'adhdhin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu%27adhin)* (Arabic: مُـؤَذِّن) calls the worshippers to prayer from the minaret. In many countries like Singapore where Muslims are not the majority, mosques are prohibited from loudly broadcasting the *[Adhān](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adhan)* (Arabic: أَذَان, Call to Prayer), although it is supposed to be said loudly to the surrounding community. The *adhan* is required before every prayer. Nearly every mosque assigns a *muezzin* for each prayer to say the *adhan* as it is a recommended practice or *[Sunnah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunnah)* ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language): سُـنَّـة) of the Islamic prophet Muhammad. At mosques that do not have minarets, the *adhan* is called instead from inside the mosque or somewhere else on the ground.[^cite_note-teach-islam-61] The *[Iqâmah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iqama)* (Arabic: إِقَـامَـة), which is similar to the *adhan* and proclaimed right before the commencement of prayers, is usually not proclaimed from the minaret even if a mosque has one.&#x20;

### Courtyard



The courtyard (*[sahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahn)*) of the [Qarawiyyin Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qarawiyyin_Mosque), in [Fez](39760328.md), Morocco (established in 859), including the central fountain and one of the [Saadian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saadi_dynasty)-era pavilions

The courtyard (*[Sahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahn)*) is a common feature of many congressional mosques, it usually includes a fountain or a small water pool for ablution (*[Wudu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wudu)*).[^cite_note-94]&#x20;

### Domes



[Sixty Dome Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sixty_Dome_Mosque) in Bagerha&#x74;**,** Bangladesh (built in 1459)

Domes have been a prominent feature in mosque architecture for centuries, evolving both in form and function. Traditionally placed above the main prayer hall, they symbolize the connection between the earthly and divine, often representing the vaults of heaven and sky.[^cite_note-95] Initially, domes were small structures above the mihrab, but over time, they expanded to cover the entire roof of the prayer hall. The shape of the dome evolved from simple hemispherical forms to more complex designs, with the Mughals in India popularizing the onion-shaped dome, which became a hallmark of South Asian and Arabic mosque architecture.[^cite_note-96] The design and function of domes have been influenced by various cultures, including Persian, Byzantine, and Central Asian traditions, each contributing to the techniques and aesthetics used in their construction. Today, domes continue to serve both structural and spiritual purposes, with modern innovations further enhancing their functionality and sustainability.&#x20;

**Structural and functional roles**&#x20;

Domes not only serve as architectural focal points but also enhance mosque acoustics, amplifying the sound of the prayer and the sermon. Structurally, they allow vast interior spaces with minimal internal supports and they make the mosque appear bigger on the inside. Their placement above the prayer hall symbolizes the connection between the earthly and the divine, reinforcing the mosque's spiritual purpose.[^cite_note-97]&#x20;

**Origin of mosque domes**&#x20;

The origin of domes in mosque architecture can be traced back to the early Islamic period, particularly under the Umayyad Caliphate (661–750 CE). The Umayyads, played a key role in incorporating domes into mosque architecture, drawing heavily from pre-Islamic Byzantine and Sassanian traditions. One of the earliest and most significant examples is the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem, constructed in 691 CE by the Umayyad Caliph Abd al-Malik. This structure was not a mosque but a shrine, yet it set a precedent for the use of domes in Islamic architecture, symbolizing the vault of heaven and the connection between the divine and earthly realms.[^cite_note-98]&#x20;

Initially, domes in mosques were modest in size and positioned above the mihrab, emphasizing their symbolic function as markers of spiritual focus. As the Islamic empire expanded, architects incorporated techniques from different regions.&#x20;

**Persian and Byzantine influences**&#x20;

Whilst squinches and pendentives were not first used in mosques, they were later incorporated in dome design and were essential in transitioning from square rooms to circular domes.[^cite_note-\:2-99] Squinches, which originated in Persian and Roman architecture, fill the corners of a square space to support a dome, while pendentives, a Byzantine innovation, allowed smooth transitions from a square base to a circular dome.[^cite_note-\:2-99] These techniques are exemplified by the Dome of Soltaniyeh.&#x20;

**Domes in South Asian mosque architecture**&#x20;

Domes became a defining feature of South Asian mosque architecture during the Delhi Sultanate and reached their peak under the Mughal Empire. Influenced by Persian and Central Asian traditions, the Mughals introduced the iconic onion-shaped domes, seen in landmarks like the Jama Masjid in Delhi and the Badshahi Mosque in Lahore. These domes were not only visually striking but also represented remarkable engineering, using techniques such as iron dowels for strength and timber centering for precision. The Mughal architectural style still influences mosque design today.&#x20;

**Modern innovations**&#x20;

&#x20;One prime example of modern innovation is the Masjid Raja Haji Fi Sabilillah in Malaysia, which features a Low-E (low emissivity) glass dome. The use of Low-E glass allows for significant energy efficiency by reducing heat gain while still allowing natural light to illuminate the interior space.[^cite_note-100] This technique helps to maintain a comfortable temperature inside the mosque, minimizing reliance on air conditioning, and promoting sustainability. Another example is [Zebun Nessa Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zebun_Nessa_Mosque) in Bangladesh with its iconic pink color, built with concrete in order to soften the harsh industrial surroundings.

### Ablution facilities



The *[wudu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wudu)* ("ablution") area, where Muslims wash their hands, forearm, face and feet before they pray. Example from the [Badshahi Mosque, Lahore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Badshahi_Mosque,_Lahore), Pakistan.

As [ritual purification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ritual_purification#Islam) precedes all prayers, mosques often have [ablution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wudu) fountains or other facilities for washing in their entryways or courtyards. Worshippers at much smaller mosques often have to use restrooms to perform their ablutions. In traditional mosques, this function is often elaborated into a freestanding building in the center of a courtyard.[^cite_note-mit-handout-65] This desire for cleanliness extends to the prayer halls where shoes are disallowed to be worn anywhere other than the cloakroom. Thus, foyers with shelves to put shoes and racks to hold coats are commonplace among mosques.[^cite_note-unitulsa-75]&#x20;

### Contemporary features

Modern mosques have a variety of amenities available to their congregants. As mosques are supposed to appeal to the community, they may also have additional facilities, from [health clinics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinic) and [clubs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_club) (gyms) to [libraries](17727.md) to [gymnasiums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gym), to serve the community.[^cite_note-101]&#x20;

### Symbols

Certain symbols are represented in a mosque's architecture to allude to different aspects of the Islamic religion. One of these feature symbols is the spiral. The "cosmic spiral" found in designs and on minarets is a references to heaven as it has "no beginning and no end".[^cite_note-Erzen_2011_126–129-102] Mosques also often have floral patterns or images of fruit and vegetables. These are allusions to the paradise after death.[^cite_note-Erzen_2011_126–129-102]&#x20;

### Inter-religious conversion

See also: [Conversion of non-Muslim places of worship into mosques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversion_of_non-Muslim_places_of_worship_into_mosques), [List of former mosques in Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_former_mosques_in_Spain), and [List of former mosques in Portugal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_former_mosques_in_Portugal)



The [Hagia Sophia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hagia_Sophia) in [Istanbul](3391396.md), Turkey, built in 360, was converted into a mosque after the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople in 1453.



The [Mosque–Cathedral of Córdoba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosque%E2%80%93Cathedral_of_C%C3%B3rdoba) in Spain, built in 785, was converted into a cathedral in 1236.

According to early [Muslim historians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Muslim_historians), towns that surrendered without resistance and made treaties with the Muslims were allowed to retain their churches and the towns captured by Muslims had many of their churches converted to mosques.[^cite_note-103] One of the earliest examples of these kinds of conversions was in [Damascus](8914.md), Syria, where in 705 [Umayyad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Caliphate) [caliph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliph) [Al-Walid I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Walid_I) bought the church of [St. John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_the_Baptist) from the [Christians](18337522.md) (which used to be a Roman temple and an Aramean temple prior to that),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBurns200538–40-104][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGeorge200144-105][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBurns200765-106] and had it demolished and rebuilt as a mosque in exchange for building a number of new churches for the Christians in Damascus. Overall, [Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_al-Malik_ibn_Marwan) (Al-Waleed's father) is said to have transformed 10 churches in Damascus into mosques.[^cite_note-107]&#x20;

The process of turning churches into mosques were especially intensive in the villages where most of the inhabitants [converted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_conversion) to Islam. The [Abbasid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Caliphate) caliph [al-Ma'mun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ma%27mun) turned many churches into mosques. Several of the early mosques in the Ottoman Empire were originally churches or cathedrals from the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire), with the [Hagia Sophia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hagia_Sophia) (one of those converted cathedrals) informing the architecture of mosques from after the [Ottoman conquest of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Constantinople).[^cite_note-108] In some instances mosques have been established on the places of [Jewish](15624.md) or Christian sanctuaries associated with [Biblical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible) personalities who were also recognized by Islam.[^cite_note-Masdjid1-64]&#x20;

Mosques have also been converted for use by other religions, notably in southern Spain, following the conquest of the Moors in 1492.[^cite_note-109] The most prominent of them is the [Great Mosque of Cordoba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral%E2%80%93Mosque_of_C%C3%B3rdoba), itself constructed on the site of a church demolished during the period of Muslim rule. Outside of the [Iberian Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_Peninsula), such instances also occurred in southeastern Europe once regions were no longer under Muslim rule, like the [Seven Saints Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Saints_Church,_Sofia), in Sofia, Bulgaria.&#x20;

## Rules and etiquette

### Prayer leading

Appointment of a prayer leader is considered desirable, but not always obligatory.[^cite_note-mawardi112-110] The permanent prayer leader ([imam](15240.md)) must be a free honest individual and is authoritative in religious matters.[^cite_note-mawardi112-110] In mosques constructed and maintained by the government, the prayer leader is appointed by the ruler;[^cite_note-mawardi112-110] in private mosques, appointment is made by members of the congregation through [majority voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majority_rule). According to the [Hanafi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanafi) [school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madh%27hab) of Islamic jurisprudence, the individual who built the mosque has a stronger claim to the title of imam, but this view is not shared by the other schools.[^cite_note-mawardi112-110]&#x20;

Leadership at prayer falls into three categories, depending on the type of prayer: five daily prayers, Friday prayer, or optional prayers.[^cite_note-mawardi112-110] According to the Hanafi and [Maliki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maliki) school of Islamic jurisprudence, appointment of a prayer leader for Friday service is mandatory because otherwise the prayer is invalid. The [Shafi'i](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shafi%27i) and [Hanbali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanbali) schools argue that the appointment is not necessary and the prayer is valid as long as it is performed in a congregation. A slave may lead a Friday prayer, but Muslim authorities disagree over whether the job can be done by a minor.[^cite_note-mawardi112-110] An imam appointed to lead Friday prayers may also lead at the five daily prayers; [Muslim scholars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Islamic_studies_scholars) agree to the leader appointed for five daily services may lead the Friday service as well.[^cite_note-mawardi112-110]&#x20;

All Muslim authorities hold the consensus opinion that only men may lead prayer for men.[^cite_note-mawardi112-110] Nevertheless, women prayer leaders are allowed to lead prayer in front of all-female congregations.[^cite_note-111]&#x20;

### Cleanliness



Storage for shoes

See also: [Ritual purity in Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ritual_purity_in_Islam)

All mosques have rules regarding cleanliness, as it is an essential part of the worshippers' experience. Muslims before prayer are required to cleanse themselves in an ablution process known as *wudu*. Shoes must not be worn inside the carpeted prayer hall. Some mosques will also extend that rule to include other parts of the facility even if those other locations are not devoted to prayer. Congregants and visitors to mosques are supposed to be clean themselves. It is also undesirable to come to the mosque after eating something that smells, such as garlic.[^cite_note-112]&#x20;

### Dress

Islam requires that its adherents wear [clothes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_and_clothing) that portray [modesty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modesty). Men are supposed to come to the mosque wearing loose and clean clothes that do not reveal the shape of the body. Likewise, it is recommended that women at a mosque wear loose clothing that covers to the wrists and ankles, and cover their heads with a *[hijāb](68301.md)* ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language): حِجاب), or other covering. Many Muslims, regardless of their ethnic background, wear Middle Eastern clothing associated with Arabic Islam to special occasions and prayers at mosques.[^cite_note-teach-islam-61]&#x20;

### Concentration

As mosques are places of worship, those within the mosque are required to remain respectful to those in prayer. Loud talking within the mosque, as well as discussion of topics deemed disrespectful, is forbidden in areas where people are praying. In addition, it is disrespectful to walk in front of or otherwise disturb Muslims in prayer.[^cite_note-113] The walls within the mosque have few items, except for possibly Islamic calligraphy, so Muslims in prayer are not distracted.[^cite_note-114] Muslims are also discouraged from wearing clothing with distracting [images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image) and [symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbol) so as not to divert the attention of those standing behind them during prayer. In many mosques, even the carpeted prayer area has no designs, its plainness helping worshippers to focus.&#x20;

### Gender separation

See also: [Gender segregation and Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_segregation_and_Islam), [Women's mosques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_mosques), and [Islamic Bill of Rights for Women in the Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Bill_of_Rights_for_Women_in_the_Mosque)



Women in the historical [Queen Arwa Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Arwa_Mosque) in [Jibla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jibla,_Yemen), Yemen

There is nothing written in the Qur'an about the issue of space in mosques and gender separation. Traditional rules have segregated women and men. By traditional rules, women are most often told to occupy the rows behind the men. In part, this was a practical matter as the traditional posture for prayer – kneeling on the floor, head to the ground – made mixed-gender prayer uncomfortably revealing for many women and distracting for some men. Traditionalists try to argue that Muhammad preferred women to pray at home rather than at a mosque, and they cite a *[ḥadīth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadith)* in which Muhammad supposedly said: "The best mosques for women are the inner parts of their houses," although women were active participants in the mosque started by Muhammad. Muhammad told Muslims not to forbid women from entering mosques. They are allowed to go in.[^cite_note-115] The second Sunni caliph '[Umar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umar) at one time prohibited women from attending mosques especially at night because he feared they might be sexually harassed or assaulted by men, so he required them to pray at home.[^cite_note-116] Sometimes a special part of the mosque was railed off for women; for example, the governor of Mecca in 870 had ropes tied between the columns to make a separate place for women.[^cite_note-Masdjid1-64]&#x20;

Many mosques today will put the women behind a barrier or partition or in another room. Mosques in [South](21566765.md) and [Southeast Asia](28741.md) put men and women in separate rooms, as the divisions were built into them centuries ago. In nearly two-thirds of American mosques, women pray behind partitions or in separate areas, not in the main prayer hall; some mosques do not admit women at all due to the lack of space and the fact that some prayers, such as the Friday Jumuʻah, are mandatory for men but optional for women.[^cite_note-117] Although there are sections exclusively for women and children, the Grand Mosque in Mecca is desegregated.[^cite_note-118] In a number of mosques the women section is located above the main prayer hall, elevated in the background as stairs-separated gallery or plateau (surface-shortened to the back relative to the bottom main part). It usually has a perforated fence at the front, through which the [imam](15240.md) or [mullah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mullah) and the other male worshippers in the main hall can be partially seen.&#x20;

### Non-Muslim inclusion



President [Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama) and Secretary [Clinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hillary_Clinton) visiting the [Mosque-Madrasa of Sultan Hasan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosque-Madrasa_of_Sultan_Hasan) in Cairo

Under most interpretations of *sharia*, non-Muslims are permitted to enter mosques provided that they respect the place and the people inside it. A dissenting opinion and minority view is presented by followers of the [Maliki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maliki) school of Islamic jurisprudence, who argue that non-Muslims may not be allowed into mosques under any circumstances.[^cite_note-mawardi112-110]&#x20;

The Quran addresses the subject of non-Muslims, and particularly [polytheists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytheism), in mosques in two verses in its ninth chapter, [Sura At-Tawba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/At-Tawba). The seventeenth verse of the chapter prohibits those who *join gods with Allah* (polytheists) from maintaining mosques:&#x20;

> It is not for the polytheists to maintain the mosques of Allah while they openly profess disbelief. Their deeds are void, and they will be in the Fire forever.

The twenty-eighth verse of the same chapter is more specific as it only considers polytheists in the [Masjid al-Haram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masjid_al-Haram) in Mecca:&#x20;

> O believers! Indeed, the polytheists are "spiritually" impure, so they should not approach the Sacred Mosque after this year. If you fear poverty, Allah will enrich you out of His bounty, if He wills. Surely, Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.

According to [Ahmad ibn Hanbal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmad_ibn_Hanbal), these verses were followed to the letter at the times of Muhammad, when [Jews](25955086.md) and Christians, considered [monotheists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism), were still allowed to *Al-Masjid Al-Haram*. The Umayyad caliph [Umar II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umar_II) later forbade non-Muslims from entering mosques, and his ruling remains in practice in present-day Saudi Arabia.[^cite_note-Masdjid1-64] Today, the decision on whether non-Muslims should be allowed to enter mosques varies. With few exceptions, mosques in the Arabian Peninsula as well as Morocco do not allow entry to non-Muslims. For example, the Hassan II Mosque in Casablanca is one of only two mosques in Morocco currently open to non-Muslims.[^cite_note-119]&#x20;

There are many other mosques in the West and Islamic world which non-Muslims are welcome to enter. Most mosques in the United States, for example, report receiving non-Muslim visitors every month. Many mosques throughout the United States welcome non-Muslims as a sign of openness to the rest of the community as well as to encourage conversions to Islam.[^cite_note-120][^cite_note-121]&#x20;

In modern-day Saudi Arabia, the Grand Mosque and all of Mecca are open only to Muslims. Likewise, Al-Masjid Al-Nabawi and the city of [Medina](36636.md) that surrounds it are also off-limits to those who do not practice Islam.[^cite_note-122] For mosques in other areas, it has most commonly been taken that non-Muslims may only enter mosques if granted permission to do so by Muslims, and if they have a legitimate reason. All entrants regardless of [religious affiliation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_religions_and_spiritual_traditions) are expected to respect the rules and [decorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decorum) for mosques.[^cite_note-teach-islam-61]&#x20;

In modern Turkey, non-Muslim tourists are allowed to enter any mosque, but there are some strict rules. Visiting a mosque is allowed only between prayers; visitors are required to wear long trousers and not to wear shoes, women must cover their heads; visitors are not allowed to interrupt praying Muslims, especially by taking photos of them; no loud talk is allowed; and no references to other religions are allowed (no crosses on necklaces, no cross gestures, etc.) Similar rules apply to mosques in Malaysia, where larger mosques that are also tourist attractions (such as the [Masjid Negara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Mosque_of_Malaysia)) provide robes and headscarves for visitors who are deemed inappropriately attired.[^cite_note-123]&#x20;

In certain times and places, non-Muslims were expected to behave a certain way in the vicinity of a mosque: in some Moroccan cities, Jews were required to remove their shoes when passing by a mosque;[^cite_note-124] in 18th-century Egypt, Jews and Christians had to dismount before several mosques in veneration of their sanctity.[^cite_note-125]&#x20;

The association of the mosque with education remained one of its main characteristics throughout history,[^cite_note-126] and the school became an indispensable appendage to the mosque. From the earliest days of Islam, the mosque was the center of the Muslim community, a place for prayer, meditation, religious instruction, political discussion, and a school. Anywhere Islam took hold, mosques were established, and basic religious and educational instruction began.[^cite_note-127]&#x20;

### Political mobilization

See also: [Political aspects of Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_aspects_of_Islam)

The late 20th century saw an increase in the number of mosques that deliver politics and economy related lectures. While some governments in the Muslim world attempted to limit the content of Friday sermons to strictly religious topics, there are also independent preachers who deliver *khutbas* that address social and political issues, often in emotionally charged terms. Common themes include social inequalities, necessity of [jihad](16203.md) in the face of injustice, and the universal struggle between good and evil.[^cite_note-ODI-2] In Islamic countries like Bangladesh, Pakistan, Iran, and Saudi Arabia, political subjects are preached by some imams at some of the Friday congregations.[^cite_note-129] Mosques often serve as meeting points for political opposition in times of crisis.[^cite_note-ODI-2]&#x20;

Countries with a minority Muslim population are more likely than Muslim-majority countries of the [Greater Middle East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Middle_East) to use mosques as a way to promote civic participation.[^cite_note-tc-130] Studies of US Muslims have consistently shown a positive correlation between mosque attendance and political involvement. Some of the research connects civic engagement specifically with mosque attendance for social and religious activities other than prayer.[^cite_note-westfall-131] American mosques host [voter registration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voter_registration) and civic participation drives that promote involving Muslims, who are often first- or second-generation immigrants, in the political process. As a result of these efforts as well as attempts at mosques to keep Muslims informed about the issues facing the [Muslim community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ummah), regular mosque attendants are more likely to participate in [protests](266137.md), sign [petitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petition), and otherwise be involved in politics.[^cite_note-tc-130] Research on Muslim civic engagement in other Western countries "is less conclusive but seems to indicate similar trends".[^cite_note-westfall-131]&#x20;

### Frequency of attendance

The frequency by which Muslims attend mosque services vary greatly around the world. In some countries, weekly attendance at religious services is common among Muslims while in others, attendance is rare. A study of American Muslims did not find differences in mosque attendance by gender or age.[^cite_note-\:0-132]&#x20;

### Political controversy



Historic wooden [Kruszyniany Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kruszyniany_Mosque), used by the [Polish Tatar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipka_Tatars) community, and targeted by an Islamophobic attack in 2014

In the western world, and in the United States in particular, anti-Muslim sentiment and targeted domestic policy has created challenges for mosques and those looking to build them. There has been government and police surveillance of mosques in the US[^cite_note-162] and local attempts to ban mosques and block constructions,[^cite_note-163] despite data showing that in fact, most Americans oppose banning the building of mosques (79%) and the surveillance of U.S. mosques (63%) as shown in a 2018 study done by the Institute for Social Policy and Understanding.[^cite_note-164]&#x20;

Since 2017, [Chinese authorities](23239.md) have destroyed or damaged two-thirds of the [mosques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_mosques_in_China) in China's [Xinjiang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xinjiang) province.[^cite_note-165] [Ningxia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ningxia) officials were notified on 3 August 2018 that the [Weizhou Grand Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proposed_demolition_of_Weizhou_Grand_Mosque) would be forcibly demolished because it had not received the proper permits before construction.[^cite_note-A1-166][^cite_note-A2-167][^cite_note-A3-168] Officials in the town said that the mosque had not been given proper building permits, because it is built in a [Middle Eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Eastern) style and includes numerous [domes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domes) and [minarets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minarets).[^cite_note-A1-166][^cite_note-A2-167] The residents of Weizhou alarmed each other through [social media](5897742.md) and finally stopped the mosque destruction by public demonstrations.[^cite_note-A2-167]&#x20;

### Role in violent conflicts

See also: [Islamophobia](162173.md) and [Israeli–Palestinian conflict](46216.md)



Mosque in [Gaza](12047.md), destroyed during the [Gaza War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaza_War_\(2008%E2%80%9309\)) in 2009

As they are considered important to the Muslim community, mosques, like other places of worship, can be at the heart of social conflicts. The [Babri Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babri_Mosque) in India was the subject of such a conflict up until the early 1990s when it was demolished. Before a mutual solution could be devised, the mosque was destroyed on December 6, 1992, as the mosque was built by [Babur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babur) allegedly on the site of a previous [Hindu temple](2247213.md) marking the birthplace of [Rama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rama).[^cite_note-169] The controversy surrounded the mosque was directly linked to [rioting in Bombay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombay_riots) (present-day [Mumbai](19189.md)) as well as [bombings in 1993](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1993_Bombay_bombings) that killed 257 people.[^cite_note-170]&#x20;

Bombings in February 2006 and [June 2007](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_al-Askari_Mosque_bombing) seriously damaged Iraq's [al-Askari Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Askari_Mosque) and exacerbated existing tensions. Other mosque bombings in Iraq, both before and after the February 2006 bombing, have been part of the conflict between the country's groups of Muslims. In June 2005, a [suicide bombing](18835454.md) killed at least 19 people at an Afghan [Shia](26961.md) mosque near Jade Maivand.[^cite_note-171] In April 2006, [two explosions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_Jama_Masjid_explosions) occurred at India's Jama Masjid.[^cite_note-172] Following the al-Askari Mosque bombing in Iraq, imams and other Islamic leaders used mosques and [Friday prayers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jumu%27ah) as vehicles to call for calm and peace in the midst of widespread violence.[^cite_note-173]&#x20;

A study 2005 indicated that while support for suicide bombings is not correlated with personal devotion to Islam among Palestinian Muslims, it is correlated with mosque attendance because "participating in communal religious rituals of any kind likely encourages support for self-sacrificing behaviors that are done for the collective good."[^cite_note-174]&#x20;

Following the [September 11 attacks](5058690.md), several American mosques were targeted in attacks ranging from simple [vandalism](32676.md) to [arson](184695.md).[^cite_note-175] Furthermore, the [Jewish Defense League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_Defense_League) was suspected of plotting to bomb the King Fahd Mosque in [Culver City, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culver_City,_California).[^cite_note-176] Similar attacks occurred throughout the United Kingdom following the [7 July 2005 London bombings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/7_July_2005_London_bombings). Outside the Western world, in June 2001, the [Hassan Bek Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hassan_Bek_Mosque) was the target of vandalism and attacks by hundreds of Israelis after a suicide bomber killed 19 people in a night club in Tel Aviv.[^cite_note-177][^cite_note-178][^cite_note-179] Although mosquegoing is highly encouraged for men, it is permitted to stay at home when one feels at risk from Islamophobic persecution.[^cite_note-180]&#x20;

### Saudi influence

Although the Saudi involvement in Sunni mosques around the world can be traced back to the 1960s, it was not until later in the 20th century that the government of Saudi Arabia became a large influence in foreign Sunni mosques.[^cite_note-money-trails-181] Beginning in the 1980s, the Saudi Arabian government began to finance the construction of Sunni mosques in countries around the world. An estimated US\$45 billion has been spent by the Saudi Arabian government financing mosques and Sunni Islamic schools in foreign countries. *[Ain al-Yaqeen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ain_al-Yaqeen)*, a Saudi newspaper, reported in 2002 that Saudi funds may have contributed to building as many as 1,500 mosques and 2,000 other Islamic centers.[^cite_note-182]&#x20;

Saudi citizens have also contributed significantly to mosques in the [Islamic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_world), especially in countries where they see Muslims as poor and oppressed. Following the fall of the Soviet Union, in 1992, mosques in war-torn Afghanistan saw many contributions from Saudi citizens.[^cite_note-money-trails-181] The King Fahd Mosque in Culver City, California and the Islamic Cultural Center of Italy in [Rome](25458.md) represent two of Saudi Arabia's largest investments in foreign mosques as former Saudi king [Fahd bin Abdul Aziz al-Saud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fahd_of_Saudi_Arabia) contributed US\$8 million[^cite_note-money-trails-181] and US\$50 million[^cite_note-183] to the two mosques, respectively.&#x20;

[^cite_note-:24-28]: &#x20;[Bloom & Blair 2009](#CITEREFBloomBlair2009), p. 549, *Mosque*: "The first mosque, a building that Muhammad erected at Medina in 622, is usually described as the Prophet's house but was probably intended from the outset as a community center as well. Initially, it was a rectangular enclosure of unbaked brick, a little over fifty meters square, but a portico of palm trunks supporting a roof of palm-frond thatch was quickly erected on the north side of the court, facing Jerusalem, the first qibla, or direction in which Muslims sent their prayers ... In 624 when the qibla was changed to Mecca, another such arcade was built on the south side, facing that city. Muhammad and his family lived in rooms built on to one side of the enclosure, and Muhammad was buried in one of these rooms in 632. During the 7th and early 8th centuries, Muhammad's mosque was repeatedly enlarged and rebuilt, becoming a flat-roofed hypostyle structure with a central court and a prayer-hall deeper than the three other porticos ... The form of the mosque of the Prophet was closely imitated in the early congregational mosques built in the Iraqi cities of Wasit, Kufa and Basra, and in the mosque built at Daybul in Sind (now Banbhore, Pakistan)."

[^cite_note-:0522-29]: &#x20;Tabbaa, Yasser (2007). "Architecture". In Fleet, Kate; Krämer, Gudrun; Matringe, Denis; Nawas, John; Rowson, Everett (eds.). *Encyclopaedia of Islam, Three*. Brill. If Mecca provided the first Muslim shrine, the city of Medina, to which Muḥammad migrated in 622 C.E., may have provided the germ of the idea for the Muslim place of prayer, the masjid, in the form of the house of the Prophet himself. Descriptions of the house allow us to reconstruct it as a mud-brick rectangular enclosure consisting of an open courtyard, a three-aisled roofed space to the south, a one-bay space inside the northern wall, and eight separate rooms annexed to the eastern wall. The eight rooms housed Muḥammad's wives; the northern vestibule was a waiting area; the southern space served various residential, official, and ritual functions. The roof was supported by palm trunks and its southern wall, after 6/628, contained a three-stepped platform (minbar), from which Muḥammad spoke and adjudicated. Despite its rudimentary form and construction, Muḥammad's house would provide the basic model for the first mosques.
