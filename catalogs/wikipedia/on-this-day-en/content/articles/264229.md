**Louisville Muhammad Ali International Airport** ([IATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IATA_airport_code): **SDF**, [ICAO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ICAO_airport_code): **KSDF**, [FAA](11186.md) [LID](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Location_identifier): **SDF**) – also known by its former official names as **Standiford Field** and **Louisville International Airport** – is a civil-military airport in [Louisville, Kentucky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisville,_Kentucky). The airport was renamed after boxer and Louisville native [Muhammad Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Ali) in 2019, three years after his death. The airport covers 1,500 acres (610 ha)[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-6] and has three [runways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runway).[^cite_note-7] Its IATA airport code, SDF, is based on the airport's former name, Standiford Field. Despite being called an international airport, it has no regularly scheduled international passenger flights, but is a [port of entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_entry), handling many [UPS Airlines](9704675.md) international cargo flights through the [United Parcel Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Parcel_Service)'s worldwide air hub, often referred to as **UPS Worldport**.[^cite_note-8]

Over 4.8 million passengers passed through the airport in 2024, while nearly 6.6 billion pounds (3.3 million tons) of cargo passed through in 2024.[^cite_note-flylouisville.com-1] It is also the third-busiest in the United States in terms of cargo traffic, and sixth-busiest for such in the world.[^cite_note-9] The [National Plan of Integrated Airport Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Plan_of_Integrated_Airport_Systems) for 2011–2015 [categorized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FAA_airport_categories) it as a "primary commercial service" airport, since it has over 10,000 passenger boardings (enplanements) per year.[^cite_note-10] [Federal Aviation Administration](11186.md) records show the airport had 2,402,517 revenue enplanements in 2024.[^cite_note-11]

Because of UPS Airlines' operations, Louisville International Airport is the second-busiest cargo airport in the United States, and consequently, the western hemisphere, only falling short of the [Ted Stevens Anchorage International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ted_Stevens_Anchorage_International_Airport) in [Anchorage, Alaska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anchorage,_Alaska). It is also the world's [fourth-busiest airport by cargo traffic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_busiest_airports_by_cargo_traffic), behind [Hong Kong](248457.md), [Shanghai Pudong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanghai_Pudong_International_Airport), and Anchorage.[^cite_note-12] The [Kentucky Air National Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kentucky_Air_National_Guard)'s [123rd Airlift Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/123rd_Airlift_Wing) operates [Lockheed C-130 Hercules](7697.md) transport aircraft from the co-located Louisville Air National Guard Base.

## History

Standiford Field was built by the [Army Corps of Engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army_Corps_of_Engineers) in 1941 on a parcel of land south of Louisville that was found not to have flooded during the [Ohio River flood of 1937](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio_River_flood_of_1937). It was named for [Dr. Elisha David Standiford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elisha_Standiford), a local businessman and politician, who was active in transportation issues and owned part of the land. The field remained under Army control until 1947, when it was turned over to the Louisville Air Board for commercial operations.[^cite_note-history-13]

Until around 1947, [Bowman Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowman_Field_\(Kentucky\)) was Louisville's main airport, which was too close to downtown to expand. For many years, passenger traffic went through the small brick Lee Terminal at Standiford Field. Today's more modern and much larger facilities were built in the 1980s. Most of the Lee Terminal was later torn down.

When Standiford Field was built by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers in 1941, it had one 4,000-foot (1,200 m) runway. The airfield opened to the public in 1947 and all commercial service from Bowman Field moved to Standiford Field. [American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Airlines), [Eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Air_Lines), and [TWA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans_World_Airlines) were the first airlines and had 1,300 passengers a week. The airlines used [World War II](32927.md) barracks on the east side of the field until May 25, 1950, when a proper terminal opened. Lee Terminal could handle 150,000 passengers annually and included 6 new gates, which increased terminal space to 114,420 square feet (10,630 m<sup>2</sup>). The three runways (1, 6 and 11) were all 5000 ft.

The April 1957 Official Airline Guide shows 45 weekday departures on Eastern Airlines, 19 American, 9 TWA, 4 Piedmont and 2 Ozark. Scheduled jet flights (Eastern 720s to Idlewild) began in January–February 1962.

In 1970, the terminal again expanded; the main lobby was extended and the 33,000-square-foot (3,100 m<sup>2</sup>) Delta Air Lines concourse was built.[^cite_note-history-13]

The 1980s brought plans for a new terminal, the Louisville Airport Improvement plan (LAIP). Construction of a new landside terminal designed by Bickel-Gibson Associated Architects Inc. began, costing \$35 million with capacity for nearly 2 million passengers in 1985.[^cite_note-14] Parallel runways, needed for expanded [UPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Parcel_Service) operations, were part of the airport expansion.[^cite_note-history-13] Most of the improvements were completed in the 1990s and the airport was totally renewed.

During the 1990s, [Southwest Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwest_Airlines) began service to the airport which helped passenger boardings increase 97.3 percent. In 1995, the airport's name was changed from Standiford Field to Louisville International Airport. Around that time, SDF opened the two new parallel runways: runway 17L/35R, 8,578 feet (2,615 m) long and runway 17R/35L, 11,887 feet (3,623 m); both are 150 feet (46 m) wide. The Kentucky Air National Guard moved its base to SDF with 8 military aircraft; a new UPS air mail facility, new corporate hangars, a four-level parking garage and a new control tower were also added. A new FBO was added, run by Atlantic Aviation and managed by Michael Perry.

In 2005, a \$26 million terminal renovation designed by [Gensler Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gensler) was completed.[^cite_note-15] As of 2024, the airport is in the midst of a major renovation project called SDF Next, which includes more than \$1 billion in planned enhancements to the [Jerry E. Abramson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerry_Abramson) Terminal, work on the baggage claim, updates to security and lighting, and changes to the rental car counters, among other improvements.[^cite_note-16]

On January 16, 2019, the Louisville Regional Airport Authority voted to change the name of the airport to Louisville Muhammad Ali International Airport in honor of the boxer and Louisville native [Muhammad Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Ali).[^cite_note-17] On June 6, 2019, the airport unveiled its new logo, featuring "Ali's silhouette, arms up and victorious, against the background of a butterfly".[^cite_note-18]

## Facilities

### Terminal



Concourse B

The Jerry E. Abramson Terminal, named after former [Lieutenant Governor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lieutenant_Governor_of_Kentucky) and five-term [Louisville mayor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_mayors_of_Louisville,_Kentucky) [Jerry Abramson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerry_Abramson), is the airport's main commercial terminal. It consists of two floors with ground transportation and baggage claim services on the first floor and ticketing, passenger drop off, and concourse access on the second floor.[^cite_note-Terminal_Map-19] There are 24 gates in the two concourses. These concourses are connected by a [rotunda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotunda) and connector that contains a unified security checkpoint located in the main section of the terminal.

### Runways

Louisville Muhammad Ali International Airport has three concrete runways, two of which are parallel with one crosswind. The westernmost runway (17R/35L) is the longest of the three at 11,887 feet (3,623 m) and was extended in 2007 to accommodate larger aircraft flying nonstop to destinations as far away as the Pacific Rim and Asia. The other two runways, 17L/35R, measures 8,578 feet (2,615 m) and 11/29 measures 7,251 feet (2,210 m)[^cite_note-Airfield_Enhancements-20][^cite_note-FAA_Information_Effective_10_October_2019-21]

### Worldport



UPS Worldport at Louisville International Airport in 2004

Worldport is the worldwide air hub for [UPS](9704675.md) ([United Parcel Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Parcel_Service)) located at Louisville Muhammad Ali International Airport. Because of UPS, Louisville is the fourth-busiest cargo airport in the world, and the second busiest in the United States.[^cite_note-22] Although UPS has had a hub at Louisville since 1980, the name 'Worldport' was not used officially by the company until 2002, after a \$1 billion, five-year expansion.[^cite_note-23] Previously, the project was named Hub 2000. The facility is currently 5.2 million [sq ft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_foot) (48 [ha](24919513.md); 80 [football fields](18951490.md#Field_and_equipment)) in size and capable of handling 115 packages per second, or 416,000 per hour.[^cite_note-24][^cite_note-25] With more than 20,000 employees, UPS is one of the largest employers in both the city of Louisville and the state of Kentucky as a whole. The facility, which serves all of the company's major international and domestic hubs, mainly handles express and international packages and letters.

A 1,000,000-square-foot (93,000 m<sup>2</sup>) expansion was completed in spring 2006 to integrate heavy freight into the UPS system. The expansion was prefaced by the purchase of Menlo Worldwide Forwarding, formerly [Emery Worldwide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emery_Worldwide). The new facility, designated Worldport Freight Facility (HWP), went online in April 2006 and was the first of the company's regional hubs to begin integrating the Menlo volume into the system. Operations at Menlo's facility in [Dayton, Ohio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dayton,_Ohio), ended in June 2006.

In May 2006, UPS announced that for the third time in seven years it would significantly expand its Worldport hub, with a second investment of \$1 billion. The second expansion was completed in April 2010, with the facility now measuring 5,200,000 square feet (480,000 m<sup>2</sup>), with a perimeter of 7.2 miles (11.6 km). The plan was for more than 1,000,000 square feet (93,000 m<sup>2</sup>) to be added to its existing facility, with another 334,500 square feet (31,080 m<sup>2</sup>) of space to be renovated with new technology and equipment. Worldport sorting capacity was to expand from 300,000 packages per hour to 416,000 packages per hour. Additionally, several ramps at the Louisville Muhammad Ali International Airport were to be built or altered bringing a total increase of just over 3,000,000 square feet (280,000 m<sup>2</sup>).

## Accidents and incidents

- September 28, 1953: [Resort Airlines Flight 1081](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resort_Airlines_Flight_1081), a [Curtiss C-46 Commando](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtiss_C-46_Commando) leased from the [United States Air Force](32090.md), crashed on landing at Louisville-Standiford Field when the aircraft ballooned slightly during the flare-out on runway 24, causing a loss of control when it climbed to 300 feet (90 m) and stalled. Out of the 41 on board, 22 passengers and three crew were killed. Failure of the left elevator during landing was the cause.

  [^cite_note-88]

- March 10, 1957: [Eastern Air Lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Air_Lines) Flight 181, a [Martin 4-0-4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_4-0-4) crash-landed at SDF on runway 11. All 34 passengers and crew aboard survived with just one serious injury. The pilot's improper landing approach caused an excessive sink rate, causing a portion of the left wing to separate inboard of the #1 engine and left the aircraft partially inverted. The plane was damaged beyond repair.

  [^cite_note-89]

  [^cite_note-90]

- September 8, 1970: [Delta Air Lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_Air_Lines) Flight 439, a [McDonnell Douglas DC-9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McDonnell_Douglas_DC-9) inbound from [Chicago–O'Hare](248449.md) attempting an instrument landing at night at SDF landed 156 feet (48 m) short of the threshold of runway 29, hitting sloping terrain, becoming airborne, bouncing and then skidding down the runway for nearly 1,500 yards (1,370 m) before coming to a stop. All five crew and 89 passengers survived. The aircraft was substantially damaged, but repaired and later put back into service. Pilot error was the cause.

  [^cite_note-91]

- June 7, 2005: [UPS Airlines](9704675.md) Flight 6971, a [McDonnell Douglas MD-11](382371.md) with four occupants aboard, suffered a nose gear collapse after touchdown on runway 17L. The accident was due to improper handling of the aircraft by the pilot at the controls after the main landing gear touched down and the pilot-in-command's inadequate supervision during landing. The aircraft sustained substantial damage, but was repaired and returned to service.

  [^cite_note-92]

- November 4, 2025: [UPS Airlines Flight 2976](81514772.md), a [McDonnell Douglas MD-11](382371.md) crashed during takeoff from runway 17R. The preliminary cause of the crash was the detachment of the left engine from the aircraft and its cascading effects on the other two engines. This caused loss of lift, with the aircraft then rolling from imbalance and crashing into buildings near the end of runway 17R.

  [^cite_note-93]

  &#x20;This subsequently caused a large fire and 15 deaths, including all three crew members.
