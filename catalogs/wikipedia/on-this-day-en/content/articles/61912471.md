**Bird of the Year** ([Māori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ori_language): *Te Manu Rongonui o te Tau*) is an annual election-based competition run by the [New Zealand](4913064.md) conservation organisation [Forest & Bird](645998.md) to elect a New Zealand native "Bird of the Year". The competition is intended to raise awareness of the conservation threats to many endangered native birds.

It draws support from celebrities including politicians, artists, actors, and television personalities.[^cite_note-\:2-1] The election is a significant social media and public event in New Zealand; there are regular attempts at voter fraud, some winners (such as the [short-tailed bat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_lesser_short-tailed_bat)) have been controversial. In 2023, a TV campaign by British-American comedian [John Oliver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Oliver) for the [pūteketeke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puteketeke) drew international attention to the contest, leading it to win with more than 290,000 votes.[^cite_note-\:8-2]

## History

Bird of the Year (BOTY) was first launched in October 2005 by Michael Szabo, editor of *Forest & Bird* at the time, initially as an online poll featured in Forest & Bird's first [email newsletter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E-newsletter); votes were collected by email and through the post.[^cite_note-3] Szabo noticed the public outcry when [RNZ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_New_Zealand) considered dropping bird calls from its daily Morning Report, and decided to tap into this public interest with an annual poll.[^cite_note-4] The first BOTY contest included 76 native bird species and received a total of 900 votes; the [tūī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C5%AB%C4%AB) was the winner.[^cite_note-\:0-5] BOTY was later championed by Forest & Bird's communications manager Helen Bain, who saw it as a "light-hearted…way of raising the profile of native birds and the threats to them".[^cite_note-6]

In 2014, the competition was temporarily retitled to **Seabird of the Year** and only seabirds were eligible.[^cite_note-\:6-7] In 2023 the competition was dubbed **Bird of the Century** (*Te Manu Rongonui o te Rautau*) to coincide with Forest & Bird's centennial celebrations, and for the first time included extinct birds: [laughing owl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laughing_owl), [South Island snipe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Island_snipe), [huia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huia), [South Island kōkako](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Island_k%C5%8Dkako), and [bush wren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bushwren).[^cite_note-8]

Between 2022 and 2024, voters used the Forest & Bird website to rank their top-five choices for Bird of the Year. The winner was determined using the [instant-runoff voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant-runoff_voting) method.&#x20;

Because of the [transferable voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_transferable_voting) system, the Bird of the Year was not necessarily the species receiving the most number-one votes in those years. For example in BOTY 2022 (starting 17 October), seventy-one species were shortlisted as candidates, and the [rock wren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_rock_wren) was the winner, because it received the most and highest vote rankings, despite getting fewer #1 votes than the second-place winner, the [little penguin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_penguin).[^cite_note-9]

Bird of the Year runs for two weeks in September to November each year, and around 70 species usually compete. Anyone can nominate themselves as a "campaign manager" for a bird, and organise social media publicity campaign. The competition is not restricted to New Zealand: anyone with a valid email address is able to cast a vote, which became critical to the 2023 landslide victory of the [Pūteketeke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_crested_grebe).[^cite_note-\:8-2]

## Controversies

- 2008: The successful campaign to elect [kākāpō](89074.md) was accused by the [takahē](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Island_takah%C4%93) of accepting undeclared donations "from wealthy migratory birds living in Monaco." It was cleared by the fictional [Serious Feathered Fraud Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serious_Fraud_Office_\(New_Zealand\)).

  [^cite_note-Saturday-15]

- 2010: The [kākāriki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C4%81k%C4%81riki) was accused of rigging the vote. [Forest & Bird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Forest_and_Bird_Protection_Society_of_New_Zealand) confirmed these concerns in 2011, committing to improving vote security.

  [^cite_note-\:5-16]

- 2011: The [emperor penguin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_penguin) was added to the competition for one year, when a juvenile bird, dubbed [Happy Feet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Happy_Feet_\(penguin\)), was found on the [Kāpiti Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C4%81piti_Coast). It had made national and international headlines after being rescued.

  [^cite_note-17]

  [^cite_note-18]

  [^cite_note-19]

- 2015: Two teenagers from [Auckland](18660332.md) made over 200 fraudulent votes for the [kōkako](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C5%8Dkako).

  [^cite_note-20]

  &#x20;They used their father's business account to make fake email addresses.

  [^cite_note-21]

- 2017: The competition suffered a further voting scandal when 112 fraudulent votes were made for the [white-faced heron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White-faced_heron) using [internet bots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_bot) from an [IP address](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_address) in [Christchurch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christchurch).

  [^cite_note-22]

- 2018: An independent [scrutineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scrutineer) from [Dragonfly Data Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragonfly_Data_Science?action=edit\&redlink=1) was brought in to prevent further voting scandals.

  [^cite_note-23]

  &#x20;Despite this, a third voting scandal surfaced when 310 fraudulent votes were placed for the [black shag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_shag). These were traced to Australia.

  [^cite_note-24]

  [^cite_note-25]

  [^cite_note-26]

- 2019: A larger than usual number of Russian votes drew conspiracy theories alleging Russian interference despite Russians providing only 335 votes. A spokesperson for Forest and Bird concluded the votes appeared to be legitimate and suggested it was innocent interest from ornithologists in the country.

  [^cite_note-27]

- 2020: 1500 votes were placed for the [little spotted kiwi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_spotted_kiwi) using [fake email addresses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Email_address) from the same [IP address](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_address), briefly pushing it to the top of the leaderboard before the fraudulent votes were discovered.

  [^cite_note-28]

- 2021: The [long-tailed bat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_long-tailed_bat) was added to the competition with mixed responses from candidates and voters, who criticised the fact that it was not in fact a [bird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird).

  [^cite_note-29]

  &#x20;This controversial entry ended up winning 2021 Bird of the Year.

  [^cite_note-twitter.com-30]

- 2022: The kākāpō, a previous two-time winner of BOTY, was barred from competing again.

  [^cite_note-31]

- 2023: Television comedian [John Oliver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Oliver) announced on his HBO show *[Last Week Tonight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_Week_Tonight_with_John_Oliver)* that he was the campaign manager for the Southern crested grebe or [pūteketeke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C5%ABteketeke). Oliver erected billboards in [Wellington](33804.md), [Paris](22989.md), [Tokyo](30057.md), [London](17867.md), [Mumbai](19189.md), and [Manitowoc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manitowoc,_Wisconsin), and arranged for an aeroplane with a pūteketeke banner to fly over [Ipanema Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ipanema) in Brazil. He also appeared on [Jimmy Fallon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Fallon)’s *[The Tonight Show](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tonight_Show_Starring_Jimmy_Fallon)* in a pūteketeke costume. So many votes were received—over 350,000, from 195 countries—that Forest & Bird's website crashed and the results of the competition had to be delayed by two days while verification was carried out.

  [^cite_note-\:8-2]

  [^cite_note-\:9-32]

  &#x20;The previous record for verified votes was 56,733 in 2021.

  [^cite_note-\:10-33]

  &#x20;The pūteketeke won by a landslide, receiving 290,374 votes, over 100 times those of the previous year's winner, and far more than the second-placing [North Island brown kiwi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Island_brown_kiwi) with 12,904.

  [^cite_note-\:10-33]

  &#x20;The contest was also marred by fraud, with 40,000 votes cast by one person for the [eastern rockhopper penguin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_rockhopper_penguin). A [Pennsylvania](23332.md) voter cast 3,403 fraudulent votes, with one arriving every three seconds.

  [^cite_note-\:10-33]

  &#x20;Forty-five of the verified votes were cast under the name "John Oliver", one of them for the [fairy tern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairy_tern), and the rest for the pūteketeke.

  [^cite_note-\:9-32]

  &#x20;Forest & Bird claimed to be pleased by Oliver's campaign, as it furthered the contest's basic environmental awareness purpose by encouraging international attention.

  [^cite_note-34]

  &#x20;The organisation received over \$600,000 in donations, six times the previous record, as a result of the campaign.
