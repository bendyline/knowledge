**Gregory Wayne  Abbott** ([/æbət/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [*ABB-ət*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key); born November 13, 1957) is an American politician, attorney, and jurist serving since 2015 as the 48th [governor of Texas](214883.md). A member of the [Republican Party](32070.md), he served from 2002 to 2015 as the 50th [attorney general of Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Attorney_General) and from 1996 to 2001 as a justice of the [Texas Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Supreme_Court). As of 2025, Abbott is the [longest-serving incumbent governor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_current_United_States_governors) in the United States.

Born in [Wichita Falls, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wichita_Falls,_Texas), Abbott grew up between [Longview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longview,_Texas) and [Duncanville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duncanville,_Texas). He graduated from the [University of Texas at Austin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Texas_at_Austin) with a [Bachelor of Business Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Business_Administration) in 1981 and from [Vanderbilt University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanderbilt_University) with a [Juris Doctor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juris_Doctor) in 1984. After going into private practice, working for Butler and Binion, [LLP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limited_liability_partnership) between 1984 and 1992, he began his judicial career in [Houston](13774.md), where he served as a [state trial judge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_judicial_system) in the 129th District Court for three years. Before becoming attorney general, Abbott was a justice of the Texas Supreme Court, a position to which he was appointed in 1995 by Governor [George W. Bush](3414021.md). Abbott won a full term in 1998 with 60% of the vote. After resigning from the Supreme Court to run for [Texas lieutenant governor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_lieutenant_governor), Abbott returned to private practice and worked for [Bracewell & Giuliani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bracewell_&_Giuliani) in 2001.

Abbott was elected Texas attorney general with 57% of the vote in [2002](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2002_Texas_Attorney_General_election) and reelected with 60% in [2006](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_Texas_Attorney_General_election) and 64% in [2010](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Texas_Attorney_General_election). He became the longest-serving attorney general in state history,[^cite_note-1] with 12 years of service, and was the third Republican to hold that office since the [Reconstruction era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconstruction_era). As attorney general, Abbott successfully advocated for the [Texas State Capitol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_State_Capitol) to display the [Ten Commandments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_Commandments) in the 2005 [U.S. Supreme Court](31737.md) case *[Van Orden v. Perry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Orden_v._Perry)*, and unsuccessfully defended the [state's ban on same-sex marriage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Same-sex_marriage_in_Texas)*.* He was also involved in numerous lawsuits against the [administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidency_of_Barack_Obama) of [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama), seeking to invalidate the [Affordable Care Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affordable_Care_Act) and the administration's environmental regulations.

Elected [in 2014](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Texas_gubernatorial_election), Abbott is the first Texas governor and third governor of a U.S. state to use a wheelchair, the others being [Franklin D. Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt) of New York and [George Wallace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Wallace) of [Alabama](303.md). He was reelected [in 2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Texas_gubernatorial_election) and [in 2022](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Texas_gubernatorial_election), and is running for an unprecedented fourth term [in 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Texas_gubernatorial_election). As governor, Abbott supported the [first administration](52231341.md) of [Donald Trump](4848272.md) and has promoted a strongly conservative agenda, including maintaining [Texas's total abortion ban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abortion_in_Texas), loosening gun laws, increasing law enforcement funding, and tightening voting rights. In response to the [power crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Texas_power_crisis) following a [February 2021 winter storm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/February_13%E2%80%9317,_2021_North_American_winter_storm), Abbott called for reforms to [Electric Reliability Council of Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_Reliability_Council_of_Texas) (ERCOT) and signed a bill requiring power plant [weatherization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weatherization). During the [COVID-19 pandemic in Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_Texas), Abbott opposed implementing [face mask](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_masks_during_the_COVID-19_pandemic_in_the_United_States) and [vaccine mandates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaccine_mandates), while blocking local governments, businesses, and other organizations from implementing their own. He has also made a priority of fighting [illegal immigration](5044573.md), starting [Operation Lone Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Lone_Star) in 2021. Abbott was on [*Time*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_\(magazine\)) magazine's [*Time* 100](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_100) list in 2024.

## Early life, education, and legal career

Abbott was born on November 13, 1957, in [Wichita Falls, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wichita_Falls,_Texas), and is of [English descent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_American).[^cite_note-2] His mother, Doris Lechristia Jacks Abbott, was a [housewife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Housewife) and his father, Calvin Rodger Abbott, was a stockbroker and [insurance agent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insurance_agent).[^cite_note-TXMonthSweany102013-3] When he was six years old, they moved to [Longview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longview,_Texas); the family lived there for six years.[^cite_note-TXMonthSweany102013-3] When he was 12, Abbott's family moved to [Duncanville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duncanville,_Texas). In his sophomore year in high school, his father died of a heart attack; his mother went to work in a real estate office.[^cite_note-TXMonthSweany102013-3] Abbott graduated from [Duncanville High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duncanville_High_School),[^cite_note-4] where he was on the track team,[^cite_note-WashPostReid10302014-5] in the [National Honor Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Honor_Society) and was voted "Most Likely to Succeed".[^cite_note-WashPostReid10302014-5]

In 1981, Abbott earned a [Bachelor of Business Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Business_Administration) in finance from the [University of Texas at Austin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Texas_at_Austin), where he was a member of the [Delta Tau Delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_Tau_Delta) fraternity and the [Young Republicans Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_Republicans_Club). He met his wife, Cecilia Phalen, while attending UT Austin.[^cite_note-TXMonthSweany102013-3] The two married in 1981.[^cite_note-6] In 1984, he earned his [Juris Doctor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juris_Doctor) degree from the [Vanderbilt University Law School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanderbilt_University_Law_School).[^cite_note-TXMonthSweany102013-3]

Abbott went into private practice, working for Butler and Binion, [LLP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limited_Liability_Partnership) between 1984 and 1992.[^cite_note-TXAttyGenBio-7]

## Judicial career

Abbott's judicial career began in [Houston](13774.md), where he served as a [state trial judge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_judicial_system) in the 129th District Court for three years.[^cite_note-TXAttyGenBio-7] Governor [George W. Bush](3414021.md) appointed Abbott to the state's highest civil court, [Texas Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Supreme_Court), to which he was twice elected, in 1996 (for a two-year term) and in 1998 (six-year term). In 1996, Abbott had no [Democratic](5043544.md) opponent but was challenged by [Libertarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libertarian_Party_\(United_States\)) John B. Hawley of Dallas. Abbott defeated Hawley, 84% to 16%.[^cite_note-8] In 1998, Abbott defeated Democrat [David Van Os](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Van_Os), 60% to 40%.[^cite_note-9]

In 2001, after resigning from the Supreme Court, Abbott returned to private practice and worked for [Bracewell & Giuliani LLC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bracewell_&_Giuliani).[^cite_note-BusWk09192013-10] He was also an adjunct professor at [University of Texas School of Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Texas_School_of_Law).[^cite_note-11]

### 2002 election

Abbott resigned from the Texas Supreme Court on June 6, 2001, to run for [lieutenant governor of Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lieutenant_Governor_of_Texas).[^cite_note-12][^cite_note-TXMonthSweany102013-3] He had been campaigning for several months when the previous attorney general, [John Cornyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cornyn), vacated the post to run for the [U.S. Senate](24909346.md).[^cite_note-TXMonthSweany102013-3] Abbott then switched his campaign to the open attorney general's position in 2002. He defeated the Democratic nominee, former [Austin](1998.md) mayor and former state senator[^cite_note-13] [Kirk Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirk_Watson), 57% to 41%.[^cite_note-14] Abbott was sworn in on December 2, 2002, following Cornyn's election to the Senate.[^cite_note-15]

### Tenure

Abbott expanded the attorney general's office's law enforcement division from about 30 people to more than 100.[^cite_note-TXMonthSweany102013-3] He also created a new division, the Fugitive Unit, to track down convicted sex offenders in violation of their paroles or probations.[^cite_note-TXMonthSweany102013-3]

In 2003, Abbott supported the [Texas Legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Legislature)'s move to [cap non-economic damages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-economic_damages_caps) for [medical malpractice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_malpractice_in_the_United_States) cases at \$250,000, with no built-in increases for rising cost of living.[^cite_note-Malpractice-16]

In a 2013 speech to fellow Republicans, when asked what his job entails, Abbott said: "I go into the office in the morning, I sue Barack Obama, and then I go home."[^cite_note-\:3-17] Abbott filed 31 lawsuits against the Obama administration,[^cite_note-texastrib20170117-18] including suits against the [Environmental Protection Agency](58666.md); the [U.S. Department of Health and Human Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Department_of_Health_and_Human_Services), including challenges to the [Affordable Care Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affordable_Care_Act) ("Obamacare"); and the [U.S. Department of Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Department_of_Education), among many others.[^cite_note-TXMonthSweany102013-3] According to *[The Wall Street Journal](173070.md)*, from Abbott's tenure as attorney general through his first term as governor, Texas sued the Obama administration at least 44 times, more than any other state over the same period; court challenges included carbon-emission standards, health-care reform, transgender rights, and others.[^cite_note-19] *[The Dallas Morning News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dallas_Morning_News)* compared Abbott to [Scott Pruitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scott_Pruitt), noting that both attorneys general had repeatedly sued the federal government over its environmental regulations.[^cite_note-20] The *[Houston Chronicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houston_Chronicle)* noted that Abbott "led the charge against Obama-era climate regulations".[^cite_note-21]

Abbott has said that the state must not release [Tier II Chemical Inventory Reports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergency_Planning_and_Community_Right-to-Know_Act#Inventory_Reporting) for security reasons, but that Texans "can ask every facility whether they have chemicals or not".[^cite_note-22] [Koch Industries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koch_Industries) has denied that its contributions to Abbott's campaign had anything to do with his ruling against releasing the safety information.[^cite_note-23]

In March 2014, Abbott filed a motion to intervene on behalf of [Baylor Scott & White Medical Center – Plano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baylor_Scott_&_White_Medical_Center_%E2%80%93_Plano) in three federal lawsuits against the hospital, brought by patients who alleged that the hospital allowed [Christopher Duntsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Duntsch) to perform [neurosurgery](21848.md) despite knowing that he was a dangerous physician.[^cite_note-24] Abbott cited the Texas legislature's cap on malpractice cases and the statute's removal of the term "gross negligence" from the definition of legal malice as reasons for defending Baylor.[^cite_note-25]

In the late 2000s, Abbott established a unit in the attorney general's office to pursue voter-fraud prosecutions, using a \$1.4 million federal grant; the unit prosecuted a few dozen cases, resulting in small fines and little to no jail time.[^cite_note-Garrett-26] The office found no large-scale fraud that could change the outcome of any election.[^cite_note-Garrett-26]

#### Lawsuit against Sony BMG

Main article: [Sony BMG CD copy protection scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sony_BMG_CD_copy_protection_scandal)

In December 2005, Abbott sued [Sony BMG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sony_BMG).[^cite_note-TechLawJ-27][^cite_note-TXAttyGenSonyPR-28] Texas was the first state in the nation to bring legal action against Sony BMG for illegal [spyware](28951.md).[^cite_note-TechLawJ-27][^cite_note-TXAttyGenSonyPR-28] The suit was also the first filed under the state's spyware law of 2005.[^cite_note-TechLawJ-27][^cite_note-TXAttyGenSonyPR-28] It alleged the company surreptitiously installed the spyware on millions of compact music discs (CDs) that consumers inserted into their computers when they played the CDs, which can compromise the systems.[^cite_note-TXAttyGenSonyPR-28][^cite_note-29] On December 21, 2005, Abbott added new allegations to his lawsuit against Sony-BMG. He said the [MediaMax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MediaMax) copy protection technology violated Texas's spyware and deceptive trade practices laws.[^cite_note-TechLawJ-27][^cite_note-DallasBizJ12212005-30] Sony-BMG offered consumers a licensing agreement when they bought CDs and played them on their computers;[^cite_note-TechLawJ-27][^cite_note-DallasBizJ12212005-30] in the lawsuit, brought under the [Consumer Protection Against Computer Spyware Act of 2005](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Spyware_Prevention_Act) and other laws, Abbott alleged that even if consumers rejected that agreement, spyware was secretly installed on their computers, posing security risks for music buyers and deceiving Texas purchasers.[^cite_note-TechLawJ-27][^cite_note-DallasBizJ12212005-30][^cite_note-31] Sony settled the Texas lawsuit, as well as a similar suit brought by California's attorney general, for \$1.5 million.[^cite_note-32]

#### Separation of church and state

Main article: [Van Orden v. Perry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Orden_v._Perry)

In March 2005, Abbott delivered [oral argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_argument) before the [United States Supreme Court](31737.md) on behalf of Texas, defending a [Ten Commandments monument on grounds of the Texas State Capitol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_Commandments_Monument_\(Austin,_Texas\)). Thousands of similar monuments were donated to cities and towns across the nation by the [Fraternal Order of Eagles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraternal_Order_of_Eagles), who were inspired by the [Cecil B. DeMille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecil_B._DeMille) film *[The Ten Commandments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ten_Commandments_\(1956_film\))* (1956) in the years that followed.[^cite_note-33] In his deposition, Abbott said, "The Ten Commandments are a historically recognized system of law."[^cite_note-34] The Supreme Court held in a 5–4 decision that the Texas display did not violate the [First Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Amendment)'s [Establishment Clause](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Establishment_Clause) and was constitutional.[^cite_note-35] After Abbott's oral arguments in *Van Orden v. Perry*, Justice [John Paul Stevens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Paul_Stevens) said of Abbott's performance while in a wheelchair, "I want to thank you \[...] for demonstrating that it's not necessary to stand at the lectern in order to do a fine job."[^cite_note-WashPostReid10302014-5]

#### Firearms

As attorney general, Abbott opposed gun control legislation. In 2013, he criticized [legislation enacted by New York State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NY_SAFE_Act) strengthening its gun regulation laws by expanding an [assault weapons ban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assault_weapons_legislation_in_the_United_States) and creating a [high-capacity magazine ban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-capacity_magazine_ban); he also said he would sue if Congress enacted a new gun-control bill.[^cite_note-36] After the law passed, Abbott's political campaign placed Internet ads to users with [Albany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albany,_New_York) and [Manhattan](45470.md) ZIP codes suggesting that New York gun owners should move to Texas. One ad read, "Is Gov. Cuomo looking to take your guns?", and the other read, "Wanted: Law abiding New York gun owners looking for lower taxes and greater opportunity." The ads linked to a letter on Facebook in which Abbott wrote that such a move would enable citizens "to keep more of what you earn and use some of that extra money to buy more ammo".[^cite_note-Fernandez-37]

In February 2014, Abbott argued against a lawsuit brought by the [National Rifle Association of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Rifle_Association_of_America) (NRA) to allow more people access to [concealed carry of firearms](1137166.md), as he felt this would disrupt public safety.[^cite_note-38]

#### Tort reform

Abbott backed legislation in Texas to limit "[punitive damages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punitive_damages) stemming from noneconomic losses" and "noneconomic damages in medical malpractice cases" at \$750,000 and \$250,000, respectively.[^cite_note-JayRoot2-39] While the settlement in his own paralysis case was a "nonmedical liability lawsuit", which remains uncapped, Abbott has faced criticism, generally from Democrats who oppose the Republican-backed lawsuit curbs, for "tilt\[ing] the judicial scales toward civil defendants."[^cite_note-JayRoot2-39]

#### Support for ban on sex toys

As attorney general, Abbott unsuccessfully defended Texas's ban on [sex toys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sex_toy).[^cite_note-\:0-40] He said Texas had a legitimate interest in "discouraging prurient interests in [autonomous sex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masturbation) and the pursuit of [sexual gratification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_gratification) unrelated to procreation."[^cite_note-\:0-40]

#### Opposition to same-sex marriage

As attorney general, Abbott defended the [state's ban on same-sex marriage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Same-sex_marriage_in_Texas) from a constitutional challenge.[^cite_note-41] In 2014, he argued in court that Texas should be allowed to prohibit same-sex marriage because [LGBT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGBT) individuals cannot procreate. He said that as "same-sex relationships do not naturally produce children, recognizing same-sex marriage does not further these goals to the same extent that recognizing opposite-sex marriage does."[^cite_note-\:0-40] He also argued that gay people are still free to marry, saying they are "as free to marry an opposite-sex spouse as anyone else".[^cite_note-\:0-40] He suggested that same-sex marriage led to a [slippery slope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slippery_slope) in which "any conduct that has been traditionally prohibited can become a constitutional right simply by redefining it at a higher level of abstraction."[^cite_note-\:0-40]

In 2016, Abbott urged the [Texas Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Texas) to limit the impact of the [U.S. Supreme Court](31737.md)'s ruling in [Obergefell v. Hodges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obergefell_v._Hodges), the 2015 case that held that the [14th Amendment](31666.md) requires all states to recognize same-sex marriages and made same-sex couples eligible for state and federal benefits tied to marriage, including the right to be listed on a birth certificate and the right to adopt.[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43]

#### 2006 election

In the November 7, 2006, general election, Abbott was challenged by [civil rights](37071.md) attorney David Van Os, who had been his Democratic opponent in the 1998 election for state Supreme Court. He was reelected to a second term with 60% to Van Os's 37%.[^cite_note-44]

#### 2010 election

Abbott ran for a third term in 2010. He defeated the Democratic nominee, attorney [Barbara Ann Radnofsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbara_Ann_Radnofsky), with 64% of the vote to her 34%.[^cite_note-45] He was the longest-serving Texas attorney general in Texas history.[^cite_note-TXTribRoot11042014-46]

In July 2013, the *[Houston Chronicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houston_Chronicle)* alleged improper ties and oversight between many of Abbott's largest donors and the Cancer Prevention and Research Institute of Texas, of which he was a director.[^cite_note-47]

In July 2013, shortly after Governor [Rick Perry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rick_Perry) announced he would not seek a fourth full term,[^cite_note-48] Abbott announced his candidacy for [governor of Texas](214883.md) in the [2014 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_gubernatorial_election,_2014).[^cite_note-49] In the first six months of 2011, he raised more money for his campaign than any other previous Texas politician, reaching \$1.6 million. The next-highest fundraiser among state officeholders was [Texas comptroller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Comptroller) [Susan Combs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Combs), with \$611,700.[^cite_note-50]

Abbott won the Republican primary on March 4, 2014, with 91.5% of the vote. He faced [State Senator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Senate) [Wendy Davis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wendy_Davis_\(politician\)) in the general election.[^cite_note-51]

Abbott promised to "tie outcomes to funding" for pre-K programs if elected,[^cite_note-52] but said he would not require government [standardized testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standardized_testing) for 4-year-olds, as Davis accused him of suggesting.[^cite_note-53] When defending his education plan, Abbott cited [Charles Murray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Murray_\(political_scientist\)): "Family background has the most decisive effect on student achievement, contributing to a large [performance gap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Performance_gap) between children from economically disadvantaged families and those from middle class homes."[^cite_note-DallasMNHoppe04012014-54] A spokesman for Abbott's campaign pointed out that the biggest difference in spending was that Davis had proposed universal pre-K education while Abbott wanted to limit state funding to programs that meet certain standards.[^cite_note-DallasMNHoppe04012014-54] Davis's plan could reach \$750 million in cost and Abbott said that her plan was a "budget buster", whereas his education plan would cost no more than \$118 million.[^cite_note-DallasMNHoppe04012014-54] Overall, Abbott said the reforms he envisioned would "level the playing field for all students \[and] target schools which don't have access to the best resources." He called for greater access to technology in the classroom and mathematics instruction for kindergarten pupils.[^cite_note-lmtstateissues-55]

Abbott received \$1.4 million in campaign contributions from recipients of the [Texas Enterprise Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Enterprise_Fund), some of whose members submitted the proper paperwork for grants.[^cite_note-56] Elliot Nagin of the [Union of Concerned Scientists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_of_Concerned_Scientists) observed that Abbott was the recipient of large support from the fossil fuels industries, such as [NuStar Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NuStar_Energy), [Koch Industries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koch_Industries), [Valero Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valero_Energy), [ExxonMobil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ExxonMobil), [Chevron](284749.md), and [ConocoPhillips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ConocoPhillips).[^cite_note-57] Abbott was endorsed by the *[Fort Worth Star-Telegram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Worth_Star-Telegram)*,[^cite_note-58] the *Dallas Morning News*,[^cite_note-59] the *[Lubbock Avalanche-Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lubbock_Avalanche-Journal)*[^cite_note-60] and the *[Tyler Morning Telegraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyler_Morning_Telegraph)*.[^cite_note-61] He and [Dan Patrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Patrick_\(politician\)), the Republican nominee for lieutenant governor, were endorsed by the [NRA Political Victory Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NRA_Political_Victory_Fund) and given an "A" rating.[^cite_note-62][^cite_note-63]

Abbott defeated Davis by over 20 percentage points in the November general election.[^cite_note-64][^cite_note-65][^cite_note-66][^cite_note-67]

## Governor of Texas (2015–present)



Abbott speaking at the 2016 [World Travel and Tourism Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Travel_and_Tourism_Council) conference

Abbott was sworn in as governor of Texas on January 20, 2015, succeeding [Rick Perry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rick_Perry).[^cite_note-92][^cite_note-93] He is the first governor of Texas and the third elected governor of a U.S. state to use a wheelchair, after [Franklin D. Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt) of New York (1929–1932) and [George Wallace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Wallace) of [Alabama](303.md) (1963–1967; 1971–1979; 1983–1987).[^cite_note-USAToday-94][^cite_note-RooseveltWallace-95][^cite_note-96]

Abbott held his first meeting as governor with a foreign prime minister when he met with the [Irish Taoiseach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Taoiseach) [Enda Kenny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enda_Kenny) on March 15, 2015, to discuss trade and economic relations.[^cite_note-97]

During the 2015 legislative session, initiated by officials at the [Texas Health and Human Services Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Health_and_Human_Services_Commission), the Texas legislature placed a rider to cut \$150 million from its budget by ending payments and coverage for various developmental therapies for children on [Medicaid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicaid). A lawsuit was filed against the state on behalf of affected families and therapy providers, claiming the cut could cause irreparable damage to the affected children's development.[^cite_note-98] The litigation obtained a temporary injunction order on September 25, 2015, barring THHSC from implementing therapy rate cuts.[^cite_note-99]

During [Donald Trump's first presidency](52231341.md), Abbott ardently supported Trump.[^cite_note-100] The [Trump administration](52231341.md) appointed several former Abbott appointees to federal courts, which some media outlets attributed to Abbott's influence on the administration.[^cite_note-101] In 2021, Trump endorsed Abbott for reelection, choosing him over several Republican primary rivals who also positioned themselves as pro-Trump.[^cite_note-102]

Abbott's book *Broken But Unbowed* (2016) recounted Abbott's personal story and views on politics.[^cite_note-103]

In October 2016, explosive packages were mailed to Abbott, President Obama, and the commissioner of the Social Security Administration. Abbott's package did not explode when he opened it because "he did not open \[the package] as intended".[^cite_note-104]

On June 6, 2017, Abbott called for a special legislative session in order to pass several of his legislative priorities,[^cite_note-105][^cite_note-106] an agenda supported by Lieutenant Governor Dan Patrick.[^cite_note-107] Abbott vetoed 50 bills in the regular 2017 session, the most in a session since 2007.[^cite_note-108][^cite_note-109]

As governor, Abbott serves as chair of the State Preservation Board. As chair, he was part of the unanimous vote by the board to remove a plaque placed by the [Children of the Confederacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Children_of_the_Confederacy) from the [Texas State Capitol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_State_Capitol).[^cite_note-kvue-110][^cite_note-apnews-111]

Abbott appointed multiple judges to various judgeships, including several GOP-affiliated judges who had recently lost local judicial elections.[^cite_note-112]

After the regular 2021 session, *[The New York Times](30680.md)* called Abbott and Patrick "the driving force behind one of the hardest right turns in recent state history".[^cite_note-113] Other sources said Abbott and other state officials advanced strongly conservative policies.[^cite_note-114][^cite_note-115][^cite_note-116] By his 2022 reelection campaign, Abbott more prominently emphasized "[culture war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_war)" issues.[^cite_note-Wallace_2022_n697-117] He had been compared to Florida Governor [Ron DeSantis](36729527.md) in promoting conservative policies.[^cite_note-118][^cite_note-119]

According to a report by *[The Texas Tribune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Texas_Tribune)* and [ProPublica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ProPublica), Abbott centralized power under the governor's office during his tenure.[^cite_note-120]

### Abortion

In November 2016, the State of Texas, at Abbott's request, approved new rules that require facilities that perform abortions either to bury or cremate the aborted, rather than dispose of the remains in a [sanitary landfill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanitary_landfill).[^cite_note-Burial-121][^cite_note-122] The rules were intended to go into effect on December 19,[^cite_note-Burial-121] but on December 15 a federal judge blocked them from going into effect for at least one month after the [Center for Reproductive Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_for_Reproductive_Rights) and other advocacy groups filed a lawsuit.[^cite_note-123] On January 27, 2017, a federal judge ruled against the law, but the State of Texas vowed to appeal the ruling.[^cite_note-124]

On June 6, 2017, Abbott signed a bill into law banning dismemberment and [partial-birth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intact_dilation_and_extraction) abortions and requiring either burial or cremation of the aborted.[^cite_note-125][^cite_note-126][^cite_note-127] That law was also blocked by a federal judge; the state said it would appeal.[^cite_note-128][^cite_note-129]

On May 18, 2021, Abbott signed the [Texas Heartbeat Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Heartbeat_Act), a [six-week abortion ban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six-week_abortion_ban), into law.[^cite_note-130][^cite_note-131] In September 2021, he signed into law a bill preventing women from mail-ordering abortion medication seven weeks into pregnancy.[^cite_note-132]

### Artificial Intelligence and data centers

**Local government preemption** Abbott supported efforts to limit the authority of Texas cities and counties to enact local ordinances. In June 2023 he signed House Bill 2127, the Texas Regulatory Consistency Act (nicknamed the "Death Star" bill by opponents), which bars municipalities from adopting ordinances that exceed state law in eight areas, including agriculture, business and commerce, finance, labor, natural resources, and property. The *Texas Tribune* reported that Abbott had long sought the law and called it Texas Republicans' most expansive attempt to curtail the powers of the state's large, largely Democratic-led cities.[^cite_note-133] Abbott said the law would give Texas a single regulatory regime statewide and prevent cities from micromanaging businesses.[^cite_note-134]

Houston, later joined by San Antonio and El Paso, sued to block the law. A Travis County district judge ruled it unconstitutional in August 2023, though the state's appeal stayed the ruling and the law took effect on September 1. In July 2025, the Third Circuit Court of Appeals overturned that decision, finding the cities lacked standing to challenge the law.[^cite_note-135]

In June 2025, Abbott signed House Bill 2559, which restricts municipal moratoriums on property development. The law lengthens required notice from four days to 30, mandates two public hearings held at least 30 days apart, requires a three-fourths vote of a city council for a moratorium to take effect, caps any moratorium at 180 days in the aggregate, and bars municipalities from imposing comparable moratoriums in the same area for two years.[^cite_note-136] State Senator Paul Bettencourt, the Senate sponsor of House Bill 2559, invoked both laws in challenges to local efforts to block data center construction, including a moratorium in Hood County and a zoning ban San Marcos adopted in June 2026.[^cite_note-137]

### College diversity, equity, and inclusion

In 2023, Abbott signed into law Senate Bill 17, which prohibits Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion (DEI) offices at Texas's public colleges and universities. The bill, passed largely along party lines, garnered both support and criticism, with proponents arguing it would save taxpayer funds and promote a merit-based approach to education and critics expressing concern about discrimination and hindrance to diversity efforts.[^cite_note-138]

As a result of Senate Bill 17 and similar legislation, universities have been compelled to reevaluate their DEI initiatives, often leading to significant restructuring and reallocation of resources.[^cite_note-139] Senate Bill 17's implementation led the [University of Texas at Austin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Texas_at_Austin) to lay off about 60 employees and close of the Division of Campus and Community Engagement, which was formerly the Division of Diversity and Community Engagement.[^cite_note-140]

### Convention of States proposal



Governor Abbott with President [Donald Trump](4848272.md) during [Hurricane Harvey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Harvey) emergency

In 2016, Abbott spoke to the [Texas Public Policy Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Public_Policy_Foundation), calling for a [Convention of States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convention_of_States) to amend the [U.S. Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Constitution). In his speech, he proposed [the Texas Plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Texas_Plan), a series of nine new [amendments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amendments_to_the_Constitution_of_the_United_States) to "unravel the federal government's decades-long power grab "to impose fiscal restraints on the federal government and limit the federal government's power and jurisdiction." The plan would limit the power of the federal government and expand [states' rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States'_rights), allowing the states to [nullify](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nullification_\(U.S._Constitution\)) federal law under some circumstances.[^cite_note-141][^cite_note-142]

On January 8, 2016, Abbott called for a national [constitutional convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convention_to_propose_amendments_to_the_United_States_Constitution) to address what he saw as abuses by justices of the [United States Supreme Court](31737.md) in "abandoning the Constitution".[^cite_note-143] Speaking to the Texas Public Policy Foundation, Abbott said, "We the people have to take the lead to restore the rule of law in the United States."[^cite_note-144] Abbott elaborated on his proposal in a public seminar at the [Hoover Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoover_Institute) on May 17, 2016.[^cite_note-145]

### COVID-19 pandemic



Abbott speaking with President [Donald Trump](4848272.md) and members of the [White House Coronavirus Task Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_House_Coronavirus_Task_Force) in the [Oval Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oval_Office)

During the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_Texas), Abbott issued a stay-at-home order from April 2 to May 1, 2020.[^cite_note-146][^cite_note-\:2-147] This was one of the shortest stay-at-home orders implemented by any governor.[^cite_note-\:2-147] After Texas started to reopen, COVID-19 surged, leading Abbott to pause the reopening.[^cite_note-\:2-147] On June 24, Texas broke its record of new COVID-19 cases in a day.[^cite_note-\:2-147] Critics described Abbott's pause as a half-measure and argued that he should reverse the reopening in full to limit the virus's spread.[^cite_note-\:2-147]

He said Texans should wear face masks but did not issue a statewide mandate. Abbott's response to the pandemic was criticized on both sides of the political spectrum.[^cite_note-\:2-147] In July 2020, he directed counties with more than 20 COVID-19 cases to wear masks in public places after previously prohibiting local governments from mandating face masks.[^cite_note-148]

In December 2020, Abbott directed Texas restaurants to ignore local curfews that had been imposed to prevent the spread of COVID-19. Localities had implemented restrictions on indoor dining and drinking late at night on New Year's weekend amid a surge in COVID-19 cases.[^cite_note-149][^cite_note-150]

On March 2, 2021, Abbott lifted all COVID-19 restrictions in Texas, which included ending a mask mandate and allowing businesses to reopen "100 percent".[^cite_note-151]

In April 2021, Abbott signed an executive order banning state agencies and corporations that take public funding from requiring proof of vaccination against COVID-19.[^cite_note-152] In June 2021, he signed a bill that would punish businesses that require customers to have proof of COVID-19 vaccination for services.[^cite_note-153]

On May 18, 2021, Abbott issued an executive order banning mask mandates in public schools and governmental entities, with a fine of up to \$1,000 for failing to comply.[^cite_note-154]

On August 17, 2021, Abbott's office announced that he had tested positive for COVID-19. According to his office, Abbott was "in good health and experiencing no symptoms".[^cite_note-155] He received [Regeneron's monoclonal antibody treatment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casirivimab/imdevimab).[^cite_note-156]

Abbott emphasized personal responsibility over government restrictions, and resolutely opposed government mandates.[^cite_note-157] On July 29, 2021, during an again worsening pandemic,[^cite_note-158][^cite_note-159] he issued a superseding executive order (GA-38) that reinstated earlier orders and imposed additional prohibitions on local governmental officials, state agencies, public universities,[^cite_note-160] and businesses doing business with the state, to prohibit them from adopting measures such as requiring face masks or proof of vaccination status as a condition of service. The order also provided for a \$1,000 fine for local officials who adopted inconsistent policies.[^cite_note-161][^cite_note-162][^cite_note-163] President Biden criticized Abbott for these measures.[^cite_note-164] The ban on mask mandates led to a score of legal challenges between Abbott and local governments, including school districts.[^cite_note-165] In August, an Abbott spokesperson said, "Private businesses don't need government running their business."[^cite_note-166] In October, Abbott issued an executive order that banned any entity, including a private business, from requiring its employees to be vaccinated.[^cite_note-167]

According to the [Johns Hopkins University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johns_Hopkins_University) Coronavirus Resources Center, 93,390 COVID-19 deaths had been registered in Texas as of November 2024.[^cite_note-168]

### Criminal justice

In the wake of the [George Floyd protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Floyd_protests), Abbott called on candidates in the 2020 elections to "back the blue".[^cite_note-\:8-169] After some Texas cities redirected funding from police to social services and emergency response, he threatened to seize control of local police departments.[^cite_note-170][^cite_note-\:8-169] In 2021, Abbott spearheaded legislative efforts to fine cities that reduced spending on police.[^cite_note-171]

In 2021, Abbott vetoed a bipartisan criminal justice bill that would have made people convicted of certain crimes before they were 18 eligible for early parole and created panels to consider inmates' age and mental health at the time of their crimes when evaluating parole eligibility.[^cite_note-\:9-172] He also vetoed an animal protection bill that would have made it illegal to chain up dogs without giving them access to drinkable water and shade or shelter.[^cite_note-\:9-172]

In May 2024, Abbott granted a full pardon to former Army sergeant [Daniel Perry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Perry) after the [Texas Board of Pardons and Paroles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Board_of_Pardons_and_Paroles), whose members had all been appointed by Abbott, unanimously recommended it.[^cite_note-173] Perry was sentenced to 25 years in prison in 2023 after he was convicted of murdering Air Force veteran [Garrett Foster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garrett_Foster) during a [Black Lives Matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Lives_Matter) protest. In 2023, Abbott said he would work swiftly for a pardon after a jury convicted Perry of murder.[^cite_note-174]

### Education

Abbott supports using government funds to help families send their children to private schools. In multiple legislative sessions he advocated a bill providing what he called "[Education Savings Accounts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School_voucher)" (ESAs). The bill repeatedly hit roadblocks in the Texas House of Representatives, where Democrats and rural Republicans believed the program would harm public school funding.[^cite_note-175] When the legislation failed to pass in the 88th Regular Session, Abbott called multiple special sessions to approve it.[^cite_note-176] The special sessions expired without passing the legislation.

Leading into the 2024 elections, Abbott campaigned against incumbent House Republicans who voted against his voucher program in hope of replacing them with members supportive of it.[^cite_note-177] The campaign succeeded in securing a majority of House members in the 89th legislative session who supported Abbott's ESA program.[^cite_note-178] House Democrats attempted to put the issue of vouchers before the voters in a referendum but failed, and the bill passed the House with near unanimous Republican support.[^cite_note-179] The legislation Abbott signed in 2025 gave participating families around \$10,000 to cover tuition fees and other school-related expenses.[^cite_note-180]

### Environment



[Dharmendra Pradhan](7119332.md), India's union minister for petroleum and natural gas and skill development and entrepreneurship in a meeting with Abbott in 2018

As of 2018, Abbott rejects the [scientific consensus on climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_consensus_on_climate_change). He has said that the climate is changing, but [does not accept the consensus that human activity is the main reason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_denial).[^cite_note-181][^cite_note-182]

In early 2014, Abbott participated in sessions held at the headquarters of the [United States Chamber of Commerce](1448498.md) to devise a legal strategy to dismantle climate change regulations.[^cite_note-183] In 2016, he supported [Scott Pruitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scott_Pruitt)'s appointment as head of the [Environmental Protection Agency](58666.md) (EPA), saying, "He and I teamed up on many lawsuits against the EPA."[^cite_note-184] As Texas attorney general, Abbott often sued the federal government over environmental regulations.[^cite_note-185]

After [Joe Biden](145422.md) was elected president, Abbott vowed to pursue an aggressive legal strategy against the Biden administration's environmental regulations.[^cite_note-186]

### February 2021 North American ice storm

See also: [February 13–17, 2021 North American winter storm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/February_13%E2%80%9317,_2021_North_American_winter_storm) and [2021 Texas power crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Texas_power_crisis)



Abbott and President [Joe Biden](145422.md) at the Harris County Emergency Operations Center in 2021

During the [February 13–17, 2021 North American winter storm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/February_13%E2%80%9317,_2021_North_American_winter_storm), power-plant failures left four million Texas households without power.[^cite_note-187] Abbott called for investigation and reform of the [Electric Reliability Council of Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_Reliability_Council_of_Texas) (ERCOT), the electric grid operator for most of Texas.[^cite_note-WaPo_Englund_20210216-188]

On February 16, on *[Hannity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hannity)*, Abbott said, "This shows how the Green New Deal would be a deadly deal for the United States of America ... Our wind and our solar got shut down, and they were collectively more than 10 percent of our power grid, and that thrust Texas into a situation where it was lacking power on a statewide basis... It just shows that fossil fuel is necessary." The Texas energy department clarified that failure to winterize the state's power grid had caused most of the losses.[^cite_note-WaPo_Shepherd_20210217-189][^cite_note-WaPo_Englund_20210216-188] Most power plants in Texas are gas-fired, with wind generators providing about 10% during the winter.[^cite_note-WaPo_Englund_20210216-188]

By February 18, Abbott had ordered Texas natural gas to sell exclusively to power generators in Texas, which had an immediate and direct impact on Mexico, where gas-fired plants generate two-thirds of all energy.[^cite_note-NYT_Lopez_20210218-190] In June, he signed a bill requiring power companies to be more prepared for extreme weather events.[^cite_note-191]

### Firearms

In 2015, Abbott signed the *[campus carry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campus_carry_in_the_United_States)* (SB 11) and the *[open carry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_carry_in_the_United_States)* (HB 910) bills into law.[^cite_note-OpenAndCampus-192] The *campus carry* law came into effect later that year, allowing licensed carry of a concealed handgun on public college campuses, with private colleges able to opt out.[^cite_note-OpenAndCampus-192][^cite_note-Open-193] The *open carry* bill went into effect in 2016, allowing licensed open carry of a handgun in public areas and private businesses unless they display a "30.07" sign, referring to state penal code 30.07, according to which a handgun may not be carried openly even by a licensed gun carrier. To do so is considered trespassing.[^cite_note-OpenAndCampus-192][^cite_note-Open-193][^cite_note-194] Texas is the 45th state to have open carry.[^cite_note-195] In 2017, Abbott signed into law a bill lowering handgun carry license fees.[^cite_note-196] In 2021, he signed into law a bill that allowed Texans to [carry guns without a license](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_carry).[^cite_note-197]

In an interview after the November 5, 2017, [Sutherland Springs church shooting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sutherland_Springs_church_shooting), Abbott urged historical reflection and the consideration that evil had been present in earlier "horrific events" during the Nazi era, the Middle Ages and biblical times.[^cite_note-198] The [Anti-Defamation League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Defamation_League) called his comparison of the shooting "to the victims of the Holocaust" "deeply offensive" and "insensitive".[^cite_note-199][^cite_note-200]

After the [2018 Santa Fe High School shooting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Santa_Fe_High_School_shooting), Abbott said he would consult across Texas in an attempt to prevent [gun violence in schools](59628.md).[^cite_note-201] A series of round-table discussions followed at the state capitol.[^cite_note-202] In a speech at an NRA convention in Dallas about two weeks after the shooting, Abbott said, "The problem is not guns, it's hearts without God".[^cite_note-203] In June 2019, he signed a bill allowing for more armed teachers, with school districts unrestricted as to the number they allow.[^cite_note-Vertuno-204] The bill created "threat assessment teams" intended to identify potentially violent students.[^cite_note-205] Although the state legislature passed measures for students services to deal with related mental health issues, proposals to adopt a [red flag law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_flag_law) failed. Abbott said such a law was "not necessary in the state of Texas".[^cite_note-Vertuno-204]

In August 2019, a gunman who had written a racist manifesto [killed 22 people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_El_Paso_shooting) in a mass shooting at a [Walmart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walmart) in [El Paso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Paso,_Texas), saying he had targeted "Mexicans".[^cite_note-SamuelsSpecialSession-206] After the shooting, Abbott convened a domestic terrorism task force to look into domestic extremism but reiterated his opposition to a red-flag law and rejected calls to convene a special session of the state legislature to address gun violence.[^cite_note-SamuelsSpecialSession-206]

In June 2021, Abbott signed into law a permitless carry bill allowing Texans to carry handguns without a license or training beginning in September.[^cite_note-207]

On May 24, 2022, Abbott said that an 18-year-old carrying a handgun and possibly a rifle (later identified as a [Daniel Defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Defense) DDM4, an [AR-15 style rifle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AR-15_style_rifle))[^cite_note-NPR_AR15_2022-208] [killed 19 students and 2 teachers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robb_Elementary_School_shooting) at Robb Elementary School in [Uvalde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uvalde,_Texas).[^cite_note-209] On May 25, Abbott held a news conference on the shooting. He said that mental health in the community was the root cause of the event. [Beto O'Rourke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beto_O'Rourke), who was running for the Democratic nomination for governor in 2022, approached the stage and said, "The time to stop the next shooting is right now and you are doing nothing." Abbott responded that it was a time for "healing and hope" for the victims' families, not "our agendas".[^cite_note-210] Rather than attend the annual NRA meeting on May 27, Abbott published a YouTube message. He said that gun laws have not been effective, noting that the shooter broke two gun laws the day he committed the multiple murders. It is a felony to possess a gun on school property, and "what he did on campus is capital murder. That's a crime that would have subjected him to the death penalty in Texas".[^cite_note-Fox_26_Houston-211]

### Homelessness

In June 2019, the city of [Austin](1998.md) introduced an ordinance that repealed a 25-year-old ban on homeless people camping, lying, or sleeping in public.[^cite_note-212] In October, Abbott sent Austin mayor [Steve Adler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Adler_\(politician\)) a widely publicized letter criticizing the ordinance and threatening to deploy state resources to "combat homelessness".[^cite_note-Bova-213]

In November 2019, Abbott directed the State of Texas to open a temporary homeless encampment on a former vehicle storage yard owned by the [Texas Department of Transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Department_of_Transportation), which camp residents dubbed "Abbottville".[^cite_note-214]

### Immigration and border security

See also: [Operation Lone Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Lone_Star) and [Standoff at Eagle Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standoff_at_Eagle_Pass)



Abbott and Secretary of Homeland Security [John F. Kelly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Kelly) in a helicopter touring the [Mexico–United States border](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico%E2%80%93United_States_border) in 2017

In June 2015, Abbott signed a bill bolstering Texas's border security operations, including hiring additional state police, expanding the use of technology, and creating intelligence operations units.[^cite_note-215] In November, after the [Paris terrorist attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/November_2015_Paris_attacks) earlier that month, he announced that Texas would refuse [Syrian refugees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refugees_of_the_Syrian_Civil_War). In December, Abbott ordered the [Texas Health and Human Services Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Health_and_Human_Services_Commission) to sue the federal government and the [International Rescue Committee](196452.md) to block refugee settlement, but a federal district court struck the lawsuit down.[^cite_note-216]

In February 2017, Abbott blocked funding to [Travis County, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travis_County,_Texas), due to its recently implemented [sanctuary city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanctuary_city) policy.[^cite_note-217][^cite_note-218] In May, he signed into law [Texas Senate Bill 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Senate_Bill_4), targeting sanctuary cities by charging county or city officials who refuse to work with federal officials and allowing police officers to check the immigration status of those they detain.[^cite_note-219][^cite_note-220]

In January 2020, Abbott made Texas the first state to decline refugee resettlement under a new rule implemented by the Trump administration.[^cite_note-221] In a joint statement, all sixteen [Catholic bishops of Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Catholic_bishops_of_Texas) condemned the move.[^cite_note-222]

In 2021, Abbott said that illegal immigrants were invading homes.[^cite_note-223] In March, he tweeted, "The Biden Administration is recklessly releasing hundreds of illegal immigrants who have COVID into Texas communities." [PolitiFact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PolitiFact) rated Abbott's claim "Mostly False", since those being released were [asylum seekers](276005.md) with a legal right to remain in the U.S., and the number was only 108 at the time of the tweet.[^cite_note-224]

In June 2021, Abbott ordered Texas child-care regulators to take the licenses of child-care facilities that housed [unaccompanied migrant minors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unaccompanied_minor). He said that housing unaccompanied minors in child-care facilities adversely affected facilities housing Texan children in foster care.[^cite_note-225] Later that month, he announced plans to build a [border wall with Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico%E2%80%93United_States_barrier), saying the state would provide \$250 million and that direct donations from the public would be solicited.[^cite_note-226][^cite_note-227]

In July 2021, Abbott advised state law enforcement officers to begin arresting illegal migrants for trespassing.[^cite_note-228][^cite_note-229] On July 27, he ordered the National Guard to begin helping arrest migrants,[^cite_note-230][^cite_note-231] and on July 28 he signed an order restricting ground transportation of migrants.[^cite_note-232][^cite_note-233][^cite_note-234] Migrants arrested under Abbott's policy were imprisoned for weeks without legal help or formal charges.[^cite_note-235] By December 2023, nearly 10,000 migrants had been arrested on trespassing charges.[^cite_note-236][^cite_note-237]

In September 2021, Abbott signed legislation spending nearly \$2 billion on Texas's border security operations, including \$750 million for border wall construction.[^cite_note-Barragán2021-238] This was a significant increase, and supplemented \$1 billion already appropriated for border security in the two-year state budget.[^cite_note-Barragán2021-238][^cite_note-239] In December, Abbott announced that Texas would continue the [U.S. border wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico%E2%80%93United_States_border_wall) President Trump had started.[^cite_note-Wall-240] The wall has the same design as Trump's and is under construction.

In April 2022, Abbott announced that Texas would increase inspections of commercial trucks entering from Mexico with the goal of seizing illegal drugs and illegal migrants.[^cite_note-241] Shortly thereafter, the inspections caused a multi-mile backup of commercial vehicles carrying produce, auto parts, household goods, and other items. A spokesman for the Fresh Produce Association of the Americas said that up to 80% of perishable fruits and vegetables had been unable to cross and in some cases were in danger of spoiling. The president of the Texas Trucking Association said the delays were affecting every kind of trucking and being felt across the country.[^cite_note-242] Mexican truckers blockaded several bridges in protest.[^cite_note-243] Under heavy pressure from Texas business owners, who strongly criticized the "secondary inspections", Abbott canceled the policy on April 15. He said the reversal was because the governors of adjacent Mexican states had agreed to exercise stronger vigilance against human trafficking, drugs, and guns.[^cite_note-244] Abbott's truck inspections cost Texas an estimated \$4.2 billion and led to no apprehensions of drugs or illegal migrants.[^cite_note-245]

In April 2022, Abbott announced in a press conference a plan to direct the Texas Division of Emergency Management to bus illegal immigrants with 900 charter buses from Texas to Washington, D.C,[^cite_note-246] citing a potential surge of immigrants crossing the border after [Title 42](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title_42_expulsion) provisions regarding communicable disease were set to be rolled back by President Biden the next month.[^cite_note-247] Any mayor, county judge, or city could request buses for immigrants who had been released from federal custody.[^cite_note-248] After initial criticism, Abbott clarified that the trip would be voluntary for immigrants.[^cite_note-249] The first bus, carrying 24 immigrants, arrived in Washington D.C after a 30-hour trip.[^cite_note-250] A second bus arrived the next day.[^cite_note-251] Abbott came under fire for both buses, with one [American Enterprise Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Enterprise_Institute) scholar suggesting he be federally prosecuted for human trafficking. Senator [Ted Cruz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ted_Cruz) supported Abbott's actions and advocated that more immigrants be bused into other predominantly Democratic areas.[^cite_note-252] In a press conference, White House press secretary [Jen Psaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jen_Psaki) said it was "nice" that Texas was "helping them get to their final destination as they await the outcome of their immigration proceedings".[^cite_note-253] Washington, D.C., mayor [Muriel Bowser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muriel_Bowser) responded to the influx of migrants by requesting [National Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/District_of_Columbia_National_Guard) support for what she called a "migrant crisis".[^cite_note-254]

On September 15, 2022, Abbott sent two buses with 101 mostly Venezuelan migrants detained after crossing the [U.S. border with Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico%E2%80%93United_States_border) to the residence of Vice President [Kamala Harris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamala_Harris), at the [Naval Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Naval_Observatory) in Washington, D.C.[^cite_note-Segraves_Swalec_2022_d089-255] On September 17, Abbott sent another bus with 50 migrants to Harris's residence.[^cite_note-256]

In June 2023, Abbott deployed floating barriers in the Rio Grande in an effort to deter illegal border crossings.[^cite_note-257] The U.S. Justice Department sued Abbott and the state of Texas after Abbott refused to remove the barriers.[^cite_note-258]

In December 2023, Abbott signed three border-security-related bills into law, including a bill making illegal immigration a state crime.[^cite_note-259][^cite_note-260][^cite_note-261]

### Jade Helm 15

See also: [Jade Helm 15 conspiracy theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jade_Helm_15_conspiracy_theories)

In April 2015, Abbott asked the [Texas State Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_State_Guard) to monitor the military training exercise *[Jade Helm 15](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jade_Helm_15)* amid Internet-fueled suspicions that the war simulation was really a hostile military takeover.[^cite_note-263][^cite_note-264][^cite_note-265][^cite_note-dallasnews.com-266] In 2018, former [CIA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CIA) and NSA director [Michael Hayden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Hayden_\(general\)) said Russian intelligence organizations had propagated the conspiracy theory and that Abbott's response convinced them of the power such a misinformation campaign could have in the U.S.[^cite_note-267]

### LGBTQ rights

In 2014, Abbott defended Texas's ban on same-sex marriage, which a federal court ruled unconstitutional.[^cite_note-\:7-270] As attorney general of Texas, he argued that the prohibition on same-sex marriage incentivized that children would be born "in the context of stable, lasting relationships".[^cite_note-\:7-270]

Abbott condemned *[Obergefell v. Hodges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obergefell_v._Hodges)*, the Supreme Court ruling that same-sex marriage bans are unconstitutional.[^cite_note-\:1-271] He said, "the Supreme Court has abandoned its role as an impartial judicial arbiter."[^cite_note-\:1-271] Shortly thereafter, Abbott filed a lawsuit to stop same-sex spouses of city employees from being covered by benefit policies.[^cite_note-272]

In a letter dated May 27, 2017, the CEOs of 14 large technology companies, including [Facebook](7529378.md), [Apple Inc.](856.md), [Microsoft](19001.md), and [Amazon.com](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon.com), urged Abbott not to pass what came to be known as the "bathroom bill":[^cite_note-273] legislation requiring people to use the bathroom of the sex listed on their birth certificates, not the one of their choice. Abbott revived the bill and Lieutenant Governor [Dan Patrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Patrick_\(politician\)) supported it.[^cite_note-274] In March 2018, [Byron Cook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byron_Cook_\(politician\)), the chairman of the House State Affairs committee who blocked the bill, claimed that Abbott privately opposed the bill.[^cite_note-275] The bill was never signed; Abbott later said, "it's not on my agenda" in a debate with [Lupe Valdez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lupe_Valdez), the [2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Texas_gubernatorial_election) Democratic nominee for governor.[^cite_note-276]

In 2017, Abbott signed legislation to allow taxpayer-funded adoption agencies to refuse same-sex families from adopting children for religious reasons.[^cite_note-277]

In 2021, a Republican primary challenger criticized Abbott because Texas's child welfare agency included content regarding LGBTQ youths. Shortly thereafter, the agency, whose members Abbott appoints, removed the webpage that included a suicide prevention hotline and other resources for LGBT youths.[^cite_note-278]

In 2022, Abbott instructed Texas state agencies to treat [gender-affirming medical treatments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender-affirming_medical_treatments) (such as puberty blockers or hormone treatments) for [transgender youths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transgender_youth) as child abuse.[^cite_note-279][^cite_note-280][^cite_note-281]

### Marijuana

Further information: [Cannabis in Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis_in_Texas)

In June 2019, Abbott signed into law House Bill 1325, which legalized the cultivation of industrial [hemp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemp) (cannabis containing less than 0.3% [THC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrahydrocannabinol)).[^cite_note-282] It also legalized possession and sale of hemp-derived [CBD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CBD-DMH) products without need for a doctor's approval. HB 1325 passed the Senate 31–0 and the House 140–3. An unintended consequence of the legislation was that numerous local prosecutors announced that they would stop prosecuting low-level marijuana offenses, due to a lack of testing equipment.[^cite_note-283] Abbott has said that legal hemp products had to come with a "hemp certificate" and instructed the municipalities to continue enforcing marijuana laws.[^cite_note-284][^cite_note-\:6-285][^cite_note-286]

In 2022, a poll of Texas voters found that 55% of Texans either support or strongly support legalizing cannabis.[^cite_note-287]

After the enactment of HB 1325, the sale of consumable hemp products exploded in the state, including the sale products containing [delta-8-THC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%CE%948-Tetrahydrocannabinol). In 2025, the legislature passed SB 3, which would have completely banned the sale of THC products in the state. Abbott vetoed the bill after public outcry, which put him at odds with Lieutenant Governor Patrick, the primary advocate for a complete ban.[^cite_note-288][^cite_note-289] In explaining his veto, Abbott said the complete ban went too far and could face legal challenges. Instead of a full ban, he pushed for "regulation" including restrictions on the age of consumers, time of purchase, and testing requirements similar to those in the alcohol industry.[^cite_note-290] Abbott included the issue of THC regulation in the two special legislative sessions he called over the summer of 2025, but due to continued disagreement with Patrick, comprehensive legislation on the issue failed to pass.[^cite_note-291]

On September 10, 2025, Abbott issued an executive order directing the [Department of State Health Services (DSHS)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Department_of_State_Health_Services) and the [Texas Alcoholic Beverage Commission (TABC)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Alcoholic_Beverage_Commission) to develop rules, regulations, and oversight of THC products. These included a prohibition on sales of the product to anyone under 21 and requiring a government-issued ID to purchase it.[^cite_note-292]

### Religion

In 2015, Abbott signed the Pastor Protection Act, which allows members of the clergy to refuse to officiate at same-sex weddings if they feel doing so violates their beliefs.[^cite_note-293]

In 2017, Abbott signed into law Senate Bill 24, preventing state or local governments from subpoenaing pastors' sermons.[^cite_note-SvitekSubpoenas-294][^cite_note-295] The bill was inspired by an anti-discrimination ordinance in [Houston](13774.md), where five pastors' sermons were subpoenaed.[^cite_note-SvitekSubpoenas-294]

Also in 2017, Abbott signed House Bill 3859, which allows faith-based groups working with the Texas child welfare system to deny services "under circumstances that conflict with the provider's sincerely held religious beliefs." Democrats and civil rights advocates said the adoption bill could allow such groups to discriminate against those who practice a different religion or who are lesbian, gay, bisexual, or transgender, and LGBT rights groups said they would challenge the bill in court.[^cite_note-296][^cite_note-297] In response, California added Texas to a list of states to which it banned official government travel.[^cite_note-298]

In 2025, Abbott signed [Texas Senate Bill 10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Senate_Bill_10), which requires all public schools to display the [Ten Commandments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_Commandments) in their classrooms. A federal court blocked a [similar law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_Bill_71) in [Louisiana](18130.md) the previous day.[^cite_note-299]

In November 2025, Abbott declared the [Muslim Brotherhood](20742.md) and the [Council on American–Islamic Relations (CAIR)](159138.md) "foreign terrorist organizations and transnational criminal organizations".[^cite_note-300][^cite_note-301] He also called for an investigation of so-called "sharia courts", saying, "The Constitution's religious protections provide no authority for religious courts to skirt state and federal laws simply by donning robes and pronouncing positions inconsistent with western civilization".[^cite_note-302]

### Voting rights

See also: [Republican efforts to restrict voting following the 2020 presidential election § Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_efforts_to_restrict_voting_following_the_2020_presidential_election#Texas)

Abbott pressed for a purge of nearly 100,000 registered voters from Texas voter rolls. Texas officials initially claimed that the voters to be purged were not [U.S. citizens](25151566.md). The purge was canceled in April 2018 after voting rights groups challenged it, and officials at the Office of the Texas Secretary of State admitted that tens of thousands of legal voters (naturalized citizens) were wrongly flagged for removal. Abbott claimed that he played no role in the voter purge, but emails released in June 2019 showed he was the driving force behind the effort.[^cite_note-303]

In September 2020, Abbott issued a proclamation that each Texas county could have only one location where voters could drop off [early voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_voting#United_States) ballots. He said that would prevent "illegal voting" but cited no examples of [voter fraud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voter_fraud_in_the_United_States). Election security experts say voter fraud is extremely rare.[^cite_note-\:4-304][^cite_note-\:5-305] Also in September 2020, Abbott extended the early voting period for that year's general election due to COVID-19; the [Republican Party of Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_Party_of_Texas) opposed his decision.[^cite_note-306]

Abbott made "election integrity" a legislative priority after President Trump's failed [attempts to overturn the election results of 2020 United States presidential election](65905202.md) by using baseless claims that the results were fraudulent.[^cite_note-307] Voting rights advocates and civil rights groups denounced the resulting legislation, saying it disproportionately affected voters of color and people with disabilities.[^cite_note-308]

In July 2021, Democratic lawmakers in the Texas legislature fled the state on a chartered flight to Washington, D.C., in an effort to block the passage of a bill that would reform the state election procedures.[^cite_note-309] Abbott threatened to have the lawmakers arrested upon their return to Texas.[^cite_note-310] In August, the [Supreme Court of Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Texas) made a ruling allowing for the arrest of the absent lawmakers, so they could be brought to the state capitol.[^cite_note-311]

In October 2021, Abbott appointed [John Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_B._Scott_\(Texas_politician\)) as Texas secretary of state, putting him in a position to oversee Texas elections. Scott aided Trump in his failed efforts to throw out election results in the 2020 presidential election.[^cite_note-312][^cite_note-313]

## Personal life



Abbott (far right) and his wife [Cecilia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecilia_Abbott) (far left) with President [Donald Trump](4848272.md) and First Lady [Melania Trump](1209075.md) in 2020

### Wheelchair use

On July 14, 1984, at age 26, Abbott was [paralyzed below the waist](410866.md) when an oak tree fell on him while he was jogging in [River Oaks, Houston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Oaks,_Houston), after a storm.[^cite_note-TXAttyGenBio-7][^cite_note-318] Two steel rods were implanted in his spine, and he underwent extensive rehabilitation at [TIRR Memorial Hermann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TIRR_Memorial_Hermann) in Houston and has used a wheelchair ever since.[^cite_note-319][^cite_note-320] He sued the homeowner and a tree service company, resulting in an insurance settlement that provided him with lump sum payments every three years until 2022 along with monthly payments for life, both of which were adjusted for inflation.[^cite_note-JayRoot1-321] As of August 2013, the monthly payment was US\$14,000 and the three-year lump sum payment was US\$400,000, all tax-free. Abbott has said he relied on the money to pay for nearly three decades of medical expenses and other costs.[^cite_note-JayRoot1-321]
