In [Christianity](5211.md), a **bishop** is an [ordained](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordained) member of the [clergy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clergy) who is entrusted with a position of [authority and oversight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episcopal_polity) in a religious institution. Bishops are normally responsible for the governance and administration of [dioceses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese), although some function in the various Christian denominations and traditions as [auxiliaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auxiliary_bishop), [apostolic administrators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostolic_administration), and as [titular bishops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titular_bishop).

The role or office of the bishop is called **episcopacy** or the **episcopate**. Organisationally, several [Christian denominations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_denomination) utilise ecclesiastical structures that call for the position of bishops, while other denominations have dispensed with this office, seeing it as a symbol of power. Bishops have also exercised political authority within their dioceses. Historically, [Manichaeism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manichaeism) also employed a similar office within their [church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manichaeism#Organization), having a fixed number of 72 bishops.[^cite_note-1]

Traditionally, bishops claim to be within [apostolic succession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostolic_succession) and the [historic episcopacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_episcopate), a direct historical lineage dating back to the original [Twelve Apostles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_Apostles), [Saint Paul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Paul), or one of the [Seventy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seventy_disciples). The bishops are by doctrine understood as those who possess the full [priesthood](23707.md#Christianity) given by [Jesus Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_in_Christianity), and therefore may ordain other clergy, including other bishops.[^cite_note-2] A person ordained as a [deacon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deacon), priest (i.e. [presbyter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyter)), and then bishop is understood to hold the fullness of the [ministerial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_\(Christianity\)) priesthood, given responsibility by Christ to govern, teach and [sanctify](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanctify) the [Body of Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_of_Christ) (the [Christian Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Church)). Priests, deacons and [lay ministers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laity) co-operate and assist their bishops in [pastoral](215153.md) ministry.

Some [Pentecostal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentecostal) and other [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant) denominations have bishops who oversee congregations, though they do not necessarily claim apostolic succession, with exception to those Pentecostals and Charismatics affiliated to churches founded by [J. Delano Ellis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Delano_Ellis) and [Paul S. Morton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_S._Morton).

## Etymology and terminology

The English word *bishop* derives, via [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) *episcopus*, [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English) *biscop*, and [Middle English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_English) *bisshop*, from the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) word ἐπίσκοπος, *epískopos*, meaning "overseer" or "supervisor".[^cite_note-BIA-3] Greek was the language of the early Christian church,[^cite_note-ccel_Earl-4] but the term *epískopos* did not originate in Christianity: it had been used in Greek for several centuries before the advent of Christianity.[^cite_note-BIA-3]

The English words *priest* and *presbyter* both derive, via Latin, from the Greek word πρεσβύτερος, *presbýteros*, meaning "elder" or "senior", and not originally referring to priesthood.[^cite_note-5]

In the early Christian era the two terms were not always clearly distinguished, but *epískopos* is used in the sense of the order or office of bishop, distinct from that of *presbýteros*, in the writings attributed to [Ignatius of Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignatius_of_Antioch) in the second century.[^cite_note-ccel_Earl-4]

## Christian episcopal development



Ignatius, bishop of Antioch, student of [John the Apostle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_the_Apostle)

According to most scholars, the earliest organization of the [Christian church in Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_centers_of_Christianity#Jerusalem) was similar to that of Jewish [synagogues](48559.md), but it had a council or college of ordained [presbyters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyters) (πρεσβύτεροι, *elders*). In Acts 11\:30[^cite_note-6] and Acts 15\:22,[^cite_note-7] a collegiate system of government in Jerusalem is chaired by [James the Just](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James,_brother_of_Jesus), according to tradition the [first bishop of the city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Orthodox_Patriarch_of_Jerusalem#Jewish_Bishops_of_Jerusalem). In Acts 14\:23,[^cite_note-8] the [Apostle Paul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_of_Tarsus) ordains presbyters for [churches in Anatolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_centers_of_Christianity#Anatolia).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHill2007-9] The word *presbyter* was not yet distinguished from *overseer* (ἐπίσκοπος, *episkopos*, later used exclusively to mean *bishop*), as in Acts 20\:17 and 20\:28,[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11] Titus 1\:5–7[^cite_note-12] and 1 Peter 5\:1.[^cite_note-13][^cite_note-15][^cite_note-17] The earliest writings of the [Apostolic Fathers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostolic_Fathers), the *[Didache](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Didache)* and the [First Epistle of Clement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Epistle_of_Clement), for example, show the church used two terms for local church offices—presbyters (seen by many as an interchangeable term with *episkopos* or overseer) and deacon.

In the [First Epistle to Timothy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Epistle_to_Timothy) and [Epistle to Titus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistle_to_Titus) in the New Testament a more clearly defined episcopate can be seen. Both letters state that Paul had left Timothy in Ephesus and Titus in [Crete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_centers_of_Christianity#Greece) to oversee the [local church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_\(congregation\)).[^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19] Paul commands Titus to ordain presbyters/bishops and to exercise general oversight. John Zizioulas argues that "The task of the Bishop was from the beginning principally liturgical, consisting in the offering of the Divine Eucharist."[^cite_note-20] The [authorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authorship_of_the_Pauline_epistles) of both those letters is questioned by many scholars in the field and the question whether they reflect a first or second century structure of church hierarchy is among the arguments used in the debate as to their authenticity.

Early sources are unclear but various groups of Christian communities may have had the bishop surrounded by a group or college functioning as leaders of the local churches.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEO'Grady1997140-21][^cite_note-22] Eventually the head or "monarchic" bishop came to rule more clearly,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEVan_Hove1907-23] and all local churches would eventually follow the example of the other churches and structure themselves after the model of the others with the one bishop in clearer charge,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEO'Grady1997140-21] though the role of the body of presbyters remained important.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEVan_Hove1907-23]

### Apostolic Fathers



A 6th-century image of [Saint Augustine](2030.md), bishop of [Hippo Regius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippo_Regius)

[^cite_note-WDL-24]

Around the end of the [1st century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_the_1st_century), the early church's organization became clearer in historical documents. In the works of the Apostolic Fathers, and Ignatius of Antioch in particular, the role of the *episkopos*, or bishop, became more important, or became more clearly defined. Other contemporary Christian writers do not describe monarchial bishops, either continuing to equate them with the presbyters or speaking of *episkopoi* (bishops, plural) in a city.

[Clement of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clement_of_Alexandria) (end of the 2nd century) writes about the ordination of a certain Zachæus as bishop by the imposition of [Simon Peter Bar-Jonah's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Peter) hands. The words bishop and ordination are used in their technical meaning by the same Clement of Alexandria.[^cite_note-25] The bishops in the 2nd century are defined also as the only clergy to whom the ordination to priesthood ([presbyterate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyterate)) and diaconate is entrusted: "a priest (presbyter) [lays on hands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laying_on_of_hands), but does not [ordain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordain)." (*cheirothetei ou cheirotonei*).[^cite_note-26]

At the beginning of the 3rd century, [Hippolytus of Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippolytus_of_Rome) describes another feature of the ministry of a bishop, which is that of the *"Spiritum primatus sacerdotii habere potestatem dimittere peccata"*: the primate of sacrificial priesthood and the power to forgive sins.[^cite_note-web._Whyt-27]

### Canonical age



Patriarch Stephen I of Constantinople was ordained (consecrated) as bishop at the age of 19

As the bishop's role further developed into the 4th century, the [First Council of Nicaea](11118.md) decreed that bishops should be ordained by at least three others.[^cite_note-28] Age requirements for episcopal ordination or consecration were neither universal nor fixed in early Christian churches.\[^cite_note-[www.newadvent.org-29](http://www.newadvent.org-29/)] It was, however, universally required that a bishop be male; and lacking a definitive ecumenical age requirement for holy orders—between the early [ecumenical councils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecumenical_council) of the [Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Church) and imperial Roman churches, and after schism into the Latin and Greek churches—young men had been ordained, appointed, and/or enthroned as bishops, archbishops, patriarchs and cardinals, some as young as 5.[^cite_note-30]

## Christian bishops and civil government

See also: [State church of the Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_church_of_the_Roman_Empire)

The efficient organization of the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire) became the template for the organisation of the [Great Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Church) in the [4th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_the_4th_century), particularly after [Constantine's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_the_Great_and_Christianity) [Edict of Milan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edict_of_Milan). As the church moved from the shadows of privacy into the public forum it acquired land for churches, burials and [clergy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clergy). In 391, [Theodosius I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodosius_I) decreed that any land that had been confiscated from the church by Roman authorities be returned.[^cite_note-67]



A bishop with other officials on an 11th-century grave in [Sweden](5058739.md)

The most usual term for the geographic area of a bishop's authority and ministry, the [diocese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese), began as part of the structure of the Roman Empire under [Diocletian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocletian). As [Roman authority began to fail in the western portion of the empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_Western_Roman_Empire), the church took over much of the civil administration.[^cite_note-68] This can be clearly seen in the ministry of two [popes](23056.md): [Pope Leo I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Leo_I) in the [5th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_the_5th_century), and [Pope Gregory I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Gregory_I) in the [6th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_the_6th_century). Both of these men were statesmen and public administrators in addition to their role as Christian pastors, teachers and leaders. In the [Eastern churches](10186.md), [latifundia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latifundia) entailed to a bishop's [see](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episcopal_see) were much less common, the state power did not collapse the way it did in the West, and thus the tendency of bishops acquiring civil power was much weaker than in the West. However, the role of Western bishops as civil authorities, often called [prince bishops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince-Bishop), continued throughout much of the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages).[^cite_note-69]

### Bishops holding political office



[Peder Winstrup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peder_Winstrup), a Danish bishop



[Johann Otto von Gemmingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Otto_von_Gemmingen), [Prince-Bishop of Augsburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince-Bishopric_of_Augsburg)

As well as being [archchancellors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archchancellor) of the [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire) after the 9th century, bishops generally served as [chancellors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancellor) to medieval monarchs, acting as head of the *justiciary* and chief [chaplain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaplain). The [Lord Chancellor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Chancellor) of [England](9316.md) was almost always a bishop up until the dismissal of [Cardinal](6221.md) [Thomas Wolsey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Wolsey) by [Henry VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_VIII).[^cite_note-70] Similarly, the position of [Kanclerz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanclerz) in the [Polish kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Poland_\(1385%E2%80%931569\)) was always held by a bishop until the [16th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_the_16th_century).[^cite_note-71]

In modern times, the principality of [Andorra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andorra) is headed by [Co-Princes of Andorra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Co-Princes_of_Andorra), one of whom is the [Bishop of Urgell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_Urgell) and the other, the sitting [President of France](24899.md), an arrangement that began with the [Paréage of Andorra (1278)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Par%C3%A9age_of_Andorra_\(1278\)), and was ratified in the 1993 constitution of Andorra.[^cite_note-unesco-72]

The [office of the Papacy](23056.md) is inherently held by the sitting Roman Catholic Bishop of Rome.[^cite_note-73][^cite_note-74] Though not originally intended to hold temporal authority, since the Middle Ages the power of the Roman papacy gradually expanded deep into the secular realm and for centuries the sitting Bishop of Rome was the most powerful governmental office in Central Italy.[^cite_note-Faus-75] In modern times, the Pope of Rome is also the sovereign Prince of [Vatican City](32408.md), an internationally recognized [micro-state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micro-state) located entirely within the city of Rome.[^cite_note-76][^cite_note-77][^cite_note-78][^cite_note-Geopolitics-79]

In [France](5843419.md), prior to [the Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution), representatives of the clergy—in practice, bishops and [abbots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbot) of the largest [monasteries](45856.md)—comprised the [First Estate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estates_of_the_realm#First_Estate) of the [Estates-General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_States-General). This role was abolished after separation of church and state was implemented during the French Revolution.[^cite_note-80]

In the 21st century, the more senior bishops of the [Church of England](5955.md) continue to sit in the [House of Lords](13658.md) of the [Parliament of the United Kingdom](13964.md), as representatives of the [established church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Established_church), and are known as [Lords Spiritual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lords_Spiritual). The [Bishop of Sodor and Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Sodor_and_Man), whose diocese lies outside the [United Kingdom](31717.md), is an [*ex officio* member](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ex_officio_member) of the [Legislative Council of the Isle of Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislative_Council_of_the_Isle_of_Man).[^cite_note-81] In the past, the [Bishop of Durham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Durham) had extensive vice-regal powers within his northern diocese, which was a [county palatine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_palatine), the [County Palatine of Durham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Palatine_of_Durham), (previously, [Liberty of Durham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberty_of_Durham)) of which he was *ex officio* the [earl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earl). In the 19th century, a gradual process of reform was enacted, with the majority of the bishop's historic powers vested in [The Crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Crown) by 1858.[^cite_note-durham1858-82]

[Eastern Orthodox](10186.md) bishops, along with all other members of the clergy, are [canonically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_law) forbidden to hold political office.[^cite_note-83] Occasional exceptions to this rule are tolerated when the alternative is political chaos. In the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md), the [Patriarch of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecumenical_Patriarch_of_Constantinople), for example, had de facto administrative, cultural and legal jurisdiction,[^cite_note-84] as well as spiritual authority, over all Eastern Orthodox Christians of the empire, as part of the Ottoman [millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millet_\(Ottoman_Empire\)) system. An Eastern Orthodox bishop headed the [Prince-Bishopric of Montenegro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince-Bishopric_of_Montenegro) from 1516 to 1852, assisted by a secular *[guvernadur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guvernadur)*. More recently, Archbishop [Makarios III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makarios_III) of [Cyprus](5593.md), served as [President](1671074.md) of the [Cyprus](5593.md) from 1960 to 1977, an extremely turbulent time period on the island.[^cite_note-85]

In 2001, [Peter Hollingworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Hollingworth), [AC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Companions_of_the_Order_of_Australia), [OBE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_the_British_Empire)—then the Anglican Archbishop of [Brisbane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Diocese_of_Brisbane)—was controversially appointed [Governor-General of Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governor-General_of_Australia). Although Hollingworth gave up his episcopal position to accept the appointment, it still attracted considerable opposition in a country which maintains a formal [separation between Church and State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separation_of_church_and_state).[^cite_note-86][^cite_note-87]

### Episcopacy during the English Civil War

Main articles: [Episcopacy during the English Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episcopacy_during_the_English_Civil_War) and [Bishops in Calvinism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishops_in_Calvinism)

During the period of the [English Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Civil_War), the role of bishops as wielders of political power and as upholders of the established church became a matter of heated political controversy. [Presbyterianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyterianism) was the polity of most [Reformed Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reformed_Christianity) in Europe, and had been favored by many in England since the English Reformation. Since in the [primitive Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostolic_Age) the offices of *presbyter* and *episkopos* were not clearly distinguished, many [Puritans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puritans) held that this was the only form of government the church should have. The Anglican divine, [Richard Hooker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Hooker), objected to this claim in his famous work *Of the Laws of Ecclesiastic Polity* while, at the same time, defending Presbyterian ordination as valid (in particular [Calvin's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Calvin) ordination of [Beza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Beza)). This was the official stance of the English Church until the Commonwealth, during which time, the views of Presbyterians and Independents ([Congregationalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congregationalists)) were more freely expressed and practiced.

## Christian churches

### Catholic, Eastern and Oriental Orthodox, Lutheran and Anglican churches

Further information: [Bishops in the Catholic Church](8799424.md)



A [mitre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitre) is used as a symbol of the bishop's ministry in Western Christianity.



One form for the [coat of arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coat_of_arms) of a Catholic bishop



*Karl XIV Johans kröning* by [Per Krafft the Younger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Per_Krafft_the_Younger) (1818) depicting [Evangelical-Lutheran](23371382.md) bishops and priests in [vestments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vestment) during the coronation of [Charles XIV John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_XIV_John) in [Stockholm Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm_Cathedral)

Bishops exercise leadership roles in the [Catholic Church](606848.md), the [Eastern Orthodox Church](10186.md), the [Oriental Orthodox Churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriental_Orthodox_Churches), certain [Evangelical-Lutheran churches](23371382.md), the [Anglican Communion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Communion), the [Independent Catholic churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_Catholic_Churches), the [Independent Anglican churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuing_Anglican_movement), and certain other, smaller, denominations.

The traditional role of a bishop is as pastor of a [diocese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese) (also called a bishopric, [synod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synod), [eparchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eparchy) or see), and so to serve as a "diocesan bishop", or "eparch" as it is called in many Eastern Christian churches. Dioceses vary considerably in size, geographically and population-wise. Some dioceses around the [Mediterranean Sea](19006.md) which were Christianised early are rather compact, whereas dioceses in areas of rapid modern growth in Christian commitment, as in some parts of [Sub-Saharan Africa](27067.md), [South America](26769.md) and the [Far East](11672.md), are much larger and more populous.



Mitre worn by an Eastern bishop with [icons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icon) of Christ, the [Theotokos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theotokos) (Mary, Mother of God) and [Forerunner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_the_Baptist) (John the Baptist)

As well as traditional diocesan bishops, many churches have a well-developed structure of church leadership that involves a number of layers of authority and responsibility:

[Archbishop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop)

An archbishop is the bishop of an [archdiocese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archdiocese). This is usually a prestigious diocese with an important place in local church history. In the Catholic Church, the title is purely honorific and carries no extra jurisdiction, though most archbishops are also metropolitan bishops, as above, and are always awarded a [pallium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pallium). In most provinces of the Anglican Communion, however, an archbishop has metropolitical and primatial power.

Area bishop

Some Anglican suffragans are given the responsibility for a geographical area within the diocese (for example, the [Bishop of Stepney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Stepney) is an *area bishop* within the [Diocese of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_London)).

[Assistant bishop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assistant_bishop)

Honorary assistant bishop, assisting bishop, or bishop emeritus: these titles are usually applied to retired bishops who are given a general licence to minister as episcopal pastors under a diocesan's oversight. The titles, in this meaning, are not used by the Catholic Church.

Auxiliary bishop

An auxiliary bishop is a full-time assistant to a diocesan bishop (the Catholic and Eastern Orthodox equivalent of an Anglican suffragan bishop). An auxiliary bishop is a titular bishop, and he is to be appointed as a [vicar general](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vicar_general) or at least as an [episcopal vicar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episcopal_vicar) of the diocese in which he serves.

[^cite_note-88]

[Catholicos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholicos)

Catholicoi are the heads of some of the Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, and Eastern Rite Catholic sui iuris churches (notably the Armenian), roughly similar to a Patriarch.

Chorbishop

A chorbishop is an official of a diocese in some Eastern Christian churches. Chorbishops are not generally ordained bishops – they are not given the sacrament of Holy Orders in that degree – but function as assistants to the diocesan bishop with certain honorary privileges.

[Coadjutor bishop](1338403.md)

A coadjutor bishop is an auxiliary bishop who is given almost equal authority in a diocese with the diocesan bishop, and the automatic right to succeed the incumbent diocesan bishop. The appointment of coadjutors is often seen as a means of providing for continuity of church leadership.

General bishop

A title and role in some churches, not associated with a diocese. In the Coptic Orthodox Church the episcopal ranks from highest to lowest are metropolitan archbishops, metropolitan bishops, diocesan bishops, bishops exarchs of the throne, suffragan bishops, auxiliary bishops, general bishops, and finally chorbishops. Bishops of the same category rank according to date of consecration.

[Major archbishop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_archbishop)

Major archbishops are the heads of some of the Eastern Catholic Churches. Their authority within their *sui juris* church is equal to that of a patriarch, but they receive fewer ceremonial honors.

[Metropolitan bishop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_bishop)

A metropolitan bishop is an archbishop in charge of an [ecclesiastical province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesiastical_province), or group of dioceses, and in addition to having immediate jurisdiction over his own archdiocese, also exercises some oversight over the other dioceses within that province. Sometimes a metropolitan may also be the head of an autocephalous, *sui iuris*, or [autonomous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_area) church when the number of adherents of that tradition are small. In the [Latin Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Church), metropolitans are always archbishops; in many Eastern churches, the title is "metropolitan", with some of these churches using "archbishop" as a separate office.

[Patriarch](175409.md)

Patriarchs are the bishops who head certain ancient [autocephalous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocephalous) or [sui iuris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sui_iuris) churches, which are a collection of metropolitan sees or [provinces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province). After the First Ecumenical Council at Nicea, the church structure was patterned after the administrative divisions of the Roman Empire wherein a metropolitan or bishop of a metropolis came to be the ecclesiastical head of a civil capital of a province or a metropolis. Whereas, the bishop of the larger administrative district, diocese, came to be called an exarch. In a few cases, a bishop came to preside over a number of dioceses, i.e., Rome, Antioch, and Alexandria. At the Fourth Ecumenical Council at Chalcedon in 451, Constantinople was given jurisdiction over three dioceses for the reason that the city was "the residence of the emperor and senate". Additionally, Jerusalem was recognized at the Council of Chalcedon as one of the major sees. In 692, the Quinisext Council formally recognized and ranked the sees of the Pentarchy in order of preeminence, at that time Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem. In the Catholic Church, Patriarchs sometimes call their leaders *Catholicos*; the Patriarch of the Coptic Orthodox Church of Alexandria, Egypt, is called *Pope*, meaning 'Father'. While most patriarchs in the [Eastern Catholic Churches](214903.md) have jurisdiction over a [particular church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particular_church) *[sui iuris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sui_iuris)*, all Latin Church patriarchs, except for the Pope, have only honorary titles. In 2006, [Pope Benedict XVI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XVI) gave up the title of [Patriarch of the West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarch_of_the_West). The first recorded use of the title by a Roman Pope was by [Theodore I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Theodore_I) in 620. However, early church documents, such as those of the [First Council of Nicaea](11118.md) (325) had always listed the Pope of Rome first among the [Ancient Patriarchs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentarchy_\(Christianity\)) (first three, and later five: Rome, Constantinople, [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarch_of_Alexandria), [Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarch_of_Antioch) and [Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Orthodox_Patriarch_of_Jerusalem)—collectively referred to as the *Pentarchy*). Later, the heads of various national churches became Patriarchs, but they are ranked below the Pentarchy.

Te Pīhopa

The [Anglican Church in Aotearoa, New Zealand and Polynesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Church_in_Aotearoa,_New_Zealand_and_Polynesia) uses — even in English language usage — this [Māori language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ori_language) term for its tikanga Māori bishops.

[Primate](24672.md)

A primate is usually the bishop of the oldest church of a [nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nation). Sometimes this carries jurisdiction over metropolitan bishops, but usually it is purely honorific. The primate of the [Scottish Episcopal Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Episcopal_Church) is chosen from among the diocesan bishops, and, while retaining diocesan responsibility, is called *Primus*.

[Presiding bishop](1468007.md) or president bishop

These titles are often used for the head of a national Anglican church, but the title is not usually associated with a particular episcopal see like the title of a primate.

[Suffragan bishop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suffragan_bishop)

A suffragan bishop is a bishop subordinate to a metropolitan. In the Catholic Church this term is applied to all non-metropolitan bishops (that is, diocesan bishops of dioceses within a metropolitan's province, and [auxiliary bishops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auxiliary_bishop)). In the Anglican Communion, the term applies to a bishop who is a full-time assistant to a diocesan bishop: the [Bishop of Warwick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Warwick) is suffragan to the [Bishop of Coventry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Coventry) (the diocesan), though both live in [Coventry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coventry).

[Supreme bishop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_bishop)

The [obispo maximo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obispo_maximo), or supreme bishop, of the [Philippine Independent Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine_Independent_Church) is elected by the General Assembly of the church. He is the chief executive officer of the church. He also holds an important pastoral role, being the spiritual head and chief pastor of the church. He has precedence of honor and prominence of position among, and recognized to have primacy, over other bishops.

[Titular bishop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titular_bishop)

A titular bishop is a bishop without a diocese. Rather, the bishop is head of a [titular see](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titular_see), which is usually an ancient city that used to have a bishop, but, for some reason or other, does not have one now. Titular bishops often serve as auxiliary bishops. In the [Ecumenical Patriarchate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecumenical_Patriarchate), bishops of modern dioceses are often given a titular see alongside their modern one (for example, the archbishop of [Thyateira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thyateira) *and* [Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Britain)).

In the Latin Church of the Catholic Church the administration of [Confirmation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confirmation) is normally reserved to the local bishop.

In Catholicism, Eastern Orthodoxy, Oriental Orthodoxy, [High Church Lutheranism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Church_Lutheranism), and Anglicanism, only a bishop can ordain other bishops, priests, and deacons.[^cite_note-COS2022-89]

The bishop is the ordinary minister of the [sacrament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacraments_of_the_Catholic_Church) of confirmation in the Latin Church, and in the [Old Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Catholic) communion only a bishop may administer this sacrament. In the [Lutheran](23371382.md) and [Anglican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican) churches, the bishop normatively administers the rite of confirmation, although in those denominations that do not have an episcopal polity, confirmation is administered by the priest.[^cite_note-Wordsworth1911-90] However, in the [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Rite) and other Eastern rites, whether Eastern or Oriental Orthodox or [Eastern Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Catholic), [chrismation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chrismation) is done immediately after [baptism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptism), and thus the priest is the one who confirms, using chrism blessed by a bishop.[^cite_note-91]

#### Ordination of bishops

Bishops in all of these communions are [ordained](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Orders) or consecrated by other bishops through the laying on of hands. Ordination of a bishop, and thus continuation of apostolic succession, takes place through a ritual centred on the [imposition of hands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imposition_of_hands) and [prayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prayer).[^cite_note-92][^cite_note-93][^cite_note-94]

In Scandinavia and the Baltic region, Lutheran churches participating in the [Porvoo Communion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porvoo_Communion) (those of Iceland, Norway, Sweden, Finland, Estonia, and Lithuania), as well as many non-Porvoo membership Lutheran churches (including those of Kenya, Latvia, and Russia), as well as the confessional [Communion of Nordic Lutheran Dioceses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communion_of_Nordic_Lutheran_Dioceses), believe that they ordain their bishops in the apostolic succession in lines stemming from the original apostles.[^cite_note-König2010-95][^cite_note-Obare-96][^cite_note-97] *The New Westminster Dictionary of Church History* states that "In Sweden the apostolic succession was preserved because the Catholic bishops were allowed to stay in office, but they had to approve changes in the ceremonies."[^cite_note-98]

While traditional teaching maintains that any bishop with apostolic succession can validly perform the ordination of another bishop, some churches require two or three bishops participate, either to ensure sacramental validity or to conform with church law.

##### Peculiar to the Catholic Church

[Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic) doctrine holds that one bishop can validly ordain another (priest) as a bishop. Although a minimum of three bishops participating is desirable (there are usually several more) in order to demonstrate collegiality, canonically only one bishop is necessary.[^cite_note-99] The [Second Vatican Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Vatican_Council)'s *[Constitution on the Sacred Liturgy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_on_the_Sacred_Liturgy)* stated that "when a bishop is consecrated, the laying of hands may be done by all the bishops present".[^cite_note-100]

Apart from the ordination, which is always done by other bishops, there are different methods as to the actual selection of a candidate for ordination as bishop. The [Dicastery for Bishops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dicastery_for_Bishops) generally oversees the selection of new bishops, with recommendations sent for the approval of the pope.[^cite_note-101] The papal nuncio usually solicits names from the bishops of a country, consults with priests and leading members of a laity, and then selects three to be forwarded to the [Holy See](13393.md). In Europe, some cathedral chapters have duties to elect bishops. The Eastern Catholic churches generally elect their own bishops. Most Eastern Orthodox churches allow varying amounts of formalised laity or [lower clergy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_clergy) influence on the choice of bishops. This also applies in those Eastern churches which are in union with the pope, though it is required that he give assent.

The pope, in addition to being the [Bishop of Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Rome) and spiritual head of the Catholic Church, is also the Patriarch of the Latin Church. Each bishop within the Latin Church is answerable directly to the Pope and not any other bishop except to metropolitans in certain oversight instances. In this instance, the pope uses the title *Patriarch of the West*, although this title was dropped from use between 2006 and 2024, when Pope Francis reinstituted it.[^cite_note-102]

##### Recognition of other churches' ordinations

The Catholic Church does recognise as valid (though illicit) ordinations done by breakaway Catholic, Old Catholic or Oriental bishops, and groups descended from them; it also regards as both valid and licit those ordinations done by bishops of the Eastern churches,[^cite_note-103] so long as those receiving the ordination conform to other canonical requirements (for example, is an adult male) and an eastern orthodox rite of episcopal ordination, expressing the proper functions and sacramental status of a bishop, is used; this has given rise to the phenomenon of *[episcopi vagantes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episcopi_vagantes)* (for example, clergy of the Independent Catholic groups which claim apostolic succession, though this claim is rejected by both Catholicism and Eastern Orthodoxy). With respect to Lutheranism, "the Catholic Church has never officially expressed its judgement on the validity of orders as they have been handed down by episcopal succession in these two national Lutheran churches" (the [Evangelical Lutheran Church of Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelical_Lutheran_Church_of_Sweden) and the [Evangelical Lutheran Church of Finland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelical_Lutheran_Church_of_Finland)) though it does "question how the ecclesiastical break in the 16th century has affected the apostolicity of the churches of the Reformation and thus the apostolicity of their ministry".[^cite_note-104][^cite_note-105]

Since [Pope Leo XIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Leo_XIII) issued the bull *[Apostolicae curae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostolicae_curae)* in 1896, the Catholic Church has insisted that Anglican orders are invalid because of the Reformed changes in the Anglican ordination rites of the 16th century and divergence in understanding of the theology of priesthood, episcopacy and Eucharist. However, since the 1930s, Utrecht Old Catholic bishops (recognised by the Holy See as validly ordained) have sometimes taken part in the ordination of Anglican bishops. According to the writer Timothy Dufort, by 1969, all Church of England bishops had acquired Old Catholic lines of apostolic succession recognised by the Holy See.[^cite_note-106] This development has been used to argue that the strain of apostolic succession has been re-introduced into Anglicanism, at least within the Church of England.[^cite_note-107] However, other issues, such as the Anglican ordination of women, is at variance with Catholic understanding of Christian teaching, and have contributed to the reaffirmation of Catholic rejection of Anglican ordinations.[^cite_note-Congregation_for_the_Doctrine_of_the_Faith-108][^cite_note-Handley-2003-109]

The Eastern Orthodox Church does not accept the validity of any ordinations performed by the Independent Catholic or Independent Orthodox groups, as Eastern Orthodoxy considers to be spurious any consecration outside the church as a whole. Eastern Orthodoxy considers apostolic succession to exist only within themselves as the one true church, and not through any authority held by individual bishops; thus, if a bishop ordains someone to serve outside the (Eastern Orthodox) Church, the ceremony is ineffectual, and no ordination has taken place regardless of the ritual used or the ordaining prelate's position within the Eastern Orthodox Church.



The consecrated bishop is the only minister of Holy Orders. Photo of pre-Vatican II ceremony.

The position of the Catholic Church is slightly different. Whilst it does recognise the validity of the orders of certain groups which separated from communion with Holy See (for instance, the ordinations of the Old Catholics in communion with Utrecht, as well as the [Polish National Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_National_Catholic_Church), which received its orders directly from Utrecht, and was until recently part of that communion), Catholicism does not recognise the orders of any group whose teaching is at variance with what they consider the core tenets of Christianity; this is the case even though the clergy of the Independent Catholic groups may use the proper ordination ritual. There are also other reasons why the Holy See does not recognise the validity of the orders of the independent clergy:

- They hold that the continuing practice among many independent clergy of one person receiving multiple ordinations in order to secure apostolic succession, betrays an incorrect and mechanistic theology of ordination.

- They hold that the practice within independent groups of ordaining women (such as within certain member communities of the [Anglican Communion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Communion)) demonstrates an understanding of priesthood that they vindicate is totally unacceptable to the Catholic and Eastern Orthodox churches as they believe that the Universal Church does not possess such authority; thus, they uphold that any ceremonies performed by these women should be considered sacramentally invalid.

- The theology of male clergy within the Independent movement is also suspect according to the Catholics, as they presumably approve of the ordination of females, and may have even undergone an (invalid) ordination ceremony conducted by a woman.

Whilst members of the [Independent Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_Catholic) movement take seriously the issue of valid orders, it is highly significant that the relevant Vatican Congregations tend not to respond to petitions from Independent Catholic bishops and clergy who seek to be received into communion with the Holy See, hoping to continue in some sacramental role. In those instances where the pope does grant reconciliation, those deemed to be clerics within the Independent Old Catholic movement are invariably admitted as laity and not priests or bishops.

There is a mutual recognition of the validity of orders amongst Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Old Catholic, Oriental Orthodox and Assyrian Church of the East churches.[^cite_note-110]

Some provinces of the Anglican Communion have begun [ordaining women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordination_of_women) as bishops in recent decades—for example, England, Ireland, Scotland, Wales, the United States, Australia, New Zealand, Canada and Cuba. The first woman to be consecrated a bishop within Anglicanism was [Barbara Harris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbara_Harris_\(bishop\)), who was ordained in the United States in 1989.[^cite_note-111] In 2006, Katharine Jefferts Schori, the Episcopal [Bishop of Nevada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episcopal_Diocese_of_Nevada), became the first woman to become the presiding bishop of the Episcopal Church.[^cite_note-112]



[Mikael Agricola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikael_Agricola) (1510–1557), a Finnish [Lutheran](23371382.md) clergyman and the [Bishop of Turku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_bishops_of_Turku)

In the [Evangelical Lutheran Church in America](248255.md) (ELCA) and the [Evangelical Lutheran Church in Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelical_Lutheran_Church_in_Canada) (ELCIC), the largest Lutheran church bodies in the United States and Canada, respectively, and roughly based on the [Nordic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nordic_countries) Lutheran national churches (similar to that of the Church of England), bishops are elected by Synod Assemblies, consisting of both lay members and clergy, for a term of six years, which can be renewed, depending upon the local synod's "constitution" (which is mirrored on either the ELCA or ELCIC's national constitution). Since the implementation of concordats between the ELCA and the [Episcopal Church of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episcopal_Church_of_the_United_States) and the ELCIC and the [Anglican Church of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Church_of_Canada), all bishops, including the presiding bishop (ELCA) or the national bishop (ELCIC), have been consecrated using the historic succession in line with bishops from the Evangelical Lutheran Church of Sweden,[^cite_note-VelikoGros2005-113] with at least one Anglican bishop serving as co-consecrator.[^cite_note-114][^cite_note-115]

Although ELCA agreed with the Episcopal Church to limit ordination to the bishop "ordinarily", ELCA pastor-*ordinators* are given permission to perform the rites in "extraordinary" circumstance. In practice, "extraordinary" circumstance have included disagreeing with Episcopalian views of the episcopate, and as a result, ELCA pastors ordained by other pastors are not permitted to be deployed to Episcopal Churches (they can, however, serve in [Presbyterian Church USA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyterian_Church_\(USA\)), United Methodist Church, [Reformed Church in America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reformed_Church_in_America), and [Moravian Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moravian_Church) congregations, as the ELCA is in full communion with these denominations). The [Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lutheran_Church%E2%80%93Missouri_Synod) (LCMS) and the [Wisconsin Evangelical Lutheran Synod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisconsin_Evangelical_Lutheran_Synod) (WELS), the second and third largest Lutheran bodies in the United States and the two largest [Confessional Lutheran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confessional_Lutheran) bodies in North America, do not follow an episcopal form of governance, settling instead on a form of quasi-congregationalism patterned off what they believe to be the practice of the early church. The second largest of the three predecessor bodies of the ELCA, the [American Lutheran Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Lutheran_Church), was a congregationalist body, with national and synod presidents before they were re-titled as bishops (borrowing from the Lutheran churches in [Germany](11867.md)) in the 1980s. With regard to ecclesial discipline and oversight, national and synod presidents typically function similarly to bishops in episcopal bodies.[^cite_note-116]

#### Christian Methodist Episcopal Church

In the [Christian Methodist Episcopal Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Methodist_Episcopal_Church) in the United States, bishops are administrative superintendents of the church; they are elected by "delegate" votes for as many years deemed until the age of 74, then the bishop must retire. Among their duties, are responsibility for appointing clergy to serve local churches as pastor, for performing ordinations, and for safeguarding the doctrine and discipline of the church. The General Conference, a meeting every four years, has an equal number of clergy and lay delegates. In each Annual Conference, CME bishops serve for four-year terms. In 2010, [Teresa E. Jefferson-Snorton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teresa_E._Jefferson-Snorton) was elected as a bishop, becoming the first woman to hold that position.[^cite_note-118] As of 2024, she remains the only female bishop in CME.[^cite_note-119]

#### United Methodist Church



United Methodist Episcopal Shield

In the United Methodist Church (the largest branch of Methodism in the world) bishops serve as administrative and pastoral superintendents of the church. They are elected for life from among the [ordained elders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elder_\(Methodism\)) (presbyters) by vote of the delegates in regional (called jurisdictional) conferences, and are consecrated by the other bishops present at the conference through the laying on of hands. In the United Methodist Church bishops remain members of the "Order of Elders" while being consecrated to the "[Office of the Episcopacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episcopal_polity)". Within the United Methodist Church only bishops are empowered to consecrate bishops and ordain clergy. Among their most critical duties is the ordination and appointment of clergy to serve local churches as pastor, presiding at sessions of the Annual, Jurisdictional, and General Conferences, providing pastoral ministry for the clergy under their charge, and safeguarding the doctrine and discipline of the church. Furthermore, individual bishops, or the Council of Bishops as a whole, often serve a prophetic role, making statements on important social issues and setting forth a vision for the denomination, though they have no legislative authority of their own. In all of these areas, bishops of the United Methodist Church function very much in the historic meaning of the term. In each Annual Conference, United Methodist bishops serve for four-year terms, and may serve up to three terms before either retirement or appointment to a new Conference. United Methodist bishops may be male or female, with [Marjorie Matthews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marjorie_Matthews) being the first woman to be consecrated a bishop in 1980.

### The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints

Main article: [Bishop (Latter Day Saints)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_\(Latter_Day_Saints\))

In [The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Church_of_Jesus_Christ_of_Latter-day_Saints), the [bishop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_\(Latter_Day_Saints\)) is the leader of a local congregation, called a [ward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ward_\(LDS_Church\)). As with most LDS priesthood holders, the bishop is a part-time lay minister and earns a living through other employment. As such, it is his duty to preside, call local leaders, and judge the worthiness of members for certain activities. The bishop does not deliver sermons at every service (generally asking members to do so), but is expected to be a spiritual guide for his congregation. It is therefore believed that he has both the right and ability to receive divine inspiration (through the [Holy Spirit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Spirit_in_Christianity)) for the ward under his direction. Because it is a part-time position, all able members are expected to assist in the management of the ward by holding delegated lay positions (for example, women's and youth leaders, teachers) referred to as callings. The bishop is especially responsible for leading the youth,[^cite_note-121] in connection with the fact that a bishop is the president of the [Aaronic priesthood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aaronic_priesthood_\(LDS_Church\)) in his ward (and is thus a form of Mormon [Kohen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kohen)). Although members are asked to confess serious sins to him, unlike the Catholic Church, he is not the instrument of divine forgiveness, but merely a guide through the repentance process (and a judge in case transgressions warrant excommunication or other official discipline). The bishop is also responsible for the physical welfare of the ward, and thus collects [tithing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tithe) and [fast offerings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_offering) and distributes financial assistance where needed.

A literal descendant of Aaron has "legal right" to act as a bishop[^cite_note-122] after being found worthy and ordained by the [First Presidency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Presidency_\(LDS_Church\)).[^cite_note-Doctrine_and_Covenants_68\:20-123] In the absence of a literal descendant of Aaron, a [high priest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_priest_\(Latter_Day_Saints\)) in the [Melchizedek priesthood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melchizedek_priesthood) is called to be a bishop.[^cite_note-Doctrine_and_Covenants_68\:20-123] Each bishop is selected from resident members of the ward by the [stake presidency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stake_\(Latter_Day_Saints\)) with approval of the First Presidency, and chooses two *counselors* to form a *bishopric*. An priesthood holder called as bishop must be ordained a high priest if he is not already one, unlike the similar function of branch president.[^cite_note-124] In special circumstances (such as a ward consisting entirely of young university students), a bishop may be chosen from outside the ward. Traditionally, bishops are married, though this is not always the case.[^cite_note-125] A bishop is typically released after about five years and a new bishop is called to the position. Although the former bishop is released from his duties, he continues to hold the Aaronic priesthood office of bishop. Church members frequently refer to a former bishop as "Bishop" as a sign of respect and affection.

Latter-day Saint bishops do not wear any special clothing or insignia the way clergy in many other churches do, but are expected to dress and groom themselves neatly and conservatively per their local culture, especially when performing official duties. Bishops (as well as other members of the priesthood) can trace their line of authority back to [Joseph Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Smith), who, according to church doctrine, was ordained to lead the church in modern times by the ancient apostles Peter, [James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James,_son_of_Zebedee), and John, who were ordained to lead the Church by Jesus Christ.[^cite_note-126]

At the global level, the [presiding bishop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presiding_Bishop_\(LDS_Church\)) oversees the temporal affairs (buildings, properties, commercial corporations, and so on) of the worldwide church, including the church's massive global humanitarian aid and social welfare programs. The presiding bishop has two counselors; the three together form the presiding bishopric.[^cite_note-127] As opposed to ward bishoprics, where the counselors do not hold the office of bishop, all three men in the presiding bishopric hold the office of bishop, and thus the counselors, as with the presiding bishop, are formally referred to as "Bishop".[^cite_note-128]

### Irvingism

#### New Apostolic Church

The [New Apostolic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Apostolic_Church) (NAC) teaches three classes of ministries: deacons, priests and apostles. The [apostles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Apostolic_Church#Apostle_ministry_and_exclusivism), who are all included in the apostolate with the [Chief Apostle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_Apostle) as head, are the highest ministries. Of the several kinds of priestly ministries, the bishop is the highest. Nearly all bishops are set in line directly from the chief apostle. They support and help their superior apostle.

### Pentecostalism

#### Church of God in Christ

In the [Church of God in Christ (COGIC)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_God_in_Christ), the ecclesiastical structure is composed of large dioceses that are called "jurisdictions" within COGIC, each under the authority of a bishop, sometimes called "state bishops". They can either be made up of large geographical regions of churches or churches that are grouped and organized together as their own separate jurisdictions because of similar affiliations, regardless of geographical location or dispersion. Each state in the U.S. has at least one jurisdiction while others may have several more, and each jurisdiction is usually composed of between 30 and 100 churches. Each jurisdiction is then broken down into several districts, which are smaller groups of churches (either grouped by geographical situation or by similar affiliations) which are each under the authority of District Superintendents who answer to the authority of their jurisdictional/state bishop. There are currently over 170 jurisdictions in the United States, and over 30 jurisdictions in other countries. The bishops of each jurisdiction, according to the COGIC Manual, are considered to be the modern day equivalent in the church of the early apostles and overseers of the New Testament church, and as the highest ranking clergymen in the COGIC, they are tasked with the responsibilities of being the head overseers of all religious, civil, and economic ministries and protocol for the church denomination.[^cite_note-Church_of_God_in_Christ_Publishing_House-1973-129] They also have the authority to appoint and ordain local [pastors](215153.md), [elders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elder_\(Christianity\)), [ministers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_\(Christianity\)), and [reverends](99176.md) within the denomination. The bishops of the COGIC denomination are all collectively called "The Board of Bishops".[^cite_note-Church_Of_God_In_Christ-130] From the Board of Bishops, and the General Assembly of the COGIC, the body of the church composed of clergy and lay delegates that are responsible for making and enforcing the bylaws of the denomination, every four years, twelve bishops from the COGIC are elected as "The General Board" of the church, who work alongside the delegates of the General Assembly and Board of Bishops to provide administration over the denomination as the church's head executive leaders.[^cite_note-131] One of twelve bishops of the General Board is also elected the "presiding bishop" of the church, and two others are appointed by the presiding bishop himself, as his first and second assistant presiding bishops.

Bishops in the Church of God in Christ usually wear black clergy suits which consist of a black suit blazer, black pants, a purple or scarlet clergy shirt and a white [clerical collar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clerical_collar), which is usually referred to as "Class B Civic attire". Bishops in COGIC also typically wear the Anglican Choir Dress style vestments of a long purple or scarlet chimere, cuffs, and tippet worn over a long white rochet, and a gold pectoral cross worn around the neck with the tippet. This is usually referred to as "Class A Ceremonial attire". The bishops of COGIC alternate between Class A Ceremonial attire and Class B Civic attire depending on the protocol of the religious services and other events they have to attend.[^cite_note-Church_Of_God_In_Christ-130][^cite_note-Church_of_God_in_Christ_Publishing_House-1973-129]

#### Church of God (Cleveland, Tennessee)

In the polity of the [Church of God (Cleveland, Tennessee)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_God_\(Cleveland,_Tennessee\)), the international leader is the presiding bishop, and the members of the executive committee are executive bishops. Collectively, they supervise and appoint national and state leaders across the world. Leaders of individual states and regions are administrative bishops, who have jurisdiction over local churches in their respective states and are vested with appointment authority for local pastorates. All ministers are credentialed at one of three levels of licensure, the most senior of which is the rank of ordained bishop. To be eligible to serve in state, national, or international positions of authority, a minister must hold the rank of ordained bishop.

### Seventh-day Adventists

According to the Seventh-day Adventist understanding of the doctrine of the church:

"The "elders" (Greek, *presbuteros*) or "bishops" (*episkopos*) were the most important officers of the church. The term elder means older one, implying dignity and respect. His position was similar to that of the one who had supervision of the synagogue. The term bishop means "overseer". Paul used these terms interchangeably, equating elders with overseers or bishops (Acts 20\:17, [28](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_20\:28); Titus 1\:5, 7).

"Those who held this position supervised the newly formed churches. Elder referred to the status or rank of the office, while bishop denoted the duty or responsibility of the office—"overseer". Since the apostles also called themselves elders (1 Peter 5\:1; 2 John 1; 3 John 1), it is apparent that there were both local elders and itinerant elders, or elders at large. But both kinds of elder functioned as shepherds of the congregations.[^cite_note-132]"

The above understanding is part of the basis of Adventist organizational structure. The world wide Seventh-day Adventist church is organized into local districts, conferences or missions, union conferences or union missions, divisions, and finally at the top is the general conference. At each level (with exception to the local districts), there is an elder who is elected president and a group of elders who serve on the executive committee with the elected president. Those who have been elected president would in effect be the "bishop" while never actually carrying the title or ordained as such because the term is usually associated with the episcopal style of church governance most often found in Catholic, Anglican, Methodist and some Pentecostal/Charismatic circles.

### Others

Some Baptists also have begun taking on the title of *bishop*;[^cite_note-bost_More-133] and the position was recognized in several early [Baptist confessions of faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Baptist_confessions_of_faith) (although, considering elders and bishops as the same). In some smaller Protestant denominations and independent churches, the term *bishop* is used in the same way as *pastor*, to refer to the leader of the local congregation; and these bishops may be male or female. This usage is especially common in African-American churches, particularly through the [Full Gospel Baptist Church Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_Gospel_Baptist_Church_Fellowship).

In the [Church of Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Scotland), which has a Presbyterian church structure, the word "bishop" refers to an ordained person, usually a normal parish minister, who has temporary oversight of a trainee minister. Previously, in the [Presbyterian Church (USA)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyterian_Church_\(USA\)) the term "bishop" was an expressive name for a Minister of Word and Sacrament who served a congregation and exercises "the oversight of the flock of Christ."[^cite_note-presby-134] However the title was not normally used and no longer appears in the [Book of Order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Order).[^cite_note-135] The use of the term "bishop" in American Presbyterianism is traceable to the 1789 Form of Government of the denomination that would become the contemporary PC(USA).[^cite_note-136][^cite_note-137] [Reformed churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishops_in_Calvinism) on the whole do not tend to have bishops, although there are exceptions.

While not considered orthodox Christian, the [Ecclesia Gnostica Catholica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesia_Gnostica_Catholica) uses roles and titles derived from Christianity for its clerical hierarchy, including bishops who have much the same authority and responsibilities as in Catholicism.

The [Salvation Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvation_Army) does not have bishops but has appointed leaders of geographical areas, known as Divisional Commanders. Larger geographical areas, called territories, are led by a territorial commander, who is the highest-ranking officer in that territory.

Jehovah's Witnesses do not use the title 'bishop' within their organizational structure, but appoint elders to be overseers (to fulfill the role of oversight) within their congregations.[^cite_note-138]



[Ludwig Ingwer Nommensen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Ingwer_Nommensen), the [German](152735.md) Lutheran missionary from [Rhenish Missionary Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhenish_Missionary_Society), also first Ephorus of the Batak Christian Protestant Church

The [Batak Christian Protestant Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batak_Christian_Protestant_Church) of [Indonesia](14579.md), the most prominent [Protestant denomination in Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism_in_Indonesia), uses the term *Ephorus* instead of *bishop*.[^cite_note-139]

In the Vietnamese syncretist religion of [Caodaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caodaism), bishops (*giáo sư*) comprise the fifth of nine hierarchical levels, and are responsible for spiritual and temporal education as well as record-keeping and ceremonies in their parishes. At any one time there are seventy-two bishops. Their authority is described in Section I of the text *Tân Luật* (revealed through seances in December 1926). Caodai bishops wear robes and headgear of embroidered silk depicting the Divine Eye and the Eight Trigrams. (The color varies according to branch.) This is the full ceremonial dress; the simple version consists of a seven-layered turban.

In [Amish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amish) and [Conservative Mennonite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Mennonite) church organization, bishop is the highest rank in the clergy, although Amish and Mennonite bishops usually oversee regions comparable to a [parish priest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parish_priest) in more highly-organized denominations. Liberal Mennonite conferences do not have bishops.

## Dress and insignia in Christianity

Main article: [Pontifical vestments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontifical_vestments)

Traditionally, a number of items are associated with the office of a bishop, most notably the mitre and the [crosier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crosier). Other vestments and insignia vary between Eastern and Western Christianity.

In the Latin Church of the broader Catholic Church, the [choir dress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choir_dress) of a bishop includes the purple cassock with amaranth trim, [rochet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rochet), purple [zucchetto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zucchetto) (skull cap), purple [biretta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biretta), and pectoral cross. The [cappa magna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cappa_magna) may be worn, but only within the bishop's own diocese and on especially solemn occasions.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStehle1914-140] The mitre, zucchetto, and [stole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stole_\(vestment\)) are generally worn by bishops when presiding over liturgical functions. For liturgical functions other than the [Mass](5684685.md) the bishop typically wears the cope. Within his own diocese and when celebrating solemnly elsewhere with the consent of the local [ordinary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_\(officer\)), he also uses the crosier.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStehle1914-140] When celebrating Mass, a bishop, like a [priest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priest_\(Catholic_Church\)), wears the [chasuble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chasuble). The *[Caeremoniale Episcoporum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caeremoniale_Episcoporum)* recommends, but does not impose, that in solemn celebrations a bishop should also wear a [dalmatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalmatic), which can always be white, beneath the chasuble, especially when administering the sacrament of [holy orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_orders), blessing an abbot or abbess, and dedicating a church or an altar.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStehle1914-140] The *Caeremoniale Episcoporum* no longer makes mention of [episcopal gloves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episcopal_gloves), [episcopal sandals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episcopal_sandals), [liturgical stockings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liturgical_stockings) (also known as [buskins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buskins)), or the accoutrements that it once prescribed for the bishop's horse. The coat of arms of a Latin Church Catholic bishop usually displays a [galero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galero) with a cross and crosier behind the [escutcheon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escutcheon_\(heraldry\)); the specifics differ by location and ecclesiastical rank (see [Ecclesiastical heraldry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesiastical_heraldry)).

Anglican bishops generally make use of the mitre, crosier, ecclesiastical ring, purple cassock, purple zucchetto, and pectoral cross. However, the traditional choir dress of Anglican bishops retains its late mediaeval form, and looks quite different from that of their Catholic counterparts; it consists of a long rochet which is worn with a [chimere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chimere).

In the [Eastern churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Churches) (Eastern Orthodox, [Eastern Rite Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Rite_Catholic)) a bishop will wear the [mandyas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandyas), [panagia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panagia) (and perhaps an [enkolpion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enkolpion)), [sakkos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sakkos), omophorion and an Eastern-style mitre. Eastern bishops do not normally wear an episcopal ring; the faithful kiss (or, alternatively, touch their forehead to) the bishop's hand. To seal official documents, he will usually use an inked stamp. An Eastern bishop's coat of arms will normally display an Eastern-style mitre, cross, Eastern-style crosier and a red and white (or red and gold) [mantle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesiastical_heraldry#Mantle). The arms of Oriental Orthodox bishops will display the episcopal insignia (mitre or turban) specific to their own liturgical traditions. Variations occur based upon jurisdiction and national customs.

### Cathedra

In Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, Lutheran and Anglican [cathedrals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral) there is a special chair set aside for the exclusive use of the bishop. This is the bishop's *[cathedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedra)* and is often called the [throne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Throne). In some Christian denominations, for example, the Anglican Communion, parish churches may maintain a chair for the use of the bishop when he visits; this is to signify the parish's union with the bishop.

## In non-Christian religions

### Buddhism

The leader of the [Buddhist Churches of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_Churches_of_America) (BCA) is their [bishop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_the_Buddhist_Churches_of_America).[^cite_note-141][^cite_note-142][^cite_note-Ama-143] The Japanese title for the bishop of the BCA is *sochō*,[^cite_note-Ama-143][^cite_note-144][^cite_note-145] although the English title is favored over the Japanese. When it comes to many other [Buddhist terms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_Terms_and_Concepts), the BCA chose to keep them in their original language (terms such as *[sangha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sangha)* and *[dana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C4%81na)*), but with some words (including *sochō*), they changed/translated these terms into English words.[^cite_note-146][^cite_note-147][^cite_note-148]

Between 1899 and 1944, the BCA held the name Buddhist Mission of North America. The leader of the Buddhist Mission of North America was called *kantoku* (superintendent/director) between 1899 and 1918. In 1918 the *kantoku* was promoted to bishop (*sochō*).[^cite_note-149][^cite_note-150][^cite_note-151] However, according to George J. Tanabe, the title "bishop" was in practice already used by Hawaiian Shin Buddhists (in [Honpa Hongwanji Mission of Hawaii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honpa_Hongwanji_Mission_of_Hawaii)) even when the official title was *kantoku*.[^cite_note-152]

Bishops are also present in other Japanese Buddhist organizations. [Higashi Hongan-ji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higashi_Hongan-ji)'s North American District, Honpa Honganji Mission of Hawaii, [Jodo Shinshu Buddhist Temples of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jodo_Shinshu_Buddhist_Temples_of_Canada),[^cite_note-153] a [Jodo Shu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C5%8Ddo-sh%C5%AB) temple in Los Angeles, the [Shingon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shingon_Buddhism) temple [Koyasan Buddhist Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koyasan_Buddhist_Temple),[^cite_note-154] Sōtō Mission in Hawai'i (a [Soto Zen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soto_Zen) Buddhist institution),[^cite_note-155][^cite_note-156] and the Sōtō Zen Buddhist Community of South America (*Comunidade Budista Sōtō Zenshū da América do Sul*) all have or have had leaders with the title bishop. As for the Sōtō Zen Buddhist Community of South America, the Japanese title is *sōkan*, but the leader is in practice referred to as "bishop".[^cite_note-157]

### Tenrikyo

[Tenrikyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenrikyo) is a Japanese New Religion with influences from both Shinto and Buddhism.[^cite_note-Williams-158] The leader of the Tenrikyo North American Mission has the title of bishop.[^cite_note-Williams-158][^cite_note-159]
