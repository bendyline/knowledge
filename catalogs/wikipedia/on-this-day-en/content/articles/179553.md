The **Southern United States** (sometimes **Dixie**, also referred to as the **Southern States**, the **American South**, the **Southland**, **Dixieland**, or simply **the South**) is one of the four [census regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_regions_of_the_United_States) of the [United States](3434750.md) as defined by the [U.S. Census Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Census_Bureau). It is between the Atlantic Ocean and the [Western United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_United_States), with the [Midwestern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midwestern_United_States) and [Northeastern United States](431669.md) to its north and the [Gulf of Mexico](21076367.md) and [Mexico](3966054.md) to its south. Historically, the South was defined as all states south of the 18th-century [Mason–Dixon line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mason%E2%80%93Dixon_line), the [Ohio River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio_River), and the [36°30′ parallel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_36%C2%B030%E2%80%B2_north).[^cite_note-The_South-4] Within the South are different [subregions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subregions) such as the [Southeast](816925.md), [South Central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Central_United_States), [Upper South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upland_South), and [Deep South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_South). [Maryland](18858.md), [Delaware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delaware), [Washington, D.C.](108956.md), and [Virginia](32432.md) (especially [Northern Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Virginia)) have become more culturally, economically, and politically aligned in certain aspects with the Northeastern United States and are sometimes identified as part of the Northeast or [Mid-Atlantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid-Atlantic_\(United_States\)).[^cite_note-5] The U.S. Census Bureau continues to define all four places as formally being in the South.[^cite_note-CensusRegionsMap-6]

To account for cultural variations across the region, some scholars have proposed definitions of the South that do not coincide neatly with state boundaries.[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8] The South does not precisely correspond to the entire [geographic south](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Belt) of the United States, but primarily includes the [south-central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Central_United_States) and [southeastern](816925.md) states. For example, California, which is geographically in the southwestern part of the country, is not considered part of the South; however, the geographically southeastern state of [Georgia](48830.md) is.[^cite_note-geography.vt.edu-9][^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11]

The politics and economy of the region were historically dominated by a small rural elite.[^cite_note-12] The historical and cultural development of the South has been profoundly influenced by the institution of [slave labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_the_United_States), especially in the Deep South and coastal plain areas, from the early 1600s to mid-1800s. This includes the presence of a large proportion of African Americans within the population, support for the doctrine of [states' rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States'_rights), and legacy of racism magnified by the Civil War and [Reconstruction era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconstruction_era) (1865–1877). Following effects included thousands of [lynchings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynching_in_the_United_States), a [segregated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_segregation_in_the_United_States) system of separate schools and public facilities established from [Jim Crow laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Crow_laws) that remained until the 1960s, and the widespread use of [poll taxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poll_taxes_in_the_United_States) and other methods to deny Black and poor people the ability to vote or hold office until the 1960s. Scholars have characterized pockets of the Southern United States as being [authoritarian enclaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authoritarian_enclave) from Reconstruction until the [Civil Rights Act of 1964](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Rights_Act_of_1964).[^cite_note-Mickey_2015-13][^cite_note-University_of_Chicago_Press-14][^cite_note-kuo-15][^cite_note-Gibson_2013-16]

The South, being home to some of the most racially diverse areas in the United States, is known for having developed its own distinct [culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_the_Southern_United_States), with different customs, fashion, [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture_of_the_United_States), [musical styles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_the_Southern_United_States), and [cuisines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuisine_of_the_Southern_United_States), which have distinguished it in many ways from other areas of the United States. Sociological research indicates that Southern collective identity stems from political, historical, demographic, and cultural distinctiveness from the rest of the United States; however, this has declined since around the late 20th century, with many Southern areas becoming a melting pot of cultures and people. When looked at broadly, studies have shown that Southerners tend to be more [conservative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservatism_in_the_United_States) than most non-Southerners, with [liberalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_liberalism_in_the_United_States) being mostly predominant in places with a [Black](2154.md) majority or urban areas in the South.[^cite_note-Christopher_A_2010-17][^cite_note-Tom_W\._Rice_2000-18] The region contains almost all of the [Bible Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible_Belt), an area of high [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism_in_the_United_States) church attendance, especially [evangelical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelicalism_in_the_United_States) churches such as the [Southern Baptist Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Baptist_Convention). In the 21st century, it is the fastest-growing region in the United States, with [Houston](13774.md) being the region's largest city.[^cite_note-19][^cite_note-20]

The South is a diverse meteorological region with numerous climatic zones, including [temperate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperate), [sub-tropical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtropics), [tropical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_climate), and [arid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arid)—though the South generally has a reputation as hot and humid, with long summers and short, mild winters. Most of the South—except for the areas of higher elevations and areas near the western, southern, and some northern fringes—falls in the [humid subtropical climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humid_subtropical_climate) zone. Crops grow readily in the South due to its climate consistently providing growing seasons of at least six months before the first frost. Some common environments include [bayous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayou) and swamplands, the southern [pine forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loblolly_pine), the warm temperate montane forest of the Appalachians, the savannas of the southern Great Plains, and the [subtropical jungle and maritime forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurel_forest) along the Atlantic and Gulf coasts. Unique flora include various species of magnolia, rhododendron, "cane" (bamboo), palm, hickory and oak, among others. [Fauna of the region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fauna_of_the_Southern_United_States) is also diverse, encompassing a plethora of [amphibian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphibian) species, reptiles such as the [green anole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_anole), the venomous [cottonmouth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cottonmouth) snake, and the [American alligator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_alligator), mammals like the [American black bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_black_bear), the [swamp rabbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swamp_rabbit), and the [nine-banded armadillo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nine-banded_armadillo), and birds such as the [roseate spoonbill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roseate_spoonbill) and the extinct but symbolic [carolina parakeet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carolina_parakeet).

The question of how to define the boundaries and subregions of the South has long been the focus of research and debate.[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-22] As defined by the [United States Census Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Census_Bureau),[^cite_note-CensusBureau-1] the Southern region of the United States includes sixteen states and the District of Columbia. As of 2010, an estimated 114,555,744 people, or thirty-seven percent of all U.S. residents, lived in the South, the nation's most populous region.[^cite_note-23] The Census Bureau defines three smaller divisions:

- **The [South Atlantic states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Atlantic_states)**: Delaware, the District of Columbia, Florida, Georgia, Maryland, North Carolina, South Carolina, Virginia and West Virginia.

- **The [East South Central states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_South_Central_states)**: Alabama, Kentucky, Mississippi and Tennessee.

- **The [West South Central states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_South_Central_states)**: Arkansas, Louisiana, Oklahoma and Texas.

A survey conducted by social geographers in 2010 selected thirteen states as the *cultural* South: Alabama, Arkansas, Florida, Georgia, Kentucky, Louisiana, Mississippi, North Carolina, Oklahoma, South Carolina, Tennessee, Virginia and West Virginia.[^cite_note-24] The [Council of State Governments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_State_Governments), an organization for communication and coordination between states, includes the same thirteen states as well as Texas and Missouri in its South regional office.[^cite_note-25]

Other terms related to the South include:

- **The [Old South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_South)**: Can mean either southern states that were among the [Thirteen Colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirteen_Colonies) (Virginia, Delaware, Maryland, Georgia, North Carolina and South Carolina) or all southern slave states before 1860 (which also includes Kentucky, Tennessee, Alabama, Florida, Mississippi, Missouri, Arkansas, Louisiana and Texas).

- **The [New South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_South)**: All southern states following the American Civil War, post–[Reconstruction era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconstruction_era).

  [^cite_note-27]

- **[Southeastern United States](816925.md)**: Usually includes [the Carolinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Carolinas), [the Virginias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Virginias), [Tennessee](30395.md), [Kentucky](16846.md), [Georgia](48830.md), [Alabama](303.md), [Mississippi](16949861.md) and Florida.

  [^cite_note-28]

- **[Southern Appalachia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appalachia)**: Mainly refers to areas situated in the southern [Appalachian Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appalachian_Mountains), namely [Eastern Kentucky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Mountain_Coal_Fields), [East Tennessee](646800.md), [Western North Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_North_Carolina), [Western Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Maryland), [West Virginia](32905.md), [Southwest Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwest_Virginia), [North Georgia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Georgia) and [Northwestern South Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upstate_South_Carolina).

  [^cite_note-29]

- **[Upper South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upland_South)**: Usually includes Kentucky, Virginia, West Virginia, [Tennessee](30395.md), [North Carolina](21650.md) and on rare occasions Missouri, Maryland and Delaware.

  [^cite_note-30]

  &#x20;When combined with the southern Appalachian Mountains, it is sometimes referred to as "[Greater Appalachia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Appalachia)" following [Ulster Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotch-Irish_Americans) migrations to the United States in the 18th and 19th centuries.

  [^cite_note-31]

- **[Deep South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_South)**: Various definitions, usually includes Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, Mississippi and South Carolina.

  [^cite_note-32]

- **[Border States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Border_states_\(American_Civil_War\))**: Included Missouri, Kentucky, Maryland, and Delaware. These were Southern slave states on or near the border of the Confederacy that did not secede from the U.S. Some of their residents joined the [Union armed forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_army) and some joined the [Confederate armed forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_forces_of_the_Confederate_States). In addition, West Virginia separated from the Confederate state of Virginia and was admitted to the Union as a slave state, but with its [state constitution providing for gradual abolition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Virginia_in_the_American_Civil_War#Slavery).

  [^cite_note-33]

  &#x20;Although Kentucky and Missouri had not seceded, they had separate Confederate state governments, with the [Confederate government of Missouri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederate_government_of_Missouri) and the [Confederate government of Kentucky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederate_government_of_Kentucky). The Confederacy controlled more than half of Kentucky and the southern portion of Missouri early in the war but were in exile after 1862 and were represented in the Confederate Congress and by stars on the Confederate battle flag.

- **[Dixie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dixie)**: Nickname applied to Southern U.S. region, various definitions include certain areas more than others, but most commonly associated with the eleven former Confederate States.

- **[Solid South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_South)**: Electoral voting bloc largely controlled by the Democratic Party from 1877 to 1964, largely resulting from [disenfranchisement after the Reconstruction era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disenfranchisement_after_the_Reconstruction_era) in the late 19th century. Disfranchisement effectively denied most of the black and sometimes poor white population from voting or holding public office during this time period.

  [^cite_note-34]

- **[Gulf Coast](11969.md)**: Includes Gulf coasts of Florida, Louisiana, Mississippi, Texas and Alabama.

- **[Tidewater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidewater_\(region\))**: Low-lying [Atlantic coastal plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_coastal_plain) regions of Maryland, Delaware, Virginia and North Carolina.

- **[Mid-South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid-South_\(region\))**: Various definitions, includes states within the Census Bureau of the East and West South Central United States.

Historically, the South was defined as all states south of the 18th-century [Mason–Dixon line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mason%E2%80%93Dixon_line), the [Ohio River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio_River), and the [36°30′ parallel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_36%C2%B030%E2%80%B2_north).[^cite_note-The_South-4] Newer definitions of the South today are harder to define, due to cultural and sub-regional differences throughout the region; however, definitions usually refer to states that are in the southeastern and south-central geographic regions of the United States.[^cite_note-39]

Although not included in the Census definition, two [U.S. territories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territories_of_the_United_States) located southeast of Florida ([Puerto Rico](23041.md) and the [U.S. Virgin Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Virgin_Islands)) are sometimes included as part of the Southern United States. The [Federal Aviation Administration](11186.md) includes Puerto Rico and the U.S. Virgin Islands as part of the South,[^cite_note-40] as does the [Agricultural Research Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_Research_Service) and the [U.S. National Park Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._National_Park_Service).[^cite_note-41][^cite_note-42]

The first well-dated evidence of human occupation in the south United States occurs around 9500 BC with the appearance of the earliest documented Americans, who are now referred to as [Paleo-Indians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleo-Indians).[^cite_note-guy_prentice-43] Paleoindians were hunter-gatherers that roamed in bands and frequently hunted [megafauna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megafauna). Several cultural stages, such as Archaic (c. 8000–1000 BC) and the Woodland (c. 1000 BC – AD 1000), preceded what the Europeans found at the end of the 15th century – the [Mississippian culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippian_culture).[^cite_note-guy_prentice-43]

The Mississippian culture was a complex, [mound-building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mound_builders) [Native American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas) culture that flourished in what is now the Southeastern United States from approximately 800 AD to 1500 AD. Natives had elaborate and lengthy trading routes connecting their main residential and ceremonial centers extending through the river valleys and from the East Coast to the Great Lakes.[^cite_note-guy_prentice-43] Some noted explorers who encountered and described the Mississippian culture, by then in decline, included [Pánfilo de Narváez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C3%A1nfilo_de_Narv%C3%A1ez) (1528), [Hernando de Soto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hernando_de_Soto) (1540), and [Pierre Le Moyne d'Iberville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Le_Moyne_d'Iberville) (1699).

Native American descendants of the mound-builders include [Alabama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alabama_\(people\)), [Apalachee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apalachee), [Caddo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caddo), [Cherokee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherokee), [Chickasaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chickasaw), [Choctaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choctaw), [Creek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muscogee_\(Creek\)), [Guale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guale), [Hitchiti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hitchiti), [Houma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houma_Tribe), and [Seminole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seminole) peoples, all of whom still reside in the South.

Other peoples whose ancestral links to the Mississippian culture are less clear, but those who were in the region before the European incursion include the [Catawba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catawba_people) and the [Powhatan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powhatan).

### European colonization

Further information: [White Southerners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Southerners)



Map of the [Southern Colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Colonies), with the [Proclamation Line of 1763](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proclamation_Line_of_1763) shown in red

The arrival of European settlers caused a massive [population decline in Native Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_history_of_indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas#Depopulation_from_disease), due to Europeans unknowingly spreading [diseases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infectious_disease) that the natives had no immunities towards, numerous violent conflicts, and forcibly relocating them.[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46][^cite_note-47]

The predominant culture of the original Southern states was [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Americans). In the 17th century, most voluntary immigrants were of English origin and settled chiefly along the eastern coast but had pushed as far inland as the [Appalachian Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appalachian_Mountains) by the 18th century. The majority of early English settlers were [indentured servants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indentured_servant), who gained freedom after working off their passage. The wealthier men, typically members of the English [landed gentry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landed_gentry),[^cite_note-Albion's_Seed-48] who paid their way received land grants known as [headrights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Headright) to encourage settlement.[^cite_note-49]

The Spanish and French established settlements in [Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Florida), [Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Texas), and [Louisiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_\(New_France\)). The Spanish settled Florida in the 16th century, reaching a peak in the late 17th century, but the population was small because the Spaniards were relatively uninterested in agriculture, and Florida had no mineral resources.

There were regional differences in the Southern colonies, with the three main regions of [Tidewater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidewater_\(region\)), the [Deep South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_South), and [Appalachia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appalachia). The first region to be settled was Tidewater, containing the low-lying plains of southeast [Virginia](32432.md), [northeastern North Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_Banks#Albemarle_Region), southern [Maryland](18858.md) and the [Chesapeake Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chesapeake_Bay).[^cite_note-boon-50] The next region to be settled was the Deep South, beginning in [Province of Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Carolina) and later the [Province of Georgia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Georgia). The last region to be settled was Appalachia, also settled by the [Scotch-Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotch-Irish_Americans).[^cite_note-Albion's_Seed-48]

King [Charles II of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_II_of_England) granted the [Charter of Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_North_Carolina) in 1663 for land south of the British [Colony of Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colony_of_Virginia) and north of [Spanish Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Florida). He granted the land to eight [lords proprietor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lords_proprietor). Charles granted the land in return for their financial and political assistance in [restoring him to the throne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_Restoration) in 1660.[^cite_note-Prince2011-51] The granted lands included all or part of the present-day U.S. states of North Carolina, Tennessee, South Carolina, Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi, and Florida.

In the British colonies, immigration began in 1607 and continued until the outbreak of the Revolution in 1775. Settlers cleared land, built houses and outbuildings, and on their own farms. The Southern rich owned large [plantations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plantations_in_the_American_South) that dominated export agriculture and used slaves. Many were involved in the labor-intensive cultivation of tobacco, the first cash crop of Virginia. Tobacco exhausted the soil quickly, requiring that farmers regularly clear new fields. They used old fields as pasture, and for crops such as corn wheat, or allowed them to grow into woodlots.[^cite_note-52]

In the mid-to-late-18th century, large groups of [Ulster Scots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_Scots_people) (later called the [Scotch-Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotch-Irish_American)) and people from the [Anglo-Scottish border](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Scottish_border) region immigrated and settled in the back country of [Appalachia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appalachia) and the [Piedmont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piedmont_\(United_States\)). They were the largest group of non-English immigrants from the [British Isles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Isles) before the [American Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolution).[^cite_note-Albion's_Seed-48] In the [1980 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980_census), 34% of Southerners reported that they were of English ancestry. English was the largest reported European ancestry in every Southern state by a large margin.[^cite_note-ReferenceB-53]

The early colonists engaged in [warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endemic_warfare), trade, and cultural exchanges. Those living in the backcountry were more likely to encounter [Creek Indians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muscogee_\(Creek\)), [Cherokee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherokee), and [Choctaws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choctaw) and other regional native groups.

The oldest university in the South, the [College of William & Mary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_of_William_&_Mary), was founded in 1693 in Virginia; it pioneered in the teaching of [political economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_economy) and educated future U.S. Presidents [Jefferson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Jefferson), [Monroe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Monroe) and [Tyler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Tyler), all from Virginia. Indeed, the entire region dominated politics in the [First Party System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Party_System) era: for example, four of the first five presidents – [Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington), Jefferson, [Madison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Madison) and Monroe – were from Virginia. The two oldest public universities are also in the South: the [University of North Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_North_Carolina_at_Chapel_Hill) (1789) and the [University of Georgia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Georgia) (1785).

### Instituting slavery



A map of the [Thirteen Colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirteen_Colonies) in 1770, showing the number and proportion of slaves in each colony

[^cite_note-54]

[Slavery was legal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_the_colonial_history_of_the_United_States) in all of the [Thirteen Colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirteen_Colonies) prior to the [American Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolution) in 1776.[^cite_note-55] Britain had [slave island colonies in the Caribbean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_the_British_and_French_Caribbean), including [Jamaica](15660.md), [Barbados](3455.md), [Nevis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nevis), and [Antigua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antigua), which provided a steady flow of profits from the slave labor that produced sugar.[^cite_note-56] The [Southern Colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Colonies) differed in that the proportion of their populations that were African American slaves was much higher than in the [Middle Colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Colonies) and [New England Colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_England_Colonies), as shown on the map.[^cite_note-57]

According to [Bertram Wyatt-Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertram_Wyatt-Brown), "Bondage was an answer to an economic need. The South was not founded to create slavery; slavery was recruited to perpetuate the South."[^cite_note-58] The [Barbados Slave Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbados_Slave_Code) of 1661 served as the basis for the slave codes adopted in the [British American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_America) colonies of the [Province of Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Carolina) and the [Province of Georgia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Georgia). In other colonies where the slaves codes were not an exact copy, such as the [Colony of Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colony_of_Virginia) and the [Province of Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Maryland), the influence of the Barbados Slave Code can be traced throughout various provisions.[^cite_note-menard-59][^cite_note-60]

### American Revolution

Main article: [Southern theater of the American Revolutionary War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_theater_of_the_American_Revolutionary_War)



1st Maryland Regiment holding the line at the [Battle of Guilford Courthouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Guilford_Courthouse) in [North Carolina](21650.md), 1781



The [siege of Yorktown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Yorktown_\(1781\)) prompted Great Britain's surrender in North America during the [American Revolutionary War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War), 1781.

During the [American Revolutionary War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War), the Southern colonies helped embrace the [Patriot cause](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriot_\(American_Revolution\)). Virginia would provide leaders such as commander-in-chief [George Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington), and the author of the Declaration of Independence, [Thomas Jefferson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Jefferson).

In 1780 and 1781, the British largely halted reconquest of the northern states and concentrated on the south, where they were told there was a large Loyalist population ready to leap to arms once the royal forces arrived. The British took control of Savannah and Charleston, capturing a large American army in the process, and set up a network of bases inland. Although there were [Loyalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loyalist_\(American_Revolution\)) within the Southern colonies,[^cite_note-61] they were concentrated in larger coastal cities and were not great enough in number to overcome the revolutionaries. The British forces at the [Battle of Monck's Corner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Monck's_Corner) and the [Battle of Lenud's Ferry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Lenud's_Ferry) consisted entirely of Loyalists with the exception of the commanding officer ([Banastre Tarleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banastre_Tarleton)).[^cite_note-62] Both white and Black Loyalists fought for the British at the [Battle of Kemp's Landing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Kemp's_Landing) in Virginia.[^cite_note-63][^cite_note-64] Led by [Nathanael Greene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathanael_Greene) and other generals, the Americans engaged in [Fabian tactics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fabian_strategy) designed to wear down the British invasion force and to neutralize its strong points one by one. There were numerous battles large and small, with each side claiming some victories.

By 1781, however, British [General Cornwallis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Cornwallis,_1st_Marquess_Cornwallis) moved north to Virginia, where an approaching army forced him to fortify and await rescue by the British Navy. The British Navy did arrive, but so did a stronger French fleet, and Cornwallis was trapped. American and French armies, led by George Washington, forced Cornwallis to surrender his entire army in [Yorktown, Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yorktown,_Virginia), in October 1781, effectively winning the North American part of the war.[^cite_note-65]

The Revolution provided a shock to slavery in the South and other regions of the new country. Thousands of slaves took advantage of wartime disruption to find their own freedom, catalyzed by the British Governor Dunmore of Virginia's promise of freedom for service. Many others were removed by Loyalist owners and became slaves elsewhere in the British Empire. Between 1770 and 1790, there was a sharp decline in the Black population – from 61% to 44% in South Carolina and from 45% to 36% in Georgia.[^cite_note-66] In addition, some slaveholders were inspired to free their slaves after the Revolution. They were moved by the principles of the Revolution, along with Quaker and Methodist preachers who worked to encourage slaveholders to free their slaves. Planters such as George Washington often freed slaves by their wills. In the [Upper South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upland_South), more than 10% of the Black population were free by 1810, a significant expansion from pre-war proportions of less than 1% free.[^cite_note-67]

### Antebellum years



[Slaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery) on a [South Carolina](27956.md) plantation (*[The Old Plantation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Old_Plantation)*, c. 1790)

[Cotton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Cotton) became dominant in the lower South after 1800. After the invention of the [cotton gin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton_gin), short staple cotton could be grown more widely. This led to an explosion of cotton cultivation, especially in the frontier uplands of Georgia, Alabama and other parts of the Deep South, as well as riverfront areas of the Mississippi Delta. Migrants poured into those areas in the early decades of the 19th century, when county population figures rose and fell as swells of people kept moving west. [The expansion of cotton cultivation required more slave labor, and the institution became even more deeply an integral part of the South's economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_African_Americans_in_Texas).[^cite_note-68]



Grove Plantation in Tallahassee, Florida. Known officially as the Call/Collins House at the Grove. Built c. 1840.

With the opening up of frontier lands after the government forced most Native Americans to move west of the Mississippi, there was a major migration of both whites and blacks to those territories. From the 1820s through the 1850s, more than one million enslaved Africans were transported to the Deep South in forced migration, two-thirds of them by slave traders and the others by masters who moved there. Planters in the Upper South sold slaves in excess of their needs as they shifted from tobacco to mixed agriculture. Many enslaved families were broken up, as planters preferred mostly strong males for field work.[^cite_note-69]

Two major political issues that festered in the first half of the 19th century caused political alignment along sectional lines, strengthened the identities of North and South as distinct regions with certain strongly opposed interests, and fed the arguments over states' rights that culminated in secession and the Civil War. One of these issues concerned the protective tariffs enacted to assist the growth of the manufacturing sector, primarily in the North. In 1832, in resistance to federal legislation increasing tariffs, South Carolina passed an ordinance of [nullification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nullification_\(U.S._Constitution\)), a procedure in which a state would, in effect, repeal a Federal law. Soon a naval flotilla was sent to [Charleston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charleston,_South_Carolina) harbor, and the threat of landing ground troops was used to compel the collection of tariffs. A compromise was reached by which the tariffs would be gradually reduced, but the underlying argument over states' rights continued to escalate in the following decades.



[Horse race](74711.md) meeting at [Jacksonville, Alabama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacksonville,_Alabama), 1841

The second issue concerned slavery, primarily the question of whether slavery would be permitted in newly admitted states. The issue was initially finessed by political compromises designed to balance the number of "free" and "slave" states. The issue resurfaced in a more virulent form, however, around the time of the [Mexican–American War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican%E2%80%93American_War), which raised the stakes by adding new territories primarily on the Southern side of the imaginary geographic divide. Congress opposed allowing slavery in these territories.

Before the Civil War, the number of immigrants arriving at Southern ports began to increase, although the North continued to receive the most immigrants. [Huguenots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huguenots) were among the first settlers in Charleston, along with the largest number of Orthodox Jews outside of New York City. Numerous Irish immigrants settled in New Orleans, establishing a distinct [ethnic enclave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_enclave) now known as the [Irish Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Channel,_New_Orleans). Germans also went to New Orleans and its environs, resulting in a large area north of the city (along the Mississippi) becoming known as the German Coast. Still greater numbers immigrated to Texas (especially after 1848), where many bought land and were farmers. Many more German immigrants arrived in Texas after the Civil War, where they created the brewing industry in Houston and elsewhere, became grocers in numerous cities, and also established wide areas of farming.

By 1840, [New Orleans](53842.md) was the wealthiest city in the country and the third largest in population. The success of the city was based on the growth of international trade associated with products being shipped to and from the interior of the country down the Mississippi River. New Orleans also had the largest slave market in the country, as traders brought slaves by ship and overland to sell to planters across the Deep South. The city was a cosmopolitan port with a variety of jobs that attracted more immigrants than other areas of the South.[^cite_note-70] Because of lack of investment, however, construction of railroads to span the region lagged behind the North. People relied most heavily on river traffic for getting their crops to market and for transportation.

Between 1830 and 1850, Native Americans were removed from their home states in the South and Eastern United States and were sent to Oklahoma.[^cite_note-71]

### Civil War

Main articles: [American Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War) and [Confederate States of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederate_States_of_America)

By 1856, the South had lost control of Congress, and was no longer able to silence calls for an end to slavery – which came mostly from the more populated, [free states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slave_and_free_states) of the North. The Republican Party, founded in 1854, pledged to stop the spread of slavery beyond those states where it already existed. After Abraham Lincoln was elected the first Republican president in 1860, seven cotton states declared their secession and formed the [Confederate States of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederate_States_of_America) before Lincoln was inaugurated. The United States government, both outgoing and incoming, refused to recognize the Confederacy, and when the new Confederate President [Jefferson Davis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jefferson_Davis) ordered his troops to open fire on [Fort Sumter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Fort_Sumter) in April 1861, war broke out. Only the state of [Kentucky](16846.md) attempted to remain neutral, and it could only do so briefly. When Lincoln called for troops to suppress what he referred to as "combinations too powerful to be suppressed by the ordinary" judicial or martial means,[^cite_note-73] four more states decided to secede and join the Confederacy (which then moved its capital to Richmond, Virginia). Although the Confederacy had large supplies of captured munitions and many volunteers, it was slower than the Union in dealing with the border states. While the Upland South [border states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Border_states_\(American_Civil_War\)) of Kentucky, Missouri, West Virginia, Maryland, and Delaware, as well as the [District of Columbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/District_of_Columbia), continued to permit [slavery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_the_United_States) during the Civil War, they remained with the [Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_\(American_Civil_War\)) though Kentucky and Missouri both had rival Confederate governments that formed that were admitted and recognized by the Confederacy. Though early in the war, the Confederacy controlled more than half of Kentucky and the southern portion of Missouri. By March 1862, the Union largely controlled all the border state areas, had shut down all commercial traffic from all Confederate ports, had prevented European recognition of the Confederate government, and was poised to seize New Orleans. The rugged mountainous [East Tennessee](646800.md) region [attempted to rejoin the Union as a new state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Tennessee_Convention), having opposed secession and slavery compared to most of Tennessee.[^cite_note-lacy-74]

In the four years of war, from 1861 to 1865, the South was the primary battleground, with all but two of the major battles taking place on Southern soil. Union forces led numerous campaigns into the western Confederacy, controlling the border states in 1861, the Tennessee River, the Cumberland River and New Orleans in 1862, and the Mississippi River in 1863. In the East, however, the Confederate Army under [Robert E. Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_E._Lee) beat off attack after attack in its defense of the Confederate capital of Richmond. But when Lee tried to move north, he was repulsed (and nearly captured) at Sharpsburg in 1862 and Gettysburg in 1863.



[Atlanta](3138.md)'s railroad roundhouse in ruins shortly after the end of the Civil War

The Confederacy had the resources for a short war, but was unable to finance or supply a longer war. It reversed the traditional low-tariff policy of the South by imposing a new 15% tax on all imports from the Union. The [Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_Army) blockade stopped most commerce from entering the South, and smugglers avoided the tax, so the Confederate tariff produced too little revenue to finance the war. Inflated currency was the solution, but that created distrust of the Richmond government. Because of low investment in railroads, the Southern transportation system depended primarily on river and coastal traffic by boat; both were shut down by the [Union Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_Navy). The small railroad system virtually collapsed, so that by 1864 internal travel was so difficult that the Confederate economy was crippled.

The Confederate cause was hopeless by the time Atlanta fell and [William T. Sherman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_T._Sherman) marched through Georgia in late 1864, but the rebels fought on until Lee's army surrendered in April 1865. Once the Confederate forces surrendered, the region moved into the [Reconstruction Era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconstruction_Era) (1865–1877), in a partially successful attempt to rebuild the destroyed region and grant civil rights to freed slaves.

Southerners who were against the Confederate cause during the Civil War were known as [Southern Unionists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Unionists). They were also known as Union Loyalists or Lincoln's Loyalists. Within the eleven Confederate states, states such as Tennessee (especially [East Tennessee](646800.md)), Virginia (which included [West Virginia](32905.md) at the time), and North Carolina were home to the largest populations of Unionists. Many areas of [Southern Appalachia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appalachia) harbored pro-Union sentiment as well. As many as 100,000 men living in states under Confederate control would serve in the [Union Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_Army) or pro-Union guerrilla groups. Although Southern Unionists came from all classes, most differed socially, culturally, and economically from the regions dominant pre-war [planter class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planter_class).[^cite_note-75]

The South suffered more than the North overall, as the Union strategy of attrition warfare meant that Lee could not replace his casualties, and the total war waged by Sherman, Sheridan, and other Union armies devastated the infrastructure and caused widespread poverty and distress. The Confederacy suffered military losses of 95,000 soldiers killed in action and 165,000 who died of disease, for a total of 260,000,[^cite_note-76] out of a total white Southern population at the time of around 5.5 million.[^cite_note-77] Based on 1860 census figures, 8% of all white males aged 13 to 43 died in the war, including 6% in the North and about 18% in the South.[^cite_note-78] Northern military deaths were greater than Southern military deaths in absolute numbers, but were two-thirds smaller in terms of proportion of the population affected.

### Reconstruction and Jim Crow era

Main articles: [Reconstruction Era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconstruction_Era), [Disenfranchisement after the Reconstruction Era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disenfranchisement_after_the_Reconstruction_Era), and [Voting rights in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_rights_in_the_United_States)



An African American family, photographed by O'Pierre Havens, c. 1868

After the Civil War, the South was devastated in terms of infrastructure and economy. Because of states' reluctance to grant voting rights to freedmen, Congress instituted Reconstruction governments. It established military districts and governors to rule over the South until new governments could be established. Many white Southerners who had actively supported the Confederacy were temporarily disenfranchised. Rebuilding was difficult as people grappled with the effects of a new labor economy of a free market in the midst of a widespread [agricultural depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_depression). In addition, the limited infrastructure the South had was mostly destroyed by the war. At the same time, the North was rapidly industrializing. To avoid the social effects of the war, most of the Southern states initially passed [Black Codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Codes_\(United_States\)). During Reconstruction, these were mostly legally nullified by federal law and anti-Confederate legislatures, which existed for a short time during Reconstruction.[^cite_note-79]

There were thousands of people on the move, as African Americans tried to reunite families separated by slave sales, and sometimes migrated for better opportunities in towns or other states. Other freed people moved from plantation areas to cities or towns for a chance to get different jobs. At the same time, whites returned from refuges to reclaim plantations or town dwellings. In some areas, many whites returned to the land to farm for a while. Some freedpeople left the South altogether for states such as Ohio and Indiana, and later, Kansas. Thousands of others joined the migration to new opportunities in the Mississippi and Arkansas Delta bottomlands, and Texas.



*[A Home on the Mississippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Home_on_the_Mississippi)*, by [Currier and Ives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currier_and_Ives), 1871

With passage of the [13th Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirteenth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution) to the [Constitution of the United States](31644.md) (which outlawed slavery), the [14th Amendment](31666.md) (which granted full U.S. citizenship to African Americans), and the [15th Amendment](31667.md) (which extended the right to vote to African American males), African Americans in the South were made free citizens and were given the right to vote. Under Federal protection, white and Black [Republicans](32070.md) formed constitutional conventions and state governments. Among their accomplishments were creating the first public education systems in Southern states, and providing for welfare through orphanages, hospitals and similar institutions.

Northerners came south to participate in politics and business. Some were representatives of the [Freedmen's Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedmen's_Bureau) and other agencies of Reconstruction; some were [humanitarians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanitarianism) with the intent to help Black people. Some were adventurers who hoped to benefit themselves by questionable methods. They were all condemned with the pejorative term of [carpetbagger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpetbagger). Some Southerners would also take advantage of the disrupted environment and made money off various schemes, including bonds and financing for railroads.[^cite_note-80] White Southerners who supported Reconstruction policies and efforts became known as [scalawags](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalawag).

Secret [vigilante](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vigilante) organizations such as the [Ku Klux Klan](16779.md) – an organization sworn to perpetuate [white supremacy](33178.md) – had arisen quickly after the war's end in the 1860s, and used [lynching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynching), physical attacks, house burnings and other forms of intimidation to keep African Americans from exercising their political rights. Although the first Klan was disrupted by prosecution by the Federal government in the early 1870s, other groups persisted. By the mid-to-late-1870s, some upper class Southerners created increasing resistance to the altered social structure. [Paramilitary organizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paramilitary_organizations) such as the [White League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_League) in [Louisiana](18130.md) (1874), the [Red Shirts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Shirts_\(Southern_United_States\)) in [Mississippi](16949861.md) (1875) and rifle clubs, all "White Line" organizations, used organized violence against [Republicans](32070.md), both Black and white, to remove Republicans from political office, repress and bar Black voting, and restore the [Democratic Party](5043544.md) to power.[^cite_note-81] In 1876 white Democrats regained power in most of the state legislatures. They began to pass laws designed to strip African Americans and [Poor Whites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poor_White) from the voter registration rolls. The success of late-19th century interracial coalitions in several states inspired a reaction among some white Democrats, who worked harder to prevent both groups from voting.[^cite_note-82]

Despite discrimination, many Black Southerners became property owners in areas that were still developing. For instance, 90% of the Mississippi's bottomlands were still frontier and undeveloped after the war. By the end of the century, two-thirds of the farmers in Mississippi's Delta bottomlands were Black. They had cleared the land themselves and often made money in early years by selling off timber. Tens of thousands of migrants went to the Delta, both to work as laborers to clear timber for lumber companies, and many to develop their own farms.[^cite_note-83] Nonetheless, the long agricultural depression, along with disenfranchisement and lack of access to credit, led to many Black Americans in the Delta losing their property by 1910 and becoming sharecroppers or landless workers over the following decade. More than two generations of free African Americans lost their stake in property.[^cite_note-84]



Child laborers in [Bluffton, South Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluffton,_South_Carolina), 1913

Nearly all Southerners, Black and white, suffered economically as a result of the Civil War. Within a few years cotton production and harvest was back to pre-war levels, but low prices through much of the 19th century hampered recovery. They encouraged immigration by [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Americans_in_the_Mississippi_Delta) and [Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Italians_in_Mississippi) laborers into the Mississippi Delta. While the first Chinese Americans entered as indentured laborers from [Cuba](5042481.md), the majority came in the early 20th century. Neither group stayed long at rural farm labor.[^cite_note-85] The Chinese population became merchants and established stores in small towns throughout the Delta, establishing a place between white and Black.[^cite_note-86]

Migrations continued in the late 19th and early 20th centuries among both Black and white populations. In the last two decades of the 19th century, about 141,000 Black Americans left the South, and more after 1900, totaling a loss of 537,000. After that, the movement increased in what became known as the Great Migration from 1910 to 1940, and the Second Great Migration through 1970. Even more whites left the South, some going to California for opportunities and others heading to Northern industrial cities after 1900. Between 1880 and 1910, the loss of whites totaled 1,243,000.[^cite_note-87] Five million more left between 1940 and 1970.

From 1890 to 1908, ten of the eleven former Confederate states, along with Oklahoma upon statehood, passed disenfranchising constitutions or amendments that introduced voter registration barriers – such as [poll taxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poll_tax_\(United_States\)), residency requirements and [literacy tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literacy_test) – that were hard for minorities to meet. Most African Americans, most Mexican Americans, and tens of thousands of poor whites were disenfranchised, losing the vote for decades. In some states, [grandfather clauses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grandfather_clause) temporarily exempted white illiterates from literacy tests. The numbers of voters dropped drastically throughout the former Confederacy as a result. This can be seen via the feature "Turnout in Presidential and Midterm Elections" at the University of Texas' *Politics: Barriers to Voting*. Alabama, which had established universal white suffrage in 1819 when it became a state, also substantially reduced voting by poor whites.[^cite_note-88][^cite_note-89] [Democrat](5043544.md)-controlled legislatures passed [Jim Crow laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Crow_laws) to segregate public facilities and services, including transportation.

While African Americans, poor whites and civil rights groups started litigation against such provisions in the early 20th century, for decades [Supreme Court](31737.md) decisions overturning such provisions were rapidly followed by new state laws with new devices to restrict voting. Most Black Americans in the former Confederacy and Oklahoma could not vote until 1965, after passage of the Voting Rights Act and Federal enforcement to ensure people could register. Despite increases in the eligible voting population with the inclusion of women, Blacks, and those eighteen and over throughout this period, turnout in ex-Confederate states remained below the national average throughout the 20th century.[^cite_note-90] Not until the late 1960s did all American citizens regain protected civil rights by passage of legislation following the leadership of the [American Civil Rights Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_Rights_Movement).

Historian [William Chafe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Chafe) has explored the defensive techniques developed inside the African American community to avoid the worst features of Jim Crow as expressed in the legal system, unbalanced economic power, and intimidation and psychological pressure. Chafe says "protective socialization by Blacks themselves" was created inside the community to accommodate white-imposed sanctions while subtly encouraging challenges to those sanctions. Known as "walking the tightrope", such efforts at bringing about change were only slightly effective before the 1920s, but did build the foundation that younger African Americans deployed in their aggressive, large-scale activism during the civil rights movement in the 1950s and 1960s.[^cite_note-91]

### 1880s through 1930s

Main article: [Great Migration (African American)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Migration_\(African_American\))



Southern United States in 1908 from *The Harmsworth atlas and Gazetter*



An illustration from *Houston: Where Seventeen Railroads Meet the Sea*, 1913

At the end of the 19th century, white Democrats in the South had created state constitutions that were hostile to industry and business development, with anti-industrial laws extensive from the time new constitutions were adopted in the 1890s.[^cite_note-92] Banks were few and small; there was little access to credit. Traditional agriculture persisted across the region. Especially in Alabama and Florida, rural minorities held control in many state legislatures long after population had shifted to industrializing cities, and legislators resisted business and modernizing interests: Alabama refused to redistrict between 1901 and 1972, long after major population and economic shifts to cities. For decades Birmingham generated the majority of revenue for the state, for instance, but received little back in services or infrastructure.[^cite_note-93]

In the late 19th century, Texas rapidly expanded its railroad network, creating a network of cities connected on a radial plan and linked to the port of Galveston. Strikes and labor unrest served as a reflection of increasing industry: "in 1885 Texas ranked ninth among forty states in number of workers involved in strikes (4,000); for the six-year period it ranked fifteenth. Seventy-five of the one hundred strikes, chiefly interstate strikes of telegraphers and railway workers, occurred in the year 1886."[^cite_note-94]

By 1890, [Dallas](53838.md) became the largest city in Texas, and by 1900 it had a population of more than 42,000, which more than doubled to over 92,000 a decade later. Dallas was the harnessmaking capital of the world and a center of other manufacturing. As an example of its ambitions, in 1907 Dallas built the [Praetorian Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Praetorian_Building), fifteen storeys tall and the first skyscraper west of the Mississippi, soon to be followed by other skyscrapers.[^cite_note-95] Texas was transformed by a railroad network linking five important cities, among them Houston with its nearby port at Galveston, Dallas, Fort Worth, San Antonio, and El Paso. Each exceeded fifty thousand in population by 1920, with the major cities having three times that population.[^cite_note-96]

Business interests were ignored by the Southern Democrat ruling class. Nonetheless, major new industries started developing in cities such as Atlanta, Georgia; Birmingham, Alabama; and Dallas, Fort Worth, and Houston, Texas. Growth began occurring at a geometric rate. Birmingham became a major steel producer and mining town, with major population growth in the early decades of the 20th century.

The first major oil well in the South was drilled at [Spindletop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spindletop) near [Beaumont, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaumont,_Texas), on the morning of January 10, 1901. Other oil fields were later discovered nearby in Arkansas, Oklahoma, and under the [Gulf of Mexico](21076367.md). The resulting "Oil Boom" permanently transformed the economy of the West South Central states and produced the richest economic expansion after the Civil War.[^cite_note-97][^cite_note-98]

In the early 20th century, invasion of the [boll weevil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boll_weevil) devastated cotton crops in the South, producing an additional catalyst to African Americans' decisions to leave the South. From 1910 to 1970, more than 6.5 million African Americans left the South in the [Great Migration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Migration_\(African_American\)) to Northern and Western cities, defecting from persistent [lynching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynching), violence, [segregation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_segregation_in_the_United_States), poor education, and inability to vote. Black migration transformed many Northern and Western cities, creating new cultures and music. Many African Americans, like other groups, became industrial workers; others started their own businesses within the communities. Southern whites also migrated to industrial cities like Chicago, Detroit, [Oakland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oakland,_California), and Los Angeles, where they took jobs in the booming new auto and defense industry.



Photo of [sharecropper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharecropper) family in [Walker County, Alabama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walker_County,_Alabama), c. 1937

Later, the Southern economy was dealt additional blows by the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression) and the [Dust Bowl](59749.md). After the [Wall Street Crash of 1929](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wall_Street_Crash_of_1929), the economy suffered significant reversals and millions were left unemployed. Beginning in 1934 and lasting until 1939, an ecological disaster of severe wind and drought caused an exodus from Texas and Arkansas, the [Oklahoma Panhandle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_Panhandle) region, and the surrounding plains, in which over 500,000 [Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maps_of_American_ancestries) were homeless, hungry and jobless.[^cite_note-99] Thousands would leave the region to seek economic opportunities along the [West Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Coast_of_the_United_States).

President [Franklin D. Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt) noted the South as the "number one priority" in terms of need of assistance during the Great Depression. His administration created programs such as the [Tennessee Valley Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tennessee_Valley_Authority) in 1933 to provide rural electrification and stimulate development. Locked into low-productivity agriculture, the region's growth was slowed by limited industrial development, low levels of entrepreneurship, and the lack of capital investment.

### 1940s through late 20th century

Further information: [United States home front during World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_home_front_during_World_War_II)



Naval Air Station Miami, c. 1942–1943

[World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_home_front_during_World_War_II) marked a time of dramatic change within the South from an economic standpoint, as new industries and military bases were developed by the federal government, providing much-needed capital and infrastructure in many regions. People from all parts of the US came to the South for military training and work in the region's many bases and new industries. During and after the war millions of hard-scrabble farmers, both white and Black, left agriculture for other occupations and urban jobs.[^cite_note-100][^cite_note-101][^cite_note-102]

The United States began mobilizing for war in a major way in the spring of 1940. The warm weather of the South proved ideal for building 60% of the Army's new training camps and nearly half the new airfields. In all, 40% of spending on new military installations went to the South. For example, in 1940 the small town of 1500 people in [Starke, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starke,_Florida), became the base of [Camp Blanding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camp_Blanding). By March 1941, 20,000 men were constructing a permanent camp for 60,000 soldiers. Money flowed freely for the war effort, as over \$4 billion went into military facilities in the South, and another \$5 billion into defense plants. Major shipyards were built in Virginia, in Charleston, South Carolina, and along the Gulf Coast.

Huge warplane plants were opened in Dallas-Fort Worth and Georgia. The most secret and expensive operation was at [Oak Ridge, Tennessee](151055.md), where unlimited amounts of locally generated electricity were used to prepare uranium for the atom bomb.[^cite_note-103] The number of production workers doubled during the war. Most training centers, factories and shipyards were closed in 1945, but not all, and the families that left hardscrabble farms remained to find jobs in the growing urban South. The region had finally reached the take off stage into industrial and commercial growth, although its income and wage levels lagged well behind the national average. Nevertheless, as [George B. Tindall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_B._Tindall) notes, the transformation was, "The demonstration of industrial potential, new habits of mind, and a recognition that industrialization demanded community services."[^cite_note-104][^cite_note-105]

Per capita income jumped 140% from 1940 to 1945, compared to 100% elsewhere in the United States. Southern income rose from 59% to 65%. Dewey Grantham says the war, "brought an abrupt departure from the South's economic backwardness, poverty, and distinctive rural life, as the region moved perceptively closer to the mainstream of national economic and social life".[^cite_note-106] Since 1970, the proportion of the [African American population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Great_Migration) living in the South stabilized and began slightly increasing.[^cite_note-107]

Farming shifted from cotton and tobacco, to include cattle, rice, [soybeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soybean), [corn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize), and other foods. Industrial growth increased in the 1960s and greatly accelerated into the 1980s and 1990s. Several large urban areas in Texas, Georgia, and Florida grew to over four million people. Rapid expansion in industries such as autos, telecommunications, textiles, technology, banking, and aviation gave some states in the South an industrial strength to rival large states elsewhere in the country. By the 2000 census, the South (along with the West) was leading the nation in population growth. With this growth, however, has come long commute times and air pollution problems in cities such as Dallas, Houston, Atlanta, Austin, Charlotte, and others that rely on sprawling development and highway networks.[^cite_note-108]

### 21st century

In the 21st century, and especially after the [2010 midterm elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_United_States_elections), the Republican Party has largely dominated the South, both at the state and federal levels. As of 2024, Republicans control both houses of the [state legislatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_United_States_state_legislatures) of 10 out of the eleven former [Confederate States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederate_States_of_America), the sole exception being the [Virginia General Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia_General_Assembly).[^cite_note-109] However, there are still some Democratic statewide officeholders in the South, such as Kentucky governor [Andy Beshear](44388893.md), North Carolina governor [Josh Stein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josh_Stein), Virginia's U.S. Senators [Mark Warner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Warner) and [Tim Kaine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tim_Kaine), and Georgia's U.S. Senators [Raphael Warnock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raphael_Warnock) and [Jon Ossoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jon_Ossoff).

In 2019, Fortune 500 companies headquartered in Southern states included: Texas with 50, Virginia with 21, Florida with 18, Georgia with 17, North Carolina with 11, and Tennessee with 10. In 2022, Texas led the nation with the most Fortune 500 company headquarters with 53.[^cite_note-110] This economic expansion has enabled parts of the South to report some of the lowest unemployment rates in the United States.[^cite_note-111]

Even with certain southern states and areas doing well economically, many southern states and areas still have high poverty rates when compared to the U.S. nationally. In 2021, nine out of the ten [states with the highest poverty rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_poverty_rate) were in the South. Also, in 2023 all five states with [the lowest GDP per capita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_GDP) were in the South: Mississippi, West Virginia, Arkansas, Alabama, and South Carolina.

## Modern economy

In the late 20th century, the South changed dramatically. It saw a boom in its [service economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Service_economy), manufacturing base, high technology industries, and the financial sector. Texas in particular witnessed dramatic growth and population change with the dominance of the energy industry and tourism industries, such as the [Alamo Mission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alamo_Mission) in [San Antonio](53848.md). Tourism in Florida and along the Gulf Coast also grew steadily throughout the last decades of the 20th century.

Numerous new automobile production plants have opened in the region, or are soon to open, such as [Mercedes-Benz](18564164.md) in [Tuscaloosa, Alabama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercedes-Benz_U.S._International); [Hyundai](42257.md) in [Montgomery, Alabama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyundai_Motor_Manufacturing_Alabama); the [BMW](3772.md) production plant in [Spartanburg, South Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BMW_US_Manufacturing_Company); [Toyota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toyota) plants in [Georgetown, Kentucky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toyota_Motor_Manufacturing_Kentucky), [Blue Springs, Mississippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toyota_Motor_Manufacturing_Mississippi) and [San Antonio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toyota_Motor_Manufacturing_Texas); the [GM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Motors) manufacturing plant in [Spring Hill, Tennessee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_Hill_Manufacturing); a [Honda](13729.md) factory in [Lincoln, Alabama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln,_Alabama); the [Nissan](21240.md) North American headquarters in [Franklin, Tennessee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin,_Tennessee), and factories in [Smyrna, Tennessee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smyrna,_Tennessee) and [Canton, Mississippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canton,_Mississippi); a [Kia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kia_Motors) factory in [West Point, Georgia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Point,_Georgia); the [Volkswagen Chattanooga Assembly Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volkswagen_Chattanooga_Assembly_Plant) in [Chattanooga, Tennessee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chattanooga,_Tennessee); and [Ford Motor Company](30433662.md)’s [Blue Oval City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Oval_City) near [Stanton, Tennessee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanton,_Tennessee).

The two largest research parks in the country are located in the South: [Research Triangle Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_Triangle_Park) in North Carolina (the world's largest) and the [Cummings Research Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cummings_Research_Park) in [Huntsville, Alabama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huntsville,_Alabama) (the world's fourth largest).

In medicine, the [Texas Medical Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Medical_Center) in Houston has achieved international recognition in education, research, and patient care, especially in the fields of heart disease, cancer, and rehabilitation. In 1994 the Texas Medical Center was the largest medical center in the world including fourteen hospitals, two medical schools, four colleges of nursing, and six university systems.[^cite_note-112] The University of Texas [M.D. Anderson Cancer Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M.D._Anderson_Cancer_Center) is consistently ranked the No. 1 cancer research and treatment center in the United States.[^cite_note-113]

Many major banking corporations have headquarters in the region. [Bank of America](347756.md) and [Truist Financial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truist_Financial) are in [Charlotte](57447.md). [Wachovia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wachovia) was headquartered there before its purchase by [Wells Fargo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wells_Fargo). [Regions Financial Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regions_Financial_Corporation) is in [Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham,_Alabama), as is [AmSouth Bancorporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AmSouth_Bancorporation), and previously [BBVA Compass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBVA_Compass) before its acquisition by [PNC Financial Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PNC_Financial_Services). [Atlanta](3138.md) is the district headquarters of the [Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_Bank_of_Atlanta).

Many corporations are headquartered in Atlanta and its surrounding area, such as [The Coca-Cola Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Coca-Cola_Company), [Delta Air Lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_Air_Lines), and [The Home Depot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Home_Depot), and also many cable television networks, such as the [Turner Broadcasting System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turner_Broadcasting_System) ([CNN](62028.md), [TBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TBS_\(U.S._TV_channel\)), [TNT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TNT_\(U.S._TV_network\)), [Turner South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turner_South), [Cartoon Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartoon_Network)) and [The Weather Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Weather_Channel).

## Culture

Main article: [Culture of the Southern United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_the_Southern_United_States)

See also: [White Southerners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Southerners) and [Black Southerners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Southerners)

[^cite_note-ling.upenn.edu-118]

The American South is known for its [distinctive dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_American_English) and [accent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_accent_\(United_States\)). The predominant culture of the South is [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_diaspora), and has its origins with the settlement of the region by large groups of people from all over England including parts of the [West Midlands](351076.md), [southwest England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwest_England), and [southeast England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_England), such as [Sussex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sussex), [Shropshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shropshire), and the [West Country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Country) who moved to the [Tidewater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidewater_\(region\)) and the eastern parts of the [Deep South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_South) in the 17th and early 18th centuries. For example, the city of [Birmingham, Alabama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham,_Alabama), is named after [Birmingham, England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham) in the West Midlands. [Northern English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_England), [Scots lowlanders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_people) and [Ulster-Scots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_Scots_people) (later called the [Scotch-Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotch-Irish_American)) who settled in [Appalachia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appalachia) and the [Upland South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upland_South) in the mid to late 18th century,[^cite_note-119] and the many African people who were brought to the American South as slaves. Their descendants, identified as Black or African American people, compose the United States' second-largest racial minority, accounting for 12.1% of the total population according to the 2000 census. Despite [Jim Crow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Crow_laws) era [outflow to the North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Migration_\(African_American\)), the majority of the Black population remains concentrated in Southern states and has heavily contributed to the cultural blend of religion, food, art, and music *(see [spiritual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiritual_\(music\)), [blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blues), [jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz), [R\&B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhythm_and_blues), [soul music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_music), [country music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Country_music), [zydeco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zydeco), [bluegrass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluegrass_music) and [rock and roll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_and_roll))* that characterizes Southern culture today.

Overall, the South has had lower housing values, lower household incomes, and lower [cost of living](522934.md) than the rest of the United States.[^cite_note-120] These factors, combined with the fact that Southerners have continued to maintain strong loyalty to family ties, has led some sociologists to label [white Southerners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Southerners) an ethnic or quasi-ethnic group,[^cite_note-121][^cite_note-122] though this interpretation has been subject to criticism on the grounds that proponents of the view do not satisfactorily indicate how Southerners meet the criteria of ethnicity.[^cite_note-123] In previous censuses, the largest ancestry group identified by Southerners was [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_American) or mostly English,[^cite_note-ReferenceB-53][^cite_note-census.gov-124][^cite_note-ReferenceA-125] with 19,618,370 self-reporting "English" as an ancestry on the 1980 census, followed by 12,709,872 listing "[Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_American)" and 11,054,127 "[Afro-American](2154.md)".[^cite_note-ReferenceB-53][^cite_note-census.gov-124][^cite_note-ReferenceA-125] Almost a third of all Americans who claim English ancestry can be found in the American South, and over a quarter of all Southerners claim English descent as well.[^cite_note-126]

### Origins

The Southern [planter class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planter_class) originated from the [early modern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_modern_Britain) English [landed gentry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landed_gentry), who were aristocratic landowners but not [peers or "titled nobility"](58254.md). According to historian [G. E. Mingay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._E._Mingay), the gentry were landowners whose wealth "made possible a certain kind of education, a standard of comfort, and a degree of leisure and a common interest in ways of spending it". Leisure distinguished gentry from businessmen who gained their wealth through work.[^cite_note-127] The gentry did not work; their income came largely from rents paid by [tenant farmers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenant_farmer) living on their estates.[^cite_note-128] The concept of [Southern chivalry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_chivalry) in the [Antebellum South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antebellum_South) originated from the gentry rank of [Gentleman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gentleman), acting as a [chivalric ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chivalry) of the white planter class supposedly descended from the [knights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight) and [Cavaliers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavalier) of the Medieval and colonial eras.[^cite_note-Genovese-129][^cite_note-USHist-130]

Several Southern states (Maryland, Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina, and Georgia) were among the British colonies that sent delegates to sign the [Declaration of Independence](31874.md) and then fought against the government (Great Britain), along with the Middle and New England colonies, during the [Revolutionary War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War).[^cite_note-131] The basis for much of Southern culture derives from these states being among the original [Thirteen Colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirteen_Colonies), and from much of the population of the colonial South having ancestral links to colonists who emigrated west. Southern manners and customs reflect the relationship with England that was held by the early population.

In 1765, London philanthropist Dr. [John Fothergill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Fothergill_\(physician\)) remarked on the cultural differences of the British American colonies southward from Maryland and those to the north, suggesting that the Southerners were marked by "idleness and extravagance". Fothergill suggested that Southerners were more similar to the people of the Caribbean than to the colonies to the north.[^cite_note-cob-132] [J. Hector St. John de Crèvecœur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Hector_St._John_de_Cr%C3%A8vec%C5%93ur)'s 1782 *[Letters from an American Farmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letters_from_an_American_Farmer)* described [Charleston, South Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charleston,_South_Carolina), slaveholders as having "all that life affords most bewitching and pleasurable, without labour, without fatigue, hardly subjected to the trouble of wishing". He declared that although slavery had not yet been completely [abolished in the Northern states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End_of_slavery_in_the_United_States), the lot of northern slaves was far "different... in every respect". Crèvecœur sought to portray Southerners as stuck in the social, cultural and economic remnants of colonialism, in contrast to the Northerners whom he considered to be representative of the distinctive culture of the new nation.[^cite_note-cob-132]

Early in United States history, the contrasting characteristics of Southern states were acknowledged in a discussion between [Thomas Jefferson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Jefferson) and [François-Jean de Chastellux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois-Jean_de_Chastellux). Jefferson ascribed the Southerners' "unsteady", "generous", "candid" traits to their climate, while De Chastellux claimed that Southerners' "indelible character which every nation acquires at the moment of its origin" would "always be aristocratic" not only because of slavery but also "vanity and sloth". A visiting French dignitary concurred in 1810 that American customs seemed "entirely changed" over the [Potomac River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potomac_River), and that Southern society resembled those of the Caribbean.[^cite_note-cob-132]

The South is also influenced by [Native American culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_American_cultures_in_the_United_States).[^cite_note-133]

### Religion

[Native American religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_American_religions) were practiced before colonization.[^cite_note-134] The South has had a majority of its population adhering to [evangelical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelicalism) [Protestantism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism) ever since the early 1800s as a result of the [Second Great Awakening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Great_Awakening),[^cite_note-135] The upper classes often stayed [Episcopalian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episcopal_Church_\(United_States\)) or [Presbyterian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyterian). The [First Great Awakening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Great_Awakening) starting in the 1740s and the Second Great Awakening ending in the 1850s generated large numbers of Methodist and Baptist converts. These denominations remain the two main Christian confessions in the South.[^cite_note-136] By 1900, the [Southern Baptist Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Baptist_Convention) had become the largest Protestant denomination in the whole United States with its membership concentrated in rural areas of the South.[^cite_note-137][^cite_note-138] [Baptists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptists) are the most common religious group, followed by [Methodists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodists), [Pentecostals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentecostals) and other denominations. [Roman Catholics](410868.md) historically were concentrated in Maryland, Louisiana, and Hispanic areas such as South Texas and South Florida and along the Gulf Coast. The great majority of [Black Southerners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Southerners) are either Baptist or Methodist.[^cite_note-139] Statistics show that Southern states have the highest religious attendance figures of any region in the United States, constituting the so-called [Bible Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible_Belt).[^cite_note-140] [Pentecostalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentecostalism) has been strong across the South since the late 19th century.[^cite_note-141]

### National and international influences

Apart from its climate, the living experience in the South increasingly resembles the rest of the nation. The arrival of millions of [Northerners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_United_States) and [Westerners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_United_States), mainly since the late 20th century, has reshaped the culture of major metropolitan areas and coastal areas.[^cite_note-142]

While Hispanics have long been a major factor in Texas, millions more have arrived in other Southern states during the 1990s and early 2000s bringing values not rooted in local traditions.[^cite_note-143][^cite_note-144][^cite_note-145] Historian Raymond Mohl emphasizes the role of [NAFTA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAFTA) in lowering trade barriers and facilitating large-scale population movements. He adds other factors such as ongoing economic crisis in Mexico, new more liberal immigration policies in the United States, labor recruitment and smuggling, that have produced a major flow of Mexican and Hispanic migration to the southeast. That region's low-wage, low-skill economy readily hired cheap, reliable, nonunion labor, without asking applicants too many questions about legal status.[^cite_note-146][^cite_note-147] Richard J. Gonzales argues that the rise of [La Raza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Raza) (Mexican American community) in terms of numbers and influence in politics, education, and language and cultural rights will grow rapidly in Texas by 2030 when demographers predict Hispanics will outnumber Anglos in Texas.[^cite_note-148] However thus far their political participation and the [Latino vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latino_vote) have been low, so the potential political impact is much higher than the actual one thus far.[^cite_note-149][^cite_note-150]

Scholars have suggested that collective identity and Southern distinctiveness are thus declining in the Deep South, particularly when defined against "an earlier South that was somehow more authentic, real, more unified and distinct".[^cite_note-Edward_L._Ayers_2005_p._46-151] On the other hand, Southerners have moved west in large numbers, especially to California and to the Midwest. Thus, journalist [Michael Hirsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Hirsh_\(journalist\)) proposed that aspects of Southern culture have spread throughout a greater portion of the rest of the United States in a process termed "[Southernization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southernization)".[^cite_note-Hirsh-152]

[American football](18951490.md) is heavily considered the most popular team sport in most areas of the Southern United States.

The region is home to numerous decorated and historic [college football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_football) programs, particularly in the [Southeastern Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeastern_Conference) (known as the "SEC"), [Atlantic Coast Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_Coast_Conference) (known as the "ACC"), and the [Big 12 Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_12_Conference). ### Baseball



[Houston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houston_Astros) vs [Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Rangers_\(baseball\)) face-off during the 2013 [Lone Star Series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lone_Star_Series) in the [American League West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_League_West) division of [Major League Baseball](38776.md).

[Baseball](3850.md) has been played in the Southern United States dating back to the mid-19th century. It was traditionally more popular than American football until the 1980s and still accounts for the largest annual attendance amongst sports played in the South. The first mention of a baseball team in Houston was on April 11, 1861.[^cite_note-160][^cite_note-161] During the late 19th century and early 20th century games were common, especially once the professional leagues such as the [Texas League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_League), the [Dixie League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dixie_League_\(1916_baseball\)), and the [Southern League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_League_\(1885%E2%80%9399\)) were organized.

The short-lived [Louisville Colonels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisville_Colonels) were a part of the early [National League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_League_\(baseball\)) and [American Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Association_\(19th_century\)), but ceased to exist in 1899. The first Southern Major League Baseball team after the Colonels appeared in 1962, when the Houston Colt .45s (known today as the [Houston Astros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houston_Astros)) were enfranchised. Later, the [Atlanta Braves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlanta_Braves) came in 1966, followed by the [Texas Rangers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Rangers_\(baseball\)) in 1972, and finally the [Miami Marlins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_Marlins) and [Tampa Bay Rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tampa_Bay_Rays) in the 1990s.

[College baseball](1370306.md) appears to be more well attended in the South than elsewhere, as teams like [Florida State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_State_Seminoles_baseball), [Arkansas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arkansas_Razorbacks_baseball), [LSU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LSU_Tigers_baseball), [Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia_Cavaliers_baseball), [Mississippi State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippi_State_Bulldogs_baseball), [Ole Miss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ole_Miss_Rebels_baseball), [South Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Carolina_Gamecocks_baseball), [Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Gators_baseball) and [Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Longhorns_baseball) are commonly at the top of the [NCAA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCAA)'s attendance.[^cite_note-162] The South generally produces very successful collegiate baseball teams with Virginia, Vanderbilt, LSU, South Carolina, Florida [Coastal Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coastal_Carolina_Chanticleers) and Tennessee winning recent [College World Series](536107.md) Titles.

### Auto racing



The start of the [2015 Daytona 500](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_Daytona_500), the biggest race in [NASCAR](21114.md), at [Daytona International Speedway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daytona_International_Speedway) in [Daytona Beach, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daytona_Beach,_Florida)

The Southern states are commonly associated with [stock car racing](28349.md) and its most prominent competition level [NASCAR](21114.md), which is headquartered in [Charlotte](57447.md) and [Daytona Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daytona_Beach,_Florida). The sport was developed in the South during the early 20th century, with stock car racing's historic mecca being Daytona Beach, where cars initially raced on the wide, flat beachfront, before the construction of [Daytona International Speedway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daytona_International_Speedway). ### Golf

[Golf](19568112.md) is a popular recreational sport in most areas of the South, with the region's warm climate allowing it to host many [professional tournaments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professional_golf_tours) and numerous destination golf resorts, particularly in the state of [Florida](18933066.md). ## Health



2020 life expectancy map of the United States

The map on the right shows that [life expectancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_expectancy) is lower overall in most Southern states compared to the rest of the country. Obesity rates, hypertension, and [diabetes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diabetes) are higher relative to the rest of the nation.[^cite_note-165]

A study reported that six Southern states have the worst incidence of sleep disturbances in the nation, attributing the disturbances to high rates of obesity and smoking.[^cite_note-166]

The South has a higher percentage of obese people[^cite_note-167] and diabetics when compared to national regional averages.[^cite_note-168] The region also has the largest number of people dying from stroke complications[^cite_note-169] and the highest rates of [cognitive decline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dementia#Cognitive_testing).[^cite_note-170] Life expectancy is lower and death rates are higher, when compared to national averages of other regions in the United States.[^cite_note-171][^cite_note-172] This disparity reflects substantial divergence between the South and other regions since the middle of the 20th century.[^cite_note-173]

The [East South Central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_South_Central_states) Census Division of the United States (made up of Kentucky, Tennessee, Mississippi, and Alabama) had the highest rate of inpatient hospital stays in 2012. The other divisions, West South Central (Texas, Oklahoma, Arkansas and Louisiana) and South Atlantic (West Virginia, Delaware, Maryland, Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia and Florida) ranked seventh and fifth.[^cite_note-174] The South had a significantly higher rate of hospital discharges in 2005 than other regions of the United States, but the rate had declined to be closer to the overall national rate by 2011.[^cite_note-175]

For cancer causes, the South, particularly an axis from West Virginia through Texas, leads the nation in adult obesity, adult smoking, low exercise, low fruit consumption, low vegetable consumption, all known cancer risk factors,[^cite_note-176] which matches a similar high risk axis in "All Cancers Combined, Death Rates by State, 2011" from the [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention](6811.md).[^cite_note-177]

The South is home to some of the nation's largest [academic health systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_medical_centre), including the [Johns Hopkins Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johns_Hopkins_Hospital), [Duke University Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duke_University_Health_System), [University of Florida Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Florida_Health), [UNC Medical Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNC_Medical_Center), [University of Miami Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller_School_of_Medicine), [UT Health Science Center at Houston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UT_Health_Science_Center_at_Houston), [Emory Healthcare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emory_Healthcare), [University of South Florida Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_South_Florida_College_of_Medicine), and others.

## Politics

Main article: [Politics of the Southern United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics_of_the_Southern_United_States)

In the first decades after [Reconstruction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconstruction_era) in the 1880s and 1890s, white Democrats regained power in the state legislatures, and began to make voter registration more complicated, to reduce Black voting. With a combination of intimidation, fraud and violence by paramilitary groups, they suppressed Black voting and turned Republicans out of office. From 1890 to 1908, ten of eleven states ratified new constitutions or amendments that effectively disenfranchised most Black voters and many poor white voters. This disenfranchisement persisted for six decades into the 20th century, depriving Black and poor white Americans of all political representation. Because they could not vote, they could not sit on juries. They had no one to represent their interests, resulting in state legislatures consistently underfunding programs and services, such as schools, for Blacks and poor whites.[^cite_note-178] Scholars have characterized pockets of the Southern United States as being "authoritarian enclaves" from Reconstruction to the Civil Rights Act.[^cite_note-Mickey_2015-13][^cite_note-University_of_Chicago_Press-14][^cite_note-kuo-15][^cite_note-Gibson_2013-16]

With the collapse of the Republican Party in nearly all parts of the South, the region became known as the "[Solid South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_South)", and the Democratic Party after 1900 moved to a system of primaries to select their candidates. Victory in a primary was [tantamount to election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tantamount_to_election). From the late 1870s to the 1960s, only rarely was a state or national Southern politician a Republican, outside from Southern Republican strongholds within the Appalachian mountain districts.[^cite_note-179][^cite_note-180] Southern Republicans during this period would continue to control parts of the Appalachian Mountain areas and compete for power in the former Border States. Apart from a few states (such as the [Byrd Machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byrd_Machine) in Virginia, the [Crump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._H._Crump) [Machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_machine) in Memphis), and a few other local organizations, the Democratic Party itself was very lightly organized. It managed primaries but party officials had little other role. To be successful a politician built his own network of friends, neighbors and allies. Reelection was the norm, and the result from 1910 to the late 20th century was that Southern Democrats in Congress had accumulated seniority, and automatically took the chairmanships of all committees.[^cite_note-181] By the 1940s the Supreme Court began to find disenfranchisement measures like the "grandfather clause" and the white primary unconstitutional. Southern legislatures quickly passed other measures to keep Black citizens disenfranchised, even after suffrage was extended more widely to poor whites. Because white Democrats controlled all the Southern seats in the U.S. Congress, they had outsize power and could sidetrack or [filibuster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_filibuster) efforts to pass legislation they did not agree with.



A rally against school integration in [Little Rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Rock,_Arkansas), 1959

Increasing support for civil rights legislation by the national Democratic Party beginning in 1948 caused segregationist Southern Democrats to nominate [Strom Thurmond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strom_Thurmond) on a third-party "Dixiecrat" ticket in 1948. These Dixiecrats returned to the party by 1950, but Southern Democrats held off Republican inroads in the suburbs by arguing that only they could defend the region from the onslaught of northern liberals and the [civil rights movement](49001.md). In response to the *[Brown v. Board of Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_v._Board_of_Education)* ruling of 1954, 101 Southern congressmen (19 senators, 82 House members of which 99 were Southern Democrats and 2 were Republicans) in 1956 denounced the Brown decisions as a "clear abuse of judicial power \[that] climaxes a trend in the federal judiciary undertaking to legislate in derogation of the authority of Congress and to encroach upon the reserved rights of the states and the people". The manifesto lauded, "...those states which have declared the intention to resist enforced integration by any lawful means". It was signed by all Southern senators except Majority Leader [Lyndon B. Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson), and Tennessee senators [Albert Gore Sr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Gore_Sr.) and [Estes Kefauver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estes_Kefauver). Virginia closed schools in [Warren County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warren_County,_Virginia), [Prince Edward County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Edward_County,_Virginia), [Charlottesville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlottesville,_Virginia), and [Norfolk](57898.md) rather than integrate, but no other state followed suit. Democratic governors [Orval Faubus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orval_Faubus) of Arkansas, [Ross Barnett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ross_Barnett) of Mississippi, [John Connally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Connally) of Texas, [Lester Maddox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lester_Maddox) of Georgia, and, especially, [George Wallace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Wallace) of Alabama resisted integration and appealed to a rural and [blue-collar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue-collar) electorate.[^cite_note-182]



U.S. president Lyndon B. Johnson signs the historic [Civil Rights Act of 1964](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Rights_Act_of_1964).

The northern Democrats' support of civil rights issues culminated when Democratic President [Lyndon B. Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson) signed into law the [Civil Rights Act of 1964](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Rights_Act_of_1964) and the [Voting Rights Act of 1965](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_Rights_Act_of_1965), which ended legal segregation and provided federal enforcement of voting rights for Black Americans. In the [presidential election of 1964](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_United_States_presidential_election), [Barry Goldwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Goldwater)'s only electoral victories outside his home state of [Arizona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_presidential_election_in_Arizona,_1964) were in the states of the [Deep South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_South) where few Black Americans could vote before the 1965 Voting Rights Act.[^cite_note-183] Pockets of resistance to integration in public places broke out in violence during the 1960s by the shadowy [Ku Klux Klan](16779.md), which caused a backlash among moderates.[^cite_note-184] Major resistance to school busing extended into the 1970s.[^cite_note-185]

National Republicans such as [Richard Nixon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Nixon) began to develop their [Southern strategy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_strategy) to attract conservative white Southerners, especially the middle class and suburban voters, in addition to migrants from the North and traditional GOP pockets in Appalachia. The transition to a Republican stronghold in the South took decades. First, the states started voting Republican in presidential elections, except for native Southerners [Jimmy Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Carter) in 1976 and [Bill Clinton](3356.md) in 1992 and 1996. Then the states began electing Republican senators and finally governors. Georgia was the last state to do so, with [Sonny Perdue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonny_Perdue) taking the governorship in 2002.[^cite_note-186] In addition to its middle class and business base, Republicans cultivated the religious right and attracted strong majorities from the evangelical or Fundamentalist vote, mostly Southern Baptists, which had not been a distinct political force prior to 1980.[^cite_note-187]

### Decline of Southern liberalism during the 20th century

Southern liberals were an essential part of the [New Deal coalition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Deal_coalition) – without them Roosevelt lacked majorities in Congress. Typical leaders were [Lyndon B. Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson) in Texas, [Jim Folsom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Folsom) and [John Sparkman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Sparkman) in Alabama, [Claude Pepper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Pepper) in Florida, [Earl Long](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earl_Long) and [Hale Boggs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hale_Boggs) in Louisiana, and [Estes Kefauver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estes_Kefauver) in Tennessee. They promoted subsidies for small farmers, and supported the nascent labor union movement. An essential condition for this north–south coalition was for northern liberals to ignore the problem of racism throughout the South and elsewhere in the country. After 1945, however, northern liberals – led especially by young [Hubert Humphrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubert_Humphrey) of Minnesota – increasingly made civil rights a central issue. They convinced Truman to join them in 1948. The conservative Southern Democrats – the [Dixiecrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dixiecrat) – took control of the state parties in half the region and ran [Strom Thurmond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strom_Thurmond) for president against Truman. Thurmond carried only the Deep South, but that threat was enough to guarantee the national Democratic Party in 1952 and 1956 would not make civil rights a major issue. In 1956, 101 of the 128 southern congressmen and senators signed the [Southern Manifesto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Manifesto) denouncing forced desegregation.[^cite_note-188] The labor movement in the South was divided, and lost its political influence. Southern liberals were in a quandary – most of them kept quiet or moderated their liberalism, others switched sides, and the rest continued on the liberal path. One by one, the last group was defeated; historian Numan V. Bartley states, "Indeed, the very word 'liberal' gradually disappeared from the southern political lexicon, except as a term of opprobrium."[^cite_note-189]

### Presidents from the South



[Bill Clinton](3356.md), newly elected [Governor of Arkansas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governor_of_Arkansas), speaking with [Jimmy Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Carter) in 1978. Carter and Clinton were both [Southern Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Democrats) and elected to the presidencies in [1976](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1976_United_States_presidential_election), [1992](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1992_United_States_presidential_election) and [1996](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_United_States_presidential_election).

The South produced nine of the country's first twelve [Presidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_presidents_of_the_United_States), including the [Virginia Dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia_Dynasty). After [Zachary Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zachary_Taylor) won the presidential election of 1848, no Southern politician was elected president until [Woodrow Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodrow_Wilson) in 1912. [Andrew Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Johnson) (of [Tennessee](30395.md)) who was [vice president](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_vice_presidents_of_the_United_States) in 1865, became president after the death of [Abraham Lincoln](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Lincoln).&#x20;

Out of the last eleven U.S. presidents, seven have Southern region ties: [Lyndon B. Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson) (of [Texas](29810.md); 1963–69), [Jimmy Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Carter) (of [Georgia](48830.md); 1977–81), [George H. W. Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_H._W._Bush) (of Texas; 1989–93), [Bill Clinton](3356.md) (of [Arkansas](1930.md); 1993–2001), [George W. Bush](3414021.md) (of Texas; 2001–2009), [Donald Trump](4848272.md) (of [Florida](18933066.md); 2017–2021, 2025–present), and [Joe Biden](145422.md) (of [Delaware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delaware); 2021–2025). Johnson was a native of Texas, while Carter is from [Georgia](48830.md), and Clinton from [Arkansas](1930.md). While George H. W. Bush and George W. Bush began their political careers in Texas, they were both born in [New England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_England) and have their ancestral roots in that region. Similarly, while Joe Biden was born in [Pennsylvania](23332.md), he grew up largely in Delaware (classified as a Southern state by the U.S. Census Bureau) and spent his entire political career there.

### Other politicians and political movements

The South has produced various nationally known politicians and political movements. In 1948, a group of Democratic congressmen, led by Governor [Strom Thurmond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strom_Thurmond) of South Carolina, split from the Democrats in reaction to an anti-segregation speech given by [Minneapolis](6097240.md) mayor and future senator [Hubert Humphrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubert_Humphrey) of [Minnesota](19590.md). They founded the States Rights Democratic or [Dixiecrat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dixiecrat) Party. During that year's presidential election, the party ran Thurmond as its candidate and he carried four Deep South states.

In the [1968 Presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1968_United_States_presidential_election), Alabama Governor [George C. Wallace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_C._Wallace) ran for president on the [American Independent Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Independent_Party) ticket. Wallace ran a "law and order" campaign similar to that of Republican candidate, [Richard Nixon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Nixon). Nixon's [Southern Strategy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Strategy) of gaining electoral votes downplayed race issues and focused on culturally conservative values, such as family issues, patriotism, and cultural issues that appealed to [Southern Baptists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Baptists).

In [the 1994 mid-term elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_elections,_1994), another Southern politician, [Newt Gingrich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newt_Gingrich), led the [Republican Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_Revolution), ushering in twelve years of GOP control of the House. Gingrich became [Speaker of the United States House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speaker_of_the_United_States_House_of_Representatives) in 1995 and served until his resignation in 1999. The incumbent Speaker of the House, Louisiana Republican [Mike Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mike_Johnson), is also from the South.&#x20;

Except for [Bob Dole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Dole) from Kansas (1985–96) and [John Thune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Thune) from South Dakota (2025-present), nearly all the recent Republican Senate Leaders have all been Southerners: [Howard Baker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Baker) (1981–1985) of Tennessee, [Trent Lott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trent_Lott) (1996–2003) of Mississippi, [Bill Frist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Frist) (2003–2006) of Tennessee, and [Mitch McConnell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitch_McConnell) (2007–2024) of Kentucky.

The Republican candidates for president have won the South in elections since [1972](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1972_United_States_presidential_election), except for [1976](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1976_United_States_presidential_election). The region is not, however, entirely monolithic, and every successful Democratic candidate has won at least 2 of the 11 former Confederate states. [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama) won Florida, Maryland, Delaware, North Carolina, and Virginia in 2008 but did not repeat his victory in North Carolina during his 2012 reelection campaign.[^cite_note-190] Joe Biden also performed well for a modern Democrat in the South, winning Maryland, Delaware, Virginia, and Georgia, in the [2020 United States presidential election](48410011.md).

## Race relations

### Native Americans

Native Americans have lived in what is now termed the American South for nearly 12,000 years. Increasing amounts of their land was taken until they were defeated by settlers in a series of conflicts ending in the [War of 1812](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_1812) and the [Seminole Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seminole_Wars), and most of the surviving population was removed west to Indian Territory (now Oklahoma).

### Civil rights movement

Main articles: [Civil rights movement](49001.md), [Montgomery bus boycott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montgomery_bus_boycott), and [Martin Luther King Jr.](20076.md)

The South witnessed two major events in the lives of 20th century African Americans: the [Great Migration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Migration_\(African_American\)) and the [American Civil Rights Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_Rights_Movement). The Great Migration began during World War I, hitting its high point during World War II. During this migration, Black people left the South to find work in Northern factories and other sectors of the economy.[^cite_note-192]

The migration also empowered the growing Civil Rights Movement. While the movement existed in all parts of the United States, its focus was against disenfranchisement and the Jim Crow laws in the South. Most of the major events in the movement occurred in the South, including the [Montgomery bus boycott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montgomery_bus_boycott), the Mississippi [Freedom Summer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_Summer), the March on [Selma, Alabama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selma,_Alabama), and the [assassination of Martin Luther King Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assassination_of_Martin_Luther_King_Jr.) In addition, some of the most important writings to come out of the movement were written in the South, such as King's "[Letter from Birmingham Jail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letter_from_Birmingham_Jail)". Most of the civil rights landmarks can be found around the South. The [Birmingham Civil Rights National Monument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_Civil_Rights_National_Monument) in Birmingham includes the [Birmingham Civil Rights Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_Civil_Rights_Institute) which details Birmingham's role as the center of the Civil Rights Movement. The [16th Street Baptist Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/16th_Street_Baptist_Church) served as a rallying point for coordinating and carrying out the [Birmingham campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_campaign) as well as the adjacent [Kelly Ingram Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelly_Ingram_Park). This park served as ground zero for the infamous children's protest that eventually led to the passage of the [Civil Rights Act of 1964](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Rights_Act_of_1964); it has since been rededicated as a place of "Revolution and Reconciliation" and is now the setting of moving sculptures related to the battle for Civil Rights in the city. Both Kelly Ingram Park and the 16th Street Baptist Church are centerpieces of the [Birmingham Civil Rights District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_Civil_Rights_District). The [Martin Luther King Jr. National Historical Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Luther_King_Jr._National_Historical_Park) in Atlanta includes a museum that chronicles the American Civil Rights Movement as well as Martin Luther King Jr.'s boyhood home on Auburn Avenue. Additionally, [Ebenezer Baptist Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebenezer_Baptist_Church_\(Atlanta,_Georgia\)) is located in the Sweet Auburn district as is the King Center, the location of Martin Luther and [Coretta Scott King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coretta_Scott_King)'s gravesites.

### Racial integration

During the 1950s and 1960s, the racial integration of all-white collegiate sports teams was high on the regional agenda. Involved in it were issues of [racial equality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_equality), racism, and the alumni's demand for the top players who it needed to win high-profile games. The [Atlantic Coast Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_Coast_Conference) (ACC) would take the lead. First, they started to schedule integrated teams from the North. The wake-up call came in 1966, when [Don Haskins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Don_Haskins)'s [Texas Western College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Texas_at_El_Paso#History) team with [five Black starters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1965%E2%80%9366_Texas_Western_Miners_men's_basketball_team), upset the all-white University of Kentucky team to win the NCAA national basketball championship.[^cite_note-193] That happened at a time when there were no Black varsity basketball teams in either the Southeastern Conference or the Southwest Conference. Finally ACC schools, typically under pressure from boosters and civil rights groups, integrated their sports teams.[^cite_note-194][^cite_note-195] With an alumni base that dominated local and state politics, society and business, the ACC flagship schools were successful in their endeavor – as historian Pamela Grundy argues, they had learned how to win:

The widespread admiration that athletic ability inspired would help transform athletic fields from grounds of symbolic play to forces for social change, places where a wide range of citizens could publicly and at times effectively challenge the assumptions that cast them as unworthy of full participation in U.S. society. While athletic successes would not rid society of prejudice or stereotype – black athletes would continue to confront racial slurs...\[minority star players demonstrated] the discipline, intelligence, and poise to contend for position or influence in every arena of national life.

[^cite_note-196]

### Congress ends segregation (1964) and guarantees voting rights (1965)

Main articles: [Civil Rights Act of 1964](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Rights_Act_of_1964) and [Voting Rights Act of 1965](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_Rights_Act_of_1965)



[Racial segregation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_segregation) was required by state laws in the South and other U.S. states until 1964.

The decisive action ending segregation came when Congress, in a bipartisan fashion, overcame Southern filibusters to pass the [Civil Rights Act of 1964](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Rights_Act_of_1964) and the [Voting Rights Act of 1965](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_Rights_Act_of_1965). A complex interaction of factors came together unexpectedly in the period from 1954 to 1965 to make the momentous changes possible. The Supreme Court had taken the first initiative in [Brown v. Board of Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_v._Board_of_Education) (1954) making segregation of public schools unconstitutional. Enforcement was rapid in the North and border states but was deliberately stopped in the South by the movement called [Massive Resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massive_Resistance), sponsored by rural segregationists who largely controlled the state legislatures. Southern liberals, who counseled moderation, were shouted down by both sides and had limited impact. Much more significant was the [Civil Rights Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Rights_Movement), especially the [Southern Christian Leadership Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Christian_Leadership_Conference) (SCLC) headed by [Martin Luther King Jr.](20076.md) It largely displaced the old, much more moderate NAACP in taking leadership roles. King organized massive demonstrations, that seized massive media attention in an era when network television news was an innovative and universally watched phenomenon.[^cite_note-197] SCLC, student activists and smaller local organizations staged demonstrations across the South. National attention focused on Birmingham, Alabama, where protesters deliberately provoked [Bull Connor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bull_Connor) and his police forces by using young teenagers as demonstrators – and Connor arrested 900 in one day alone. The next day Connor unleashed billy clubs, police dogs, and high-pressure water hoses to disperse and punish the young demonstrators with a brutality that horrified the nation. It was very bad for business and the image of a modernizing progressive urban South. President John F. Kennedy, who had been calling for moderation, threatened to use federal troops to restore order in Birmingham. The result in Birmingham was a compromise by which the new mayor opened the library, golf courses, and other city facilities to both races, against the backdrop of church bombings and assassinations.[^cite_note-198][^cite_note-199]

Confrontations continued to escalate. In the summer of 1963, there were 800 demonstrations in 200 southern cities and towns, involving over 100,000 participants and 15,000 arrests. In Alabama in June 1963, Governor [George Wallace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Wallace) escalated the crisis by defying court orders to admit the first two Black students to the University of Alabama.[^cite_note-200] Kennedy responded by sending Congress a comprehensive civil rights bill and ordered Attorney General Robert Kennedy to file federal lawsuits against segregated schools and to deny funds for discriminatory programs. Doctor King launched a massive march on Washington in August 1963, bringing out 200,000 demonstrators in front of the Lincoln Memorial, the largest political assembly in the nation's history. The Kennedy administration now gave full-fledged support to the civil rights movement, but powerful southern congressmen blocked any legislation.[^cite_note-201] After Kennedy was assassinated President Lyndon Johnson called for immediate passage of Kennedy civil rights legislation as a memorial to the martyred president. Johnson formed a coalition with Northern Republicans that led to passage in the House, and with the help of Republican Senate leader [Everett Dirksen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everett_Dirksen) with passage in the Senate early in 1964. For the first time in history, the southern filibuster was broken, and the Senate finally passed its version on June 19 by a vote of 73 to 27.[^cite_note-202] The Civil Rights Act of 1964 was the most powerful affirmation of equal rights ever made by Congress. It guaranteed access to public accommodations such as restaurants and places of amusement, authorized the Justice Department to bring suits to desegregate facilities in schools, gave new powers to the [Civil Rights Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Rights_Commission); and allowed federal funds to be cut off in cases of discrimination. Furthermore, racial, religious and gender discrimination was outlawed for businesses with 25 or more employees, as well as apartment houses. The South resisted until the last moment, but as soon as the new law was signed by President Johnson on July 2, 1964, it was widely accepted across the nation. There was only a scattering of diehard opposition, typified by restaurant owner [Lester Maddox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lester_Maddox) in Georgia, who became governor, but the great majority of restaurants and hotels in Georgia followed the new law as the business community realized that peaceful integration was the only way forward.[^cite_note-203][^cite_note-204][^cite_note-205][^cite_note-206]

Since the enactment of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and [Voting Rights Act of 1965](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_Rights_Act_of_1965), black people have gone on to hold many offices within the Southern states. Black people have been elected or appointed as mayors or police chiefs in the cities of [Atlanta](3138.md), [Baltimore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltimore), [Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham,_Alabama), [Charlotte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlotte,_NC), [Columbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia,_South_Carolina), [Dover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dover,_Delaware), [Houston](13774.md), [Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackson,_Mississippi), [Jacksonville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacksonville,_Florida), [Memphis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memphis,_Tennessee), [Montgomery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montgomery,_Alabama), [Nashville](22018.md), [New Orleans](53842.md), [Raleigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raleigh), [Richmond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richmond,_Virginia), and [Washington](108956.md). They have also gone on to serve in both the U.S. Congress and state legislatures of Southern states.[^cite_note-207]

### New Great Migration

Main article: [New Great Migration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Great_Migration)

The civil rights movement of the 1950s and 1960s ended Jim Crow laws across the South and other areas of the United States. In recent decades, a second migration appears to be underway, this time with [African Americans from the North moving to the South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Great_Migration) in record numbers.[^cite_note-208] While race relations are still a contentious issue in the South and most of the U.S., the region surpasses the rest of the country in many areas of integration and racial equality. According to a 2003 report by researchers at the [University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wisconsin%E2%80%93Milwaukee), [Virginia Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia_Beach), [Charlotte](57447.md), [Nashville-Davidson](22018.md), and [Jacksonville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacksonville,_Florida) were the five most integrated of the nation's fifty largest cities, with [Memphis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memphis,_Tennessee) at number six.[^cite_note-209] Southern states tend to have a low disparity in incarceration rates between Black and white populations relative to the rest of the country.[^cite_note-210]

The New Great Migration is not evenly distributed throughout the South. The rise in net gain points to Atlanta, Charlotte, Dallas, and Houston being a growing hot spots for the migrants of The New Great Migration. The percentage of Black Americans who live in the South has been increasing since 1990, and the biggest gains have been in the region's large urban areas, according to census data. The Black population of metro Atlanta more than doubled between 1990 and 2020, surpassing 2 million in the most recent census. The Black population also more than doubled in metro Charlotte while Greater Houston and Dallas-Fort Worth both saw their Black populations surpass 1 million for the first time. Several smaller metro areas also saw sizable gains, including San Antonio;[^cite_note-expressnews.com-211] Raleigh and [Greensboro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greensboro), North Carolina; and [Orlando](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orlando,_Florida).[^cite_note-212] Primary destinations are states that have the most job opportunities, especially [Georgia](48830.md), [North Carolina](21650.md), [Maryland](18858.md), [Virginia](32432.md), [Tennessee](30395.md), [Florida](18933066.md), and [Texas](29810.md). Other southern states, including [Mississippi](16949861.md), [Louisiana](18130.md), [South Carolina](27956.md), [Alabama](303.md), and [Arkansas](1930.md), have seen little net growth in the African American population from return migration.

## Symbolism

The [Confederate battle flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flags_of_the_Confederate_States_of_America#Battle_flag) is used as a symbol to identify the South, states' rights and Southern tradition. Such groups as the [League of the South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_the_South) have a high regard for the secession movement of 1860, citing a desire to protect and defend Southern heritage.[^cite_note-213] Numerous political battles have erupted nationally and within the South over flying the Confederate flag over state capitols, and the naming of public buildings or highways after Confederate leaders, the prominence of certain statues and monuments, and the everyday display of Confederate insignia.[^cite_note-214]

Other symbols of the South include the [Bonnie Blue Flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonnie_Blue_Flag), [magnolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnolia) trees, and the song "[Dixie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dixie_\(song\))".[^cite_note-215]
