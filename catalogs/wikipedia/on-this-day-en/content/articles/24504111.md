# Mineral industry of Colombia



Monument to the miners in [Zipaquirá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zipaquir%C3%A1), [Cundinamarca](189405.md)



[Sea salt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_salt) exploitation in [Manaure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manaure,_La_Guajira)

**Mineral industry of Colombia** refers to the extraction of valuable minerals or other geological materials in [Colombia](5222.md). Colombia is well-endowed with [minerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineral) and energy resources. It has the largest [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal) reserves in Latin America, and is second to [Brazil](3383.md) in [hydroelectric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroelectric) potential. Estimates of [petroleum](23195.md) reserves in 1995 were 3.1 billion barrels (490,000,000 m<sup>3</sup>). Colombia also possesses significant amounts of [nickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel) and [gold](12240.md). Other important metals included [platinum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platinum) and [silver](27119.md), which were extracted in much smaller quantities. Colombia also produces [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper), small amounts of [iron ore](145352.md), and [bauxite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bauxite). Nonmetallic mined minerals include [salt](1605200.md), [limestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limestone), [sulfur](27127.md), [gypsum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gypsum), [dolomite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolomite_\(mineral\)), [barite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barite), [feldspar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feldspar), [clay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clay), [magnetite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetite), [mica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mica), [talcum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talcum), and [marble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marble). Colombia also produces most of the world's [emeralds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colombian_emeralds). Despite the variety of minerals available for exploitation, Colombia still had to import substances such as iron, copper, and aluminum to meet its industrial needs.

Materials recovered by mining in the country include [oil](4207510.md), with proved reserves of 1,506,000,000 bbl (239,400,000 m<sup>3</sup>) (2006 estimate) and [natural gas](22131.md), with annual production of 6.18 billion m<sup>3</sup> (2004 estimate) and reserves of 114.4 billion m<sup>3</sup> (1 January 2005 estimate).[^cite_note-1]

Minerals—in particular coal, oil, and natural gas, but also emeralds, gold, and nickel—have played an important role in Colombia's GDP and foreign trade in the last 20 years. Accounting for only 1.4 percent of GDP and 13 percent of total exports between 1980 and 1984, minerals represented about 5 percent of GDP and 42 percent of total exports in 2006. The minerals industry has compensated to a certain extent for the decreasing role of agriculture and has expanded the importance of commodities for the economy as a whole. Colombia is the world's leading source of emeralds, and [illegal mining](49640132.md) is commonplace. Illegal mining, especially of [gold](12240.md), has grown due to Colombia's aggressive counter narcotics policies, which increase the risks associated with the drug economy.[^cite_note-2] However, production of precious minerals is small scale despite high international prices for minerals such as gold.[^cite_note-sv-3]

## History



[Poporo Quimbaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poporo_Quimbaya) and pestle. Phytomorphic (fruit-shaped) [lime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lime_\(material\)) container, gold, 300 BC - 1000 AD

See also: [El Dorado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Dorado) and [Muisca economy § Mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_economy#Mining)

Mining of [kaolinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaolinite) and [hematite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hematite) for [pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pottery) pigments started in what is today Colombia since the mid-late [neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic), with archaeological evidence of ceramic production and sedentary groups living in [El Abra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Abra) settlements and the [Colombian Caribbean coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caribbean_Region_of_Colombia) (near the towns of San Jacinto, Monsú, Puerto Chacho, and [Puerto Hormiga archaeological site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puerto_Hormiga_archaeological_site)) beginning around the year 5940 BCE around the town of San Jacinto.[^cite_note-4] This would place these pottery shards among the oldest ever recovered anywhere. The earliest examples of gold mining and goldwork have been attributed to the [Tumaco](4513756.md) people of the Pacific coast and date to around 325 BCE. Gold would play a pivotal role in luring the [Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_people) [conquistadores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conquistador) to the area during the 16th century.[^cite_note-5]

Gold was considered sacred by most of the [Precolumbian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precolumbian) civilizations of the area. In [Muisca mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_mythology), Gold (Chiminigagua) was considered itself a deity, and the force of creation. [Copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper) mining was very important for the classic [Quimbaya civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quimbaya_civilization), which developed the [tumbaga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tumbaga) [alloy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alloy).[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7]

Although significant in the [colonial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colony) economy, it never commanded a large portion of [Colombia](5222.md)'s [GDP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GDP) in modern times. With the discovery and exploitation of large [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal) reserves, however, the role of mining in the national economy expanded in the late 1980s.[^cite_note-8]

## Mineral resources of Colombia

### Oil

Main article: [Petroleum industry in Colombia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_industry_in_Colombia)



Petroleum production (red) and exports (black), *1960-2012*

The discovery of 2 billion barrels (320,000,000 m<sup>3</sup>) of high-quality oil at the Cusiana and Cupiagua fields, about 200 kilometres (120 mi) east of [Bogotá](211271.md), has enabled Colombia to become a net oil exporter since 1986. The [Transandino pipeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transandino_pipeline) transports oil from [Orito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orito) in the [Department of Putumayo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Putumayo) to the Pacific port of [Tumaco](4513756.md) in the [Department of Nariño](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Nari%C3%B1o).[^cite_note-unco-9] Total crude oil production averages 620 thousand barrels per day (99,000 m<sup>3</sup>/d); about 184 thousand barrels per day (29,300 m<sup>3</sup>/d) is exported.&#x20;

The [Pastrana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andr%C3%A9s_Pastrana) government liberalized the petroleum investment policies, leading to an increase in exploration activity. Refining capacity cannot satisfy domestic demand, so some refined products, especially [gasoline](23639.md), must be imported. Plans for the construction of new refineries are under development.

### Natural gas



Pipeline map of Colombia, *2010*

While Colombia has vast hydroelectric potential, a prolonged drought in 1992 forced severe electricity rationing throughout the country until mid-1993. The consequences of the drought on electricity-generating capacity caused the government to commission the construction or upgrading of 10 [thermoelectric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermoelectric) power plants. Half will be coal-fired, and half will be fired by [natural gas](22131.md). The government also has begun awarding bids for the construction of a natural gas pipeline system that will extend from the country's extensive gas fields to its major population centers. Plans call for this project to make natural gas available to millions of Colombian households by the middle of the next decade.

Starting in 2004, Colombia became a net energy exporter, exporting electricity to [Ecuador](9334.md) and developing connections to [Peru](170691.md), [Venezuela](32374.md) and [Panama](22997.md) to export to those markets as well. The [Trans-Caribbean pipeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-Caribbean_pipeline) connecting western Venezuela to Panama through Colombia was inaugurated by October, 2007, thanks to cooperation between presidents [Álvaro Uribe](243680.md) of Colombia, [Martín Torrijos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mart%C3%ADn_Torrijos) of Panama and [Hugo Chávez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugo_Ch%C3%A1vez) of Venezuela.

Since 2022, Colombia has encountered new challenges in its energy sector. President [Gustavo Petro](13391962.md), with a strong focus on combating [climate change](5042951.md), has declined to issue licenses for new [natural gas](22131.md) exploration, despite dwindling reserves. This policy has led to expectations of increased energy costs, compelling the country to import [liquefied natural gas](832128.md) (LNG), which is substantially more expensive than domestic sources. The state oil company, [Ecopetrol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecopetrol), is attempting to address this shortfall by restarting previously suspended projects and increasing output from smaller fields. Nonetheless, projections indicate a widening gap between gas supply and demand, potentially impacting the competitiveness of Colombian industries.[^cite_note-12]

### Coal



Coal production (red) and exports (black), *1970-2012*



[Cerrejón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerrej%C3%B3n) coal mine

Colombia's [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal) output has increased consistently from 4 million tons in 1981 to 65.6 million tons in 2006, when it contributed 1.4 percent of the world's coal production. In 2006 Colombia accounted for 81 percent of the total coal production in Central and South America. Furthermore, 94 percent of Colombia's coal is of very good quality and is classified as hard, with high heat-generating capacity. Coal has been Colombia's second-largest export since 2001.[^cite_note-sv-3]

The largest coal mines—and the ones that generate the most exports—are located in the north of the country, in the departments of [La Guajira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Guajira_Department) and [Cesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cesar_Department). [Cerrejón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerrej%C3%B3n) is considered to be one of the largest [open-pit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-pit) coal mines in the world.[^cite_note-sv-3] The 2008 coal production in Cerrejon was calculated in 31.2 million tons.[^cite_note-13] There are also smaller coal mines scattered throughout the rest of the nation.[^cite_note-sv-3]

Since 2000 government participation in the production of coal has been decreasing, and there has been a shift to private domestic and foreign investors. Major changes have occurred in the institutional framework of the coal industry in recent years. In particular, in 2000 the government sold the stakes that Colombia Coal ([Carbocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbocol?action=edit\&redlink=1)), a state-owned company, had in Cerrejón, and the new mining code introduced in 2001 led the government to concentrate on its role as regulator through the [Ministry of Mines and Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Mines_and_Energy_\(Colombia\)).[^cite_note-sv-3]

In 2020, Colombia had 5 trillion short tons of proved coal reserves, mostly [bituminous coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bituminous_coal), making it the second-largest amount in [South America](26769.md) behind [Brazil](3383.md). Colombia produced 54 million short tons (MMst) of coal in 2020, with 9 MMst consumed domestically. However, coal production fell by 42% in 2020 due to the [COVID-19 pandemic](62750956.md) and a 91-day strike at a major mine in the north of the country. Most of the coal produced in Colombia is exported, making it the fourth-largest coal exporter in the world in 2020, after [Australia](4689264.md), [Indonesia](14579.md), and [Russia](25391.md). Coal was the country's second-largest export commodity by value, after oil and [petroleum products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_product). Colombia exported 75 MMst of coal in 2020, primarily to [Europe](9239.md) and [Latin America](18524.md). In the same year, Colombia was the largest source of U.S. coal imports, accounting for 71% of total U.S. coal imports, or about 6.1 MMst.[^cite_note-\:1-14]

In 2021, Colombia was South America’s largest coal producer and the second-largest producer of petroleum and other liquids, after Brazil. The country is also a significant oil exporter; in 2021, it was the fifth-largest crude oil exporter to the [United States](3434750.md). Despite being a major coal producer, Colombia uses very little coal domestically, relying mainly on [hydropower](14073.md) for its electricity needs and exporting most of its coal production.[^cite_note-\:1-14]

### Nickel

The [Cerro Matoso nickel mine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerro_Matoso_mine), located in [Montelíbano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montel%C3%ADbano), [Córdoba](549670.md) in northern Colombia, combines a [lateritic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lateritic) nickel [ore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ore) deposit with a low cost [ferronickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferronickel) smelter. It produces an average of 52,000 tons of nickel/year, which places this mine in the second place of nickel producers worldwide.[^cite_note-21] Cerromatoso is currently owned by [BHP](200825.md). Disagreement among the direction and the [trade union](17626.md) workers, with frequent [strikes](155134.md) produced heavy losses during 2008[^cite_note-22]

### Halite



Halite hand-carved [Salt Cathedral of Zipaquirá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_Cathedral_of_Zipaquir%C3%A1)

See also: [Nemocón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nemoc%C3%B3n) and [Zipaquirá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zipaquir%C3%A1)

[Halite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halite) was explored by the [Precolumbian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precolumbian) cultures such as the [Muisca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_people), as an important trade product.[^cite_note-23] Early halite mining is dated about 5th century BC.[^cite_note-24] The traditional halite mining was described by [Alexander von Humboldt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_von_Humboldt) during his visit to [Zipaquira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zipaquira) in 1801.[^cite_note-25] Nowadays, the Zipaquirá halite mine contains the [Salt Cathedral of Zipaquirá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_Cathedral_of_Zipaquir%C3%A1), entirely hand-carved in halite, including the [religious icons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_icon), ornaments and architectural details; the Salt Park and the national [mineralogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineralogy) [museum](37585.md).

## Relevance

The Colombian mining industry remains as one of the most dynamic and promising sectors of the [Colombian economy](5839.md), in just one year the investment has reached record figures in excess of two billion dollars and the trend in the short term is not reversed. The mining industry contributes with the economic growth and social development and the development of the regions where the activity is legally established. In addition this demonstrates that the contribution in the social and environmental component is higher than the industry average.[^cite_note-29]

Government efforts to expand mining in Colombia were needed to encourage private sector investment. In the late 1980s, much of Colombia remained inadequately charted, and reserve estimates were considered only marginally reliable. The government set a policy of developing infrastructure (roads, electricity, and communications), providing technical assistance, and encouraging sound credit and legal policies to minimize problems with land titling. Through joint ventures and the promotion of small mining companies, government officials believed that the mining sector could contribute more to national employment, income, and wealth.

## Human rights and crime

According to a 2013 [U.S. Department of Labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Department_of_Labor) report on the worst forms of [child labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_labor) and labor conditions around the world, the Colombian mining industry employs underage children. The report indicated that Colombia's industrial sector employed 20% of the working children who were aged 5 to 14 years old.[^cite_note-30] However, and despite the government's participation in a "4-year, \$9 million project to combat child labor and improve workplace health and safety in mining", children continue to engage in child labor. In fact, the [Bureau of International Labor Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_of_International_Labor_Affairs) issued a List of Goods Produced by Child Labor or Forced Labor in December 2014 where Colombia was mentioned for its use of underage children in brick, coal, gold and emerald mining.[^cite_note-31]

Mining [infrastructure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrastructure) is a common target of [terrorist attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrorist_attack), specially the oil and gas [pipelines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipeline_transport), mainly by the [Farc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farc) and [ELN](822032.md) [guerrillas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guerrilla). The mining companies have been implicated in [extortion](162449.md) payments to [guerrillas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guerrillas) in exchange for access to mining locations.[^cite_note-32] The [Caño Limón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ca%C3%B1o_Lim%C3%B3n)–[Coveñas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cove%C3%B1as) pipeline, which stretches 780 km from the Caño Limón to the Atlantic port of Coveñas, has come under heavy attack, including 170 attacks in 2002 alone, The pipeline remained out of operation for 266 days of that year and the government estimates that these bombings reduced the [GDP](12594.md) of Colombia by 0.5%.[^cite_note-33] The bombings, which have occurred on average once every 5 days, have caused substantial environmental damage, often in fragile [rainforests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainforests) and jungles.[^cite_note-34]

On October 14, 1998, a pipeline exploded because of bombs placed by ELN guerrillas. The burning oil spread fire across the Machuca village near [Segovia, Antioquia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Segovia,_Antioquia). 85 peasants died and over 30 were injured.[^cite_note-35] On the morning of December 13, 1998, after two days of combat between the army protecting the pipelines and the FARC, a Colombian Air Force helicopter carried out an air attack against guerrillas near the village of Santo Domingo, including the use of cluster-bombs. After the bombing was over, the bodies of seventeen civilians were found in Santo Domingo, including seven children. The case was subsequently handed over to a Colombian military court, with convictions of 31 years of prison for the accused. The decision called for the case to be judged in civil court and for comprehensive reparations to the victims.[^cite_note-36]
