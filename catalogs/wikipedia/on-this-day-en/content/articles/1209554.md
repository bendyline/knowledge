**Russian oligarchs** ([Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language): олигархи, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Russian): *oligarkhi*) are [business oligarchs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_oligarch) from the [former Soviet republics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-Soviet_states) who rapidly accumulated wealth in the 1990s through the [Russian privatisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privatization_in_Russia) that followed the [dissolution of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union). The [collapsing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Failed_state) Soviet state left the ownership of [state assets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_property) contested, which permitted [informal deals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blat_\(favors\)) with former [Soviet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) officials as a means of acquiring state property. The [Russian Union of Industrialists and Entrepreneurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Union_of_Industrialists_and_Entrepreneurs) has been nicknamed the "oligarch trade union".[^cite_note-1]

The Russian oligarchs first emerged as entrepreneurs under [Mikhail Gorbachev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Gorbachev) ([General Secretary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Secretary_of_the_Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union), 1985–1991), exploiting various loopholes during the economic liberalisation of his [perestroika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perestroika) programme.[^cite_note-2] [Boris Berezovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boris_Berezovsky_\(businessman\)), a mathematician and former researcher, became the first widely recognised Russian business oligarch.[^cite_note-3]

The oligarchs became increasingly influential in Russian politics during the presidency of [Boris Yeltsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boris_Yeltsin) (1991–1999), a period often referred to as *the wild nineties*; they helped finance his [re-election in 1996](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_Russian_presidential_election). Well-connected oligarchs such as [Roman Abramovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Abramovich), [Mikhail Khodorkovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Khodorkovsky), Boris Berezovsky and [Vladimir Potanin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Potanin) acquired key assets at a fraction of their value at the [loans for shares scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loans_for_shares_scheme) auctions conducted in the run-up to the election.[^cite_note-4] Defenders of the oligarchs who later fell out of favour have argued that the companies they acquired were not highly valued at the time because they continued to operate on Soviet principles, with non-existent [stock control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock_control), huge payrolls, no financial reporting and scant regard for profit.[^cite_note-Aslund_1995-5]

Since 2014, hundreds of Russian oligarchs and their companies have been subject to [US sanctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countering_America's_Adversaries_Through_Sanctions_Act) for their support of "the Russian government's malign activity around the globe".[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7] In 2022, many Russian oligarchs and their close family members were targeted and sanctioned by countries around the world in response to [Russia's war in Ukraine](70149799.md).[^cite_note-8]

## Yeltsin era, 1991–1999

During [Mikhail Gorbachev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Gorbachev)'s [perestroika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perestroika) period (c. 1985–1991), Soviet economic restructuring allowed limited private enterprise, enabling many entrepreneurs in [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Soviet_Federative_Socialist_Republic) to import high-demand goods such as personal computers, electronics, and clothing (e.g. jeans). These goods, scarce in the Soviet market, were sold at significant profits, laying the groundwork for the rise of a new business class.

Following the [dissolution of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union) in 1991, [Boris Yeltsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boris_Yeltsin) became [President of Russia](239646.md) in July 1991, the oligarchs emerged as well-connected entrepreneurs who started from nearly nothing and became rich through participation in the market via connections to the corrupt, but elected, [government of Russia](9787373.md) during the state's transition to a [market-based economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market-based_economy). The so-called [voucher privatization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voucher_privatization) program of 1992–1994 enabled a handful of young men to become billionaires, specifically by [arbitraging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arbitrage) the vast difference between old domestic prices for Russian commodities (such as natural gas and oil) and the prices prevailing on the world market. These oligarchs became unpopular with the Russian public and are often blamed for the turmoil that plagued the [Russian Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Federation) following the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991.[^cite_note-piie.com-9][^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11][^cite_note-Granville-12]

### Emergence

Economists [Sergei Guriev](24184605.md) and Andrei Rachinsky contrast older oligarchs with *[nomenklatura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomenklatura)* ties and younger-generation entrepreneurs such as [Kakha Bendukidze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kakha_Bendukidze) who built their wealth from scratch because Gorbachev's reforms affected a period "when co-existence of regulated and [quasi-market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-market) prices created huge opportunities for [arbitrage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arbitrage)."[^cite_note-\:0-13]

The majority of oligarchs were promoted (at least initially) by the Soviet *[apparatchiks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apparatchik)*, with strong connections to Soviet power-structures and access to the funds of the [Communist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Communist_party).[^cite_note-Aslund_1995-5][^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15] [Boris Berezovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boris_Berezovsky_\(businessman\)) himself was Head of the Department of System Design at another [Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Sciences_of_the_Soviet_Union) research centre. His private company was established by the Institute as a [joint venture](538782.md).[^cite_note-16] [Mikhail Khodorkovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Khodorkovsky) started his business importing computers under auspices of the [Komsomol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Komsomol)-authorised [Center for Scientific and Technical Creativity of the Youth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_for_Scientific_and_Technical_Creativity_of_the_Youth) in 1986, briefly serving as a deputy secretary of the Komsomol for a district in Moscow in 1987. His move into banking two years later was funded with the support of Komsomol alumni working in [Moscow city government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_City_Government). Later, he served in the Russian government as an adviser to the prime minister and a deputy minister of fuel and power while still running his business.[^cite_note-gustafson99capitalism-17] [Vladimir Vinogradov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Vinogradov) was the chief economist of [Promstroybank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Promstroybank_\(USSR\)), one of the six [banks existing in the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banking_in_the_Soviet_Union),[^cite_note-18] previously serving as the secretary of [Atommash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atommash) plant Komsomol organisation.[^cite_note-gustafson99capitalism-17]

### Political support

Economist [Yegor Gaidar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yegor_Gaidar) worked in a [Soviet Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Academy_of_Sciences) think tank modelled after [RAND](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAND).[^cite_note-19] Gaidar later became the economics editor of the *[Kommunist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kommunist)* journal, the official theoretical organ of the [CC of the CPSU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CC_of_the_CPSU). He also held various positions, including Prime Minister in the Russian government during 1991–1992.[^cite_note-20] Together with [Anatoly Chubais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatoly_Chubais), the two "Young Reformers" were chiefly responsible for privatization in the early 1990s.[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-22][^cite_note-23] According to [David Satter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Satter), "what drove the process was not the determination to create a system based on [universal values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_value) but rather the will to introduce a system of private ownership, which, in the absence of law, opened the way for the criminal pursuit of money and power".[^cite_note-24][^cite_note-Aslund_1995-5]

The [1998 Russian financial crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1998_Russian_financial_crisis) hit most oligarchs hard and those whose holdings were based mainly in [banking](9608840.md) lost much of their fortunes. The most influential oligarchs from the Yeltsin era include[^cite_note-piie.com-9] [Roman Abramovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Abramovich), [Boris Berezovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boris_Berezovsky_\(businessman\)), [Vladimir Gusinsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Gusinsky), [Mikhail Khodorkovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Khodorkovsky), [Vladimir Potanin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Potanin), [Alexander Smolensky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Smolensky) and [Vladimir Vinogradov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Vinogradov).[^cite_note-Aslund_1995-5] They formed what became known as the *[Semibankirschina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semibankirschina)* (or "seven-banker outfit", compare [Seven Boyars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Boyars)), a group of businessmen with a great influence on Boris Yeltsin and his political environment. Together they controlled from 50% to 70% of all Russian finances between 1996 and 2000. Historian [Edward L. Keenan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_L._Keenan) has compared these oligarchs to the system of powerful [boyars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boyar#Boyars_in_Muscovy) that emerged in late-medieval [Muscovy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Duchy_of_Moscow).[^cite_note-A_Normal_Country-25] *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)* reported in 2008 that "'oligarchs' from the era of former president Boris Yeltsin have been purged by the Kremlin".[^cite_note-26]

## Putin and Medvedev era, 1999–2022 (prior to the invasion of Ukraine)

Further information: [Russia under Vladimir Putin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia_under_Vladimir_Putin) and [Ozero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozero)



Putin (left), with [Mikhail Khodorkovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Khodorkovsky) (right) in December 2002. [Mikhail Khodorkovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Khodorkovsky) was jailed the following year.

With the ascent of [Vladimir Putin](32817.md) in the Kremlin the influence of the Yeltsin oligarchs dissipated, as some were imprisoned, such as [Mikhail Khodorkovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Khodorkovsky) (pictured here) and [Mikhael Mirilashvili](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhael_Mirilashvili), while others emigrated, sold off their assets or died under suspicious circumstances, such as [Vladimir Vinogradov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Vinogradov) and [Boris Berezovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boris_Berezovsky_\(businessman\)). A number of Yeltsin oligarchs first came under fire for alleged [tax evasion](2256795.md).[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28] [Vladimir Gusinsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Gusinsky) of MediaMost and [Boris Berezovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boris_Berezovsky_\(businessman\)) both avoided legal proceedings by leaving Russia, and the most prominent, [Mikhail Khodorkovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Khodorkovsky) of [Yukos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yukos) [oil](23195.md), was arrested in October 2003 and sentenced to 9 years. This was subsequently extended to 14 years, and after Putin pardoned him, he was released on 20 December 2013.[^cite_note-YukosCase-29]

A second wave of oligarchs emerged in the 2000s, friends and former colleagues of President Putin either from his years in the St Petersburg municipal administration or his Dresden tenure in the KGB. Examples are the director of the institute where Putin obtained a degree in 1996, [Vladimir Litvinenko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Litvinenko),[^cite_note-Bureau-30] and Putin's childhood friend and [judo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judo)-teacher [Arkady Rotenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arkady_Rotenberg).[^cite_note-31] [Gennady Timchenko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gennady_Timchenko) was close friends with Russian leader [Vladimir Putin](32817.md) since the early 1980s.[^cite_note-\:1-32][^cite_note-33] In 1991, Putin gave Timchenko an oil export license.[^cite_note-\:0-13] These oligarchs worked in close cooperation with the government, displacing a system of crony capitalism with a system of state capitalism whereby the new oligarchs benefited from financing by state-owned banks and access to public procurement projects.[^cite_note-Russia’s_Economy_under_Putin:_From_Crony_Capitalism_to_State_Capitalism-34]



[Gennady Timchenko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gennady_Timchenko) and [Arkady Rotenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arkady_Rotenberg) in 2015

An economic study distinguished 21 oligarchic groups as of 2003.[^cite_note-\:0-13] Between 2000 and 2004, Putin apparently engaged in a power struggle with some oligarchs, reaching a "grand bargain" with them. This bargain allowed the oligarchs to maintain their powers, in exchange for their explicit support of – and alignment with – Putin's government.[^cite_note-35][^cite_note-36] However, other analysts argue that the oligarchic structure has remained intact under Putin, with Putin devoting much of his time to mediating power-disputes between rival oligarchs.[^cite_note-Russia's_Oligarchy,_Alive_and_Well-37]

### Prominent oligarchs

Main article: [List of Russian nationals named in the CAATSA unclassified report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Russian_nationals_named_in_the_CAATSA_unclassified_report)

The ten most-prominent oligarchs of the early Putin era were [Roman Abramovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Abramovich), [Oleg Deripaska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oleg_Deripaska), [Mikhail Prokhorov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Prokhorov), [Alisher Usmanov](13020970.md), [Viktor Vekselberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viktor_Vekselberg), [Leonid Mikhelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonid_Mikhelson), [Arkady Rotenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arkady_Rotenberg), [Gennady Timchenko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gennady_Timchenko), [Andrey Guryev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrey_Guryev) and [Vitaly Malkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitaly_Malkin).[^cite_note-38] In 2004, five years after Putin's ascent to power, *[Forbes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forbes)* listed 36 billionaires of Russian citizenship, with a note: "this list includes businessmen of Russian citizenship who acquired the major share of their wealth privately, while not holding a governmental position". In 2005, the number of billionaires dropped to 30, mostly because of the [Yukos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yukos) case, with Khodorkovsky dropping from No. 1 (US\$15.2 billion) to No. 21 (US\$2.0 billion). A 2013 report by Credit Suisse found that 35% of the wealth of Russia was owned by the wealthiest 110 individuals.[^cite_note-39][^cite_note-Russia’s_Economy_under_Putin:_From_Crony_Capitalism_to_State_Capitalism-34]

Daniel Treisman proposed using a term "silovarch" ([silovik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silovik) and oligarch) for a new class of Russian oligarchs with backgrounds in Russian military and intelligence.[^cite_note-40]

On 30 January 2018, the [U.S. Treasury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Treasury) published a "list of oligarchs" as part of a document known as the "[Putin list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Russian_nationals_named_in_the_CAATSA_unclassified_report)" which was compiled under the requirement of the [CAATSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CAATSA) Act.[^cite_note-OligarchsListVOA30012018-41][^cite_note-CNN2018-42] According to the document itself, its criterion for inclusion was simply being a Russian national with a net worth of over \$1 billion.[^cite_note-CAATSA-report-43] The list was criticised for being indiscriminate, and including critics of Putin.[^cite_note-reuterslist-44]

### 2008 global recession and credit crisis

According to the financial news-agency [Bloomberg L.P.](432006.md), Russia's wealthiest 25 individuals collectively lost US\$230 billion (£146 billion) from July 2008 to July 2017.[^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46][^cite_note-47] The fall in the oligarchs' wealth relates closely to the meltdown in Russia's stock market, as by 2008 the [RTS Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RTS_Index) had lost 71% of its value due to the [capital flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_flight) after the [Russo-Georgian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Georgian_War) of August 2008.[^cite_note-48]



Putin (left) with [Oleg Deripaska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oleg_Deripaska) (right) in the Kremlin in March 2002

Billionaires in Russia were particularly hard-hit by lenders seeking repayment on [balloon loans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balloon_loan) to shore up their own balance sheets. Many oligarchs took out generous loans from Russian banks, bought shares, and then took out more loans from western banks against the value of these shares.[^cite_note-guardian.co.uk-49][^cite_note-forbes.com-50] One of the first to get hit by the global downturn was [Oleg Deripaska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oleg_Deripaska), Russia's richest man at the time, who had a net worth of US\$28 billion in March 2008. As Deripaska borrowed money from western banks using shares in his companies as collateral, the collapse in share price forced him to sell holdings to satisfy the [margin calls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margin_call).[^cite_note-guardian.co.uk-49][^cite_note-forbes.com-50]

## Putin era, 2022 invasion of Ukraine and international sanctions

After the [2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine](79400268.md), Canadian, US, European, and Japanese leaders took unprecedented steps to sanction Putin and the oligarchs directly.[^cite_note-CNBC-51] In response to the [sanctions](70323386.md), the targeted oligarchs started to hide wealth in an attempt to prevent the Western nations from freezing their assets.[^cite_note-52] These sanctions intend to directly impact the Russian ruling class as a response for their perceived contribution and acquiescence to the war with Ukraine. Although the sanctions miss some of the richest oligarchs, the impact on the war is unknown due to Putin's power over those that were sanctioned.[^cite_note-53] Since the invasion began, nine of the Russian oligarchs' yachts have turned their [navigation transponders](1825702.md) off as they sail to ports where they are less likely to be searched and seized.[^cite_note-CNBC-51]



Putin (left), with [Petr Fradkov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petr_Fradkov) (right) in the Kremlin in May 2019

On March 2, 2022, the United States announced a special task force dubbed "[Task Force KleptoCapture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Task_Force_KleptoCapture)". This team was put together to specifically target oligarchs. It is made up of officials from the [FBI](11127.md), [Marshals Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Marshals_Service), [IRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_Revenue_Service), [Postal Inspection Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Postal_Inspection_Service), [Homeland Security Investigations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeland_Security_Investigations) and [Secret Service](58221.md). The main goal of the task force is to impose the sanctions set against these individuals to freeze and seize the assets that the US government claimed were proceeds of their illegal involvement with the Russian government and the invasion of Ukraine.[^cite_note-54] On March 21, 2022, the [Organized Crime and Corruption Reporting Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organized_Crime_and_Corruption_Reporting_Project) launched [Russian Asset Tracker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Asset_Tracker) to showcase the profiles and assets of several Russian oligarchs.[^cite_note-55]

Several dozen business people with family connections to top politicians include President Putin's younger daughter [Katerina Tikhonova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katerina_Tikhonova), who through her investment fund has been the recipient of numerous large contracts from state-owned energy companies.[^cite_note-56] Her former husband [Kirill Shamalov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirill_Shamalov) runs the largest Russian petrochemicals company Sibur as well as his own investment fund.[^cite_note-57]

The son-in-law of Foreign Minister [Sergei Lavrov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergei_Lavrov) runs an investment fund with assets exceeding \$6 billion. Andrey Ryumin,[^cite_note-58] the son-in-law of [Viktor Medvedchuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viktor_Medvedchuk), President Putin's former closest ally in Ukraine, runs another investment fund with large agricultural holdings which have become recipients of state subsidies for import substitution (Rouhandeh 2022). [Petr Fradkov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petr_Fradkov), the son of a former Prime Minister and head of the Russian foreign intelligence service, Sergei Sergeevich Ivanov, the son of the former head of the presidential administration [Sergei Ivanov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergei_Ivanov), and Andrey Patrushev, the son of the current head of the Russian Security Council [Nikolai Patrushev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Patrushev) have all joined the ranks of the oligarchs.[^cite_note-59]

## Russian oligarchs in London

Main article: [Russian money in London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_money_in_London)

The British Government policy encouraged the flow of foreign capital into the United Kingdom, for example through the [foreign investor visa routes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_visa), introduced during [John Major](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Major)'s [premiership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premiership_of_John_Major) in 1994, one-fifth of whose recipients since 2008 are Russian citizens.[^cite_note-TheEconomist-98]

A significant number of Russian oligarchs have bought homes in upmarket sections of [London](17867.md),[^cite_note-99] which has been dubbed "Moscow on Thames" or "Londongrad".[^cite_note-100] Some, such as [Eugene Shvidler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Shvidler), [Alexander Knaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Knaster), [Konstantin Kagalovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konstantin_Kagalovsky), David Wilkowske and Abram Reznikov, are [expatriates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expatriate), having taken permanent residency in London. [Roman Abramovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Abramovich) bought 16 [Kensington Palace Gardens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kensington_Palace_Gardens) in London, a 15-bedroom mansion, for £120 million.[^cite_note-101] [Mikhail Fridman](354597.md) restored [Athlone House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athlone_House) in London as a primary residence in 2016.[^cite_note-102][^cite_note-103]

[Roman Abramovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Abramovich) bought the English football club [Chelsea F.C.](7473.md) in 2003, spending record amounts on players' salaries.[^cite_note-104] [Alexander Mamut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Mamut) invested £100m to [Waterstones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterstones) bookstore chain after acquiring it in 2011 for £53m. According to its managing director [James Daunt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Daunt), the intervention saved Waterstones, which managed to make its first annual profit since 2008 in 2016.[^cite_note-105] He remarked that continued Russian ownership would've been "catastrophic" for the chain in 2022.[^cite_note-106]

## Russian oligarchs in the Middle East

Dubai and Israel have been havens for oligarchs fleeing sanctions.[^cite_note-107][^cite_note-108][^cite_note-109]

The [UAE](69328.md) has become a prominent destination for relocation of yachts, real estate, and private banking services linked to sanctioned Russian elites. In May 2022, the 118-metre Motor Yacht A, owned by oligarch [Andrey Melnichenko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrey_Melnichenko_\(industrialist\)), was anchored in [Ras al-Khaimah](61770159.md), UAE, where it remained largely intact despite international sanctions.[^cite_note-110]

Russian purchases of UAE real estate also surged: Russians spent over US\$6.3 billion on [Dubai](211583.md) property in 2022 as non-resident buyers, many linked to high-net-worth individuals and oligarchs.[^cite_note-111]
