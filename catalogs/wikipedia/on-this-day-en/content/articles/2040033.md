# Alberta separatism

**Alberta separatism**, **Alberta independence**,[^cite_note-1] or **Alberta sovereignty**[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3] refers to a series of 20th- and 21st-century movements advocating the [secession](144732.md) of the province of [Alberta](717.md) from [Canada](5042916.md), with some groups also supporting the creation of a sovereign union with the other provinces of [Western Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Canada), and other groups supporting Alberta joining the [United States](3434750.md).

The main issues driving separatist sentiment have been a perceived power disparity relative to [Ottawa](22219.md) and other [western provinces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_provinces); a sense of distinctiveness with regard to Alberta's [cultural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Alberta) and [political identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics_of_Alberta); and Canadian economic policy, particularly as it pertains to [Alberta's petroleum industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Petroleum_industry_in_Alberta) and equalization payments. As such, Alberta separatism is an expression of [Western alienation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_alienation).

The concept of separation gained considerable media attention in the aftermath of the [2025 federal election](68483222.md). In 2025, the Alberta Forever Canada citizen initiative opposing Alberta's separation from Canada was launched and verified by [Elections Alberta](983952.md) pending its surpassing the required amount of signatures.[^cite_note-CBCNews_2025-12-01-4] In early 2026, Elections Alberta approved a petition which, pending legal challenges, intends to lead to [a separation referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Alberta_independence_referendum).[^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9]

On May 13, 2026, Alberta Justice Shaina Leonard ruled in favour of several [First Nations](1652747.md) whose lawyers argued that using Alberta's referendum process for possible separation of Alberta from Canada has been [unconstitutional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Canada) because First Nations were not consulted and that separation under these circumstances would violate treaty rights.[^cite_note-JackFarrell_2026-05-13-10] This issue of treaty rights arises because Alberta is within the territories of several of the [Numbered Treaties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numbered_Treaties) between First Nations and the [monarchy of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarchy_of_Canada) agreed to before Alberta became a province. On May 21, [Alberta Premier](470556.md) [Danielle Smith](23078986.md) responded to the ruling by announcing that Alberta will hold a referendum on whether to trigger the process for holding a binding referendum on Alberta separating from Canada.[^cite_note-Bellefontaine-11] The [independence referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Alberta_independence_referendum) was scheduled for October 19 alongside [nine other referendum questions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Alberta_referendums).[^cite_note-12]

Alberta's secession would be subject to the federal [Clarity Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarity_Act) and the Supreme Court's ruling in [Reference re Secession of Quebec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_re_Secession_of_Quebec), both of which require a clear referendum result followed by constitutional negotiations, not unilateral separation.[^cite_note-lexum_SCC_1998-13][^cite_note-Gilan_2023-14] Recent legal challenges have also emphasized that any separation process must comply with the [Constitution of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Canada) and uphold the treaty and constitutional rights of Indigenous peoples and other minorities.[^cite_note-JackFarrell_2026-05-13-10]

## History

### 1940s to 1970s: Early western alienation, discovery of oil, oil crises

The discovery of vast reserves of oil in the 1940s ushered in a twenty-year period of intense exploration, new discoveries, and rapid expansion of Alberta's oil industry.[^cite_note-auto-15] According to journalist Jen Gerson, however, Alberta was still "heavily rural and bitter with western grievance. Freight rates and protectionism made economic diversification in the prairies all but impossible. It was said to be cheaper to send cows to slaughterhouses or grain to mills in Ontario than it was to ship meat or flour."[^cite_note-National_Post-16]

According to political scientists David Elton and Roger Gibbins, a 1969 provincial poll found that only 5 per cent of those polled "expressed interest in even discussing the merits of separation."[^cite_note-\:0-17]

In the 1970s, the world experienced two major oil crises. The first, the [1973 oil crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_oil_crisis), coincided with the [Yom-Kippur War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yom-Kippur_War) that same year: the decision by the US to support Israel in the conflict caused retaliation by [Egypt](8087628.md) and [Syria](7515849.md), enacting an [oil embargo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_embargo) that resulted in oil prices spiking in North America.[^cite_note-18] The second oil crisis came [in 1979](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1979_oil_crisis), in the wake of the [Iranian Revolution](347268.md).[^cite_note-19] Some members of the Organization of Petroleum Exporting Countries ([OPEC](166346.md)) and a few similarly minded oil-rich nations had ceased all oil exports to the United States and countries that supported Israel. In both cases, the price of oil sold to North America spiked and service stations ran out of fuel; long lines were evident at [gas stations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_stations) across North America.

The Alberta government and the Canadian federal government responded politically to address oil reserves and conservation of petroleum resources. In 1971, the Alberta government, headed by [Harry Strom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Strom), created an environmental ministry, the first of its kind, with a mandate to manage and conserve Alberta's natural resources.[^cite_note-20] Federally, in 1974, the Office of Energy Conservation was created. Conflict arose between Alberta and Canada after the 1973 crisis, over the management and distribution of Alberta's oil resources, and financial wealth. Nevertheless, support for independence remained a fringe phenomenon. A 1974 survey conducted in Calgary found less than four per cent of respondents "expressed even the most cautious support for separatism"; three years later, 1977 survey by the *[Calgary Herald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calgary_Herald)* found that only 2.7 per cent of Albertans supported independence.[^cite_note-\:0-17]

### 1980–1984: Growth of western alienation



Prime Minister [Pierre Trudeau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Trudeau) and his federal [National Energy Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Energy_Program) (NEP) have been regarded as causing a rise of Alberta separatism in the early 1980s.



Alberta Premier [Peter Lougheed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Lougheed) and his government disputed with the Trudeau federal government over the NEP in the 1980s.



Long-term oil prices, 1861–2007 (top line adjusted for inflation)

Due to the international oil crises Alberta had experienced rapid economic growth in the oil sector because of high oil prices. In October 1980, the federal government led by Prime Minister [Pierre Trudeau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Trudeau) implemented the [National Energy Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Energy_Program) (NEP). The Trudeau government argued that Canadian ownership and control of natural resources were important to national energy security. Trudeau also argued that Canada's energy policy had become divisive and required greater fairness in revenue sharing.[^cite_note-Tupper_pg215-17-21] The program expanded domestic [price controls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price_controls), included revenue-sharing measures, incentives for oil exploration on federally owned lands,[^cite_note-Tupper_pg215-17-21] and tax on oil and gas revenues but excluded the oil sands.[^cite_note-boereport.com-22] Alberta Premier [Peter Lougheed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Lougheed) opposed these aspects of the program. He challenged the program in court and criticized it publicly, including in a televised address in which he warned that the NEP would bring more "Ottawa" into Alberta's affairs.[^cite_note-Tupper_pg215-17-21]

Lougheed threatened to reduce Alberta's oil and gas production if the federal government increased taxes on the industry. Reduced Alberta production would have required Central Canadian refineries and businesses to purchase foreign oil, which the federal government would have subsidized. Lougheed argued that the federal government could not afford that subsidy while running a \$13.7-billion deficit in 1980.[^cite_note-PerryCraig_pg534-535-23] He announced a 60,000-barrel reduction in Alberta's crude oil production over nine months beginning in April 1981. He also suspended two oil sands projects.[^cite_note-Tupper_pg215-17-21][^cite_note-PerryCraig_pg534-535-23][^cite_note-24] A 1981 [Canada West Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canada_West_Foundation) poll found that 49% of respondents agreed that "Western Canadians get so few benefits from being part of Canada that they might as well go it on their own."[^cite_note-\:0-17] In 1981, the *Oil Accord* removed parts of the NEP that Alberta opposed.[^cite_note-PerryCraig_pg534-535-23] Many Albertans viewed the NEP as federal intrusion into provincial affairs.[^cite_note-McKenzie_1981-25] Edmonton economist Alan Scarfe argued that many people in Western Canada, especially Alberta, viewed the NEP as a policy that benefited eastern provinces at Alberta's expense.[^cite_note-26]

Shortly after the NEP's implementation, the [1980s oil glut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980s_oil_glut) began. Energy prices fell sharply because of declining demand.[^cite_note-27] Lower prices contributed to a recession in Alberta, the abandonment of oil and gas projects, and high unemployment. Lower international prices also led Eastern Canada to purchase foreign oil, undermining the NEP's goal of self-sufficiency.

The NEP has been blamed for an infrastructure deficit, and reduced growth of the [Alberta Heritage Savings Trust Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberta_Heritage_Savings_Trust_Fund), which was meant to save as much of the earnings during high oil prices to act as a "rainy day" cushion if oil prices collapsed because of the oil and gas industry's cyclical nature.[^cite_note-National_Post-16] A popular slogan that appeared on [bumper stickers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bumper_stickers) was "Let the Eastern bastards freeze in the dark." Another sticker said, "I'd rather push this thing a mile than buy gas from [PetroCan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petro-Canada)."[^cite_note-abheritage-28][^cite_note-29]

In October 1980, when Trudeau dismissed the threat of Alberta separatism as a bargaining tool from Western Canadians premiers, Lougheed agreed, telling [Don Newman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Don_Newman_\(broadcaster\)) that no one in Western Canada wanted to separate and they were "part of the mainstream of Canadian life".[^cite_note-30] But some argued that Lougheed ran a hostile and polarizing campaign against federal oil and gas policy and forced Albertans to choose between provincial and national citizenship.[^cite_note-Tupper_pg215-17-21] Hostility toward the federal government contributed to Alberta separatism, which became more visible when separatist [Gordon Kesler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Kesler) was elected to the [Legislative Assembly of Alberta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislative_Assembly_of_Alberta) in a 1982 by-election.[^cite_note-Tupper_pg215-17-21] British Columbia lawyer [Doug Christie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doug_Christie_\(lawyer\)) formed the [Western Canada Concept](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Canada_Concept) to promote western separatism. The party drew 2,700 people to a speech at the Jubilee Auditorium in Edmonton.[^cite_note-31] In the [1982 provincial election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1982_Alberta_general_election), the Western Canada Concept received 111,131 votes (11.8% of the total vote), but did not elect any members.[^cite_note-32]

### 1984 to mid-2010s: Decline of separatism and rise of the Reform and Conservative Parties of Canada



In 1997, the [Supreme Court of Canada](123468.md) delivered unanimous reasons in '*[Reference Re Secession of Quebec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_Re_Secession_of_Quebec)* regarding the legality, under both Canadian and international law, of a unilateral [secession](144732.md) of Quebec from Canada.

Separatism and the Western Canada Concept's popularity declined after [Brian Mulroney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Mulroney) led the [Progressive Conservative Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_Conservative_Party_of_Canada) to victory in the [1984 federal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1984_Canadian_federal_election). Mulroney agreed to the *Western Accord on Energy*, which phased out the NEP over three years.[^cite_note-PerryCraig_pg534-535-23] But by the end of Mulroney's time in office, many Albertans viewed his government as neglectful of Western Canada.[^cite_note-33]

Two attempts at separatist political parties occurred in the early 2000s. The [Alberta Independence Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberta_Independence_Party) was founded before the [2001 Alberta general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001_Alberta_general_election), but did not gather sufficient signatures to register as a political party.[^cite_note-34] In the election it unsuccessfully ran 14 of its supporters as independent candidates, receiving approximately 7,500 votes.[^cite_note-TorontoStar_2019-03-04-35][^cite_note-36] In the [2004 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Alberta_general_election), the [Separation Party of Alberta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separation_Party_of_Alberta) nominated 12 candidates who won 4,680 votes (0.5% of provincial total), without electing a candidate.

There was significant opposition in Alberta to the [Kyoto Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto_Protocol), as it was believed to have bad effects on the provincial economy, which is based to a large degree on the oil and gas industry. (Alberta had the world's second largest proven reserves of oil, behind only Saudi Arabia.[^cite_note-37])

Naturalized Albertan [Stephen Harper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Harper) became Prime Minister of Canada in a [minority government](271434.md) in the [2006 federal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_Canadian_federal_election). Harper had been a significant figure in the Reform Party and led the [Canadian Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Alliance) from 2002 until its merger with the PCs. Due to Harper's Reform roots, Albertans believed he could be trusted to protect Alberta's interests. As a result, separatism sat on the sidelines, with uncertain prospects. Some pundits predicted that this result would cause support for separatism to ebb.

Public support for separation existed but remained limited during this period. In January 2004, Premier Ralph Klein told *[Reader's Digest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reader's_Digest)* that one in four Albertans supported separation. In August 2005, a poll commissioned by the *[Western Standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Standard)* found that 42% of Albertans and 35.6% of respondents across the four western provinces supported the statement "Western Canadians should begin to explore the idea of forming their own country."[^cite_note-38]

In 2000, Parliament passed the *[Clarity Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarity_Act)* at the urging of [Prime Minister](24135.md) [Jean Chrétien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Chr%C3%A9tien). The Act sets out the conditions under which the federal government may enter negotiations after a provincial referendum on secession. It requires a clear referendum question and a clear majority of eligible voters in favour of secession before negotiations can begin. The Act does not give a numerical threshold for a clear majority. Instead, the House of Commons decides whether the referendum question was clear and whether a clear majority voted to secede.

Support for Albertan separatism increased with the [Canadian federal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_Canadian_federal_election) victory of [Justin Trudeau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justin_Trudeau)'s Liberal Party on October 19, 2015.[^cite_note-39][^cite_note-Vice_News-40][^cite_note-GersonInterview-41] At a town hall in [Peterborough, Ontario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peterborough,_Ontario), on January 13, 2017, Trudeau said, "We can't shut down the oil sands tomorrow. We need to phase them out. We need to manage the transition off of our dependence on fossil fuels. That is going to take time."[^cite_note-42] At a hockey game in Edmonton the next day, Trudeau was loudly booed by the crowd.[^cite_note-43] His unpopularity in Alberta was a significant rallying point for separatists. The topic of Alberta separating from Canada was the subject of a number of mainstream media reports.[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-Vice_News-40][^cite_note-GersonInterview-41]

In 2015, geopolitical analyst [Peter Zeihan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Zeihan) said: "Right now, every man, woman and child in Alberta pays \$6,000 more into the national budget than they get back. Alberta is the only province that is a net contributor to that budget — by 2020, the number will exceed \$20,000 per person, \$40,000 per taxpayer. That will be the greatest wealth transfer in per capita terms in the Western world."[^cite_note-GersonInterview-41] Per [Statistics Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistics_Canada), in 2015 Alberta paid \$27 billion more into the federal treasury than it received in services.[^cite_note-45] Zeihan also said: "Alberta as an independent country doesn’t solve a huge number of problems. If it left Canada, its currency goes through the roof because all it has is oil exports, and that would drive agriculture out of business. It would be a one-horse economy in a very short time \[...] Seceding to the U.S. becomes the only political and economic option. If you do that, the inflation issue goes away, the tax problem goes away, the security problem goes away."[^cite_note-GersonInterview-41] In 2026, Zeihan said Alberta would be "screwed" if it became an independent country because oil and grain are "US dollar denominated commodities \[...] exporting for hard currency, you have an inflationary environment very, very, very quickly \[...] it would be difficult to survive in a country that now has a very, very limited labor pool".[^cite_note-Zeihan1-46]

A September 2018 [Ipsos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ipsos) poll found that 62% of Albertans believed Alberta "does not get its fair share from Confederation" (up from 45% in 1997), 46% felt "more attached to their province than to their country" (up from 39% in 1997), 34% "feel less committed to Canada than I did a few years ago" (up from 22% in 1997), 18% believed "the views of western Canadians are adequately represented in Ottawa" (down from 22% in 2001), and 25% believed "My province would be better off if it separated from Canada" (up from 19% in 2001).[^cite_note-47][^cite_note-48]

A February 2019 [Angus Reid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angus_Reid_\(market_research\)) poll found 50% of Albertans would support secession from Canada but also found the likelihood of that "remote".[^cite_note-49]

After Trudeau's Liberals were reelected with a minority government in the [federal election on October 21, 2019](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Canadian_federal_election), #Wexit (a [wordplay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wordplay) on "[Brexit](41688778.md)", the United Kingdom's departure from the [European Union](9317.md)) trended on social media.[^cite_note-\:5-50] On November 4, the separatist group "Wexit Alberta" applied for federal political party status.[^cite_note-51] On November 6, an Ipsos poll showed historically high interest in secession in both Alberta and [Saskatchewan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saskatchewan), at 33% and 27%, respectively.[^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53] On January 12, 2020, [Wexit Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wexit_Canada) was granted eligibility for the next federal election.[^cite_note-54]

A May 2020 poll by Northwest Research for [Derek Fildebrandt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derek_Fildebrandt)'s *[Western Standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Standard)* found that 41% of respondents would support independence in a referendum, 50% would be opposed, and 9% were not sure.[^cite_note-55] Removing undecideds, 45% would support and 55% would be opposed. Respondents were also asked if they would support a referendum if "the federal government is unwilling to negotiate with Alberta on a new constitutional arrangement": 48% said yes, while 52% said no. Support for independence was higher outside of Alberta's two biggest cities, with Edmonton being the most opposed.

In May 2025, supporters of Alberta independence or greater separation from Canada cited economic and political reasons as their main motivations, including resource and energy management, taxation, and federal policy.[^cite_note-56] Modern Alberta separatism also differs from the [Quebec sovereignty movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quebec_sovereignty_movement) in its partisan base. In Alberta, support comes primarily from the political right, while Quebec sovereignty has drawn support from both the left and the right. According to an [Angus Reid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angus_Reid_Institute) poll, 65% of [United Conservative Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Conservative_Party) voters would vote for separation, while 97% of [Alberta NDP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Democratic_Party_of_Alberta) voters would vote to stay in Canada. Many Albertans also said they would stop supporting separation if the federal government adopted more oil-and-gas-friendly policies, such as building pipelines or repealing energy regulations. This suggests that support for Alberta separation is primarily economic, unlike the Quebec sovereignty movement.[^cite_note-57]

In the lead-up to the [2025 federal election](68483222.md), some Alberta politicians and activists said that another Liberal Party victory after 10 years of government would increase support for secession. The rise in support for the Liberal Party was partly a response to US President [Donald Trump](4848272.md)'s call for [Canada to be annexed by the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Movements_for_the_United_States_annexation_of_Canada#Proposals_to_annex_Canada_by_Donald_Trump). Most Canadians opposed annexation, but support was higher in Alberta than in other provinces. Some Alberta residents cited stronger cultural and economic connections with the US than with [Eastern Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Canada).[^cite_note-58]

Following the election of a minority Liberal government, Alberta's government amended the *Citizen Initiative Act* to make it easier for citizens to initiate referendums, including on provincial separation from Canada. The amendments lowered the participation threshold from 20% of eligible voters to 10% of votes cast in the previous election. They also extended the signature-gathering period for petitions from 90 days to 120.[^cite_note-Johnson_20250515-59][^cite_note-Kanwar_20250623-60][^cite_note-61] Indigenous leaders criticized the amendments and raised concerns that a separation referendum could threaten existing treaty rights.[^cite_note-Johnson_20250515-59] The government introduced late-stage amendments to clarify that no referendum question would be permitted to jeopardize those treaty rights.[^cite_note-Johnson_20250515-59]



Premier [Danielle Smith](23078986.md) announced [a referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Alberta_independence_referendum) on whether Alberta should trigger the process for a binding referendum to separate from Canada.

In May 2025, Alberta Premier [Danielle Smith](23078986.md) announced that she would hold a referendum on provincial separation in 2026 if citizens gathered the required signatures.[^cite_note-62] In June 2025, [Alberta held three provincial by-elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Alberta_provincial_by-elections). The separatist [Republican Party of Alberta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_Party_of_Alberta) received 0.67% and 3.42% of the vote in two Edmonton by-elections, and 17.66% in [Olds-Didsbury-Three Hills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olds-Didsbury-Three_Hills).[^cite_note-63]

In June 2025, the separatist Alberta Prosperity Project, led by Mitch Sylvestre, submitted a referendum petition question: "Do you agree that the province of Alberta shall become a sovereign country and cease to be a province in Canada?"[^cite_note-albertaprosperityproject_20250705-64][^cite_note-Gonzalez_20250815-65] Alberta Chief Electoral Officer Gordon McClure referred the question to the [Court of King's Bench of Alberta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_King's_Bench_of_Alberta) on constitutional grounds. On December 5, 2025, Justice Colin C. J. Feasby ruled that the referendum proposal violated the Constitution of Canada.[^cite_note-66] The day before the judgement was released, the government tabled a bill amending the *Citizen Initiative Act* terminating ongoing court proceedings and removing the requirement that referendum proposals be constitutional.[^cite_note-67]



Former MLA [Thomas Lukaszuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Lukaszuk) led the Forever Canada petition, opposing Alberta's separation from Canada.

Two competing referendum petitions on Alberta sovereignty followed. In August 2025, [Thomas Lukaszuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Lukaszuk) launched Alberta Forever Canada, a citizen initiative opposing Alberta's separation from Canada. The proposed question asked, "Do you agree that Alberta should remain in Canada?" Lukaszuk's application cited concerns about the economic, citizenship, and treaty-rights consequences of Alberta separation. It also said that only a minority of Albertans supported independence.[^cite_note-MacCuish_20250730-68][^cite_note-Forever_Canadian-69][^cite_note-CIP_ElectionsAB_20250605-70] Elections Alberta validated Alberta Forever Canada after it received 404,293 signatures. The government then referred the proposal to a legislative committee.[^cite_note-CBCNews_2025-12-01-4][^cite_note-71]

[Stay Free Alberta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stay_Free_Alberta) launched a second petition in support of separation. The group said it collected more than 301,000 signatures between January 3 and May 2, 2026, though this number has been disputed.[^cite_note-72][^cite_note-73][^cite_note-74][^cite_note-75] Its proposed question asked, "Do you agree that the province of Alberta shall become a sovereign country and cease to be a province in Canada?" [Sturgeon Lake Cree Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sturgeon_Lake_Cree_Nation), [Athabasca Chipewyan First Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athabasca_Chipewyan_First_Nation), and the [Blackfoot Confederacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackfoot_Confederacy) challenged the petition. On May 13, Justice Shaina Leonard ruled that Elections Alberta could not certify it.[^cite_note-auto1-76][^cite_note-77] Later that day, Smith said the government would appeal the decision.[^cite_note-auto1-76]

On October 6, 2025, Smith gave Prime Minister [Mark Carney](13571049.md) until November 16 to amend nine federal laws that her government opposed, leading some to speculate that her government might pursue separatism if talks fail.[^cite_note-78]

On April 20, 2026, Elections Alberta revealed that it believed that the APP violated third-party advertising laws by paying for public messaging over the province's \$1,000 limit for non-registered groups and filed an injunction in March against them.[^cite_note-79] On April 30, the Republican Party of Alberta along with the Centurion Project were implicated in a large privacy breach for distributing millions of Albertans' private information. The list was given legally to the Republican Party of Alberta but ended up hosted by the Centurion Project on a public-facing website.[^cite_note-80] Elections Alberta went to court and was granted an injunction that database be taken down.[^cite_note-\:12-81] The [Alberta RCMP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RCMP) launched an investigation collaborating with other law enforcement partners in the province to determine if any other laws were broken.[^cite_note-\:12-81][^cite_note-82]

On May 13, Justice Leonard ruled in favour of several First Nations whose lawyers argued that using Alberta's referendum process for possible separation of Alberta from Canada has been unconstitutional because First Nations were not consulted and that separation under these circumstances would violate treaty rights.[^cite_note-JackFarrell_2026-05-13-10] Neil Dobson, the lawyer representing Alberta, said it was premature for the Alberta government to consult First Nations because it was not yet taking any action. Of a political discussion with First Nations over possible separation, he said, "The collection of signatures and the ability to put forward the petition in the first place is really the commencement of that political discussion".[^cite_note-JackFarrell_2026-05-13-10] Jeffrey Rath of Stay Free Alberta said the organization would file an appeal.[^cite_note-JackFarrell_2026-05-13-10] On May 21, Premier Smith announced that Alberta will [hold a referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Alberta_independence_referendum) on whether to trigger the process for holding a binding referendum on Alberta separating from Canada.[^cite_note-Bellefontaine-11]

## External support

### Quebec

On September 3, 2025, [Parti Québécois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parti_Qu%C3%A9b%C3%A9cois) leader [Paul St-Pierre Plamondon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_St-Pierre_Plamondon) expressed support for a future referendum on Alberta separatism, adding that Quebec and Alberta should "respect each other's desires for self-determination".[^cite_note-95] Plamondon later revealed in January 2026 that he met with the leaders of the Alberta Prosperity Project in September, established a positive relationship with them, and would be open to assisting them in establishing a referendum.[^cite_note-96]

### United States

Since 2025, Alberta's independence movement has benefited from support and encouragement from the United States under the second Trump administration.

Between April 2025 and January 2026, the Alberta Prosperity Project met three times with [United States Department of State](31975.md) officials. A joint meeting with the US State Department and [United States Department of the Treasury](53667.md) was planned for February 2026 for a half trillion credit mechanism upon achieving independence. Officials for the State Department and White House said these were routine meetings with civil society groups without commitments. In January 2026, US Treasury Secretary [Scott Bessent](38532044.md) called Albertans "a very independent people" and said Alberta was a natural US partner.[^cite_note-97] Canadian Prime Minister [Mark Carney](13571049.md) and Alberta Premier [Danielle Smith](23078986.md) said the US should respect Canadian sovereignty. Carney said they "expect the US administration to respect Canadian sovereignty – I'm always clear with President Trump to that effect". Smith said she expected US officials to leave discussions about the province's "democratic process" to Albertans and Canadians. A spokesperson for Smith said, "the overwhelming majority of Albertans are not interested in becoming a US state."[^cite_note-98][^cite_note-\:2-99] Ontario Premier [Doug Ford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doug_Ford) called on Smith to take a stand and declare that "enough is enough." He called the meetings "unethical" and "unacceptable".[^cite_note-::3-100][^cite_note-101] Jeffrey Rath, legal counsel for the Alberta Prosperity Project, said the group believed the US was "extremely enthusiastic" about an independent Alberta, claiming that its representatives had met with senior US officials who proceeded directly to the [Oval Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oval_Office) following the discussions. Carlo Dade, an international policy expert at [University of Calgary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Calgary), said, "the Americans are more than happy to continue to play Canadians off each other."[^cite_note-\:2-99]

On January 29, 2026, [Andy Ogles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andy_Ogles), a Republican US representative, said Albertans would prefer to join the US.[^cite_note-102] Rath responded that joining the US was not possible, preferring an independent Alberta with zero tariffs with the US and a [single market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_market).[^cite_note-\:2-99][^cite_note-\:3-103] The US said the meetings were regular meetings that it pursues with various civic society actors and that no commitment to separatism had been pursued.[^cite_note-CBCNews_2025-12-01-4][^cite_note-104]

## Criticisms

### Legal framework

Any attempt by Alberta to secede would be governed by the federal [Clarity Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarity_Act) and the [Supreme Court of Canada](123468.md)'s opinion in *[Reference re Secession of Quebec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_re_Secession_of_Quebec)*.[^cite_note-Justice_Canada-105][^cite_note-Russell_2019-106] Alberta would first be required to hold a province-wide referendum posing a clear question about secession. Parliament would then decide whether the question and any resulting majority met the threshold for negotiations to begin.[^cite_note-EBSCO_2023-107][^cite_note-Bowal_Bowal_2020-108]

The Clarity Act sets no numerical threshold for a "clear majority," leaving that determination to Parliament based on factors such as turnout, the size of the majority, and the broader political context.[^cite_note-Gilan_2023-14]

In the Quebec Secession Reference, the Supreme Court held that a clear majority on a clear question would create a mutual obligation on all parties to the [Canadian Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Confederation) to negotiate constitutional changes, but found that no province has a unilateral right to secede — and that any negotiations must respect the [Constitution of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Canada), including the rights and interests of [indigenous peoples in Canada](196075.md) and the protection of minorities.[^cite_note-lexum_SCC_1998-13][^cite_note-Centre_for_Constitutional_Studies_2019-109][^cite_note-110]

#### Numbered Treaties



Map of Canada showing the Numbered Treaties between First Nations and the Crown.

There is considerable First Nations opposition to Alberta separation. Alberta is covered by five [Numbered Treaties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numbered_Treaties) between First Nations and the [Crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarchy_of_Canada) (i.e., the Canadian state), four of which were agreed to before Alberta became a province.

The [Confederacy of Treaty No. 6 First Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederacy_of_Treaty_No._6_First_Nations) stated that Alberta separation would be unconstitutional, illegal, and a threat to the treaties. It made the statement in solidarity with other First Nations in Alberta, including First Nations under [Treaty 7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_7) and [Treaty 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_8).[^cite_note-McIntosh_20250815-111] The Confederacy's claims are that separation and the proposed referendum threatens and violates Aboriginal and treaty rights protected under [section 35 of the *Constitution Act, 1982*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Section_35_of_the_Constitution_Act,_1982).[^cite_note-McIntosh_20250815-111] The Confederacy stated that it would continue opposing the Alberta government and the Alberta Prosperity Project over the proposed separation referendum; and reinforced that treaties are not only historical documents, but living agreements that bind all parties.[^cite_note-McIntosh_20250815-111]

The Assembly of Treaty Chiefs, representing First Nations across Alberta, unanimously voted in June 2026 to ask RCMP to look into whether the province's Fall referendum amounts to [criminal treason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treason_Act_1351) by Danielle Smith and her United Conservative Party. The chiefs posited that organizing the vote is an intentional treaty violation and that Smith's government is ignoring serious risks to Canada's sovereignty. In response, Smith told the chiefs to "check themselves", claiming there was a collaborative relationship between the government and ministers.[^cite_note-SmithChiefs-112]

### Creating economic uncertainty

Some economists have warned that separatist discourse could discourage investment in Alberta and make energy infrastructure negotiations more difficult.[^cite_note-CTV_2025-05-16-113] Retired [McGill University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McGill_University) economist [Reuven Brenner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reuven_Brenner) compared the movement to [Quebec separatism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quebec_separatist_movement), which contributed to major companies and thousands of residents leaving Quebec.[^cite_note-114] Trevor Tombe, a professor of economics at the [University of Calgary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Calgary) estimates separation would reduce Alberta's economic activity by \$20 billion (roughly \$3,900 per Albertan), and make Alberta's economy \$30 billion smaller.[^cite_note-115][^cite_note-116] Economist Claude Lavoie notes that although an independent Alberta would collect its own taxes, it would also pay more per capita for services currently provided by the federal government, including [national defence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_defence), [embassies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embassies), [border control](247927.md), [employment insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Employment_insurance), [old age security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Age_Security) and the [criminal justice system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_justice_system).[^cite_note-\:4-117] Lavoie argued that separation would weaken Alberta's trade position because an independent Alberta would not automatically inherit Canada's trade relationships with other countries.[^cite_note-\:4-117]

In March 2026, the [Calgary Chamber of Commerce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calgary_Chamber_of_Commerce) released a poll of Calgary businesses on the economic effects of separatist discourse. The poll found that 83% of respondents believed the discourse increased the risk of recession and reduced business investment. It also found that 74% believed businesses were considering relocation or expansion outside Alberta; 71% believed the discourse made it harder to attract workers; 60% believed it made trade or business expansion with other provinces more difficult; and 56% believed the discourse was affecting Alberta's economy.[^cite_note-118][^cite_note-119]

## Online disinformation by foreign actors

After the Liberal Party of Canada won a minority government in the [October 21, 2019, federal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Canadian_federal_election), #Wexit trended on social media. The term was a [wordplay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wordplay) on "[Brexit](41688778.md)," the name used for the United Kingdom's departure from the [European Union](9317.md).[^cite_note-\:5-50] [Hill+Knowlton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill+Knowlton)'s analysis attributed part of the online activity to [disinformation](142690.md) and [bots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_bot).[^cite_note-\:6-120][^cite_note-\:7-121] In April 2026, the Canadian Digital Media Research Network identified roughly 20 YouTube channels as part of a coordinated network that promoted separatism in western Canadian provinces through [slopaganda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slopaganda).[^cite_note-\:8-122][^cite_note-123] [CBC News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CBC_News) later traced some of these accounts to three individuals in the Netherlands who hired actors to appear on the YouTube channels.[^cite_note-\:8-122] A CBC Investigates reportage from June 8, 2026, identified 14 foreign Facebook accounts posting slopaganda that are monetizing the issue.[^cite_note-124]

After the [2025 federal election](68483222.md), a website known as albertaseparatist.com appeared alongside a YouTube and TikTok account using the same name. Investigators later found that a Russian covert influence network, [Storm-1516](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storm-1516), created the website and social media accounts. Storm-1516 has created fictional websites that target audiences in several countries.[^cite_note-125] A 2026 joint report by the Global Centre for Democratic Resilience, the Centre for Artificial Intelligence, Data and Conflict, and DisinfoWatch said "Russian-aligned information infrastructure" and other social media accounts used Albertan grievances to promote separatist narratives and undermine Canada's democratic integrity, national security, and cognitive sovereignty.[^cite_note-126][^cite_note-127]
