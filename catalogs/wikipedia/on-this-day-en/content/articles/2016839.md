**Hội An** (Vietnamese: [\[hoi˨˩ˀ an˧\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Vietnamese) [//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/6/6b/Hoi_An.ogg/Hoi_An.ogg.mp3](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/6/6b/Hoi_An.ogg/Hoi_An.ogg.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup>) is a former [city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provincial_city_\(Vietnam\)) of [Quảng Nam Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qu%E1%BA%A3ng_Nam_Province) in [Central Vietnam](14624160.md). [Hội An Old Town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%E1%BB%99i_An_Old_Town) has been registered as a [UNESCO](21786641.md) [World Heritage Site](44940.md) since 1999 and is recognized as a well-preserved former site of a once-thriving Southeast Asian [trading port](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trading_post) dating from the [15th–19th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%AA_dynasty).[^cite_note-\:1-1][^cite_note-2]

Prior to Vietnam's [2025 administrative reforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Vietnamese_administrative_reform), Hội An was a [Class-3 provincial city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provincial_city_\(Vietnam\)) of the former [Quảng Nam Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qu%E1%BA%A3ng_Nam_Province), which was merged into the city of [Da Nang](425140.md), a [direct-controlled municipality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct-controlled_municipality) of [Vietnam](202354.md).[^cite_note-\:1-1]

Along with the [Cù Lao Chàm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C3%A0m_Islands) archipelago, it is part of the [Cù Lao Chàm-Hội An Biosphere Reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C%C3%B9_Lao_Ch%C3%A0m-H%E1%BB%99i_An_Biosphere_Reserve), designated in 2009.[^cite_note-3] In 2023, Hội An was registered in the [UNESCO Creative Cities Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNESCO_Creative_Cities_Network) list.[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5]

The town's buildings and street plan reflect a blend of indigenous Vietnamese and [foreign influences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_architecture). Prominent in Hội An's old town is the "[Japanese Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_Bridge)" dating to the 16th–17th century.

## Etymology

[Hội](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/h%E1%BB%99i) [An](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/an#Vietnamese) ([chữ Hán](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%E1%BB%AF_H%C3%A1n): [會](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%9C%83)[安](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%AE%89)) translates as "peaceful meeting place" from Sino-Vietnamese.

The name "Hội An" appears early in historical records, though its precise origin is unclear. According to [Dương Văn An](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C6%B0%C6%A1ng_V%C4%83n_An)'s 1553 work *[Ô Châu Cận Lục](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%94_Ch%C3%A2u_C%E1%BA%ADn_L%E1%BB%A5c?action=edit\&redlink=1)*, [Điền Bàn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90i%E1%BB%87n_B%C3%A0n) County listed 66 villages, including Hoài Phố, Cẩm Phố, and Lai Nghi, but no mention of Hội An. A map by [Lê dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%AA_dynasty) official Đỗ Bá, *Thiên Nam Tứ Chí Lộ Đồ Sách*, records Hội An Citadel and Hội An Bridge. Inscriptions at the Phước Kiến Cave in the [Marble Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marble_Mountains_\(Vietnam\)) mention Hội An three times. During [Nguyễn Phúc Lan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguy%E1%BB%85n_Ph%C3%BAc_Lan)'s rule, the [Minh Hương](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minh_H%C6%B0%C6%A1ng) village was established near Hội An village. Records from the [Minh Mạng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minh_M%E1%BA%A1ng) era indicate that Hội An comprised six villages: Hội An, Minh Hương, Cổ Trai, Đông An, Diêm Hộ, and Hoài Phố. French scholar Albert Sallet noted that Hội An village was the most significant among five villages (Hội An, Cẩm Phố, Phong Niên, Minh Hương, and An Thọ).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETạ_Thị2007207-6]

Westerners historically referred to Hội An as *Faifo*. The origin of this name is debated. The 1651 *[Dictionarium Annamiticum Lusitanum et Latinum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictionarium_Annamiticum_Lusitanum_et_Latinum)* by missionary [Alexandre de Rhodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandre_de_Rhodes) defines *Faifo* as a Japanese settlement in [Cochinchina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cochinchina).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETạ_Thị2007206-8] Some suggest *Faifo* derives from "Hội An Phố" (Hội An Town), a name found in Vietnamese and Chinese historical records. Another theory posits that the [Thu Bồn River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thu_B%E1%BB%93n_River), once called Hoài Phố River, evolved into Phai Phố and then Faifo through phonetic shifts.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEĐặng1991190-9] Western missionaries and scholars used variations like Faifo, Faifoo, Fayfoo, Faiso, and Facfo in their records. [Alexandre de Rhodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandre_de_Rhodes)' 1651 map of Annam, including [Đàng Trong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90%C3%A0ng_Trong) and [Đàng Ngoài](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90%C3%A0ng_Ngo%C3%A0i), clearly marks "Haifo." [French colonial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Indochina) maps later consistently used "Faifo" for Hội An.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEĐặng1991191-10] This word is derived from Vietnamese *Hội An phố* (the town of Hội An), which was shortened to "Hoi-pho", and then to "Faifo".[^cite_note-11] It has also been known by various other Vietnamese names, including Hải Phố, Hoài Phố, Hội Phố, and Hoa Phố.[^cite_note-12] During the Champa period, it was named Lam Ap Pho.[^cite_note-13]

## History

Though the name "Hội An" emerged around the late 16th century, the area's history is far older, having been home to the [Sa Huỳnh culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sa_Hu%E1%BB%B3nh_culture) and [Champa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Champa) culture. The Sa Huỳnh culture, first identified by French archaeologists in [Quảng Ngãi Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qu%E1%BA%A3ng_Ng%C3%A3i_Province), was confirmed as a distinct culture by [Madeleine Colani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madeleine_Colani) in 1937. Over 50 Sa Huỳnh sites have been found in Hội An, mostly along ancient Thu Bồn River sand dunes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200619-14] Artifacts, including [Han dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_dynasty) coins and Western Han-style iron tools, indicate trade as early as the 1st century BCE.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELê2008116-15] Notably, only late Sa Huỳnh culture is evident in Hội An, suggesting its prominence in this period.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200621-16][^cite_note-FOOTNOTELê2008116-15]

### Cham period (2nd–15th centuries)

See also: [Sa Huỳnh culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sa_Hu%E1%BB%B3nh_culture) and [Champa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Champa)

Between the 7th and 10th centuries, the [Chams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chams) (people of [Champa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Champa)) controlled the strategic [spice trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spice_trade) and with this came increasing wealth.[^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18]

The early history of Hội An is that of the Chams. These [Austronesian-speaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austronesian_languages) [Malayo-Polynesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malayo-Polynesian) people created the [Kingdom of Champa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Champa) which occupied much of what is now central and lower [Vietnam](202354.md), from [Huế](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hu%E1%BA%BF) to beyond [Nha Trang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nha_Trang). Various [linguistic connections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamic_languages#Classification) between [Cham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cham_language) and the related [Jarai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jarai_language) language and the Austronesian languages of [Indonesia](14579.md) (particularly [Acehnese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acehnese_language)), [Malaysia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malay_Peninsula), and [Hainan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hainan) have been documented. In the early years, [Mỹ Sơn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%E1%BB%B9_S%C6%A1n) was the spiritual capital,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200622-19] [Trà Kiệu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tr%C3%A0_Ki%E1%BB%87u) was the political capital and Hội An was the commercial capital of the Chams, they later moved further down towards [Nha Trang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nha_Trang). The river system was used for the transport of goods between the [highlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Highlands_\(Vietnam\)), as well as the inland countries of [Laos](17752.md) and [Thailand](30128.md) and its lowlands.

After repeated conflicts, Champa was gradually pushed south by [Đại Việt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90%E1%BA%A1i_Vi%E1%BB%87t), with its final capital at Bầu Giá ([Bình Định Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%ACnh_%C4%90%E1%BB%8Bnh_province)) overtaken in 1471 by the [Later Lê dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%AA_dynasty). Hội An then came under Đại Việt control, laying the foundation for its later commercial prosperity.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200622-19]

### Vietnamese period

Japanese merchant pays tribute to the governor of Quảng Nam at Governor palace in Hoi An, 17th century.

In 1306, [the Vietnamese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_people) and the Chams signed a land treaty, in which Cham king [Jaya Simhavarman III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%E1%BA%BF_M%C3%A2n) gave [Đại Việt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90%E1%BA%A1i_Vi%E1%BB%87t) the two provinces of [Ô and Lý](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thu%E1%BA%ADn_H%C3%B3a) in exchange for a long-term peace and marriage with emperor [Trần Nhân Tông](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tr%E1%BA%A7n_Nh%C3%A2n_T%C3%B4ng)'s daughter [Huyền Trân](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huy%E1%BB%81n_Tr%C3%A2n).[^cite_note-20]<sup>: 86–87, 205 </sup> In 1471, Emperor [Lê Thánh Tông](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%AA_Th%C3%A1nh_T%C3%B4ng) of Đại Việt annexed Champa[^cite_note-21] and Hội An became a Vietnamese territory, and also became the capital of [Quảng Nam Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qu%E1%BA%A3ng_Nam).[^cite_note-FukukawaYuichi-22]<sup>: 23 </sup>

In 1535, Portuguese explorer and sea captain [António de Faria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ant%C3%B3nio_de_Faria), coming from [Đà Nẵng](425140.md), tried to establish a major trading centre at the port village of Faifo.[^cite_note-23] Since 1570, Southern Vietnam had been under the control of the powerful [Nguyễn clan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguy%E1%BB%85n_lords), established by governor [Nguyễn Hoàng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguy%E1%BB%85n_Ho%C3%A0ng). The [Nguyễn lords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguy%E1%BB%85n_lords) were far more interested in commercial activity than the [Trịnh lords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tr%E1%BB%8Bnh_lords) who ruled the north.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200623-24] [Nguyễn Hoàng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguy%E1%BB%85n_Ho%C3%A0ng) and his son [Nguyễn Phúc Nguyên](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguy%E1%BB%85n_Ph%C3%BAc_Nguy%C3%AAn) built fortifications, focused on developing the [Đàng Trong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90%C3%A0ng_Trong) economy, and expanded foreign trade, transforming Hội An into [Southeast Asia](28741.md)'s busiest international trading port at the time.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELê2008117-25] As a result, Hội An flourished as a trading port and became the most important trade port on the [South China Sea](74209.md). Captain [William Adams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Adams_\(sailor,_born_1564\)), the English sailor and confidant of [Tokugawa Ieyasu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokugawa_Ieyasu), is known to have made one trading mission to Hội An in 1617 on a [Red Seal Ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Seal_Ship).[^cite_note-26] The early Portuguese Jesuits also had one of their two residences at Hội An.[^cite_note-27]

In the 17th century, the [Nguyễn lords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguy%E1%BB%85n_lords) continued their [conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tr%E1%BB%8Bnh%E2%80%93Nguy%E1%BB%85n_War) with the [Trịnh lords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tr%E1%BB%8Bnh_lords) while expanding south, encroaching on Champa territories. Because of the need of bronze and gunpowders to produce matchlock guns for war, The Nguyễn issued laws to protect foreign trade to seek for resource to build the weapons, fostering expatriate settlements.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200624-28] In 1567, the [Ming dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ming_dynasty) lifted its isolationist policies, enabling trade with [Southeast Asia](28741.md) but restricting certain exports to [Japan](15573.md) due to history of Japanese pirate's activities ([Wokou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wokou)). This prompted the [Toyotomi regime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toyotomi_regime?action=edit\&redlink=1) and [Tokugawa shogunate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokugawa_shogunate) to seek Chinese goods through Southeast Asian ports.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHoàng2001399-29] From 1604 to 1635, at least 356 Japanese merchant ships ventured to Southeast Asia,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200624-28] with 75 docking at Hội An within 30 years, compared to 37 at [Tonkin](56667.md) under [Trịnh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tr%E1%BB%8Bnh_lords) control.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200625-30] Japanese merchants traded hardware and daily goods for sugar, silk, and agarwood. By 1617, a Japanese quarter formed in Hội An, flourishing in the early 17th century.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200624-28][^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn_Phước200427-31] A painting by Chaya Shinroku, *Map of Cochinchina Trade Routes*, depicts two- and three-story wooden structures in the Japanese quarter.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200624-28] In 1651, Dutch captain Delft Haven noted about 60 closely built Japanese-style stone houses along the river, designed for fire prevention.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn200822-32] However, the Tokugawa shogunate's renewed isolationism and persecution of Christians led to a decline in the Japanese presence, with Chinese merchants gradually taking over.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200624-28][^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn200820-33]

The city also rose to prominence as a powerful and exclusive trade conduit between Europe, China, India, and Japan, especially for the ceramic industry. Shipwreck discoveries have shown that Vietnamese and other Asian ceramics were transported from Hội An to as far as the [Sinai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinai_Peninsula) in Egypt.[^cite_note-34]

&#x20;Unlike the Japanese, Chinese merchants were familiar with Hội An due to earlier trade with Champa. After Champa's fall, Chinese traders continued commerce with Vietnamese locals, driven by demand for salt, gold, and cinnamon from [Southeast Asia](28741.md).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn200824-35] Following the [Late Ming peasant rebellions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Ming_peasant_rebellions) and the [Ming-Qing transition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_from_Ming_to_Qing), many Chinese immigrated to central Vietnam, establishing Minh Hương (Chinese refugees) communities. Chinese merchants increasingly settled in Hội An, replacing the Japanese. The port became a hub for foreign goods, with the riverside Đại Đường district spanning several kilometers, bustling with shops. Most Chinese merchants, primarily from [Fujian](88585.md), wore Ming-style clothing and often married Vietnamese women.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200627-36] Some became Vietnamese citizens, while others, known as "guest residents", retained Chinese nationality. In 1695, Thomas Bowyer of the English East India Company attempted to establish a settlement in Hội An. Though unsuccessful, he recorded:[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200627-36]

> The Faifo district features a street near the river lined with hundreds of houses. Only four or five are Japanese-style; the rest are Chinese. Previously, the Japanese dominated the district and its commerce. Now, the Chinese have taken over most commercial activities. Though less busy than before, 10 to 12 ships from Japan, Guangdong, Siam, Cambodia, Manila, and even Indonesia still trade here annually.

### Decline and modern era



Hội An port in 18th century



The port town of Hội An and its bridge in the 18th century. Watercolour engraving by [Jacques Chereau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Chereau) (1688–1776), circa 1750.

Historical maps from the 17th and 18th centuries show Hội An on the northern bank, connected to the sea via the Đại Chiêm Estuary and linked to [Đà Nẵng's](425140.md) Đại Estuary by another river. The ancient Cổ Cò-Đế Võng River served as a navigable waterway between Hội An and the Hàn River estuary, with archaeological evidence of sunken ships and anchors found in its riverbed.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHoàng2001393-37]

&#x20;During the 18th-century [Tây Sơn rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C3%A2y_S%C6%A1n_dynasty), the Trịnh seized Quảng Nam in 1775, plunging Hội An into conflict.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200628-38] The Trịnh army destroyed much of the commercial district, sparing only religious structures.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn_Phước200431-39] Many Nguyễn elites and wealthy Chinese merchants fled south to [Saigon](66956.md)-[Chợ Lớn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%E1%BB%A3_L%E1%BB%9Bn), leaving Hội An in ruins.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn200836-40][^cite_note-FukukawaYuichi-22]<sup>: 28 </sup> In 1778, Englishman Charles Chapman lamented:[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200629-41]

> Arriving at Hội An, we found the city's well-planned brick houses gone, its paved roads desolate, a painful sight. These works now exist only in memory.

About five years later, Hội An's new port slowly revived, though trade never regained its former prominence. Vietnamese and Chinese residents rebuilt from the rubble, erecting new houses and erasing traces of the Japanese quarter.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn_Phước200432-42]

In the 19th century, the Đại Estuary narrowed, and the Cổ Cò River suffered from silting, preventing large ships from docking. The [Nguyễn dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguy%E1%BB%85n_dynasty)'s isolationist policies, restricting Western trade, further diminished Hội An's role as an international port.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn_Phước200433-43] However, the town continued as a local commercial center, with new roads and widened streets on the southern bank.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200629-41] In the fifth year of [Minh Mạng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minh_M%E1%BA%A1ng)'s reign, the emperor noted Hội An's diminished prosperity but acknowledged it remained more vibrant than other Vietnamese towns.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn200836-40] In 1888, when [Đà Nẵng](425140.md) became a French [concession](3261838.md), many Chinese merchants relocated there,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn200838-44] reducing Hội An's commercial activity. Nonetheless, most surviving residences and community halls took their current architectural form during this period.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200630-45]

In the early 20th century, despite losing its port function, Hội An remained Quảng Nam's urban and administrative center. When the [Quảng Nam-Đà Nẵng Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qu%E1%BA%A3ng_Nam-%C4%90%C3%A0_N%E1%BA%B5ng_Province?action=edit\&redlink=1) was established in 1976, Đà Nẵng became the provincial capital, and Hội An faded into obscurity.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn200838-44] Fortunately, this spared the town from Vietnam's rapid 20th-century [urbanization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urbanization).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn_Thế200115-46]

Between 1907 and 1915, Tramway de l’Îlot de l’Observatoire operated from Đà Nẵng.[^cite_note-47][^cite_note-48][^cite_note-49] As Đà Nẵng became the new centre of trade, and with maintenance difficulties, the tramway ended its operations.[^cite_note-50][^cite_note-51]

In May 1945, a group of 11 civilians of the resistance movement, including the composer [La Hối](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_H%E1%BB%91i), were executed by the Japanese imperial army.[^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53] In August, Hội An became one of the earliest towns to seize power.[^cite_note-54]

Local historians also say that Hội An lost its status as a desirable trade port due to the silting up of the river mouth. The result was that Hội An remained almost untouched by the changes to Vietnam over the next 200 years.[^cite_note-55]

The efforts to revive the city were only done in the 1990s by a Polish architect and [conservator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_and_restoration_of_cultural_heritage) from [Lublin](74594.md) and influential cultural educator, [Kazimierz Kwiatkowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazimierz_Kwiatkowski), who finally brought back Hội An to the world. There is a statue of the Polish architect in the city, and remains a symbol of the [relationship between Poland and Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland%E2%80%93Vietnam_relations), which share many historical similarities despite their distance.[^cite_note-56]

Today, the town is a tourist attraction because of its history, traditional architecture, and crafts such as textiles and ceramics. Many bars, hotels, and resorts have been constructed both in Hội An and the surrounding area. The port mouth and boats are still used for both fishing and tourism.[^cite_note-57]

## Weather



Administration map of Hoian City

Hội An has two main seasons during the year: rainy and dry seasons, with a warm average temperature of 29 °C during the year. The hottest period is from June to August when the highest temperature can reach 38 °C during day time. November to January are the coldest months, with an average temperature of 20 °C. The rainy season lasts from September to January with heavy rains which can cause floods and affect tourism. The city's dry season is between February and May, when the weather becomes very mild with moderate temperature and less humid.[^cite_note-58]

## Heritage and tourism

In 1999 the old town was declared a [World Heritage Site](44940.md) by [UNESCO](21786641.md) as a well-preserved example of a Southeast Asian trading port of the 15th to 19th centuries, with buildings that display a blend of local and foreign influences. According to the UNESCO Impact Report 2008 on Hội An, there are challenges for stakeholders to protect the heritage from tourism.[^cite_note-59][^cite_note-60]



A [đình](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90%C3%ACnh) in Hội An

Owing to the increased number of tourists visiting Hội An a variety of activities are emerging that allow guests to get out of the old quarter and explore by motorbike, bicycle, kayak, or motorboat. The [Thu Bồn River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thu_B%E1%BB%93n_River) is still essential to the region more than 500 years after [António de Faria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ant%C3%B3nio_de_Faria) first navigated it and it remains an essential form of food production and transport. As such kayak and motorboat rides are becoming an increasingly common tourist activity.[^cite_note-61]

This longtime trading port city offers a distinctive regional cuisine that blends centuries of cultural influences from East and Southeast Asia. Hội An hosts a number of cooking classes where tourists can learn to make *[Cao lầu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cao_l%E1%BA%A7u)* or braised spiced pork noodle, a signature dish of the city.[^cite_note-62]

The [Hội An wreck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%E1%BB%99i_An_wreck), a shipwreck from the late fifteenth or early sixteenth century, was discovered near the [Cham Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C3%A0m_Islands), off the coast of the city in the 1990s. Between 1996 and 1999, nearly three hundred thousand artifacts were recovered by the excavation teams, that included the Vietnamese National Salvage Corporation and Oxford University's Marine Archaeology Research Division.[^cite_note-63]

Another attraction is the *Hội An Lantern Full Moon Festival* taking place every full moon cycle. The celebrations honour the ancestors. People exchange flowers, lanterns, candles, and fruits for prosperity and good fortune.

The Hội An Memories Show, performed at the Hội An Impression Theme Park, is a large-scale outdoor theatrical performance that showcases the city's 400-year history. The show features over 500 performers on a 25,000-square-meter stage, depicting Hội An's transformation from a rural village into a major Southeast Asian trading port.[^cite_note-64]

In 2019, Hội An was listed as one of Vietnam's key culture-based tourist areas where rampant tourism growth "threatens the sustainability".[^cite_note-65] Excessive tourism in the past has also damaged the eco-system of Chàm Islands-Hội An Marine Protected Area.[^cite_note-66]

### Ancient town

#### Architecture

The ancient town, located in Minh An Ward, spans about 2 square kilometers with short, winding streets in a chessboard pattern. Near the riverbank is [Bạch Đằng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%E1%BA%A1ch_%C4%90%E1%BA%B1ng_River) Street, while [Nguyễn Thái Học](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguy%E1%BB%85n_Th%C3%A1i_H%E1%BB%8Dc) and [Trần Phú](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tr%E1%BA%A7n_Ph%C3%BA) streets connect to [Nguyễn Thị Minh Khai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguy%E1%BB%85n_Th%E1%BB%8B_Minh_Khai) Street via the [Japanese Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_Bridge). The terrain slopes gently from north to south, causing streets like [Nguyễn Huệ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quang_Trung), [Lê Lợi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%AA_L%E1%BB%A3i), Hoàng Văn Thụ, and Trần Quý Cáp to incline slightly inland.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELê2008119-67] Trần Phú Street, historically the main thoroughfare from the Japanese Bridge to the Chaozhou Community Hall, was named Rue du Pont Japonnais during [French colonial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Indochina) times.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn200846-68] Now about 5 meters wide, many houses lack porches due to late 19th- and early 20th-century expansions.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.20067-69] Nguyễn Thái Học Street, built in 1840 and later called Rue Cantonnais by the [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Indochina), and Bạch Đằng Street, completed in 1878 and known as Riverside Street, formed through river sedimentation.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn200846-68] [Phần Châu Trinh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phan_Ch%C3%A2u_Trinh) Street lies inside Trần Phú Street,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.20067-69] with numerous small alleys branching off perpendicularly.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200612-70]

Trần Phú Street hosts architectural works, including five Chinese [community halls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kongsi) built to honor hometowns: Guangdong, Chinese, Fujian, Hainan, and Chaozhou, all with even-numbered addresses. At the corner of Trần Phú and Nguyễn Huệ streets stands the Quan Công Temple, a hallmark of Minh Hương architecture. Nearby, the Hội An History and Culture Museum, originally a [Minh Hương](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minh_H%C6%B0%C6%A1ng) temple dedicated to Guanyin, joins the Sa Huỳnh Culture Museum and the Trade Ceramics Museum on this street.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.20069-71] Beyond the [Japanese Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_Bridge), Nguyễn Thị Minh Khai Street features traditional houses with red-brick sidewalks, culminating in the Cẩm Phố communal house.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn200722-72] The western side of Nguyễn Thái Học Street features French-style facades, while the eastern side is a bustling shopping area with large two-story houses. The Hội An Folklore Museum at 33 Nguyễn Thái Học Street, the largest preserved residence, measures 57 meters long and 9 meters wide. This area often floods during the rainy season, requiring residents to use boats for shopping and dining.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn200724,26-73] The French quarter on the eastern side includes single-story European-style houses on Phan Bội Châu Street, once used as colonial civil servant dormitories.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200615-74]

#### Traditional architecture



Two-story wooden house with balcony.



Portico.

Hội An's most common buildings are single- or two-story row houses, narrow and deep to suit the region's harsh climate and frequent flooding. Constructed with durable materials, these wooden structures with brick sidewalls are typically 4–8 meters wide and 10–40 meters deep, depending on the street.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETạ_Thị2007136-75] A typical layout includes an entrance, porch, main house, annex, corridor, courtyard, another porch, three-room backyard, and rear garden. Some houses integrate commercial, living, and worship spaces for narrow settings.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETạ_Thị2007141-76] These designs reflect Hội An's regional culture.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETạ_Thị2007144-77]

The main house, divided by 16 pillars in a 4×4 grid, forms a 3×3 space. The central area, slightly larger, serves as a commercial space: the first section from the entrance to the courtyard, the second for storing goods behind a partition, and the third for an inward-facing [shrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shrine).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETạ_Thị2007136-75] Inward-facing shrines are a key feature, though some face the street.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200663-78] The annex, typically a low two-story building, is open to the street, separate from commercial activities, and used for receiving guests.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETạ_Thị2007141-76] The corridor and courtyard, sometimes paved with stone or decorated with ponds and [bonsai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonsai), connect the house's sections, adapting to the rainy and sunny climate. The backyard, enclosed by wooden walls, houses kitchens and bathrooms.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETạ_Thị2007143-79] Shrines, often placed in lofts or central areas, are compact to avoid obstructing trade and daily activities.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETạ_Thị2007138-80][^cite_note-FOOTNOTETạ_Thị2007137-81]

Hội An's houses often feature double roofs, with separate roofs for the main house, annex, and corridor.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200663-78] Hội An tiles, made of clay, are thin, rough, square (about 22 cm per side), and slightly curved, arranged in alternating upward and downward rows, secured with mortar to form sturdy ridges.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.2006135-82] Gabled roofs, sometimes with elevated sides, and ornate gables contribute to the town's architectural distinctiveness.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.2006136-83]

#### Architectural landmarks

Despite many buildings dating to the [colonial period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Indochina), Hội An preserves landmarks reflecting its historical rise and fall. From the 16th to early 18th centuries, buildings served practical purposes like docks, wells, temples, bridges, tombs, [shrines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_temple), and shops.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETạ_Thị2007102-85] From the 18th century, as the port declined, structures like [Confucian temples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Confucius), cultural sites, [communal houses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_communal_house), churches, and community halls became prevalent, showcasing the town's transformation.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETạ_Thị2007135-86] French colonial influences introduced blended architectural styles, harmonizing with urban spaces.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETạ_Thị2007135-86][^cite_note-FOOTNOTETạ_Thị2007130-87] As of December 2000, Hội An's World Heritage Site included 1,360 relics: 1,068 ancient houses, 11 wells, 38 shrines, 19 pagodas, 43 temples, 23 city god pavilions, 44 special tombs, and one bridge, mostly within the ancient town.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn_Thế200118-88]

##### Temples



Quan Công Temple.

Hội An was once an early center of [Buddhism](3267529.md) in [Đàng Trong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90%C3%A0ng_Trong), primarily hosting [Hinayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinayana) Buddhist temples. Many temples trace their origins to ancient times, but due to continuous changes and renovations, most original structures no longer remain.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETạ_Thị200766-89] The earliest known temple, Chúc Thánh Temple, located about 2 kilometers north of the ancient town center, is said to date back to 1454.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn_Thế200144-90] It preserves numerous relics, statues, and inscriptions related to the introduction and development of Buddhism in Nội Đường.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn200732-91] In the suburbs of the ancient town, there are several relatively newer temples, such as Phước Lâm, Vạn Đức, Kim Bồng, and Viên Giác. In the early 20th century, many new temples were established, with the most notable being Long Tuyền Temple, built in 1909.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEĐặng1991344-92] Beyond those built along ancient streams far from villages, Hội An also has village temples near settlements, forming an integral part of the community. This reflects the monks' attachment to the secular world and indicates the strong community cultural institutions of the Minh Hương society here.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETạ_Thị200768-93] The Hội An History and Culture Museum, located within the ancient town, was originally a 17th-century temple dedicated to Guanyin, constructed by Vietnamese and Minh Hương residents.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn_Phước2004218-94]



Pháp Bảo Temple.

Hội An's temples primarily honor the pioneers who founded the city, society, and the Minh Hương community. These temples, typically located in villages, are simple in design with a single entrance and three-bay layout, constructed from fire-resistant bricks, topped with yin-yang tiled roofs, and feature a central shrine.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETạ_Thị200769-95] The most representative is the Quan Công Temple, also known as Ông Temple, located at 24 Trần Phú Street in the heart of the ancient town. Built in 1653 by Minh Hương and Vietnamese residents to venerate [Guan Yu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guan_Yu), the "Paragon of Loyalty," the temple has retained its original appearance despite multiple renovations.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELê2008138-96] The Quan Công Temple consists of several buildings with green glazed tile roofs, divided into three sections: the front hall, courtyard, and main hall. The front hall stands out with its red paint, intricate decorations, and sturdy tiled roof, featuring two large doors adorned with blue dragon carvings coiled among clouds. Flanking the walls are a half-ton bronze bell and a large drum, gifted by Emperor [Bảo Đại](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%E1%BA%A3o_%C4%90%E1%BA%A1i), mounted on a wooden frame.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELê2008139-97] The courtyard, decorated with rock gardens, creates a bright and airy atmosphere, with two additional halls on either side. A stone tablet embedded in the eastern wall records the temple's first renovation in 1753.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn_Phước2004207-98] The main hall, or rear hall, is dedicated to worship, housing a nearly 3-meter-tall statue of Guan Yu, depicted with a red face, phoenix eyes, long beard, and clad in green robes while riding a white horse. Statues of his trusted aides, [Guan Ping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guan_Ping) and [Zhou Cang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhou_Cang), stand on either side. Historically, the Quan Công Temple served as a religious hub for Hội An's merchants, with Guan Yu's sanctity fostering trust in commercial dealings. Today, the temple hosts vibrant festivals on the 13th and 14th of the sixth lunar first and sixth lunar months, known as the "Ông Temple Festival," attracting numerous devotees and visitors.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELê2008140-99]

##### Clan shrines

See also: [Ancestral shrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancestral_shrine)

As in many parts of Vietnam, every clan in Hội An maintains a place to honor ancestors, known as [clan shrines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancestral_shrine) or ancestral temples. These distinctive structures were established by prominent village clans at the founding of Hội An and passed down for ancestral worship. Smaller Chinese families often convert the residences of elders into shrines, with descendants responsible for offerings and maintenance as needed.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETạ_Thị200775-100] Most shrines are concentrated on Phan Châu Trinh and Lê Lợi streets, with a few scattered behind houses on Nguyễn Thị Minh Khai or Trần Phú streets.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200611-101] Chinese immigrant shrines date back to the early 17th century, with only a small portion built after the 18th century.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETạ_Thị200775-100] Unlike rural counterparts, Hội An's shrines typically exhibit an urban architectural style.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn200872-102] Designed as places for ancestor worship, these shrines are constructed in a garden-like form with strict layouts, incorporating gardens, gates, fences, and auxiliary buildings. Many are grand and ornately built, such as the Trần Clan Shrine, Trương Clan Shrine, Nguyễn Clan Shrine, and Minh Hương Ancestral Shrine.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETạ_Thị200776-103]

The Trần Clan Shrine, constructed in the early 19th century, is located at 21 Lê Lợi Street in Hội An. Like other clan shrines in the region, it is nestled within a 1,500-square-meter courtyard, surrounded by high walls and adorned with a front garden featuring bonsai, flowers, and fruit trees. The shrine's architecture blends Chinese, Japanese, and Vietnamese influences, built with precious timber in a two-entry, three-bay layout, topped with a sloping roof covered in yin-yang tiles. The interior is divided into two main sections: the primary area dedicated to ancestral worship and a secondary space for the clan leader's residence and guest accommodations.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELê2008134-104] The worship area has three doors—left for men, right for women, and the central door reserved for elders during significant occasions. In front of the ancestral tablets, wooden boxes containing relics and genealogical records of the Trần Clan are arranged according to the family lineage. During festivals or ancestral commemoration days, the clan leader opens these boxes to honor the deceased. Behind the shrine lies the clan cemetery, planted with [starfruit trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Averrhoa_carambola) symbolizing the concept of "returning to one’s roots."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELê2008135-105]



Fujian Community Hall

##### Community halls

One defining characteristic of Chinese immigrants is their tradition of establishing community halls in their places of residence abroad, based on shared regional origins, to provide spaces for communal activities and cultural practices. In Hội An, five such halls remain, each corresponding to a distinct immigrant group: Fujian, Chinese (general), Chaozhou, Hainan, and Guangdong. These large-scale halls are situated along the axis of Trần Phú Street, facing the Thu Bồn River.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETạ_Thị2007112,114-106] Their traditional design includes a main gate, a courtyard decorated with bonsai and rock gardens, side temples, a ceremonial hall, and the grand main hall—the largest structure—featuring elaborate wood carvings, gold lacquer, and tiled roofs with painted ceramic figurines. Despite multiple renovations, the wooden frameworks retain many original elements. Beyond fostering hometown connections, these halls serve a vital religious function, with the deities worshipped varying according to the customs and traditions of each group's homeland.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEĐặng1991346-107]

Among Hội An's five community halls, the Fujian Community Hall, located at 46 Trần Phú Street, is the largest. Originally a thatched-roof Buddhist temple built by Vietnamese locals in 1697, it fell into disrepair due to lack of maintenance. Fujian merchants purchased it in 1759, and after several renovations, transformed it into a community hall by 1792.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.20069-71] [^cite_note-FOOTNOTELê2008122-108] The building is laid out in a "三" (trident-shaped) configuration, extending from Trần Phú Street to Phan Châu Trinh Street, comprising a main gate ([tam quan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tam_quan)), front courtyard, east and west wing buildings, main hall, rear courtyard, and rear hall. The current gate was reconstructed during a major renovation in the early 1970s.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn_Phước2004226-109] The entrance is striking, with seven green glazed tile roofs arranged symmetrically. A white plaque with red inscriptions reading "Kim Sơn Temple" hangs beneath the upper roof, while a blue stone tablet with red seal script reading "Fujian Community Hall" is positioned under the lower roof. Walls on either side of the gate separate the inner and outer courtyards. The main hall is adorned with vermilion pillars and wooden urns praising the Heavenly Mother ([Mazu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mazu)), and enshrines a statue of [Avalokiteśvara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avalokite%C5%9Bvara) in [meditative pose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zazen). In front of the Guanyin statue is a large incense burner, flanked by statues of Mazu's guardians, Qianli Yan (Thousand-Mile Eye) and Shunfeng Er (Wind-Following Ear).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELê2008123-110] Crossing the rear courtyard leads to the rear hall, where the central shrine honors six Ming Dynasty generals from Fujian, with altars to the left for [Zhusheng Niangniang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songzi_Niangniang) (Goddess of Birth) and the Twelve Midwives, and to the right for the [God of Wealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caishen). Additionally, the rear hall commemorates donors who funded the construction of the community hall and Kim Sơn temple.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELê2008124-111] Every year on the 23rd day of the third lunar month, the Chinese community holds a festival for Mazu, featuring [lion dances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lion_dance), fireworks, fortune-telling, prayers, and other rituals, attracting crowds from Hội An and beyond.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn_Phước2004236-112]

##### Japanese Bridge



[Japanese Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_Bridge) (*Chùa Cầu*), the symbol of Hội An.



Hội An port

Main article: [Japanese Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_Bridge)

The only surviving ancient bridge, the [Japanese Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_Bridge) (Chùa Cầu), spans a small tributary of the Thu Bồn River, connecting Trần Phú and [Nguyễn Thị Minh Khai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguy%E1%BB%85n_Th%E1%BB%8B_Minh_Khai) streets.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELê2008121-114] Approximately 18 meters long, it is said to date to 1593, though evidence is inconclusive. It appears as "Hội An Bridge" in the 1630 *Thiên Nam Tứ Chí Lộ Đồ Sách[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200625-30]* and as the "Japanese Bridge" in [Đại Nam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%90%E1%BA%A1i_Nam)'s 1695 *Overseas Chronicles*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn199555-115] Renovated in the 18th and 19th centuries,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELê2008120-116] its current form features [enamel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitreous_enamel) decorations typical of Nguyễn dynasty architecture.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200614-117]

The [Japanese Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_Bridge) features a distinctive "house-over-bridge" architectural style, with a house structure above and a bridge below, a design common in tropical Asian countries.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELê2008121-114] Despite its name, multiple renovations have left few traces of Japanese architectural elements.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFukukawaal.200614-117] The bridge's striking appearance stems from its curved wooden roof supported by stone arch pillars. The bridge deck, shaped like a rainbow, is paved with wooden planks and features small wooden platforms on the sides, originally used for displaying goods. On the upstream side, a small temple dedicated to [Xuân Vũ Đế](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xuanwu_\(god\)) (the God of the North) was constructed approximately half a century after the bridge's completion. The bridge and temple are separated by a wooden wall and a door with a window above and panels below, creating a relatively independent space. Above the temple's entrance hangs a red plaque inscribed with the words "Lai Viễn Kiều" (Bridge for Faraway Guests), penned by [Nguyễn Phúc Chu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguy%E1%BB%85n_Ph%C3%BAc_Chu) in 1719. At each end of the bridge, animal statues adorn the bridgeheads: monkeys on one side and dogs on the other. Carved from jackfruit wood, each statue is accompanied by an incense bowl placed in front.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELê2008121-114] According to legend, a [giant catfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Namazu), with its head in Japan, tail in the [Indian Ocean](14580.md), and body in Vietnam, causes earthquakes, natural disasters, and floods when disturbed. The Japanese are said to have built the bridge with monkey and dog deities to suppress this monster. Another hypothesis suggests the monkey and dog statues symbolize the construction period, beginning in the Year of the Monkey and ending in the Year of the Dog. This small bridge has become an iconic symbol of Hội An.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELê2008122-108]

### Museums

The city has four museums highlighting the history of the region. These museums are managed by the Hội An Center for Cultural Heritage Management and Preservation. Entrance to the museum is permitted with a Hội An Entrance Ticket.[^cite_note-118]

The Museum of History and Culture, at 13 Nguyen Hue St, was originally a pagoda, built in the 17th century by Minh Huong villagers to worship the [Guanyin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guanyin), and is adjacent to the [Guan Yu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guan_Yu) temple. It contains original relics from the Sa Huynh, Champa, Dai Viet and Dai Nam periods, tracing the history of Hoi An's inhabitants from its earliest settlers through to French colonial times.[^cite_note-119]

The Hội An Folklore Museum, at 33 Nguyen Thai Hoc Street, was opened in 2005, and is the largest two-storey wooden building in the old town, at 57m long and 9m wide, with fronts at Nguyen Thai Hoc St and Bach Dang St. On the second floor, there are 490 artifacts, organised into four areas: plastic folk arts, performing folk arts, traditional occupations and artifacts related to the daily life of Hội An residents.[^cite_note-120]



[Museum of Trade Ceramics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Trade_Ceramics)

The [Museum of Trade Ceramics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Trade_Ceramics) is located at 80 Tran Phu Street, and was established in 1995, in a restored wooden building, originally built around 1858. The items originating from Persia, China, Thailand, India and other countries are proof of the importance of Hội An as a major trading port in South East Asia.[^cite_note-121]

The [Museum of Sa Huỳnh Culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Sa_Hu%E1%BB%B3nh_Culture), is located at 149 Tran Phu Street. Established in 1994, this museum displays a collection of over 200 artifacts from the Sa Huỳnh culture—considered to be the original settlers on the Hội An site—dating to over 2000 years ago. This museum is considered to be the most unusual collection of Sa Huỳnh artefacts in Vietnam.[^cite_note-122]

The [Precious Heritage Art Gallery Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precious_Heritage_Art_Gallery_Museum) is located at 26 Phan Boi Chau. It includes a 500m2 display of photos and artifacts.[^cite_note-123]

The [Hội An Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%E1%BB%99i_An_Museum) is a history museum located at 10B Trần Hưng Đạo.

## Transportation

### Air

The nearest airport to Hội An is [Da Nang International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Da_Nang_International_Airport) which is 28 km to the north, about a 40 minutes drive, and [Chu Lai International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chu_Lai_International_Airport) is 73 km away in the south which is a one and a half hours' drive. There are other transportation such as regular car and bus services to and from Hội An.

Hội An main routes connect to Đà Nẵng including: National Route 14B – Provincial Road 607 (which is known as Ngô Quyền Street and Lê Văn Hiến Street in Đà Nẵng urban core), coastal road from [Sơn Trà Mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C6%A1n_Tr%C3%A0_Mountain) to Cửa Đại Beach.

## Gastronomy



A restaurant in the ancient town.

For centuries, Hội An's position at the crossroads of waterways and its role as a hub of economic and cultural exchange have fostered a diverse culinary tradition influenced by Vietnamese, Chinese, Japanese, and Western cultures.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBùi200576-125] Although the region lacks the vast expanses of the [Mekong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mekong) or [Red River Deltas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_River_Delta), its fertile riverbank dunes and narrow alluvial lands shape local lifestyles and customs, including culinary practices.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETrần200011-126] Seafood dominates Hội An's daily diet, with fish and other marine products often outselling other meats by double in local markets.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBùi200576-125] Fish is so integral that markets are commonly referred to as "fish markets."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETrần200012-127] Hội An's Chinese community continues to preserve traditional Chinese cooking habits and customs. During festivals and weddings, they prepare signature dishes such as Fujian [fried noodles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fried_noodles), [Yangzhou fried rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yangzhou_fried_rice), and "money chicken," which serve as opportunities to strengthen community bonds. The Chinese have significantly enriched Hội An's culinary landscape, contributing to the creation of many unique local dishes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBùi200580-128]

According to [CNN](62028.md), Hội An is the "[banh mi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banh_mi) capital of Vietnam."[^cite_note-cnn-129] Banh Mi is a type of Vietnamese sandwich, consisting of a baguette, pâté, meats and fresh herbs.[^cite_note-130]

Com ga (chicken rice) is a signature dish.[^cite_note-\:0-131] Made with fragrant broth-cooked rice, poached or shredded chicken, topped with scallion oil, fried shallots, and a tangy fish sauce dip. Often served with soup or pickles



[Cao lầu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cao_l%E1%BA%A7u).

[Cao lầu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cao_l%E1%BA%A7u) is a signature dish of the town, consisting of rice noodles, meat, greens, bean sprouts, and herbs, often served with a little broth made from pork and bone broth,[^cite_note-\:0-131][^cite_note-FOOTNOTETrần200031-132] with a strong resemblance to Japanese [udon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Udon).[^cite_note-133] The water for the broth has been traditionally taken from the Ba Le Well, thought to have been built in the 10th century by the [Chams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chams).[^cite_note-134] Local Chinese residents do not claim it as a Chinese dish. Some Japanese researchers note similarities with noodles from the Ise region, but the flavor and preparation methods differ significantly.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETrần200030-135]

In addition to [cao lầu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cao_l%E1%BA%A7u), Hội An also have dishes like [wonton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wonton) and white rose dumplings, alongside a variety of rustic specialties such as steamed rice cakes, mussels, pancakes, rice paper, and notably [Quảng noodles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%AC_Qu%E1%BA%A3ng) (mì Quảng). As the name suggests, Quảng noodles originate from Quảng Nam Province. Like phở and bún, they are made from rice but possess a distinct color, aroma, and flavor.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETrần200026-136] The preparation begins with soaking high-quality rice in water to soften it, grinding it into fine flour, and adding alum to make the dough crisp and firm. The dough is pressed into flat, leaf-shaped cakes, which are boiled, cooled, lightly oiled to prevent sticking, and cut into noodles. The broth is typically made from shrimp, pork, or chicken, though snakehead fish or beef may also be used, resulting in a clear, sweet, and non-spicy flavor.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETrần200027-137] Quảng noodles are ubiquitous in Hội An, from urban restaurants to rural street stalls, particularly at roadside noodle shops.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETrần200028-138]

White rose dumplings is typical in Hội An's ancient town, consisting of two types of rice flour-based delicacies: bánh bao (steamed dumplings) and bánh vạc (boiled dumplings).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETrần2000103-139] The grinding water must be pure, unsalted, and free of alum, typically sourced from the ancient Bá Lễ Well. The rice flour is ground multiple times and poured into clean basins. While grinding, workers prepare the fillings and fried onions. The fillings differ between the two dumplings: bánh bao's filling is primarily made by pounding shrimp and spices in a mortar, while bánh vạc's filling is more diverse, including shrimp paste, bean sprouts, wood ear mushrooms, bamboo shoots, diced pork, and green onions, all stir-fried with salt and fish sauce.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETrần2000104-140] Each type is crafted by two to four artisans. Bánh bao's skin is extremely thin, resembling white rose petals, while bánh vạc is larger, shaped like a pot handle.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETrần2000105-141] After being filled, the dumplings are steamed for 10 to 15 minutes. Both are served together, allowing diners to enjoy them as preferred. Bánh bao is typically placed centrally on the top layer and bánh vạc arranged around the bottom, sprinkled with fried onions and drizzled with cooked oil.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETrần2000106-142] White rose dumplings are paired with a fish sauce that balances the sweet flavor of shrimp, the tang of lemon, and the spice of yellow chili slices.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETrần2000107-143]

Hội An's restaurants are not only known for their delectable cuisine but also for their distinctive ambiance. Many eateries in the ancient town are decorated with antique paintings, surrounded by ornamental potted plants, bonsai, or handicrafts. Some feature fishponds or rock gardens, creating a relaxing and comfortable environment for diners. Restaurant names often carry traditional significance, passed down through generations.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETrần2000138,139-144] Beyond local specialties, French, Japanese, and Western dishes and customs have been preserved and developed, contributing to the diversity of Hội An's culinary scene and catering to the varied preferences of tourists.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBùi200580-128]

Other regional specialties include [Banh bao banh vac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banh_bao_banh_vac), [Hoanh thanh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoanh_thanh), com ga (chicken with rice), [bánh xèo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%A1nh_x%C3%A8o), sweet corn soup and baby clam salad are also regional specialties.[^cite_note-145] Chili sauce, Ớt Tương Triều Phát, is also produced locally.[^cite_note-146]

## Culture

Hội An stands out for its history and cultural diversity. Since the late 15th century, Vietnamese settlers coexisted with Cham residents, and the town's role as a trading port welcomed diverse cultures, fostering a multilayered cultural identity expressed through customs, literature, cuisine, and festivals.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBùi2005102-147] Unlike the royal heritage of [Huế](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Complex_of_Hu%E1%BA%BF_Monuments), Hội An's culture is rooted in everyday life,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBùi2005103-148] with vibrant intangible heritage complementing its physical landmarks.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBùi2005104-149]

### Religion

In addition to [ancestor worship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veneration_of_the_dead), Hội An residents practice the worship of the Five Deities (Ngũ Tế), rooted in the local belief that "the state has its king, and the house has its master." These Five Deities are considered the household guardians, believed to govern and arrange the family's fate. The Vietnamese typically recognize these as the [Kitchen God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitchen_God) (Táo Thần), Well God (Tỉnh Thần), Door God (Môn Thần), Patron Saint (Bản Mệnh Tiên Sư), and the Nine Heavenly Maidens (Cửu Thiên Huyền Nữ). Some Chinese residents, however, identify them as the Kitchen God (Táo Quân), Door God (Môn Thần), Household God (Hộ Thần), Well God (Tỉnh Thần), and Central Deity (Trung Lưu). The altars for these Five Deities are solemnly placed in the center of the house, above the ancestral tablets.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBùi200538-150] In practice, each deity has a designated worship space within the household: the Kitchen God is venerated in the kitchen, the Door God at the entrance, and the Well God near the well. Chinese residents, however, do not worship the Kitchen God in the kitchen but place the altar in the courtyard, adjacent to the altar for the Heavenly Official (Thiên Quan) who grants blessings.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBùi200538-150]

Hội An's religious landscape is diverse, encompassing [Buddhism](3267529.md), [Catholicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholicism), [Protestantism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism), and [Caodaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caodaism), with Buddhism remaining the dominant faith. Many families, even those not formally religious, practice vegetarianism and venerate Buddhist figures, primarily [Avalokiteśvara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avalokite%C5%9Bvara) (Goddess of Mercy) and [Gautama Buddha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Buddha), with some also honoring [Mahasthamaprapta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahasthamaprapta). Buddhist altars are placed in solemn, purified spaces, typically elevated above ancestral altars. Some households dedicate significant space for Buddhist worship and scripture recitation.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBùi200539-151]

Another distinctive feature of Hội An's folk beliefs is the widespread worship of [Guan Yu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guan_Yu) (Quan Công), which, though rare in rural areas, is particularly prevalent in urban settings.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBùi200539-151] Among the many deities revered in Hội An, Guan Yu is regarded as the most sacred. The Quan Công Temple, located at the heart of the ancient town, is a focal point of this faith, with incense burning year-round. For generations, residents have prayed to Guan Yu for protection and family harmony. Altars often feature statues or images of Guan Yu alongside his aides, [Guan Ping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guan_Ping) and [Zhou Cang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhou_Cang).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBùi200540-152]

In the cultural heritage of Chinese immigrants, particularly within community halls, the deities worshipped vary according to each group's traditions. The Fujian Community Hall venerates [Mazu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mazu) (Heavenly Mother) and the Six Loyal Fujian Ministers of the Ming Dynasty. The [Hainan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hainan) Community Hall honors 108 merchants from Hainan who perished at sea while trading in Vietnam and were deified by the [Nguyễn dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguy%E1%BB%85n_dynasty) as Zhao Ying Gong (Protective Lords).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBùi200541-153] The Chaozhou Community Hall worships [General Ma Yuan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ma_Yuan_\(Han_dynasty\)), the God of Waves, revered for rescuing merchant ships. Other forms of worship in Hội An include veneration of female spirits (Bà Cô), heroic figures (Ông Mãnh), anonymous spirits (Vô Danh Vô Vị), talismanic stones (Thờ Đá Bùa), and [Shi Gandang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shi_Gandang) (Thạch Cảm Đương), protective stone deities.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBùi200542-154]

### Entertainment



Bầu nậm performance in Hội An

The music, theater, and folk games of Hội An are a testament to the ingenuity of its residents, developed through labor and preserved as vital components of local spiritual life. These include hò khoan (work chants), chèo chài (rowing chants), hò kéo neo (anchor-pulling chants), lý (lyrical folk tunes), vè (Vietnamese rhyming verses),[^cite_note-155] tuồng (classical opera), bầu nậm (ceremonial rowing songs), and hát bài chòi (card-singing games). Hội An also maintains traditions of playing ancient music during weddings and funerals, as well as performing đờn ca tài tử (southern amateur chamber music) alongside renowned artists.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBùi200557-156] Local folk games include card games, đánh đỏ (red stick gambling), scholar riddles, poetry, and calligraphy contests.

Hát bài chòi is a culturally significant recreational activity in [Quảng Nam Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qu%E1%BA%A3ng_Nam_province) and Vietnam's central coast, held on the 14th night of each lunar month in a small park at the intersection of Nguyễn Thái Học and Bạch Đằng streets. Eight to ten players sit in elevated thatched huts, divided into two teams, with a lead singer, known as the hiệu ca (caller), positioned at the head or center. Thirty-two cards, called hạ phó bài (lower deck cards), are evenly distributed among the players, with each receiving three cards inscribed with different words. These cards are printed on bamboo paper using woodblock techniques, coated with a layer of shell and stiff paper, and feature red, green, or blue-gray backs.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBùi200558-157] Two additional cards are reserved. The caller has a separate deck stored in a bamboo tube hung on a tree, drawing cards without seeing them and singing a verse for each to prompt players to guess the card.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBùi200558-157] When a player's card matches the caller's, they strike a wooden fish, and a "soldier" runner exchanges the card for a flag. The first player to collect three flags shouts "Tới!" (Got it!), ending the game. The caller is the heart of the game, requiring proficiency in folk songs and the ability to improvise poetic verses related to the card's content, ensuring the game remains engaging and unpredictable.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBùi200560-158] This blend of performance and spontaneity is the primary allure of hát bài chòi.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBùi200558-157]

Hát Bả trạo a ceremonial folk singing form, plays a significant role in the spiritual and emotional life of Hội An's residents.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBùi200563-159] Its performance structure narrates a boat's journey from departure to safe landing, combining the narrative style of tuồng opera, a theatrical form beloved in Quảng Nam.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBùi200562-160] Beyond its artistic depiction of rowing, bầu nậm boasts a rich variety of singing techniques, including standard methods like chanting and calling, as well as folk styles such as hò khoan, lý, low murmurs, and high-pitched songs. The performers’ talent makes it highly captivating for audiences.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBùi200563-159] During the Whale God Festival (Lễ Hội Cá Ông), fishermen sing bầu nậm to express reverence and mourning for Ngọc Lân Nam Hải, the deity believed to rescue distressed mariners, while praying for calm seas and safe voyages. Coastal residents also perform bầu nậm at funerals, lamenting tragic fates and honoring the deceased's virtues.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBùi200563-159]

## Festivals



Hội An on the 14th night of the seventh lunar month, 2006.

Hội An continues to preserve numerous traditional festivals, encompassing reverence for city gods, commemoration of ancestors, veneration of saints, and religious observances. Among the most representative is the [City God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_God_\(China\)) Pavilion Festival held in suburban villages. Typically, each village has a pavilion dedicated to the City God and local pioneers. Every early spring, villages organize this festival to honor their City God and remember their forebears. The event is usually overseen by village elders, who, in preparation, form a festival committee. Villagers collectively contribute funds, participate in cleaning, and decorate the pavilion and temples. The festival spans two days: the first day marks the opening, while the second is the formal ceremonial day.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBùi200534-161]

&#x20;On the [15th days of the first](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lantern_Festival) and [seventh lunar months](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost_Festival), Hội An residents hold [Dragon Boat Festivals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_Boat_Festival) at rural city god pavilions. These dates coincide with the transition between the rainy and dry seasons, a period prone to epidemics. Local belief holds that diseases are caused by malevolent natural forces, prompting universal participation in the festival. On the ceremonial day, villagers row dragon boats to the pavilion, where the chief officiant blesses and consecrates the boats with holy water. After numerous rituals, at night, sturdy men transport the dragon boats to designated areas, where they are burned and released into the sea.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn_Thế200126-162] The townspeople also set up tables of fruits, incense, and [hell money](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hell_money) as offerings to the ancestors.[^cite_note-163]



The scene of the lantern festival

In Hội An's riverine and coastal fishing villages, [canoe](99960.md) racing is an essential cultural activity, held from the second to seventh days of the first lunar month, praying for abundant catches in the second month and safety in the third. According to folk beliefs, boat racing pleases the deities of mountains and waters, bringing blessings to the community. Before each race, households diligently practice and prepare. Winning a race is a source of pride, symbolizing an impending bountiful harvest. While rituals and racing were once equally significant, today the competitive aspect often overshadows the ceremonial.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn_Thế200127-164] During the fishing season's opening, residents of Hội An's fishing villages also worship the Whale God (a blue whale revered for rescuing mariners).[^cite_note-165] These rituals often feature bầu nậm singing, a distinctive folk music depicting life and labor on the river. As in other [central Vietnamese](14624160.md) coastal regions, when a whale beaches, fishermen bury it respectfully and conduct solemn rituals.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn_Thế200127-164]

Since 1998, the Hội An municipal government has hosted the Full Moon Festival on the 14th night of each lunar month, from 5 to 10 p.m. This unique initiative was proposed by Polish architect [Kazimierz Kwiatkowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazimierz_Kwiatkowski_\(architect\)), who dedicated himself to preserving Hội An Ancient Town and [Mỹ Sơn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%E1%BB%B9_S%C6%A1n) Sanctuary, both UNESCO World Heritage Sites. During the festival, all households, including shops and restaurants, turn off electric lights, allowing the moonlight and lanterns to illuminate the streets. Vehicles are prohibited, and the streets are reserved for pedestrians. Activities include music performances, folk games, chess, bầu nậm card-singing, and lantern displays. During major festivals, additional events such as masquerades, poetry recitals, and lion dances enhance the cultural experience. The Full Moon Festival immerses visitors in the ambiance of Hội An's centuries-old heritage.[^cite_note-166]

To celebrate [sister city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sister_city) status with [Wernigerode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wernigerode), Germany, selected residents of Hội An lead a replica of the lantern festival as a sign of respect towards [Vietnam–Germany relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam%E2%80%93Germany_relations).[^cite_note-167]
