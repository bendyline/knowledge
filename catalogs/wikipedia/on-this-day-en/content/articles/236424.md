# Street performance

"Busking" and "Busker" redirect here. For other uses, see [Busking (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Busking_\(disambiguation\)).

"Street music" and "Street singer" redirect here. For other uses, see [Street Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_Music_\(disambiguation\)) and [Street singer (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_singer_\(disambiguation\)).

For the fundraising pledge system also known as the Street Performer Protocol, see [Threshold pledge system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threshold_pledge_system).



A busker in south London. Their violin case is open nearby to accept donations from passers by.

**Street performance** or **busking** is the act of performing in public places for [gratuities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gratuity). In many countries, the rewards are generally in the form of money but other gratuities such as food, drink or gifts may be given. Street performance is practiced all over the world and dates back to [antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_history). People engaging in this practice are called **street performers** or **buskers.**

Performances are anything that people find [entertaining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entertainment), including [acrobatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acrobatics), [animal tricks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_training), [balloon twisting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balloon_modelling), [caricatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caricature), [clowning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clown), [comedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comedy), [contortions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contortion), [escapology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escapology), [dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dance), [singing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singing), [fire skills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_performance), [flea circus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flea_circus), [fortune-telling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortune-telling), [juggling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juggling), [magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_\(illusion\)), [mime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mime_artist), [living statue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Living_statue), [musical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music) performance, [one man band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_man_band), [puppeteering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puppeteer), [snake charming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snake_charming), [storytelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storytelling) or reciting [poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetry) or [prose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prose), [street art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_art) such as [sketching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sketch_\(drawing\)) and painting, [street theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_theatre), [sword swallowing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sword_swallowing), [ventriloquism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ventriloquism), [weightlifting](37894764.md) and [washboarding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washboard_\(musical_instrument\)). Buskers may be solo performers or small groups.

## Etymology

The term *busking* was first noted in the English language around the middle 1860s in the United Kingdom. The verb *to busk*, from the word *busker*, comes from the Spanish root word *buscar*, with the meaning "to seek".[^cite_note-Webster-1] The Spanish word *buscar* in turn evolved from the [Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European_languages) word *\*bhudh-skō* ("to win, conquer").[^cite_note-Diccionario_RAE-2] It was used for many street acts, and was the title of a famous Spanish book about one of them, *[El Buscón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Busc%C3%B3n)*.

## History



1855 painting of a street musician, [O Pobre Rabequista](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O_Pobre_Rabequista) (The Poor Rabeca Player), by [José Rodrigues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Rodrigues)



An organ grinder in Paris, photographed by [Eugène Atget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eug%C3%A8ne_Atget), c. 1898–99

There have been performances in public places for [gratuities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gratuities) in every major culture in the world, dating back to [antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_history). For many musicians, street performance was the most common means of employment before the advent of [recording](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_recording) and personal electronics.[^cite_note-BF-3] Prior to that, a person had to produce any music or entertainment, save for a few mechanical devices such as the [barrel organ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barrel_organ), the [music box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_box), and the [piano roll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piano_roll). [Organ grinders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organ_grinder) were commonly found busking in the 19th century and early 20th century.

Busking is common among some [Romani people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romani_people). Romantic mention of Romani music, dancers and fortune tellers are found in all forms of song poetry, prose and lore. The Roma brought the word busking to England by way of their travels along the [Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean) coast to Spain and the Atlantic Ocean and then up north to England and the rest of Europe.

In medieval France, buskers were known by the terms [troubadours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troubadour) and *jongleurs*. In northern France, they were known as *trouveres*. In old German, buskers were known as [Minnesingers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minnesingers) and *Spielleute*. In obsolete French, it evolved to *busquer* for "seek, prowl" and was generally used to describe [prostitutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prostitute). In Russia, buskers are called *[skomorokh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skomorokh)*, and their first recorded history appears around the 11th century.

[Mariachis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariachi), Mexican bands that play a style of music by the same name, frequently busk when they perform while traveling through streets and plazas, as well as in restaurants and bars.[^cite_note-WebsterMariachi-4]

> We like playing for big crowds, and the goal all along has been for people to pay a little to come and see us. But it all started on street corners, and that is still very connected to what we do. It's such a validating musical experience. Busking is a very humble and brave act that takes courage to do well. It's also about the energy of music being alive outside in a city ... You can walk right by it right in front of you. Sure, to some people you're just another guy with his hand out, so sometimes busking can be great social barometer. You're able to gauge who you live with on earth.[^cite_note-Ferris-5]

— Ketch Secor, [Old Crow Medicine Show](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Crow_Medicine_Show)

Around the mid-19th century Japanese *[Chindonya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chindonya)* started to be seen using their skills for advertising, and these street performers are still occasionally seen in Japan. Another Japanese street performance form dating from the [Edo period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo_period) is [Nankin Tamasudare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nankin_Tamasudare), in which the performer creates large figures using a bamboo mat.



*I Viggianesi*, street musicians from [Viggiano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viggiano), Italy. Work by [Filippo Palizzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filippo_Palizzi), 1853

In the 19th century, Italian street musicians (mainly from [Liguria](71864.md), [Emilia Romagna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emilia_Romagna), [Basilicata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilicata)) began to roam worldwide in search of fortune. Musicians from [Basilicata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilicata), especially the so-called *[Viggianesi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viggiano)*, would later become professional instrumentalists in symphonic orchestras, especially in the [United States](3434750.md).[^cite_note-6] The street musicians from Basilicata are sometimes cited as an influence on [Hector Malot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hector_Malot)'s *[Sans Famille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sans_Famille)*.[^cite_note-7]

In the United States, [medicine shows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine_show) proliferated in the 19th century. They were traveling vendors selling [elixirs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elixir) and [potions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potion) which purportedly improved people's health. They would often employ entertainment acts as a way of drawing in potential clients and relaxing them. The people would often associate this feeling of well-being with the products sold. After these performances, they would "pass the hat".

[One-man bands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-man_band) have historically performed as buskers playing a variety of instruments simultaneously. One-man bands proliferated in urban areas in the 19th and early 20th centuries and still perform to this day. A current one-man band plays all their instruments acoustically usually combining a guitar, a harmonica, a drum and a tambourine. They may also include singing. Many still busk but some are booked to play at festivals and other events.

[Folk music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_music) has always been an important part of the busking scene. Cafe, restaurant, bar and pub busking is a mainstay of this art form. The [delta bluesmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_blues) were mostly itinerant musicians emanating from the Mississippi Delta region of the USA around the early 1940s and on. [B.B. King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B.B._King) is one famous example who came from these roots.



Dancers in [Sutton High Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sutton_High_Street), [Sutton, London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sutton,_London), England

The [counterculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterculture) of the [hippies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippie) of the 1960s occasionally staged "be-ins", which resembled some present-day [buskers festivals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buskers_festival). Bands and performers would gather at public places and perform for free, passing the hat to make money. The [San Francisco Bay Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_Bay_Area) was at the epicenter of this movement – be-ins were staged at [Golden Gate Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Gate_Park) and [San Jose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Jose,_California)'s Bee Stadium and other venues. Some of the bands that performed in this manner were [Janis Joplin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janis_Joplin) with [Big Brother and the Holding Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Brother_and_the_Holding_Company), the [Grateful Dead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grateful_Dead), [Jefferson Airplane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jefferson_Airplane), [Quicksilver Messenger Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quicksilver_Messenger_Service), [Country Joe and the Fish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Country_Joe_and_the_Fish), [Moby Grape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moby_Grape) and [Jimi Hendrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimi_Hendrix).

[Christmas caroling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christmas_carol) can also be a form of busking, as [wassailing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wassailing) included singing for alms, [wassail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wassail) or some other form of refreshment such as [figgy pudding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Figgy_pudding). In the Republic of Ireland, the traditional Wren Boys, and in England [Morris Dancing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morris_Dancing) can be considered part of the busking tradition.

In India and Pakistan's [Gujarati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gujarati_people) region, [Bhavai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhavai) is a form of street art where there are plays enacted in the village, the [barot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barot_\(caste\)) or the village singer also is part of the local entertainment scene.

In the 2000s, some performers have begun "Cyber Busking". Artists post work or performances on the Internet for people to download or "stream" and if people like it they make a donation using [PayPal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PayPal).

## Forms



A walk-by street performer blowing large bubbles in [Bath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bath,_Somerset), United Kingdom

There are three basic forms of street performance: circle shows, walk-by acts, and stoplight performances.

"**Circle shows**" are shows that tend to gather a crowd around them. They usually have a distinct beginning and end. Usually these are done in conjunction with [street theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_theatre), [puppeteering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puppeteer), [magicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_\(illusion\)), comedians, [acrobats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acrobatics), [jugglers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juggle) and sometimes musicians. Circle shows can be the most lucrative. Sometimes the crowds attracted can be very large. A good busker will control the crowd so the patrons do not obstruct foot traffic.

"**Walk-by acts**" are acts where the busker performs a musical, living statue or other act that does not have a distinct beginning or end, and the public usually watches for a brief time. A walk-by act may turn into a circle show if the act is unusual or very popular.

"**Stoplight performances**" are performances in which performers present their act and get contributions from vehicle occupants on a crosswalk while the traffic lights are red. A variety of disciplines can be used in such a format (juggling, break dancing, even magic tricks). Because of the short period of time available to them, stoplight performers must have a very brief, condensed routine. This form is seen more commonly in Latin America than elsewhere.

### Collecting money



Violinist with a [MobilePay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MobilePay) QR code at [Jorcks Passage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jorcks_Passage) in [Copenhagen](5166.md)

Buskers collect donations and tips from the public in a variety of containers and by different methods depending on the type of busking they are performing. For walk-by acts, their open, empty instrument case or a special can, box, or hat is often used. For circle shows the performer will typically collect money at the end of the show, although some performers will also collect during the show, as some audience members do not stay for the entire performance.&#x20;

Sometimes a performer will employ a *bottler*, *hat man*, or *pitch man* to collect money from the audience and encourage them to contribute, sometimes by cajoling them in a humorous fashion. The term *bottler* is a British term that originated from the use of the top half of a bottle to collect money. The bottle had a leather flap inserted in the bottleneck and a leather pouch attached. This design allowed coins to be put in the bottle but did not allow them to be removed easily without the coins jingling against the glass. The first use of such contrivances was recorded by the famous [Punch and Judy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punch_and_Judy) troupe of puppeteers in early Victorian times.[^cite_note-8]

The increasing use of cashless payments in the 21st century, and the corresponding lessening of the amount of cash typically carried, has affected buskers, some of whom have begun using electronic payment systems including contactless payment terminals and web or app based payment systems (sometimes reachable by [QR code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QR_code)). This trend accelerated after [COVID-19](63030231.md) lockdowns, but predates this.[^cite_note-Wollaston-9][^cite_note-Shular-10]

### Pitches

[Toss juggling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toss_juggling) street performance in Denmark

The place where a performance occurs is called a "pitch". A good pitch can be the key to success as a busker. An act that might make money at one place and time may not work at all in another setting. Popular pitches tend to be public places with large volumes of pedestrian traffic, high visibility, low background noise and as few elements of interference as possible. Good locations may include tourist spots, popular parks, entertainment districts including many restaurants, cafés, bars and pubs and theaters, [subways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapid_transit) and bus stops, outside the entrances to large concerts and sporting events, almost any plaza or [town square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Town_square) as well as [zócalos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z%C3%B3calo) in Latin America and [piazzas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piazza) in other regions. Other places include shopping malls, strip malls, and outside supermarkets, although permission is usually required from management for these.

In her book, *Underground Harmonies: Music and Politics in the Subways of New York*, Susie J. Tanenbaum examined how the adage "Music hath charms to soothe the savage beast" plays out in regards to busking. Her sociological studies showed that in areas where buskers regularly perform, crime rates tended to go down, and that those with higher education attainment tended to have a more positive view of buskers than did those of lesser educational attainment.[^cite_note-Tanenbaum-11] Some cities encourage busking in particular areas,[^cite_note-Startz-12] giving preference to city government-approved buskers and even publishing schedules of performances.[^cite_note-MTANY-13]

Many cities in the United States have particular areas known to be popular spots for buskers. Performers are found at many locations like Mallory Square in [Key West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_West), in [New Orleans](53842.md), in New York around [Central Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Park), [Washington Square](745249.md), and the subway systems, in San Francisco, in Washington, D.C. around the transit centers, in Los Angeles around [Venice Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venice,_Los_Angeles), the [Santa Monica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Monica) [Third Street Promenade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Street_Promenade), and the Hollywood area, in Chicago on [Maxwell Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell_Street), in the [Delmar Loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delmar_Loop) district of [St. Louis](27687.md), and many other locations throughout the US. Busking is still quite common in Scotland, Ireland ([Grafton Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grafton_Street#Busking), [Dublin](8504.md)), and England with musicians and other street performers of varying talent levels.

## Legislation

See also: [Street performing (U.S. case law)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_performing_\(U.S._case_law\))



A sign in a tourist area of Edinburgh, Scotland, advises buskers that guitar amplifiers and PA speakers are not allowed.

The first recorded instances of laws affecting buskers were in [ancient Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome) in 462 BC. [The Law of the Twelve Tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Law_of_the_Twelve_Tables) made it a crime to sing about or make parodies of the government or its officials in public places; the penalty was death.[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15] [Louis the Pious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_the_Pious) "excluded *[histriones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jester)* and *[scurrae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clown)*, which included all entertainers without noble protection, from the privilege of justice".[^cite_note-16] In 1530 [Henry VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_VIII_of_England) ordered the licensing of minstrels and players, fortune-tellers, pardoners and fencers, as well as beggars who could not work. If they did not obey they could be whipped on two consecutive days.[^cite_note-17]

In the United States under [constitutional law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_law) and most European [common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law), the protection of [artistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artistic) [free speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_speech) extends to busking. In the U.S. and many countries, the designated places for free speech behavior are the public [parks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Park), streets, [sidewalks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidewalk), [thoroughfares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thoroughfare) and [town squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Town_square) or plazas. Under certain circumstances even private property may be open to buskers, particularly if it is open to the general public and busking does not interfere with its function and management allows it or other forms of free speech behaviors or has a history of doing so.[^cite_note-autogenerated1-18]

While there is no universal [code of conduct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_of_conduct) for buskers, there are common law practices that buskers must conform to. Most jurisdictions have corresponding [statutory laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statutory_laws). In the UK busking regulation is not universal with most laws (if there are any) being governed by local councils.[^cite_note-19] Some towns in the British Isles limit the licenses issued to bagpipers because of the volume and difficulty of the instrument. In Great Britain, schemes that license buskers may also require performers to pass an audition before being granted a pitch; for example, Transport for London's licensed busking scheme on the London Underground selects performers through panel auditions.[^cite_note-20] Oxford City Council have decided to enact a [public spaces protection order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_spaces_protection_order). Some venues that do not regulate busking may still ask performers to abide by voluntary rules. Some places require a special [permit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/License) to use electronically amplified sound and may have limits on the [volume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loudness) of sound produced.[^cite_note-21] It is common law that buskers or others should not impede pedestrian traffic flow, block or otherwise obstruct entrances or exits, or do things that [endanger](3722991.md) the public. It is common law that any disturbing or noisy behaviors may not be conducted after certain hours in the night. These curfew limitations vary from jurisdiction to jurisdiction. It is common law that "performing blue" (i.e. using material that is sexually explicit or any vulgar or obscene remarks or gestures) is generally prohibited unless performing for an adults-only environment such as in a bar or pub.

In London, busking is prohibited in the entire area of the City of London. The [London Underground](17839.md) provides busking permits for up to 39 pitches across 25 central London stations.[^cite_note-22] Most London boroughs do not license busking, but they have optional powers, under the [London Local Authorities Act 2000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Local_Authorities_Act_2000?action=edit\&redlink=1), if there is sufficient reason to do so. Where these powers have not been adopted, councils can rely on other legislation including the [Environmental Protection Act 1990](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_Protection_Act_1990) to deal with noise nuisance from buskers and the [Highways Act 1980](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highways_Act_1980) to deal with obstructions. Camden Council is currently looking into further options to control the problem of nuisance buskers and the playing of amplified music to the detriment of local residents and businesses.[^cite_note-23] The Westminster City Council banned busking in [Leicester Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leicester_Square), previously a popular location, following a 2025 court ruling that busking was a "nuisance" and their repetitive performances were "psychological torture".[^cite_note-24]

Buskers may find themselves targeted by thieves due to the very open and public nature of their craft. Buskers may have their earnings, instruments or props stolen. One particular technique that thieves use against buskers is to pretend to make a donation while actually taking money out instead, a practice known as "dipping" or "skimming". [George Burns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Burns) described his days as a youthful busker this way:[^cite_note-CigarBurns-25]

> Sometimes the customers threw something in the hats. Sometimes they took something out of the hats. Sometimes they took the hats.
