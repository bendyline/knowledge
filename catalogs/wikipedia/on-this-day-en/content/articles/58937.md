**Diphtheria** is an [infection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infection) caused by the [bacterium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteria) *[Corynebacterium diphtheriae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corynebacterium_diphtheriae)*.[^cite_note-CDC2012Pink-2] Most infections are asymptomatic or have a mild [clinical course](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Course_\(medicine\)), but in some outbreaks, the mortality rate approaches 10%.[^cite_note-WHO2006-1]

Signs and symptoms may vary from mild to severe,[^cite_note-WHO2006-1] and usually start two to five days after exposure.[^cite_note-CDC2012Pink-2] Symptoms often develop gradually, beginning with a sore throat and [fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fever).[^cite_note-WHO2006-1] In severe cases, a grey or white patch develops in the throat called a pseudomembrane.[^cite_note-WHO2006-1][^cite_note-CDC2012Pink-2] This tough, leathery membrane slowly blocks the airway. Starting with a barking cough, similar to that observed in [croup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croup),[^cite_note-WHO2006-1] the neck may also swell, in part due to the enlargement of the [facial lymph nodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facial_lymph_nodes).[^cite_note-CDC2012Pink-2] The pseudomembrane can grow to cover a wide area of the throat, leading to strangulation and death.

Diphtheria can also involve the skin, eyes, or genitals, and can cause complications,[^cite_note-WHO2006-1][^cite_note-CDC2012Pink-2] including [myocarditis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myocarditis) (which in itself can result in an [abnormal heart rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiac_arrhythmia)), [inflammation of nerves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peripheral_neuropathy) (which can result in [paralysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paralysis)), [kidney problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proteinuria), and bleeding problems due to [low levels of platelets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrombocytopenia).[^cite_note-CDC2012Pink-2]

Diphtheria is usually spread between people by direct contact, [through the air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airborne_disease), or through contact with [contaminated objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fomite).[^cite_note-CDC2012Pink-2][^cite_note-5] Asymptomatic transmission and chronic infection are also possible.[^cite_note-CDC2012Pink-2] Different strains of *C. diphtheriae* are the main cause in the variability of lethality.[^cite_note-CDC2012Pink-2] The lethality and symptoms themselves are caused by the [exotoxin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exotoxin) produced by the bacteria.[^cite_note-WHO2006-1] Diagnosis can often be made based on the appearance of the throat, with confirmation by [microbiological culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microbiological_culture).[^cite_note-WHO2006-1] Previous infection may not protect against reinfection.[^cite_note-WHO2006-1]

A [diphtheria vaccine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diphtheria_vaccine) is effective for prevention and is available in several formulations.[^cite_note-CDC2012Pink-2] Three or four doses, given along with [tetanus vaccine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetanus_vaccine) and [pertussis vaccine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pertussis_vaccine), are recommended during childhood.[^cite_note-CDC2012Pink-2] Further doses of the diphtheria–tetanus vaccine are recommended every ten years.[^cite_note-CDC2012Pink-2] Protection can be verified by measuring the antitoxin level in the blood.[^cite_note-CDC2012Pink-2] Diphtheria can be prevented in those exposed, as well as treated with the [antibiotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibiotics) [erythromycin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erythromycin) or [benzylpenicillin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzylpenicillin).[^cite_note-CDC2012Pink-2] In severe cases a [tracheotomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tracheotomy) may be needed to open the airway.[^cite_note-WHO2006-1]

In 2015, 4,500 cases were officially reported worldwide, down from nearly 100,000 in 1980.[^cite_note-WHO2015-3] About a million cases a year are believed to have occurred before the 1980s.[^cite_note-WHO2006-1] Diphtheria currently occurs most often in [sub-Saharan Africa](27067.md), [South Asia](21566765.md), and [Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_in_Indonesia).[^cite_note-WHO2006-1][^cite_note-6] In 2015, it resulted in 2,100 deaths, down from 8,000 deaths in 1990.[^cite_note-GBD2015De-4][^cite_note-GDB2013-7]

In areas where it is still common, children are most affected.[^cite_note-WHO2006-1] It is rare in the [developed world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developed_world) due to widespread [vaccination](32473.md), but can re-emerge if vaccination rates decrease.[^cite_note-WHO2006-1][^cite_note-8] In the United States, 57 cases were reported between 1980 and 2004[^cite_note-CDC2012Pink-2] and only 11 cases from 2000 to 2025.[^cite_note-9] Death occurs in 5–10% of those diagnosed.[^cite_note-CDC2012Pink-2] The disease was first described in the 5th century BC by [Hippocrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocrates).[^cite_note-CDC2012Pink-2] The bacterium was identified in 1882 by [Edwin Klebs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Klebs).[^cite_note-CDC2012Pink-2]

## Signs and symptoms



Diphtheria can cause a swollen neck, sometimes referred to as a *bull neck*.

[^cite_note-CDC2012Pink-2]

The symptoms of diphtheria usually begin two to seven days after infection. They include fever of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or above; chills; [fatigue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatigue_\(medical\)); bluish skin coloration ([cyanosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanosis)); sore throat; [hoarseness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dysphonia); [cough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cough); headache; difficulty swallowing; painful swallowing; [difficulty breathing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortness_of_breath); rapid breathing; foul-smelling and bloodstained nasal discharge; and [lymphadenopathy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lymphadenopathy).[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-MedlinePlusEncyclopedia_Diphtheria-11] Within two to three days, diphtheria may destroy healthy tissues in the respiratory system. The dead tissue forms a thick, gray coating, known as a pseudo membrane, that can build up in the throat or nose. It can cover tissues in the nose, tonsils, voice box, and throat, making it very hard to breathe and swallow.[^cite_note-12] Symptoms can also include [cardiac arrhythmias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiac_arrhythmia), [myocarditis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myocarditis), and [cranial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cranial_nerve_palsy) and [peripheral nerve palsies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peripheral_nerve_palsy?action=edit\&redlink=1).[^cite_note-13]

### Diphtheritic croup

Laryngeal diphtheria can lead to a characteristic swollen neck and throat, or "bull neck". The swollen throat is often accompanied by a serious respiratory condition, characterized by a brassy or "barking" cough, [stridor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stridor), hoarseness, and difficulty breathing; and historically referred to variously as "diphtheritic croup",[^cite_note-loving-14] "true croup",[^cite_note-cormack-15][^cite_note-bennett-16] or sometimes simply as "croup".[^cite_note-beard-17] Diphtheritic croup is extremely rare in countries where [diphtheria vaccination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diphtheria_vaccination) is customary. As a result, the term "[croup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croup)" nowadays most often refers to an unrelated viral illness that produces similar but milder respiratory symptoms.[^cite_note-vanderpool-18]

## Transmission



A diphtheria skin lesion on the leg

Human-to-human transmission of diphtheria typically occurs through the air when an infected individual coughs or sneezes. Breathing in particles released from the infected individual leads to infection.[^cite_note-19] Contact with any lesions on the skin can also lead to transmission of diphtheria, but this cause is uncommon.[^cite_note-20] Indirect infections can occur, as well. If an infected individual touches a surface or object, the bacteria can be left behind and remain viable. Also, some evidence indicates diphtheria has the potential to be [zoonotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoonosis), but this has yet to be confirmed. *Corynebacterium ulcerans* has been found in some animals, which would suggest zoonotic potential.[^cite_note-21]

## Mechanism

[Diphtheria toxin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diphtheria_toxin) (DT) is produced only by *C. diphtheriae* infected with a certain type of [bacteriophage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteriophage).[^cite_note-22][^cite_note-23] [Toxinogenicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toxin) is determined by phage conversion (also called [lysogenic conversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lysogenic_conversion)); i.e., the ability of the bacterium to make DT changes as a consequence of infection by a particular phage. DT is encoded by the *tox* gene. Strains of corynephage are either *tox*<sup>+</sup> (e.g., corynephage β) or *tox*<sup>−</sup> (e.g., corynephage γ). The *tox* gene becomes integrated into the bacterial genome.[^cite_note-Holmes-24] The chromosome of *C. diphtheriae* has two different but functionally equivalent bacterial attachment sites (attB) for integration of β prophage into the chromosome.

The diphtheria toxin precursor is a [protein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein) of [molecular weight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_mass) 60 [kDa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalton_\(unit\)). Certain proteases, such as trypsin, selectively cleave DT to generate two [peptide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peptide) chains, amino-terminal fragment A (DT-A) and carboxyl-terminal fragment B (DT-B), which are held together by a [disulfide bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disulfide_bond).[^cite_note-Holmes-24] DT-B is a recognition subunit that gains entry of DT into the host cell by binding to the EGF-like domain of [heparin-binding EGF-like growth factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heparin-binding_EGF-like_growth_factor)  on the cell surface. This signals the cell to internalize the toxin within an [endosome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endosome) via [receptor-mediated endocytosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Receptor-mediated_endocytosis). Inside the endosome, DT is split by a trypsin-like [protease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protease) into DT-A and DT-B. The acidity of the endosome causes DT-B to create pores in the endosome membrane, thereby catalysing the release of DT-A into the [cytoplasm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cytoplasm).[^cite_note-Holmes-24]

Fragment A inhibits the synthesis of new proteins in the affected cell by catalyzing [ADP-ribosylation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ADP-ribosylation) of [elongation factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elongation_factor) [EF-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EF-2)—a protein that is essential to the translation step of protein synthesis. This ADP-ribosylation involves the transfer of an [ADP-ribose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ADP-ribose) from [NAD+](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAD+) to a [diphthamide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diphthamide) (a modified [histidine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Histidine)) residue within the EF-2 protein. Since EF-2 is needed for the moving of [tRNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TRNA) from the A-site to the P-site of the [ribosome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ribosome) during protein translation, ADP-ribosylation of EF-2 prevents protein synthesis.[^cite_note-entrez-25]

ADP-ribosylation of EF-2 is reversed *[in vitro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_vitro)* by high doses of [nicotinamide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicotinamide) (a form of vitamin B<sub>3</sub>), since this is one of the reaction's end products, and high amounts drive the reaction in the opposite direction.[^cite_note-26]

## Diagnosis

The current [clinical case definition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinical_case_definition) of diphtheria used by the [United States](3434750.md)' [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention](6811.md)  is based on both laboratory and clinical criteria.

### Laboratory criteria

- Isolation of *C. diphtheriae* from a Gram stain or throat culture from a clinical specimen.

  [^cite_note-MedlinePlusEncyclopedia_Diphtheria-11]

- Histopathologic diagnosis of diphtheria by Albert's stain.

### Toxin demonstration

- In vivo tests (guinea pig inoculation): Subcutaneous and intracutaneous tests.

  [^cite_note-27]

- In vitro test: Elek's gel precipitation test, detection of tox gene by PCR, ELISA, ICA.

### Clinical criteria

- Upper respiratory tract illness with sore throat.

- Low-grade fever (above 39 °C (102 °F) is rare).

- An adherent, dense, grey pseudomembrane covering the posterior aspect of the pharynx; in severe cases, it can extend to cover the entire tracheobronchial tree.

### Case classification

- Probable: a clinically compatible case that is not laboratory-confirmed, and is not epidemiologically linked to a laboratory-confirmed case.

- Confirmed: a clinically compatible case that is either laboratory-confirmed or epidemiologically linked to a laboratory-confirmed case.

Empirical treatment should generally be started in a patient in whom suspicion of diphtheria is high.

## Prevention

Main article: [Diphtheria vaccine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diphtheria_vaccine)



Diphtheria prevention poster from the UK (around 1939–1945)

Diphtheria prevention relies primarily on vaccination with the diphtheria toxoid, an inactivated form of the toxin produced by *Corynebacterium diphtheriae*. Introduced in the 1920s and widely adopted after World War II, the toxoid vaccine triggers strong antitoxin immunity that prevents the severe respiratory and systemic effects of the disease. Today, it is administered as part of combination vaccines such as [DTaP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DTaP) (for children) and [Tdap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tdap) or Td (for adolescents and adults), with booster doses recommended every 10 years to maintain protection. Widespread immunization has reduced diphtheria from a common childhood killer to a rare disease in most countries, though cases can still occur in areas with low vaccination coverage.[^cite_note-28]

[Pentavalent vaccines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentavalent_vaccine), which vaccinate against diphtheria and four other childhood diseases simultaneously (diphtheria, [tetanus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetanus), [pertussis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pertussis) \[whooping cough], [hepatitis B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepatitis_B), and [*haemophilus influenzae* type b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haemophilus_influenzae_type_b) \[Hib]), are frequently used in disease prevention programs in [developing countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developing_countries) by organizations such as [UNICEF](19698110.md).[^cite_note-29]

## Treatment

The disease may remain manageable, but in more severe cases, [lymph nodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lymph_nodes) in the neck may swell, and breathing and swallowing are more difficult. People in this stage should seek immediate medical attention, as obstruction in the throat may require [intubation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intubation) or a [tracheotomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tracheotomy). Abnormal cardiac rhythms can occur early in the course of the illness or weeks later, and can lead to [heart failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart_failure). Diphtheria can also cause paralysis in the eye, neck, throat, or respiratory muscles. Patients with severe cases are put in a hospital [intensive care unit](6332859.md), and given diphtheria [antitoxin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antitoxin) (consisting of [antibodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibodies) isolated from the [serum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serum_\(blood\)) of horses that have been challenged with diphtheria toxin).[^cite_note-\:1-30] Since antitoxin does not neutralize toxin that is already bound to tissues, delaying its administration increases the risk of death. Therefore, the decision to administer diphtheria antitoxin is based on clinical diagnosis, and should not await laboratory confirmation.[^cite_note-Pinkbook-31]

Antibiotics have not been demonstrated to affect the healing of local infection in diphtheria patients treated with antitoxin. Antibiotics are used in patients or carriers to eradicate *C. diphtheriae,* and prevent its transmission to others. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommends[^cite_note-32] either:

- [Metronidazole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metronidazole)

- [Erythromycin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erythromycin) is given (orally or by injection) for 14 days (40 mg/kg per day with a maximum of &#x32;*&#x20;*&#x67;/d), or

- [Procaine penicillin G](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procaine_penicillin_G) is given intramuscularly for 14 days (300,000 U/d for patients weighing <10 kg, and 600,000 U/d for those weighing >10 kg); patients with allergies to penicillin G or erythromycin can use [rifampin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rifampin) or [clindamycin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clindamycin).

In cases that progress beyond a throat infection, diphtheria toxin spreads through the blood and can lead to potentially life-threatening complications that affect other organs, such as the heart and kidneys. Damage to the heart caused by the toxin affects the heart's ability to pump blood or the kidneys' ability to clear wastes. It can also cause nerve damage, eventually leading to paralysis. About 40–50% of those left untreated can die.[^cite_note-33]

## History

### Discovery and science

Records of diphtheria begin as early as the 5th century BCE in the writings of [Hippocrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocrates). Epidemics were first described by [Aëtius of Amida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A%C3%ABtius_of_Amida) in the 6th century.[^cite_note-CDC2012Pink-2]

Before 1826, diphtheria was known by different names across the world. In [England](9316.md), it was known as "Boulogne sore throat", as the illness had spread from France. In 1826, [Pierre Bretonneau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Bretonneau) gave the disease the name *diphthérite* (from Greek διφθέρα, *diphthera* 'leather'), describing the appearance of pseudomembrane in the throat.[^cite_note-35][^cite_note-36]

In 1883 [Edwin Klebs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Klebs) identified the bacterium causing diphtheria,[^cite_note-37] and named it [Klebs–Loeffler bacterium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corynebacterium_diphtheriae). The club shape of this bacterium helped Edwin to differentiate it from other bacteria. Over time, it has been called *Microsporon diphtheriticum*, *Bacillus diphtheriae*, and *Mycobacterium diphtheriae*. Current nomenclature is *Corynebacterium diphtheriae*.[^cite_note-38]

&#x20;In 1884 German [bacteriologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteriologist) [Friedrich Loeffler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Loeffler) became the first person known to cultivate *C. diphtheriae*.[^cite_note-39] Löffler found that the bacteria were always present in the mucous membranes of the larynx and trachea of patients with diphtheria. In the process of finding the bacteria, he isolated the organism, cultured it, and reproduced the disease in susceptible animals (guinea pigs and rabbits), and then recovered the same bacillus from the diseased animal, thus fulfilling [Koch's postulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koch's_postulates). He believed there was a toxin that was made by the bacteria that caused the disease symptoms, and subsequently showed that some animals are immune to diphtheria.[^cite_note-40]&#x20;



A diphtheria immunisation scheme in London (1941)

In 1885 [Joseph P. O'Dwyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_O'Dwyer) introduced the O'Dwyer tube for laryngeal intubation in patients with an obstructed larynx. It soon replaced [tracheostomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tracheotomy) as the emergency diphtheric intubation method.[^cite_note-41]

In 1888 [Emile Roux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emile_Roux) and [Alexandre Yersin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandre_Yersin) showed that a substance ([exotoxin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exotoxin)) produced by *C. diphtheriae* caused symptoms of diphtheria in animals.[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43]

In 1890 [Shibasaburō Kitasato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitasato_Shibasabur%C5%8D) and [Emil von Behring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emil_von_Behring) immunized guinea pigs with heat-treated diphtheria toxin.[^cite_note-44] They also immunized goats and horses in the same way, and showed that an "[antitoxin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antitoxin)" made from [serum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serum_\(blood\)) of immunized animals could cure the disease in non-immunized animals. Behring used this antitoxin (now known to consist of [antibodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibodies) that neutralize the toxin produced by *C. diphtheriae*) for human trials in 1891, but they were unsuccessful. Successful treatment of human patients with horse-derived antitoxin began in 1894, after production and quantification of antitoxin had been optimized.[^cite_note-45][^cite_note-\:1-30] In 1901, Von Behring won the first [Nobel Prize in medicine](52502.md) for his work on diphtheria.[^cite_note-46]

In 1895 [H. K. Mulford Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._K._Mulford_Company) of [Philadelphia](50585.md) started production and testing of diphtheria antitoxin in the United States.[^cite_note-47] [Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Hallock_Park) and [Biggs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Biggs) described the method for producing serum from horses for use in diphtheria treatment.[^cite_note-48]

In 1897 [Paul Ehrlich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Ehrlich) developed a standardized unit of measure for diphtheria antitoxin. This was the first ever standardization of a biological product, and played an important role in future developmental work on sera and vaccines.[^cite_note-49]

In 1901, 10 of 11 inoculated [St. Louis](27687.md) children died from contaminated diphtheria antitoxin. The horse from which the antitoxin was derived died of [tetanus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetanus). This incident, coupled with a tetanus outbreak in [Camden, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camden,_New_Jersey),[^cite_note-50] played an important part in initiating federal regulation of biologic products.[^cite_note-51]

In 1905 Franklin Royer, from Philadelphia's Municipal Hospital, published a paper urging timely treatment for diphtheria and adequate doses of antitoxin.[^cite_note-52]

In 1906 [Clemens Pirquet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clemens_Pirquet) and [Béla Schick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%A9la_Schick) described [serum sickness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serum_sickness) in children receiving large quantities of horse-derived antitoxin. Schick and Pirquet reported that children treated for diphtheria with very large doses of horse-derived antitoxin frequently developed delayed symptoms, including swelling, fever, rash, and joint pain. It is now understood that these reactions occur because the human immune system recognizes the foreign antibodies in horse serum as antigens, triggering a cascade of immune responses. In the clinical experience of Pirquet and Schick, these reactions were self-limited rather than life-threatening, and children who survived diphtheria were not permanently harmed by the antitoxin treatment.[^cite_note-pmid11231202-53]

Between 1910 and 1911 Béla Schick developed the [Schick test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schick_test) to detect pre-existing immunity to diphtheria in an exposed person. Only those who had not been exposed to diphtheria were vaccinated. A massive, five-year campaign was coordinated by Dr. Schick. As a part of the campaign, 85 million pieces of literature were distributed by the [Metropolitan Life Insurance Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Life_Insurance_Company), with an appeal to parents to "Save your child from diphtheria."[^cite_note-54]

In 1914 [William H. Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_H._Park) investigated the use of carefully calibrated mixtures of diphtheria toxin and antitoxin to induce active immunity in experimental animals and later humans. Building on earlier antitoxin work, he systematically adjusted the relative concentrations to identify formulations that produced durable protective immunity while minimizing adverse reactions to the toxin–antitoxin complex. This mixture, TAT (for toxin-antitoxin), became the first vaccine against diphtheria.[^cite_note-auto-55]

In 1919, in [Dallas, Texas](53838.md), 10 children were killed and 60 others made seriously ill by toxic antitoxin, which had passed the tests of the [New York State Health Department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_State_Department_of_Health). The manufacturer of the antitoxin, the [Mulford Company of Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merck_Sharp_&_Dohme), paid damages in every case.[^cite_note-56]

In 1926 Alexander Thomas Glenny increased the effectiveness of diphtheria [toxoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toxoid) (a modified version of the toxin used for vaccination) by treating it with aluminum salts.[^cite_note-57] Vaccination with toxoid was not widely used until the early 1930s.[^cite_note-58] In 1939, Dr. [Nora Wattie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nora_Wattie), who was the [Principal Medical Officer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_Medical_Officer) (Maternity and Child Welfare) of Glasgow between 1934– 1964,[^cite_note-59] introduced immunisation clinics across [Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glasgow), and promoted mother and child health education, resulting in virtual eradication of the infection in the city.[^cite_note-60]

Widespread vaccination pushed cases in the United States down from 4.4 per 100,000 inhabitants in 1932 to 2.0 in 1937. In [Nazi Germany](21212.md), where authorities preferred treatment and isolation over vaccination (until about 1939–1941), cases rose over the same period from 6.1 to 9.6 per 100,000 inhabitants.[^cite_note-61]

### List of epidemics

- In 1613 [Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_in_Spain) experienced an epidemic of diphtheria, referred to as *El Año de los Garrotillos* (The Year of Strangulations).

  [^cite_note-\:0-34]

- In 1705 the [Mariana Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariana_Islands) experienced an epidemic of diphtheria and [typhus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhus) simultaneously, reducing the population to about 5,000 people.

  [^cite_note-62]

- In 1735 a diphtheria epidemic swept through [New England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_England).

  [^cite_note-63]

  &#x20;Then known as "throat distemper," in one New Hampshire town, 32% of children under 10 died. Of the persons known to have acquired diphtheria, nearly 40% died. [Noah Webster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noah_Webster) wrote: "It was literally the plague among children. Many families lost three of four children—many lost all."

  [^cite_note-auto-55]

- In 1856 [Victor Fourgeaud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Fourgeaud) described an epidemic of diphtheria in [California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_in_California).

  [^cite_note-64]

- In 1925 in [Nome, Alaska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nome,_Alaska); the ["Great Race of Mercy"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1925_serum_run_to_Nome) to deliver diphtheria antitoxin is now celebrated by the [Iditarod Trail Sled Dog Race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iditarod_Trail_Sled_Dog_Race).

  [^cite_note-65]

  &#x20;During the 1920s, an annual estimate of 100,000 to 200,000 diphtheria cases and 13,000 to 15,000 deaths occurred in the United States.

  [^cite_note-Pinkbook-31]

- Between June 1942 and February 1943, 714 cases of diphtheria were recorded at [Sham Shui Po Barracks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sham_Shui_Po_Barracks) in [Hong Kong](13404.md), resulting in 112 deaths because the [Imperial Japanese Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Japanese_Army) did not release supplies of anti-diphtheria serum.

  [^cite_note-66]

- In 1943 diphtheria outbreaks accompanied war and disruption in [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe_in_World_War_II). The 1 million cases in Europe resulted in 50,000 deaths.

  [^cite_note-67]

  [^cite_note-68]

- During 1948 in [Kyoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto), 68 of 606 children died after diphtheria immunization due to improper manufacture of aluminum phosphate toxoid.

  [^cite_note-69]

- In 1974 the [World Health Organization](33583.md) included [DPT vaccine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DPT_vaccine) in their [Expanded Programme on Immunization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expanded_Programme_on_Immunization) for [developing countries](78449.md).

  [^cite_note-70]

  [^cite_note-71]

- In 1975 an outbreak of cutaneous diphtheria in [Seattle, Washington](11388236.md), was reported.

  [^cite_note-72]

- After the breakup of the former [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) in 1991, vaccination rates in its constituent countries fell so low that an explosion of diphtheria cases occurred. In 1991, 2,000 cases of diphtheria occurred in the USSR. Between 1991 and 1998, as many as 200,000 cases were reported in the [Commonwealth of Independent States](36870.md), resulting in 5,000 deaths.

  [^cite_note-\:0-34]

  &#x20;In 1994, the [Russian Federation](25391.md) had 39,703 diphtheria cases. By contrast, in 1990, only 1,211 cases were reported.

  [^cite_note-73]

* In early May 2010 a case of diphtheria was diagnosed in [Port-au-Prince](57391.md), [Haiti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_in_Haiti), after the devastating [2010 Haiti earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Haiti_earthquake). The 15-year-old male patient died while workers searched for antitoxin.

  [^cite_note-74]

* In 2013 three children died of diphtheria in [Hyderabad, India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyderabad,_India).

  [^cite_note-75]

The six-year-old child who died of the illness had not been previously vaccinated due to parental [opposition to vaccination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaccine_controversies).

  [^cite_note-76]

  &#x20;It was the first case of diphtheria in the country since 1986, as reported by the Spanish daily newspaper *[El Mundo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Mundo_\(Spain\)),*

  [^cite_note-77]

  &#x20;or from 1998, as reported by the WHO.

  [^cite_note-78]

* In March 2016 a three-year-old girl died of diphtheria in the University Hospital of [Antwerp](32149462.md), [Belgium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_in_Belgium).

  [^cite_note-79]

* In June 2016 a three-year-old, five-year-old, and seven-year-old girl died of diphtheria in [Kedah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kedah), [Malacca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malacca), and [Sabah](28678.md), [Malaysia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_in_Malaysia).

  [^cite_note-80]

* In January 2017 more than 300 cases were recorded in [Venezuela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_in_Venezuela).

  [^cite_note-81]

  [^cite_note-82]

* In 2017 outbreaks occurred in a [Rohingya refugee camp in Bangladesh](53544313.md), and amongst children unvaccinated due to the [Yemeni Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yemeni_Civil_War_\(2014%E2%80%93present\)).

  [^cite_note-83]

* In November and December 2017 an outbreak of diphtheria occurred in [Indonesia](14579.md), with more than 600 cases found and 38 fatalities.

  [^cite_note-84]

* In November 2019 two cases of diphtheria occurred in the [Lothian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lothian) area of [Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_in_Scotland).

  [^cite_note-85]

  &#x20;Additionally, in November 2019, an unvaccinated eight-year-old boy died of diphtheria in [Athens](1216.md), [Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_in_Greece).

  [^cite_note-86]

* In July 2022 two cases of diphtheria occurred in northern [New South Wales](21654.md), [Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_in_Australia).

  [^cite_note-87]

* In October 2022 there was an outbreak of diphtheria at the former [Manston airfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAF_Manston), a former [Ministry of Defence](203793.md) (MoD) site in [Kent](16766.md), [England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_in_England), which had been converted to an [asylum seeker processing centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asylum_seekers_in_the_United_Kingdom). The capacity of the processing centre was 1,000 people, although about 3,000 were living at the site, with some accommodated in tents. The [Home Office](225695.md), the government department responsible for asylum seekers, refused to confirm the number of cases.

  [^cite_note-88]

[UK Health Security Agency](64959819.md) (UKHSA) issued a statement saying specialists have been providing public health support following confirmation of the diphtheria case at a primary school in Luton. The agency said it is working closely with local and national partners "to ensure all necessary public health measures are implemented" following the discovery of the new case. The statement added: "We have conducted a risk assessment and close contacts of the case have been identified, and where appropriate, vaccination and advice will be given to prevent the spread of the infection."

  [^cite_note-89]

* In May 2026, an outbreak occurred in Australia Northern Territory with 133 cases reported to the National Notifiable Disease Surveillance System with one death. The outbreak is spreading into aboriginal land across the state border in South Australia (6 cases), as well as in the Kimberley region of Western Australia (79 cases) and Queensland (5 cases).

  [^cite_note-90]

* In August 2026 a four-year-old girl (not vaccinated) died of diphtheria in [Palermo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palermo), [Italy](14532.md). About 10 relatives had tested positive, while the girl’s family members were given antibiotic prophylaxis and her siblings were vaccinated after her death. A 4-year-old cousin also tested positive was hospitalized in isolation.

  [^cite_note-91]

### Notable deaths

On April 8, 1861, [Elisha Graves Otis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elisha_Graves_Otis) died from diphtheria at age 49.[^cite_note-Invent.org_Article-92]

In December 1863 Eliza Arabella Garfield, the eldest daughter of [James A. Garfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_A._Garfield), died from diphtheria at the age of three, right before Christmas.[^cite_note-93]

In 1878 [Princess Alice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princess_Alice_of_the_United_Kingdom) ([Queen Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Victoria)'s second daughter) and her family became infected with diphtheria; Princess Alice and her four-year-old daughter, [Princess Marie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princess_Marie_of_Hesse_and_by_Rhine_\(1874%E2%80%931878\)), both died. Their deaths caused the Sanitary Journal to warn readers of the “kiss of death” that may have spread the disease through the royal family.[^cite_note-94]

On December 7, 1892, [Eleanor Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleanor_Roosevelt)'s mother, [Anna Hall Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Hall_Roosevelt), died of diphtheria at age 29. Five months later, Elliott Roosevelt Jr., Eleanor's younger brother, died of the disease.[^cite_note-95]

On January 7, 1904, [Ruth Cleveland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruth_Cleveland) died of diphtheria at age 12 in [Princeton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey). She was the eldest daughter of former [US President](24113.md) [Grover Cleveland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grover_Cleveland) and the former [First Lady](10847.md), [Frances Folsom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frances_Folsom).[^cite_note-96]
