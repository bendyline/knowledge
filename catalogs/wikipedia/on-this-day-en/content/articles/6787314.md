A **political demonstration** is an action by a mass group or collection of groups of people in favor of a [political](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political) or other cause or people partaking in a [protest](266137.md) against a cause of concern; it often consists of walking in a mass march formation and either beginning with or meeting at a designated endpoint, or **rally**, in order to hear speakers. It is different from [mass meeting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_meeting).

Demonstrations may include actions such as blockades and [sit-ins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sit-in). They can be either [nonviolent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonviolent) or [violent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Violent), with participants often referring to violent demonstrations as "[militant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Militant_\(word\))." Depending on the circumstances, a demonstration may begin as nonviolent and escalate to violence. [Law enforcement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_enforcement_agency), such as [riot police](1148542.md), may become involved in these situations. [Police involvement at protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protest_policing) is ideally to protect the participants and their right to assemble. However, officers do not always fulfill this responsibility and it is well-documented that many cases of protest intervention result in power abuse.[^cite_note-1] It may be to prevent clashes between rival groups, or to prevent a demonstration from spreading and turning into a [riot](146704.md).

## History

The term has been in use since the mid-19th century, as was the term "**monster meeting**", which was coined initially with reference to the huge assemblies of protesters inspired by [Daniel O'Connell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_O'Connell) (1775–1847) in Ireland.[^cite_note-OED-2] Demonstrations are a form of [activism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activism), usually taking the form of a public gathering of people in a rally or walking in a **march**. Thus, the opinion is *demonstrated* to be significant by gathering in a crowd associated with that opinion.

Demonstrations can promote a viewpoint (either positive or negative) regarding a public issue, especially relating to a perceived [grievance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grievance) or [social injustice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_injustice). A demonstration is usually considered more successful if more people participate. Research shows that anti-government demonstrations occur more frequently in affluent countries than in poor ones.[^cite_note-3]

Widely recognized political demonstrations include the [Boston Tea Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston_Tea_Party), [March on Washington](255046.md), and the [George Floyd protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Floyd_protests).[^cite_note-4] However, political demonstrations have been occurring for many centuries before these famous ones.

## Types



During the [American Civil Rights Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_Rights_Movement) and the [March on Washington](255046.md), leaders marched from the [Washington Monument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_Monument) to the [Lincoln Memorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln_Memorial), August 28, 1963.

Video of a demonstration in Argentina to commemorate the [1976 coup d'état](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1976_Argentine_coup_d'%C3%A9tat)

There are many types of demonstrations, including a variety of elements. These may include:

- Marches, in which a [parade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parade) demonstrate while moving along a set route.

- Rallies, in which people gather to listen to speakers or musicians.

- [Picketing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picketing_\(protest\)), in which people surround an area (normally an employer).

- [Sit-ins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sit-in), in which demonstrators occupy an area, sometimes for a stated period but sometimes indefinitely, until they feel their issue has been addressed, or they are otherwise convinced or forced to leave.

- [Nudity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nudity_and_protest), in which they protest naked – here the antagonist may give in before the demonstration happens to avoid embarrassment.

Demonstrations are sometimes spontaneous gatherings, but are also utilized as a tactical choice by movements. They often form part of a larger campaign of [nonviolent resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonviolent_resistance), often also called [civil resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_resistance). Demonstrations are generally staged in public, but private demonstrations are certainly possible, especially if the demonstrators wish to influence the opinions of a small or very specific group of people. Demonstrations are usually physical gatherings, but virtual or [online](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online) demonstrations are certainly possible.

Topics of demonstrations often deal with [political](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics), [economic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics), and [social](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society) issues. Particularly with controversial issues, sometimes groups of people opposed to the aims of a demonstration may themselves launch a counter-demonstration with the aim of opposing the demonstrators and presenting their view. Clashes between demonstrators and counter-demonstrators may turn violent.

[Government-organized demonstrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government-organized_demonstrations) are demonstrations which are organized by a government. The [Islamic Republic of Iran](14653.md),[^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6] the [People's Republic of China](5405.md),[^cite_note-7] [Republic of Cuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Cuba),[^cite_note-8] the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union)[^cite_note-9] and [Argentina](18951905.md),[^cite_note-10] among other nations, have had government-organized demonstrations.

## Times and locations



[Orange Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange_Revolution) demonstrations lasted so long that demonstrators set up tents.



Crowd in front of a McDonald's in [Wangfujing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wangfujing) on the [2011 Chinese pro-democracy protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Chinese_pro-democracy_protests)



[2013 Peace March for Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2013_Peace_March_for_Hungary?action=edit\&redlink=1) in Budapest

Sometimes the date or location chosen for the demonstration is of historical or cultural significance, such as the [anniversary](477069.md) of some event that is relevant to the topic of the demonstration.

Locations are also frequently chosen because of some relevance to the issue at hand. For example, if a demonstration is targeted at issues relating to foreign [nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nation), the demonstration may take place at a location associated with that nation, such as an [embassy](8970.md) of the nation in question.

While fixed demonstrations may take place in [pedestrian zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedestrian_zones), larger marches usually take place on roads. It may happen with or without an official authorization.

## Nonviolence or violence

&#x20;Protest marches and demonstrations are a common nonviolent tactic. They are thus one tactic available to proponents of strategic [nonviolence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonviolence). However, the reasons for avoiding the use of violence may also derive, not from a general doctrine of [nonviolence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonviolence) or [pacifism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacifism), but from considerations relating to the particular situation that is faced, including its legal, cultural and power-political dimensions: this has been the case in many campaigns of [civil resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_resistance).[^cite_note-11]



Demonstration turned riot at the US Capitol Building on [January 6, 2021](66307490.md).

A common tactic used by nonviolent campaigners is the "dilemma demonstration." Activist trainer Daniel Hunter describes this term as covering "actions that force the target to either let you do what you want, or be shown as unreasonable as they stop you from doing it".[^cite_note-12] A study by Srdja Popovic and Sophia McClennen won the 2020 Brown Democracy Medal for its examination of 44 examples of dilemma demonstrations and the ways in which they were used to achieve goals within civil resistance campaigns.[^cite_note-13]

Some demonstrations and [protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protests) can turn, at least partially, into [riots](146704.md) or mob [violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Violence) against objects such as [automobiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automobile) and [businesses](39206.md), bystanders and the [police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police).[^cite_note-14] Police and military authorities often use [non-lethal force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-lethal_force) or less-lethal weapons, such as [tasers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroshock_gun), [rubber bullets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubber_bullet), [pepper spray](157302.md), and [tear gas](1202646.md) against demonstrators in these situations.[^cite_note-15] Sometimes violent situations are caused by the preemptive or offensive use of these weapons which can provoke, destabilize, or escalate a conflict.

The protests following the [murder of George Floyd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murder_of_George_Floyd) are well-known examples of political demonstrations addressing [racial injustice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_inequality_in_the_United_States) and [police brutality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_brutality). These demonstrations, which spread across the United States and around the world, brought attention to systemic issues within law enforcement and the broader society. This movement highlighted the importance of political demonstrations in driving social change and influencing public policy, but also showed how protests can turn violent through police intervention.[^cite_note-16]

As a known tool to prevent the infiltration by [agents provocateurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agents_provocateurs),[^cite_note-17] the organizers of large or controversial assemblies may deploy and coordinate [demonstration marshals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demonstration_marshal), also called stewards.[^cite_note-OCSE2007-18][^cite_note-Bryan06-19]

## Policing

This section is an excerpt from [Protest policing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protest_policing).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Protest_policing\&action=edit)]



[Seattle Police Department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seattle_Police_Department) officers at the [George Floyd protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Floyd_protests), 2020

[Protest policing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protest_policing) or public order policing is part of a state’s response to [political dissent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_dissent) and [social movements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_movement). [Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police) maintenance of public order during protest is an essential component of [liberal democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_democracy), with [military response to protest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_response_to_protest) being more common under [authoritarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authoritarian) regimes.[^cite_note-Protest_policing_\:0-20]

Australasian, European, and North American democratic states have all experienced increased surveillance of protest movements and more militarized protest policing since 1995 and through the first decades of the 21st century.[^cite_note-Protest_policing_\:1-21][^cite_note-Protest_policing_\:3-22]

[Criminalization of dissent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocratic_legalism) is legislation or law enforcement that penalizes political dissent. It may also be accomplished through media that controls public discourse to delegitimize critics of the state. Study of protest criminalization places protest policing in a broader framework of criminology and sociology of law.[^cite_note-Protest_policing_\:1-21]

### International

The right to demonstrate peacefully is guaranteed by international conventions, in particular by the articles 21 and 22 of the [International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Covenant_on_Civil_and_Political_Rights) ([right of peaceful assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_assembly) and [right of association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_of_association)). Its implementation is monitored by the [United Nations special rapporteur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_special_rapporteur) on the right of peaceful assembly and association. In 2019, its report expressed alarm at the restrictions on the freedom of peaceful assembly:[^cite_note-23]

> The Special Rapporteur has expressed concern regarding laws adopted in many countries that impose harsh restrictions on assemblies, including provisions relating to blanket bans, geographical restrictions, mandatory notifications and authorizations. \[...] The need for prior authorization in order to hold peaceful protests \[is] contrary to international law \[...].

### Australia

A report released by the Human Rights Law Centre in 2024 states that based on British common law, "*Australian courts regard \[the right to assembly] as a core part of a democratic system of government.*" However, there are a number of limitations placed on demonstrations and protest under state, territory and federal legislation, with forty-nine laws introduced regarding them since 2004.[^cite_note-24]

### Brazil

[Freedom of assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_assembly) in [Brazil](3383.md) is granted by art. 5th, item XVI, of the [Constitution of Brazil](26847507.md) (1988).

### Germany

In Germany, the right to protest is considered a [fundamental right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_rights_in_the_German_Constitution) in the *[Grundgesetz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grundgesetz)*.[^cite_note-\:0-25] For open-air assemblies, this right may be restricted.[^cite_note-\:0-25]

### Russia

Main article: [Freedom of assembly in Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_assembly_in_Russia)

[Freedom of assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_assembly) in the [Russian Federation](25391.md) is granted by Art. 31 of the [Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_the_Russian_Federation) adopted in 1993:&#x20;

> Citizens of the Russian Federation shall have the right to gather peacefully, without weapons, and to hold meetings, rallies, demonstrations, marches and [pickets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picketing_\(protest\)).[^cite_note-26]

Demonstrations and protests are further regulated by the Federal Law of the Russian Federation No.54-FZ "*On Meetings, Rallies, Demonstrations, Marches and Pickets*". If the assembly in public is expected to involve more than one participant, its organisers are obliged to notify executive or local self-government authorities of the upcoming event few days in advance in writing. However, legislation does not foresee an authorisation procedure, hence the authorities have no right to prohibit an assembly or change its place unless it threatens the security of participants or is planned to take place near hazardous facilities, important [railways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_transport_in_Russia), [viaducts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viaduct), [pipelines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipeline_transport), [high voltage electric power lines](1205325.md#Power_lines), [prisons](19008450.md), [courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court), presidential residences or in the border control zone. The right to gather can also be restricted in close proximity of cultural and historical monuments.

### Singapore

Main article: [Public demonstrations in Singapore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_demonstrations_in_Singapore)

Public demonstrations in [Singapore](27318.md) are not common, in part because cause-related events require a licence from the authorities. Such laws include the Public Entertainment and Meetings Act and the Public Order Act.

### United Kingdom



Demonstration in front of the [British parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_parliament)

Under the [Serious Organised Crime and Police Act 2005](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serious_Organised_Crime_and_Police_Act_2005) and the [Terrorism Act 2006](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrorism_Act_2006), there are areas designated as 'protected sites' where people are not allowed to go. Previously, these were military bases and nuclear power stations, but the law changed in 2007 to include other, generally political areas, such as [Downing Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downing_Street), the [Palace of Westminster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_Westminster), and the headquarters of [MI5](100076.md) and [MI6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MI6). Previously, [trespassers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trespasser) to these areas could not be arrested if they had not committed another crime and agreed to be escorted out, but this will change following amendments to the law.[^cite_note-27]

[Human rights](13831.md) groups fear the powers could hinder peaceful protest. [Nick Clegg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_Clegg), the then [Liberal Democrat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Democrats_\(UK\)) home affairs spokesman, said: "I am not aware of vast troops of trespassers wanting to invade MI5 or MI6, still less running the gauntlet of security checks in [Whitehall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitehall) and Westminster to make a point. It's a sledgehammer to crack a nut." [Liberty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberty_\(pressure_group\)), the [civil liberties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_liberties) pressure group, said the measure was "excessive".[^cite_note-28]

One of the biggest demonstration in the UK was the people vote march, on 19 October 2019, with around 1 million demonstrators related to the [Brexit](41688778.md).

In 2021, the [Supreme Court of the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_the_United_Kingdom) ruled that blocking roads can be a lawful way to demonstrate.[^cite_note-29]

### United States

The [First Amendment of the United States Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Amendment_of_the_United_States_Constitution) specifically allows the [freedom of assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_assembly) as part of a measure to facilitate the redress of such grievances. "Amendment I: Congress shall make no law ... abridging ... the right of the people peaceably to assemble, and to petition the Government for a redress of grievances."[^cite_note-30]

A growing trend in the [United States](3434750.md) has been the implementation of "[free speech zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_speech_zone)", or fenced-in areas which are often far-removed from the event which is being protested; critics of free-speech zones argue that they go against the First Amendment of the [United States Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Constitution) by their very nature, and that they lessen the impact the demonstration might otherwise have had. In many areas it is required to get permission from the government to hold a demonstration.[^cite_note-31]
