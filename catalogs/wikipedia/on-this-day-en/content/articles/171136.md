A **public utility company** (usually just **utility**) is an organization that maintains the [infrastructure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrastructure) for a [public service](1291782.md) (often also providing a service using that infrastructure). Public utilities are subject to forms of [public control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_ownership) and [regulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulation) ranging from local community-based groups to statewide [government monopolies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_monopoly).

Public utilities are meant to supply goods and services that are considered essential; [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_industry), [gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_industry), [electricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_utility), [telephone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephone_company), [waste disposal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waste_management_industry), and other [communication systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communications_system) represent much of the public utility market. The transmission lines used in the transportation of [electricity](9550.md), or natural [gas pipelines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipeline_transport), have [natural monopoly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_monopoly) characteristics. A [monopoly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monopoly) can occur when it finds the best way to minimize its costs through economies of scale to the point where other companies cannot compete with it.[^cite_note-marketbusinessnews-1] If the infrastructure already exists in a given area, minimal benefit is gained through competing. In other words, these [industries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industry_City) are characterized by *[economies of scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economies_of_scale)* in production.[^cite_note-2] Though it can be mentioned that these natural monopolies are handled or watched by a public utilities commission, or an institution that represents the government.[^cite_note-marketbusinessnews-1]

There are many different types of public utilities. Some, especially large [companies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Company), offer multiple products, such as electricity and natural gas. Other companies specialize in a specific product, such as water. Modern public utilities may also be partially (or completely) sourced from clean and [renewable energy](25784.md) in order to produce sustainable electricity. Of these, [wind turbines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_turbine) and [solar panels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_panel) are those used most frequently.

Whether broadband internet access should be a public utility is a question that was being discussed with the rise of internet usage. This is a question that was being asked due to the telephone service being considered a public utility. Since arguably broadband internet access has taken over telephone service, perhaps it should be a public utility. [The Federal Communications Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Communications_Commission) (FCC) in the United States in 2015 made its stance on this issue clear.[^cite_note-marketbusinessnews-1] Due to the telephone service having been considered a public utility, the FCC made broadband internet access a public utility in the United States.[^cite_note-marketbusinessnews-1]

## Management

The utility model has historically been considered most applicable in the case of [natural monopoly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_monopoly). Such monopolies are defined by high [barriers to entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barriers_to_entry) or large [network effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_effects) making it more cost-efficient to do business through a single firm or small number of firms. Utilities are usually capital-intensive businesses with unusually large economies of scale and high fixed costs associated with building and operating the infrastructure, e.g. power plants, telephone lines and water treatment facilities.[^cite_note-3] However, such contexts can be fluid, particularly in the face of technological or regulatory [innovation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Innovation). Regulators can choose to ensure some level of competition in an effort to improve service, as has been witnessed with [electricity retailing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electricity_retailing) and customer choice[^cite_note-4]. Also, over time some aspects of a traditional utilities' monopoly position can erode. For instance, in the [OECD](33853117.md), wholesale [electricity generation](9540.md) markets have become far more decentralised with the advent of [solar power](13690575.md) and [wind power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_power). Nevertheless, infrastructure used to distribute utility products and services and other aspects of public service often remain more centralised. For example, ensuring the safety and stability of electric transmission networks and electricity provision still often requires heavy government involvement [^cite_note-5]. Similar hybrid approaches to regulating utlities have been made in some countries for [telecommunications](33094374.md), and some types of [public transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_transit). For example, the United Kingdom disaggregated [British Rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Rail) into a government utility governing the rails itself ([Network Rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_Rail)), and separate private (or even foreign public) providers of train service. As of 2026, the UK government is discussing reversing this partial privatisation. Even [postal services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mail) have been mooted as potentially privatisable, though this is again heavily contested. Conservative political parties often promise [liberalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberalization), [deregulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deregulation) and [privatization](24661.md) of public utilities. Certain sectors such as [gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas) and [oil](4207510.md) are often nationalised e.g. by countries in [OPEC](166346.md), because profits from these are also seen to be a form of [public good](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_good), though profits themselves are neither service nor infrastructure.&#x20;

Key players in the public utility sector include:[^cite_note-investopedia-6]

- **Generators** produce or collect the specific product to be used by customers: for example, electricity or water.

- **Network operators** (grid operators, regional network operators, and [distribution network operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_network_operator)) sell access to their networks to retail service providers, who deliver the product to the end user.

- **Traders and marketers** buy and sell the actual product and create further complex structured products, combined services and derivative products. Depending on the product structure, these companies may provide utilities and businesses with a reliable supply of a product like electricity at a stable, predictable price, or a shorter-term supply at a more volatile price.

- **Service providers and retailers** are the last segment in the [supply chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supply_chain), selling directly to the final [consumer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumer). In some markets, final consumers can choose their own [retail](183515.md) service provider.

Public utilities must pursue the following objective given the [social responsibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_responsibility) their services attribute to them:

- Ensuring services are of the highest quality and responsive to the needs and wishes of [patients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patients'_rights);

- Ensuring that health services are effectively targeted to improve the health of local populations;

- Improving the efficiency of the services so the volume of well-targeted effective services is the widest, given the available resources.

  [^cite_note-Gilardoni2015-7]

The management of public utilities continues to be important for local and general [governments](12229.md). By creating, expanding, and improving public utilities, a governmental body may attempt to improve its image or attract investment. Traditionally, public services have been provided by public legal entities, which operate much like corporations, but differ in that profit is not necessary for a functional business. A significant factor in government ownership has been to reduce the [risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk) that an activity, if left to private initiative, may be considered not sufficiently [profitable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Profit_\(economics\)) and neglected. Many utilities are essential for human life, national defense, or commerce, and the risk of public harm with mismanagement is considerably greater than with other goods. The principle of [universality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universality_\(philosophy\)) of utilities maintains that these services are best owned by, and operating for, the public. The government and the [society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society) itself would like to see these services being economically accessible to all or most of the population. Furthermore, other economic reasons support the idea: public services need huge [investments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Investment_\(macroeconomics\)) in [infrastructures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrastructure), crucial for competitiveness but with a slow [return of capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Return_of_capital); last, technical difficulties can occur in the management of a plurality of networks, for example in the city [subsoil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsoil).[^cite_note-Gilardoni2015-7]

Public pressure for [renewable energy](25784.md) as a replacement for legacy fossil fuel power has steadily increased since the 1980s. As the technology needed to source the necessary amount of [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) from renewable sources is still under study, public [energy policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_policy) has been focused on short-term alternatives such as natural gas (which still produces substantial [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide)) or [nuclear power](22153.md). In 2021 a power and utilities industry outlook report by Deloitte identified a number of trends for the utilities industry:

- Enhanced [competition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Competition), sparked by regulations such as [FERC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Energy_Regulatory_Commission)'s Order 2222 that open up the market to smaller, innovative firms using renewable energy sources, like wind or [solar power](13690575.md)

- Expansions in infrastructure, to manage new renewable energy sources

- Greater [electrification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrification) of transportation, and longer-range batteries for cars and trucks

- [Oil companies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_companies) and other traditional-energy players entering the renewable-energy field

- A greater emphasis on disaster readiness

  [^cite_note-8]

## Finance

See also: [Infrastructure and economics § Ownership and financing of infrastructure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrastructure_and_economics#Ownership_and_financing_of_infrastructure)

Issues faced by public utilities include:

- Service area: [regulators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Competition_regulator) need to balance the economic needs of the companies and the [social equity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_equity) needed to guarantee access to [primary services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_servicer) for everyone.

- [Autonomy](191145.md): Economic efficiency requires that markets be left to work by themselves with little [intervention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intervention_\(law\)). Such instances are often not equitable for some consumers that might be priced out of the market.

- [Pricing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pricing): Equity requires that all citizens get the service at a fair price.

  [^cite_note-9]

Alternative pricing methods include:

- **Average [production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Production_\(economics\)) costs**: the utility calculates the break-even point and then sets the prices equal to [average costs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Average_cost). The equity issue is basically overcome since most of the market is being served. As a defect regulated firms do not have [incentives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incentive) to minimize [costs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost).

- **[Rate of return](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rate_of_return) regulation**: regulators let the firms set and charge any price, as long as the rate of return on [invested capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invested_capital) does not exceed a certain rate. This method is flexible and allows for pricing freedom, forcing regulators to monitor prices. The drawback is that this method could lead to [overcapitalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overcapitalization). For example, if the rate of return is set at five percent, then the firm can charge a higher price simply by investing more in capital than what is actually needed (i.e., 5% of \$10 million is greater than 5% of \$6 million).

- **Price cap regulation**: regulators directly set a limit on the maximum price. This method can result in a loss of service area. One benefit of this method is that it gives firms an incentive to seek cost-reducing technologies as a strategy to increase utility [profits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Profit_\(economics\)).

Utility stocks are considered stable investments because they typically provide regular [dividends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dividend) to [shareholders](146081.md) and have more stable demand.[^cite_note-investopedia-6] Even in periods of [economic downturns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_downturn_of_2008) characterized by low [interest rates](163115.md), such stocks are attractive because [dividend yields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dividend_yield) are usually greater than those of other stocks, so the utility sector is often part of a long-term [buy-and-hold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buy_and_hold) strategy.[^cite_note-investopedia-6]

Utilities require expensive critical infrastructure which needs regular [maintenance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maintenance_\(technical\)) and replacement. Consequently, the industry is [capital intensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_intensive_industry), requiring regular access to the capital markets for external financing. A utility's [capital structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_structure) may have a significant debt component, which exposes the company to [interest rate risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interest_rate_risk).[^cite_note-kalkine-10] Should rates rise, the company must offer higher yields to attract [bond](60737.md) [investors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Investor), driving up the utility's interest expenses. If the company's debt load and interest expense become too large, its credit rating will deteriorate, further increasing the cost of capital and potentially limiting access to the capital markets.[^cite_note-11]

### United Kingdom and Ireland

In the [United Kingdom](31717.md) and [Ireland](147575.md), the state, private firms, and charities ran the traditional public utilities. For instance, the [Sanitary Districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanitary_District) were established in [England and Wales](275187.md) in 1875 and in Ireland in 1878.

The term can refer to the set of services provided by various organizations that are used in everyday life by the public, such as: [electricity generation](9540.md), [electricity retailing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electricity_retailing), [electricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_utility) supplies, [natural gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas_utility) supplies, [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_utility) supplies, [sewage works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sewage_treatment), [sewage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_industry) systems and [broadband](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadband) [internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet) services.[^cite_note-14] They are regulated by [Ofgem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ofgem), [Ofwat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ofwat), [Ofcom](411296.md), the [Water Industry Commission for Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_Industry_Commission_for_Scotland) and the [Utility Regulator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_regulators_in_the_United_Kingdom#Utilities) in the United Kingdom, and the [Commission for Regulation of Utilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commission_for_Regulation_of_Utilities) and the [Commission for Communications Regulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commission_for_Communications_Regulation) in the Republic of Ireland. Disabled community transport services may occasionally be included within the definition. They were mostly privatised in the UK during the 1980s.

### United States



Map of the two major and three minor [NERC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_Electric_Reliability_Corporation) interconnections, and the nine Regional Reliability Councils

The first public utility in the United States was a [grist mill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grist_mill) erected on [Mother Brook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mother_Brook) in [Dedham, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dedham,_Massachusetts), in 1640.[^cite_note-growth-15]

In the U.S., public utilities provide services at the consumer level, be it residential, commercial, or industrial consumer. Utilities, merchant power producers and very large consumers buy and sell bulk electricity at the wholesale level through a network of [regional transmission organizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_transmission_organization) (RTO) and independent system operators (ISO) within one of three grids, the [Eastern Interconnection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Interconnection), the [Texas Interconnection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Interconnection), which is a single ISO, and the [Western Interconnection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Interconnection).

U.S. utilities historically operated with a high degree of financial leverage and low interest coverage ratios compared to industrial companies. Investors accepted these credit characteristics because of the regulation of the industry and the belief that there was minimal bankruptcy risk because of the essential services they provide. In recent decades several high-profile utility company bankruptcies have challenged this perception.[^cite_note-16]

#### Monopoly vs. competition

Public utilities were historically regarded as [natural monopolies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_monopoly) because the infrastructure required to produce and deliver a product such as electricity or water is very expensive to build and maintain. Once assets such as power plants or transmission lines are in place, the cost of adding another customer is small, and duplication of facilities would be wasteful.[^cite_note-17] As a result, utilities were either government monopolies, or if investor-owned, [regulated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulation) by a [public utilities commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_utilities_commission).[^cite_note-merriam-webster1-18][^cite_note-investorwords1-19]

In the electric utility industry, the monopoly approach began to change in the 1990s. In 1996, the [Federal Energy Regulatory Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Energy_Regulatory_Commission) (FERC) issued its [Order No. 888](https://www.ferc.gov/industries-data/electric/industry-activities/open-access-transmission-tariff-oatt-reform/history-oatt-reform/order-no-888), which mandated that electric utilities open access to their transmission systems to enhance competition and "functionally unbundle" their transmission service from their other operations. The order also promoted the role of an independent system operator to manage power flow on the electric grid.[^cite_note-20][^cite_note-21] Later, FERC Order No. 889 established an electronic information system called OASIS (open access same-time information system) which would give new users of transmission lines access to the same information available to the owner of the network.[^cite_note-22] The result of these and other regulatory rulings was the eventual restructuring of the traditional monopoly-regulated regime to one in which all bulk power sellers could compete. A further step in industry restructuring, "customer choice", followed in some 19 states, giving retail electric customers the option to be served by non-utility retail power marketers.[^cite_note-23][^cite_note-24][^cite_note-25]

#### Ownership structure

Public utilities can be [privately owned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privately_held_company) or [publicly owned](204768.md). Publicly owned utilities include cooperative and municipal utilities. [Municipal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Municipal) utilities may actually include territories outside of city limits or may not even serve the entire city. [Cooperative utilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utility_cooperative) are owned by the customers they serve. They are usually found in [rural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rural) areas. Publicly owned utilities are non-profit. Private utilities, also called [investor-owned utilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Investor-owned_utility), are owned by [investors](63121.md),[^cite_note-26][^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28] and operate for profit, often referred to as a [rate of return](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rate_of_return).

#### Regulation

A [public utilities commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_utilities_commission) is a governmental agency in a particular jurisdiction that regulates the commercial activities related to associated electric, [natural gas](22131.md), telecommunications, water, railroad, rail transit, and/or passenger transportation companies. For example, the [California Public Utilities Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Public_Utilities_Commission) (CPUC)[^cite_note-Cpuc.ca.gov-29] and the [Public Utility Commission of Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Utility_Commission_of_Texas) regulate the utility companies in California and Texas, respectively, on behalf of their citizens and ratepayers (customers). These public utility commissions (PUCs) are typically composed of commissioners, who are appointed by their respective governors, and dedicated staff that implements and enforces rules and regulations, approve or deny rate increases, and monitor/report on relevant activities.[^cite_note-puc-30]

Ratemaking practice in the U.S. holds that rates paid by a utility's customers should be set at a level which assures that the utility can provide reliable service at reasonable cost.[^cite_note-31]

Over the years, various changes have dramatically re-shaped the mission and focus of many public utility commissions. Their focus has typically shifted from the up-front regulation of rates and services to the oversight of competitive marketplaces and enforcement of regulatory compliance.
