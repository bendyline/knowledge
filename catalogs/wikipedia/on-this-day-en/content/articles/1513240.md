The **Yakovlev Yak-130** ([NATO reporting name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NATO_reporting_name): **Mitten**)[^cite_note-DS1-4] is a [subsonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsonic_aircraft), two-seat, advanced [jet trainer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_trainer) and [light combat aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_combat_aircraft).

The aircraft began as the **Yak/AEM-130**, a joint project by [Yakovlev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakovlev) and [Aermacchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aermacchi). Development of the aircraft began in 1991, and it first flew in April 1996. In 2000, the companies ended the partnership, and Yakovlev continued to develop the aircraft as the Yak-130.

In 2002, it won a Russian government tender for training aircraft, and in 2010, the aircraft entered service with the [Russian Air Force](1526308.md).[^cite_note-butowski-1] The Yak-130 can replicate the characteristics of several four-plus generation fighters and the fifth-generation [Sukhoi Su-57](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sukhoi_Su-57). It can also perform light-attack and reconnaissance duties, carrying a combat load of 3,000 kg (6,600 lb).

## Development



A AEM/YAK-130D at the Zhukovsky Airfield, 1999

In the early 1990s, the Soviet government asked the industry to develop a new aircraft to replace the Czech-made [Aero L-29 Delfín](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aero_L-29_Delf%C3%ADn) and [Aero L-39 Albatros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aero_L-39_Albatros) jet trainers. Five design bureaus put forward proposals. Among them were the [Sukhoi S-54](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sukhoi_S-54), [Myasishchev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myasishchev) M-200, [Mikoyan MiG-AT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikoyan_MiG-AT), and [Yakovlev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakovlev) Yak-UTS. In 1991, the other proposals were dropped and only the MiG-AT and Yak-UTS remained. The air forces of the newly independent Russia estimated that its requirement would be about 1,000 aircraft.[^cite_note-IBT_2002-5]

Development of Yak-UTS started in 1991, and the design was completed in September 1993.[^cite_note-succès_exportation-2] With the collapse of the Soviet Union, Yakovlev was compelled to look for a foreign partner. After having entered discussions in 1992, it agreed the next year with the Italian company [Aermacchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aermacchi) to jointly develop the plane, which now became the AEM/YAK-130D.[^cite_note-6] Aermacchi would be responsible for the project's financial and technical support.[^cite_note-7]

The first prototype, dubbed AEM/YAK-130D, was built by Sokol at Nizhny Novgorod, Russia, and was publicly unveiled in June 1995.[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9] The aircraft made its first flight on 25 April 1996 from Zhukovsky Airport at the hands of Yakovlev chief test pilot Andrey Sinitsyn.[^cite_note-10]

In 2000, differences in priorities between the two firms brought about an end to the partnership, with each developing the aircraft independently. The Italian version was named the [M-346](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M-346). Yakovlev received US\$77 million, in the form of a partial write-off of the USSR/Russian debt towards Italy and for the legal right to use some of the technical documents of the aircraft.[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-IBT_2002-5] Yakovlev would be able to sell the aircraft to countries such those in the [Commonwealth of Independent States](36870.md), India, Slovakia, and Algeria. Aermacchi would be able to sell to NATO countries, among others.[^cite_note-IBT_2002-5]

In March 2002, Commander-in-Chief Vladimir Mikhailov said that the Yak-130 and the MiG-AT had been chosen as the Russian Air Force's new trainers.[^cite_note-IBT_2002-5][^cite_note-Selection-12] The Yak-130, however, was said to be superior, as it could serve the dual role of a trainer and combat jet.[^cite_note-13] In April 2002, the Yak-130 was chosen as the winner of the tender for trainer aircraft for basic and advanced pilot training, beating the MiG-AT.[^cite_note-janes_yak130-14] By then, the Russian Air Force had ordered 10 Yak-130s. The total cost of research and development, which included the construction and testing of the four preproduction aircraft, had amounted to some \$200 million, 84% of which was financed by Yakovlev and the rest by the Russian government.[^cite_note-IBT_2002-5][^cite_note-Selection-12] It was reported that as much as \$500 million had been spent in as early as 1996.[^cite_note-Figures-15]

Plans to develop a light-attack aircraft based on the Yak-130 came to a halt in the late 2011. Dubbed Yak-131, the aircraft failed to meet critical pilot safety requirements put forward by the Russian Air Force. The air force had instead shifted focus to a dedicated replacement for its [Su-25s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sukhoi_Su-25) instead.[^cite_note-16]

## Design



The underside of a Yak-130

The Yak-130 is an advanced pilot-training aircraft, able to replicate characteristics of Russian fourth- and fifth-generation fighters.[^cite_note-ato_yak130-17] This is possible through the use of [open architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_architecture) digital avionics compliant with a 1553 Databus, a full [digital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_electronics) [glass cockpit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glass_cockpit), four-channel digital [fly-by-wire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fly-by-wire) system (FBWS), and instructor-controlled and variable FBWS handling characteristics and embedded simulation. It has a [head-up display](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head-up_display)  and a [helmet-mounted sighting system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmet-mounted_display), with a double [GPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPS)/[GLONASS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GLONASS) receiver updating an [inertial reference system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_reference_system)  for highly accurate navigation and precision targeting.[^cite_note-FLIGHT_TEST-18] The developer estimated that the plane can cover up to 80% of the entire pilot flight-training program.[^cite_note-uac_yak130-19]



A Yak-130 deploying [flares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flare_\(countermeasure\))

The aircraft is capable of fulfilling light-attack and reconnaissance duties.[^cite_note-ato_yak130-17] It can carry a combat load of 3,000 kg (6,600 lb), consisting of guided and unguided weapons, auxiliary fuel tanks, and electronic pods.[^cite_note-uac_yak130-19] According to its chief designer, Konstantin Popovich, the plane was tested with "all airborne weapons with a weight of up to 500 kg that are in service in the Russian Air Force".[^cite_note-ato_yak130-17] The Yak-130 has nine [hardpoints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardpoint) - two wingtip, six under-wing, and one under-fuselage.[^cite_note-FLIGHT_TEST-18]



A Yak-130 at the MAKS 2005 air show

The aircraft's twin engines are mounted under extended wing roots, which reach as far forward as the cockpit canopy. Two Ivchenko Progress AI-222-25 [Full Authority Digital Engine Control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FADEC)  produce a combined total of 49 kilonewtons (11,000 pound-force) of thrust. An upgraded, "-28" engine is also on offer, increasing the thrust to 53 kN (12,000 lbf). At a normal take-off weight of 7,250 kg (15,980 lb), a thrust-to-weight ratio of 0.70 is achieved with the "-25", or 0.77 with the "-28" engines. This compares with 0.65 for the [BAE Systems Hawk 128](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BAE_Hawk-128) and 0.49 for the [Aero Vodochody L-159B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aero_L-159).[^cite_note-FLIGHT_TEST-18]

Maximum internal fuel capacity is 1,700 kg (3,700 lb). With two external combat fuel tanks, the figure increases to 2,600 kg (5,700 lb). Maximum [true airspeed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_airspeed) is Mach 0.93 (572 knots), service ceiling is 12,500 m (41,000 ft), and load factors are from −3 to +9 g. Typical take-off speed and distance in a "clean" configuration are 209 km/h (113 kn) and 550 m (1,800 ft). Landing figures are 191 km/h (103 kn) and 750 m (2,460 ft). The cross-wind limit is 56 km/h (30 kn).[^cite_note-FLIGHT_TEST-18]

The Yakovlev Yak-130 is equipped with engine intake blanking doors, controlled by the FBWS, to prevent the aircraft's engines from sustaining [foreign object damage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_object_damage) when operating from unpaved runways and grass strips.[^cite_note-20]

The large canopies are sideways hinged.

The combat training suite on the Yak-130 includes simulated and real firing systems with air-to-air and air-to-surface missiles, bomb dropping, gun firing, and on-board self-protection systems.

## Orders and deliveries

### Firm orders

#### Russia

In 2005, the Russian Air Force ordered 12 Yak-130s. The [Russian Aerospace Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Aerospace_Forces) intended to buy at least 72 Yak-130s, enough to equip four training regiments.[^cite_note-21] In November 2011, the [Russian Defence Ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Defence_Ministry) signed a contract with [Irkut Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irkut_Corporation) for 65 additional aircraft[^cite_note-more_Yak-130s-22] – 55 firm orders plus 10 options.[^cite_note-FLIGHT_TEST-18] Deliveries were expected to be completed by 2017.[^cite_note-more_Yak-130s-22]

The first serial aircraft was handed to a training center in [Lipetsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipetsk) in February 2010.[^cite_note-butowski-1] Once the 2005 contract for 12 [Sokol plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sokol_plant)-made Yak-130s for the [Russian Ministry of Defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Ministry_of_Defense) (MoD) was fulfilled in June 2011, a decision was made that all subsequent Yak-130 orders, both domestic and export ones, would be handled by the Irkutsk Aviation Plant of the Irkut Corporation.[^cite_note-Takeoff12413-23] In October 2012, the Russian Air Force took delivery of the first Yak-130 built by the [Irkutsk](56872.md) plant.[^cite_note-24]

In February 2014, Irkut Corporation revealed a contract with the MoD to supply additional Yakovlev Yak-130 advanced jet trainers to the air force. According to Irkut president Oleg Demchenko, the company in December signed a contract with the MoD on the delivery of 12 Yak-130s to form a new aerobatics team.[^cite_note-25] At the same time, a second contract for 10 more aircraft for the [Russian Naval Aviation](5698051.md) was signed.

#### Algeria

[Algeria](358.md) was the first export customer for the Yak-130, ordering 16 aircraft in March 2006. Their delivery started only a few months after the arrival of the first Yak-130s in the Russian Air Force, in 2011, and the order was completed the next year.[^cite_note-succès_exportation-2]



Yak-130s of the [Bangladesh Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh_Air_Force)

#### Bangladesh

In January 2014, [Bangladesh](3454.md) ordered 24 Yak-130s. The aircraft were bought with an extended loan from Russia[^cite_note-26] Later the order was reduced to 16 aircraft.[^cite_note-27] The first batch of six aircraft was delivered on 20 September 2015.[^cite_note-28]

#### Belarus

In December 2012, the [government of Belarus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Belarus) signed an agreement with Russia to provide four Yak-130s to [Belarus](3457.md) by April 2015. Another order for four aircraft was placed in August 2015, and these were delivered in November 2016.[^cite_note-succès_exportation-2] A further four aircraft were delivered in 2019, bringing the total to 12. All were delivered to the 206th Flight Training Centre.[^cite_note-29]

#### Laos

Laos ordered 10 Yak-130s in August 2017. Deliveries started in 2018.[^cite_note-succès_exportation-2]

#### Myanmar

Myanmar ordered six Yak-130s in June 2015. All six aircraft were delivered in December 2017. Six additional aircraft were ordered later.[^cite_note-succès_exportation-2] Following the 2021 coup, Myanmar received a further six jets, which were revealed at the 74th-anniversary ceremony of Myanmar's Air Force.[^cite_note-auto-30]

#### Iran

Iran acquired Yak-130 jet trainers. The first Yak-130 aircraft were delivered to Iran in September 2023, with photos showing at least two jets arriving at [Isfahan](223149.md)'s Shahid Babaei Air Base.[^cite_note-31] Subsequent imagery and reporting suggest more may have been delivered later.[^cite_note-32] Exact numbers and deal cost were not publicly disclosed.[^cite_note-auto2-33] These jet trainers are being utilized by the Iranian Air Force for various roles, including drone interception patrols and air-to-air combat missions.[^cite_note-34]

### Potential orders

In April 2012, Irkut Corporation president Alexey Fedorov claimed it had "more than ten potential customers".[^cite_note-35]

#### Argentina

In 2021, Russia offered the [Argentine Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_Air_Force) a batch of 15 [MiG-29](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MiG-29) fighters and another batch of 12 [Su-30](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Su-30) fighters and sought also the sale of Yak-130 training jet and [Mil Mi-17](1493374.md) helicopters.[^cite_note-36]

#### Bolivia

[Bolivia](3462.md) is considering the Yak-130 as a candidate for the replacement of its retired [Lockheed T-33s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_T-33).[^cite_note-37]

#### Kazakhstan

Two rounds of negotiations with Russia regarding a potential order for Yak-130s took place in 2010 and 2012. No firm orders came out of it, but the Yak-130 may be bought to replace Kazakhstan's current [Aero L-39](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aero_L-39)C trainers, at the end of their service life.[^cite_note-succès_exportation-2] It was reported in late August 2026 that Kazakhstan was negotiating with China for the purchase of up to six [K-8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hongdu_JL-8)s to fill the role instead.[^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39]

#### Malaysia

In November 2012, Sergey Kornev, a representative of [Rosoboronexport](3094695.md) (Russia's state intermediary agency for exports/imports of defense-related products), said [Malaysia](3607937.md) and several other countries were also interested in the Yak-130. He was speaking at the [China Airshow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China_Airshow) at [Zhuhai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhuhai).[^cite_note-english.ruvr.ru-40]

#### Uruguay

The [Uruguayan Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uruguayan_Air_Force) is considering the aircraft for the future replacement of the [A-37](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cessna_A-37_Dragonfly)[^cite_note-41] with presumably used examples of the [F-5 Freedom Fighter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-5_Freedom_Fighter) as another possible candidate.[^cite_note-42]

#### Vietnam

In addition to the 12 delivered Yak-130, Vietnam is reportedly signing for 18 Yak-130M, as reported by [Irkutsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irkutsk_Aviation_Plant) officials.[^cite_note-\:4-43][^cite_note-44]

### Cancelled and unimplemented orders

#### Libya

[Libya](17633.md) placed an order for six planes. Deliveries were expected in 2011–2012,[^cite_note-ato_yak130-17] but the Libyan [National Transitional Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Transitional_Council) cancelled the order for Yak-130s in September 2011 as part of a review of all existing arms contracts.[^cite_note-45]

#### Syria

[Syria](7515849.md) had agreed to purchase 36 aircraft,[^cite_note-TA.ch-46] but delivery of these was postponed by Russia due to the conflict in Syria.[^cite_note-47] In May 2014 Russia announced that it would supply Syria with Yakovlev Yak-130s. Syria was expected to receive nine aircraft by the end of 2014, 12 in 2015, and 15 in 2016, for a total of 36 airplanes.[^cite_note-48][^cite_note-49] However, as of 2019, no deliveries had taken place.[^cite_note-succès_exportation-2]

## Operational history



A Yak-130 at MAKS-2011

The first prototype, designated AEM/YAK-130D and registered as RA-43130, made its maiden flight on 25 April 1996 at [Zhukovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhukovsky_International_Airport).[^cite_note-succès_exportation-2]

On 30 April 2004, the first preseries Yak-130, assembled at the Sokol plant in [Nizhny Novgorod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nizhny_Novgorod), performed its maiden flight.[^cite_note-butowski-1] The plane was put on display for the first time at the [Paris Air Show](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Air_Show) in June 2005.[^cite_note-janes_yak130-14] It was followed by three more preseries aircraft.[^cite_note-succès_exportation-2]

In December 2009, the aircraft completed state trials and was accepted for service in the Russian Air Force.[^cite_note-succès_exportation-2]

The Yak-130 has been used in combat in the [internal conflict in Myanmar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_conflict_in_Myanmar), at least since 2020.[^cite_note-50] Its use has been criticised after verifiable evidence of military air strikes against civilians emerged online.[^cite_note-51][^cite_note-52]

In the [2026 Iran war](82533582.md), Iran used its Yak-130s to complement [MiG-29s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MiG-29) in air defence duties over Tehran.[^cite_note-53] An Israeli [F-35](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-35) Adir was confirmed to have shot down a Yak-130, reportedly in [Tehran](57654.md) airspace, making it the first crewed aircraft air-air killed for the F-35.[^cite_note-\:5-54]

Yakovlev Yak-130M

Modernized version with more weapons and equipment which was unveiled in August 2024. The suite includes the BRLS-130R Airborne AESA radar, the SOLT-130K electro-optical/laser targeting system, the President-S130 self-protection suite, and the new KSS-130 communications link. This fit increases target detection range, improves weapons delivery accuracy, and strengthens resistance to electronic warfare. The podded defensive aids suite is intended to counter air- and surface-launched missile threats.

Modernization also targets the powerplant. At the Army-2023 forum, United Engine Corporation (UEC) unveiled the SM-100 engine, derived from the AI-222-25. The new engine delivers 20% higher thrust with no increase in weight or dimensions and doubles service life. The gains result from redesigned compressor, combustor, and turbine modules.[^cite_note-77]

## Specifications (Yak-130)



*Data from* YAK-130[^cite_note-Yak-95]

**General characteristics**

- **Crew:** 2

- **Length:** 11.49 m (37 ft 8 in)

- **Wingspan:** 9.84 m (32 ft 3 in)

- **Height:** 4.76 m (15 ft 7 in)

- **Wing area:** 23.52 m

  2

  &#x20;(253.2 sq ft)

- **Empty weight:** 4,600 kg (10,141 lb)

- **Gross weight:** 7,250 kg (15,984 lb)

- **Max takeoff weight:** 10,290 kg (22,686 lb)

- **Powerplant:** 2 × [Ivchenko-Progress AI-222-25](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivchenko-Progress_AI-222-25) [turbofan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbofan) engines, 24.52 kN (5,510 lbf) thrust each

**Performance**

- **Maximum speed:** 1,060 km/h (660 mph, 570 kn)

- **Cruise speed:** 887 km/h (551 mph, 479 kn)

- **Stall speed:** 165 km/h (103 mph, 89 kn)

- **Range:** 2,100 km (1,300 mi, 1,100 nmi)

- **Service ceiling:** 12,500 m (41,000 ft)

- **g limits:** +8.0 −3.0

- **Rate of climb:** 65 m/s (12,800 ft/min)

- **Wing loading:** 276.4 kg/m

  2

  &#x20;(56.6 lb/sq ft)

- **[Thrust/weight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrust-to-weight_ratio):** 0.70

  [^cite_note-collins-96]

**Armament**\


- **Guns:** SNPU-130 gun pod

- **Hardpoints:** 8 (1 on each wingtip, usable only for flares/chaff, 3 under each wing)

  [^cite_note-FLIGHT_TEST-18]

  &#x20;with a capacity of up to 3,000 kg (6,614 lb), with provisions to carry combinations of:&#x20;

  - **Rockets:** 80 mm S-8 rocket, S-25 rocket

  - **Missiles:** [R-73](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R-73_\(missile\)) infrared-homing short-range air-to-air missiles

  - **Bombs:** FAB-M62, ZB-500, KAB-500Kr
