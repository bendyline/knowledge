**Bandar Siraf** ([Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language): بندر سیراف)[^cite_note-7] is a city in, and the capital of, [Siraf District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siraf_District) in [Kangan County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kangan_County), [Bushehr province](448037.md), [Iran](14653.md).[^cite_note-Siraf_District_Formation-8] As the village of Taheri, it was the capital of [Taheri Rural District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taheri_Rural_District)[^cite_note-Kangan_County_Rural_Districts-9] until its capital was transferred to the village of [Parak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parak,_Bushehr).[^cite_note-Siraf_District_Formation-8] Taheri was converted to a city in 2005.[^cite_note-Taheri_City-3]

According to legend, Siraf was an ancient [Sasanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_Empire) [port](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port), destroyed around 970 CE, which was located on the north shore of the [Persian Gulf](24761.md) in what is now the Iranian province of Bushehr. Its ruins are approximately 220 km east of the city of [Bushehr](205669.md), 30 km east from Kangan city, and 380 km west of [Bandar Abbas](464348.md).[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] Siraf controlled three ports: Bandar-e-Taheri, [Bandar-e Kangan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandar_Kangan) and [Bandar-e Dayyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandar-e_Dayyer).[^cite_note-11] The Persian Gulf was used as a [shipping route](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shipping_route) between the [Arabian Peninsula](47858.md) and [India](14533.md) over the [Arabian Sea](2747.md). Small boats, such as [dhows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhow), could also make the long journey by staying close to the coast and keeping land in sight.[^cite_note-12]

The port was known as Taheri or Tahiri until in 2008 the government of Iran changed the official name of the city back to Bandar Siraf.[^cite_note-Taheri_Renamed_Bandar_Siraf-5]

## History

The port was known as Siraf in ancient times. At the time of the [Silk Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silk_Road), most of the commerce towards Asia was performed through Siraf. Several episodes of massive [earthquakes](10106.md) and [tsunamis](31161.md) damaged and drowned much of the port city, where its ancient quays, moorages, administrative structures, and even boat remains are found today on the sea floor of the [Persian Gulf](24761.md) via [marine archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_archaeology).

### Sasanian era



Sea routes during the [Sasanian era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_Empire)

The historical importance of Siraf to ancient trade is only now being realized. Discovered there in past archaeological excavations are ivory objects from [East Africa](260987.md), pieces of stone from [India](14533.md), and [lapis lazuli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapis_lazuli) from [Afghanistan](737.md). Siraf dates back to the [Parthian era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthian_Empire).[^cite_note-13]

According to [David Whitehouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Whitehouse), one of the first archaeologists to excavate the ancient ruins of Siraf, marine trade between the Persian Gulf and [Far East](11672.md) lands began to flourish at this port because of the vast expansion of trade in consumer goods and luxury items in ancient times.[^cite_note-Cultural_Heritage_News_Agency-14] According to legend, the first contact between Siraf and [China](5405.md) occurred in 741 AD.[^cite_note-Cultural_Heritage_News_Agency-14] However, when sea [trade routes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_route) tried shifting to the [Red Sea](26191.md), Siraf lost some business.[^cite_note-Cultural_Heritage_News_Agency-14]

Excavations at Siraf have uncovered evidence of a Sasanian port, which probably served the inland city of [Gor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firuzabad,_Fars) (now Firuzabad).[^cite_note-Bosworth_1997-15] There was also a huge castle, possibly built c. 360 by [Shapur II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shapur_II).[^cite_note-Bosworth_1997-15]

There is historical evidence of Sasanian maritime trade with the [Gulf of Cambay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Cambay) in the modern day province of [Gujarat](53707.md),[^cite_note-16] as fragments of Indian red polished ware, of predominantly [Gujarati](53707.md) provenance dating to the 5th and 6th centuries were found at coastal sites on the northern shores of the Persian Gulf, and especially at Siraf.[^cite_note-17]

### Islamic era



The [jameh mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jameh_mosque) of Siraf, begun in the 9th century

In the 9th century, Siraf was revamped and expanded exponentially as the sea trade with Asia flourished. The city's [congregational mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congregational_mosque), one of the earliest in Iran, was among the items studied by Whitehouse.[^cite_note-Archnet-18] The mosque was "a huge rectangular structure with a central courtyard set on a raised podium", with a single entrance on the east side (opposite from the [qibla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qibla)).[^cite_note-Archnet-18] The mosque was built in two phases.[^cite_note-Archnet-18] The first was in the first half of the 9th century and the second was around 850.[^cite_note-Archnet-18] There were also several smaller mosques, all with [mihrabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mihrab) projecting onto the outside of the building.[^cite_note-Archnet-18] There are ruins of the luxurious houses of extremely rich traders who made their wealth through the port's success.[^cite_note-19]

The earliest Muslim writer to mention Siraf is [Ibn al-Faqih](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_al-Faqih), who wrote around 850 that Sirafi ships traded with India.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] Around the same time, [Sulaiman al-Tajir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulaiman_al-Tajir) wrote that Middle Eastern goods bound for China were first shipped from [Basra](57580.md) to Siraf, then on to [Muscat](57053.md) in Oman and [Kollam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kollam) in India.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] Around 900, [Abu Zayd Hasan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Zayd_Hasan?action=edit\&redlink=1) - himself a merchant from Siraf - wrote that Sirafi ships were engaged in commerce with both [Jeddah](277718.md) on the Red Sea and [Zanzibar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zanzibar) in East Africa.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] Abu Zayd also wrote that trade between the Persian Gulf and China had decreased after the [Guangzhou massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guangzhou_massacre) in 878 killed many foreign merchants, although Chinese coins were still circulating in Siraf at the time he was writing.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] Later, when [Al-Masudi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Masudi) visited [Madagascar](18964.md) between 916 and 926, he noted that Sirafi ships were present there, along with ships from [Oman](22316.md).[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10]

During its heyday, Siraf was the largest and wealthiest port city on the Iranian side of the Persian Gulf.[^cite_note-Christensen_1993-20] It was a center of commerce with Africa, India, and China.[^cite_note-Christensen_1993-20] The anonymous author of the 10th-century *[Hudud al-'Alam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hudud_al-'Alam)* called Siraf "the merchants' haunt and the emporium of Fars".[^cite_note-Bosworth_1997-15] The later author [Ibn al-Balkhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_al-Balkhi) wrote of the period from 908 to 932, the annual value of goods traded in Siraf was 2.53 million [dinars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinar).[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] The most detailed account of Siraf comes from [Istakhri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istakhri), shortly before 950.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] He described it as the second-largest city in the district of [Ardashir-Khwarrah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ardashir-Khwarrah) (southwestern Fars), behind only [Shiraz](39622.md) - which it rivaled in size.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] He listed some of the goods that were traded here: [ebony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebony), [ivory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory), [sandalwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandalwood) and other aromatics, [bamboo](39029.md), spices, paper, [aloe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aloe), [camphor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camphor), [ambergris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambergris), and precious stones.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] Among the goods manufactured in Siraf itself were linen napkins and veils.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] Siraf was also an important market for [pearls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl)[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] - nearby [Ganaveh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandar_Ganaveh) was renowned for its pearl fishing industry.[^cite_note-Christensen_1993-20] Merchants and ship captains from Siraf amassed huge fortunes off all this maritime trade,[^cite_note-Christensen_1993-20] and they lived in "richly decorated, multi-story houses" built from [teak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teak) wood, imported from East Africa, and fired brick.[^cite_note-Bosworth_1997-15] According to contemporary accounts, a merchant might spend 30,000 dinars on one of these houses.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] The "rather puritanical" 10th-century author [al-Maqdisi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Maqdisi) regarded Siraf as a nest of corruption and wrote that [adultery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adultery), [usury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usury), and general extravagance were rampant here.[^cite_note-Christensen_1993-20] When an earthquake in 977 caused serious damage to Siraf, al-Maqdisi viewed it as "a fitting punishment from God".[^cite_note-Christensen_1993-20]

Siraf served an international clientele of merchants including those from [South India](678583.md) ruled by the [Western Chalukyas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Chalukya_Empire) dynasty who were feasted by wealthy local merchants during business visits. An indicator of the Indian merchants' importance in Siraf comes from records describing dining plates reserved for them.[^cite_note-21]

However, Siraf lacked drinking water (apart from one small [qanat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qanat), according to al-Maqdisi)[^cite_note-Bosworth_1997-15] and good farmland, so food and water had to be imported from the [Jam](24988256.md) plain.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10]

According to al-Maqdisi, Siraf's decline began with the [Buyid dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buyid_dynasty) gaining power in Fars; many Sirafis relocated to Oman at this point according to him.[^cite_note-Christensen_1993-20] On the other hand, Ibn al-Balkhi says nothing about such a migration and instead attributes the town's decline to the period after the fall of the Buyids in 1055.[^cite_note-Christensen_1993-20] Pirates from the nearby [Qays Island](847277.md) then took advantage of the resulting power vacuum to attack Sirafi ships with impunity.[^cite_note-Christensen_1993-20] Commercial traffic on the Persian Gulf started to bypass Siraf altogether and instead go straight to Basra.[^cite_note-Bosworth_1997-15]

Siraf was not the only Gulf port to decline around this time.[^cite_note-Christensen_1993-20] [Ganaveh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandar_Ganaveh), [Tawwaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tawwaz), [Siniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siniz?action=edit\&redlink=1), and [Mahruban](1851356.md) all declined at about the same time.[^cite_note-Christensen_1993-20] However, this decline "can only have been relative" - in the early 12th century, the wealthy ship-owner and merchant tycoon [Abu'l-Qasim Ramisht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramisht_of_Siraf) (died 1140) is known to have operated a prosperous commercial enterprise based out of Siraf that did business as far as China.[^cite_note-Bosworth_1997-15] In the 12th century, a [Jat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jats) chief named Abu al-Qasim controlled Siraf and surrounding districts.[^cite_note-22] By the 13th century, though, [Yaqut al-Hamawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaqut_al-Hamawi) left a less than sanguine description of Siraf - he called it a small place (*bulayd*) inhabited by "wretched people", with its buildings in ruins.[^cite_note-Bosworth_1997-15] By this point, the name Siraf had become distorted to ***Shīlāw***.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] This name is still used to refer to a small valley south of the site's main ridge (see above).[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10]

Yaqut may have painted a rather bleak picture of Siraf, but its role as a commercial port was far from over.[^cite_note-Bosworth_1997-15] It remained a regional trade center on a smaller scale until the 15th or 16th century.[^cite_note-Christensen_1993-20] It served as the port for the [Khunj u Fal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khunj_u_Fal?action=edit\&redlink=1) region, as a point of departure for [Qatif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qatif) and the Arabian Peninsula.[^cite_note-Bosworth_1997-15] [Ibn Battuta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Battuta) knew of "Shilaw" and may have visited in 1347 when he crossed the Persian Gulf from "Khunju Pal" over to the Arabian Peninsula.[^cite_note-Bosworth_1997-15] Shilaw was also mentioned by 16th-century European travelers, such as António Tenreiro, who visited "Chilaão" in 1528, and Gasparo Balbi, who visited "Silaú" in 1590.[^cite_note-Bosworth_1997-15] After that, however, sources only describe a very small and basic harbor at the modern village of Taheri.[^cite_note-Bosworth_1997-15]

In 1812, [James Morier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Justinian_Morier) wrote about the existence of ruins at Taheri.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] Someone named Brucks then visited the site but thought it was Portuguese.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] The British naval officer G.N. Kempthorne later visited the site in 1835 and was the first to identify the ruins with Siraf.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] In 1933, [Aurel Stein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurel_Stein) visited Siraf and left a description of a massive "sea wall", which extended for some 400 m along the beach and was reinforced with buttresses but has since disappeared.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10]

Many of the finds (over 16,000 in all) excavated at Siraf by Whitehouse and his archaeological team in the 1960s and 1970s are kept in the [British Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Museum) in London.[^cite_note-23]

Siraf has not yet been registered on the list of national heritage sites of Iran. This is needed so that it will be preserved and maintained in the future.[^cite_note-24]

## Demographics

### Population

At the time of the 2006 National Census, the city's population was 3,500 in 722 households, when it was in the [Central District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_District_\(Kangan_County\)).[^cite_note-2006_Bushehr_Province-25] The following census in 2011 counted 7,137 people in 1,309 households.[^cite_note-2011_Bushehr_Province-26] The 2016 census measured the population of the city as 6,992 people in 1,949 households.[^cite_note-2016_Bushehr_Province-4]

In 2019, the city was separated from the district in the formation of [Siraf District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siraf_District).[^cite_note-Siraf_District_Formation-8]

## Geography

The site of Siraf is located by a shallow bay that extends for 4 km east–west.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] Just 500 m inland is a sandstone ridge running parallel to the coast.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] Siraf itself is located in the narrow habitable strip between the beach and the ridge.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] In this part of Fars, many long ridges like this one run parallel to the coast, rising as high as 1500 m within 20 km from the sea.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] Passes through the ridges are only found occasionally, making communication with the interior difficult.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10]

The narrow coastal plain is divided in two by a spur jutting out from the ridge.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] The core of the modern settlement of Taheri is on the east side of this spur, while the ruins of historical Siraf are to the west, extending for 2 km along the seashore.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] On the spur itself was the fortified residence of the local shaikh.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] To the west is the dry [wadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wadi) bed called Kunarak, which marks the western boundary of old Siraf.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] Today, the Kunarak valley is where the main road passes through to connect Taheri with the [Jam](24988256.md) plain further inland, and the same was probably true in historical times when Siraf was at its peak.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] To the west of the Kunarak valley is the Bagh-i-Shaikh plain.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] The coastal plain gradually widens toward the west, and the Bagh-i-Shaikh plain is 1 km across.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10]

Besides the Kunarak valley, there is a second gap in the main ridge known as the Tang-i-Lir.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] This is a narrow gorge located about 1.5 km east of the Kunarak pass.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] Besides the main spur that divides the coastal plain, there are two smaller spurs between the two gaps.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] The first and larger one runs almost parallel with the main ridge for about 1 km west of the Tang-i-Lir.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] Between it and the main ridge is the Shilaw valley, which retains the old variant of the name attested since the 13th century.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] The northern slopes of the Shilaw valley are covered in rock-cut graves, which are now empty.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10] A second small spur is between the Shilaw valley and the Kunarak wadi.[^cite_note-Siraf_Congress-10]
