The **Purdue Boilermakers football** team represents [Purdue University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purdue_University) in the [NCAA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCAA) [Football Bowl Subdivision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Football_Bowl_Subdivision) (FBS) of [college football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_football). Purdue plays its home games at [Ross–Ade Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ross%E2%80%93Ade_Stadium) on the campus of Purdue University in [West Lafayette, Indiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Lafayette,_Indiana). The head coach of Purdue is [Barry Odom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Odom), the 38th head coach in Purdue history. The Boilermakers compete in the [Big Ten Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Ten_Conference).

With a 644–618–48 record at the conclusion of the 2025 season, Purdue has the 59th-most victories.[^cite_note-3] Purdue was originally classified as a Major College school in the 1937 season until 1972. Purdue received [Division I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_NCAA_Division_I_institutions) classification in 1973, becoming a [Division I-A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_I-A) program from 1978 to 2006 and an FBS program from 2006 to the present.[^cite_note-4] The Boilermakers have registered 64 winning seasons in their history, with 19 of those seasons resulting in eight victories or more, 10 seasons resulting in at least nine wins, and one season with ten victories or more.[^cite_note-PU_Season-by-Season-5] Of those successful campaigns, Purdue has produced five unbeaten seasons in its history, going 4–0 in 1891, 8–0 in 1892, 8–0 in 1929, 7–0–1 in 1932 and 9–0 in 1943.[^cite_note-PU_Season-by-Season-5] The Boilermakers have won a total of 12 conference championships in their history; eight [Big Ten Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Ten_Conference) titles; four [Indiana Intercollegiate Athletic Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiana_Intercollegiate_Athletic_Association) titles and one Big Ten West Division title.

## History

### Early history (1887–1955)

The [Purdue University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purdue_University) football team traces its origin back to October 29, 1887, when its team fell to [Butler College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butler_Bulldogs_football) by a score of 48–6 in [Indianapolis, Indiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indianapolis,_Indiana).[^cite_note-6] A group of students at [Purdue University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purdue_University) formed the school's first football team in 1887.[^cite_note-7] [Albert Berg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Berg) was hired as the coach. Despite being deaf, Berg was reportedly "the only man in the territory with any knowledge of the game."[^cite_note-PW-8] Berg was 23 years old when he became Purdue's football "coacher."[^cite_note-MATI-9] He was paid \$1 for each lesson he gave to the newly organized football team and had only one week to prepare the team for its first game.[^cite_note-MATI-9][^cite_note-CH-10][^cite_note-RA-11] The 1887 Purdue team played its only game on October 29, 1887, against the [Butler College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butler_Bulldogs_football) team at Athletic Park in Indianapolis. Butler soundly defeated Berg's squad by a score of 48–6.[^cite_note-12] After the loss to Butler, Purdue did not field a football team again until 1889.[^cite_note-MATI-9][^cite_note-CH-10]



Purdue's [1890](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1890_Purdue_football_team) football team

In 1890, [Clinton L. Hare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinton_L._Hare) became the third head football coach at Purdue. He coached the team that season to a record of 3–3. Purdue won each of its two home games in convincing fashion, shutting out [Wabash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wabash_Little_Giants_football), 54–0, on October 24 and [Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illinois_Fighting_Illini_football), 62–0, on November 22. They also shut out [DePauw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DePauw_Tigers_football) in [Greencastle, Indiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greencastle,_Indiana), 32–0. Purdue suffered its worst loss of the season on November 1 in [Ann Arbor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ann_Arbor,_Michigan), falling to [Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1890_Michigan_Wolverines_football_team) by a score of 34–6. Hare's squad also dropped their season opener in [Chicago](6886.md) on October 18 to the Chicago University Football Club, 10–6, and their season finale on November 27 against Hare's former team, Butler, by a score of 12–10.[^cite_note-13] With their wins over DePauw and Wabash and their loss to Butler, Purdue tallied a 2–1 mark against their opponents from within the state of Indiana. Hare's team finished second place in the [Indiana Intercollegiate Athletic Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiana_Intercollegiate_Athletic_Association) to Butler, who beat all three of their in-state foes and was awarded the state championship.[^cite_note-14] In 1891, [Knowlton Ames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowlton_Ames) became the head coach for Purdue, where he led the Boilermakers to a 12–0 record over two years.[^cite_note-15] In the fall of 1893, [D. M. Balliet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D._M._Balliet) became the head football coach at Purdue. Balliet led the team to a 5–2–1 record in 1893 and 9–1 in 1894. During the 1894 season, Balliet's Purdue squad defeated [Amos Alonzo Stagg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amos_Alonzo_Stagg)'s [Chicago Maroons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Maroons_football) and outscored opponents by a collective score of 177–42.[^cite_note-16][^cite_note-17] His 1895 squad finished with a record of 4–3.[^cite_note-18] In 1897, Balliet was reported to have given up a successful law practice to join the [Klondike Gold Rush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klondike_Gold_Rush) in Alaska.[^cite_note-19] Balliet was re-hired as the head coach at Purdue in September 1901.[^cite_note-20] He led the 1901 Purdue team to a 4–4–1 record but finished the season with consecutive losses to [Notre Dame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notre_Dame_Fighting_Irish_football), [Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illinois_Fighting_Illini_football), and [Northwestern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwestern_Wildcats_football).[^cite_note-21] At the end of the 1901 season, Purdue opted not to renew his services. In March 1902, the *Indianapolis News* reported, "He is known to be a good coach, but he turned out a loser last year and Purdue wants a change."[^cite_note-22] In four seasons as Purdue's head coach, Balliet compiled a record of 22–10–2.[^cite_note-23] On October 31, 1903, 14 members of the 1903 squad were killed in [Indianapolis, Indiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indianapolis,_Indiana) when the train they were riding collided with a coal train.[^cite_note-24] The event became known as the [Purdue Wreck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purdue_Wreck). Purdue bounced around with many different head coaches until 1921, with most having little to no success coaching at Purdue. However, Purdue did hire [Andy Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andy_Smith_\(American_football\)) (1913–15) and [William Henry Dietz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Henry_Dietz) (1921), both of which would go on to become College Football Hall of Fame members.

For the 1922 season, Purdue hired [Missouri Tigers football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missouri_Tigers_football) coach, [James Phelan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Phelan_\(American_football\)). Phelan lead the 1929 Boilermakers to a perfect 8–0 record and what is to date their only ever outright Big Ten Title. In 1925, [Noble Kizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Kizer) became an assistant coach at Purdue under Phelan and inherited the head coaching position upon Phelan's departure for the [University of Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Washington). [Mal Elward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mal_Elward) became head coach after serving as an assistant at Purdue from 1927 to 1936. He compiled a 16–18–6 record at Purdue. [Elmer Burnham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elmer_Burnham) served as Purdue's freshman football coach for seven years before assuming the role as varsity head coach in 1942.[^cite_note-25] Burnham's 1943 squad went 9–0 and shared the [Big Ten Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Ten_Conference) title with [Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1943_Michigan_Wolverines_football_team). The 1943 squad was the only undefeated team playing a full schedule in major college football, but finished third in the country per the AP Poll. This would seemingly be sufficient grounds for Purdue to claim a 1943 National Championship as the NCAA itself did not recognize champions in the era. However, Purdue has never pursued this claim. [Cecil Isbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecil_Isbell) started out at Purdue as an assistant coach and took over as head coach in 1944. He coached there for three years with a 14–14–1 record. He was the first Purdue alumnus to become the head football coach.

During [Stu Holcomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stu_Holcomb)'s tenure as Boilermakers head coach he compiled a record of 35–42–4. His best year was 1952 when he led the Boilermakers to a [Big Ten Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Ten_Conference) co-championship and a No. 18 ranking in the final poll. Despite having only a 4–3–1 overall record, Holcomb's team played well in conference with a 4–1–1 record. Holcomb's Purdue teams are, perhaps, best remembered for ending Notre Dame's 39-game unbeaten streak when his Boilermakers defeated the Irish, 28–14, in the second game of the 1950 season. Holcomb was known for developing solid quarterbacks including [Bob DeMoss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_DeMoss), [Dale Samuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dale_Samuels) and [Len Dawson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Len_Dawson). These players helped grow a strong tradition at Purdue of great quarterback play. On December 12, 1955, after his nine seasons at Purdue, Holcomb left Purdue to accept the athletic director position at the [Northwestern University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwestern_University).[^cite_note-26] Stu Holcomb oversaw the 1947 integration of Purdue's athletic teams after pressure from student led protests. It is hard to find any black athletes in the records before 1950 – where Herman Murray is first shown with the squad. Alongside Herman Murray, One of the most notable early black athletes is Lamar Lundy. Lundy was a two sport athlete who played center for the Purdue basketball team and defensive end for the football team. Lundy was extraordinary and achieved great deal of success during and after his collegiate years. He received the 1956 Purdue MVP award not even 10 years after athletic integration and received offers to play professional basketball and football after graduation. He ultimately chose football and played for the Los Angeles Rams from 1957 to 1969. Both Murray and Lundy were pioneers who paved the way for many young gentlemen.

### Jack Mollenkopf era (1956–1969)

On January 2, 1967, [Jack Mollenkopf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Mollenkopf) coached the school's first appearance in the [Rose Bowl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rose_Bowl_Game), leading Purdue to a 14–13 victory over [USC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USC_Trojans_football). Against Purdue's in-state rivals, Mollenkopf tallied an 11–2–1 record versus [Indiana](6135487.md) and a 10–4 mark against [Notre Dame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notre_Dame_Fighting_Irish_football). From 1966 to 1969, a Purdue player finished in the top three in balloting for the [Heisman Trophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heisman_Trophy): [quarterback](25277.md) [Bob Griese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Griese) was second in 1966, [halfback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halfback_\(American_football\)) [Leroy Keyes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leroy_Keyes) placed third in 1967 and second in 1968, and quarterback [Mike Phipps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mike_Phipps) finished as runner-up in 1969. Mollenkopf's inaugural season in 1956 was the only losing campaign of his tenure as head coach at Purdue. Mollenkopf is Purdue's all-time leader in [Big Ten Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Ten_Conference) wins (58) and conference winning percentage (.637). His 84 wins at Purdue placed him first on the school's all-time wins list until [Joe Tiller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Tiller) passed him in 2008, and he ranks fourth in overall winning percentage (.670). Mollenkopf's Boilermakers were nationally ranked for 80 weeks, the most under any Purdue head coach, and captured the No. 1 spot the first five weeks of the [1968 season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1968_NCAA_University_Division_football_season).

### Bob DeMoss era (1970–1972)

Former Purdue player and assistant coach [Bob DeMoss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_DeMoss) was promoted to head coach in 1970.[^cite_note-27] DeMoss inherited a Purdue squad who was loaded at the running back position with Stan Brown, and [Otis Armstrong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otis_Armstrong), but the team struggled to find consistency out of its quarterback position.[^cite_note-28] DeMoss resigned following the 1972 season, citing his desire to be with his family more as his reason for stepping down.[^cite_note-29] DeMoss compiled a career college football record of 13–18.[^cite_note-30]

### Alex Agase era (1973–1976)

After nine seasons as the Northwestern coach, [Alex Agase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alex_Agase) accepted an offer at the end of 1972 to become head football coach at Purdue, one of the two schools for which he played.[^cite_note-Agase_takes_Purdue_job-31] Taking the job was "not an easy decision to make", he said at the time, because he was happy at Northwestern.[^cite_note-Agase_takes_Purdue_job-31] Agase coached at Purdue through the 1976 season, but his team never posted a winning record in his years there.[^cite_note-Agase_coaching_records-32] He was fired in early 1977 and took a job as [athletic director](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athletic_director) at [Eastern Michigan University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Michigan_University) in [Ypsilanti, Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ypsilanti,_Michigan).[^cite_note-Agase_takes_Eastern_Michigan_AD_job-33] He stayed in that job until 1982, when he unexpectedly resigned citing "personal reasons".[^cite_note-Agase_resigns_from_Eastern-34]

### Jim Young era (1977–1981)

In December 1976, Purdue hired 41-year-old [Jim Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Young_\(American_football_coach\)) away from [Arizona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arizona_Wildcats_football).[^cite_note-35] When Young arrived at Purdue, he named true freshman, [Mark Herrmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Herrmann) as the team's starting quarterback, and the freshman lived up to expectations, throwing for 2,041 yards through the team's first eight games.[^cite_note-36] Herrmann would break the NCAA record for passing yards (2,453) and passing touchdowns (18) for freshman.[^cite_note-37] In 1978, Young would lead Purdue to a 9–2–1 record, and a victory over [Georgia Tech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgia_Tech_Yellow_Jackets_football) in the [1978 Peach Bowl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1978_Peach_Bowl). Young was named the Big Ten's Coach of the Year, the first Boilermaker head coach to ever win the award.[^cite_note-38] Throughout his career, Herrmann would break the Big Ten's all-time career passing yards (6,734) and passing touchdowns (48) before his senior season.[^cite_note-39] After a disappointing 1981 season, Young resigned from his position as head coach at Purdue, citing his desire to concentrate on athletic administration.[^cite_note-40]

### Leon Burtnett era (1982–1986)

In November 1981, defensive coordinator [Leon Burtnett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_Burtnett) was promoted as Purdue's 30th head football coach.[^cite_note-41] During the 1984 campaign, Burtnett's team posted its best season, which the highlight of the year was beating No. 2 Ohio State 28–23.[^cite_note-42] The 1984 squad's 7–4 record earned Burtnett the Big Ten's Coach of the Year Award.[^cite_note-43] His success that year earned him a contract extension through 1990.[^cite_note-44] Burtnett's teams didn't improve after 1984, and after a 3–8 season in 1986, Burtnett resigned as head coach.[^cite_note-burtnett_resigns-45]

### Fred Akers era (1987–1990)

After Burtnett's dismissal, Purdue had verbally agreed to hire [Ron Meyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ron_Meyer) as their next head coach.[^cite_note-Meyer-46] Prior to finalizing a deal with Purdue, the [Indianapolis Colts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indianapolis_Colts) called and offered Meyer a contract. Meyer accepted the Colts offer.[^cite_note-Meyer-46] After they were left in the cold by Meyer, Purdue hired former [Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Longhorns_football) head coach [Fred Akers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Akers).[^cite_note-akers_hire-47] The Akers hiring caused starting quarterback [Jeff George](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeff_George) to transfer due to the Akers running style offense as opposed to Burtnett's passing offense.[^cite_note-akers_hire-47] Akers coached his teams to only 12 wins in four years.[^cite_note-48]

### Jim Colletto era (1991–1996)

[Jim Colletto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Colletto) was named Purdue's head coach in December 1990, accepting the position while he was serving the offensive coordinator for Ohio State.[^cite_note-colletto_hire-49] Colletto came to Purdue with the goal of recruiting kids from the [Chicago](6886.md) area, and keeping Purdue's quarterback tradition trending onward.[^cite_note-colletto_hire-49] During his introduction press conference, he stated that at practice field, he planned to install a small cemetery in which he would place a tombstone for every school Purdue upset or beat on the road.[^cite_note-50] Colletto also provided up change on offense, as he brought his [I formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_formation) with him from Ohio State.[^cite_note-51] During his first season as head coach, the Boilermakers improved winning two more games than they had the year before, and freshman tailback Corey Rogers was named the Big Ten Freshman of the Year.[^cite_note-rogers_suspension-52]

In 1992, Colletto lost Rogers to academic ineligibility, and was forced to use a new running back.[^cite_note-rogers_suspension-52] The Rogers suspension opened the door for what would become Purdue's all-time leading rusher, [Mike Alstott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mike_Alstott).[^cite_note-53] In 1994, the Boilermakers got out to a 4–1–1 start, and were starting to gain national attention.[^cite_note-keyes_excited-54] With Rogers and Alstott leading the way out of the Purdue backfield, Purdue racked up 1,206 and 17 rushing touchdowns in 6 games.[^cite_note-keyes_excited-54] However Purdue stumbled down the stretch, finishing the season 0–4–1. Colletto resigned in November 1996.[^cite_note-55]

### Joe Tiller era (1997–2008)



[Joe Tiller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Tiller), Purdue's all-time leader in victories (87).

[Joe Tiller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Tiller) was hired by Purdue in [1997](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997_NCAA_Division_I-A_football_season).[^cite_note-56] Tiller inherited a program that had only had two winning seasons in the previous 18 years. However, the Boilermakers made an immediate splash in the second game of his rookie season with a nationally televised upset of [Notre Dame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notre_Dame_Fighting_Irish_football). Tiller would go on to lead the Boilermakers to ten bowl berths in 12 years, most notably the [2001 Rose Bowl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001_Rose_Bowl). Prior to Tiller's arrival, Purdue had played in only five bowl games, most recently the [1984 Peach Bowl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1984_Peach_Bowl). On September 20, 2008, in a game versus [Central Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Michigan_Chippewas_football), Tiller won his 85th game at Purdue to become the winningest coach in school history, topping the previous mark set by [Jack Mollenkopf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Mollenkopf) (1956–1969).[^cite_note-57] Tiller's "[basketball on grass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basketball_on_grass)" offense was well renowned for its ability to score and score effectively, befuddling opposing defenses. This was especially the case when [Drew Brees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drew_Brees) ran the team from 1997 to 2000. Tiller's Purdue squads were shut out only once, by [Penn State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_Penn_State_Nittany_Lions_football_team), in a 12–0 defeat at [Ross–Ade Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ross%E2%80%93Ade_Stadium) on October 28, 2006.

Tiller was the first coach to use the [spread offense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spread_offense) in the [Big Ten Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Ten_Conference),[^cite_note-58] although many others have since brought their own version of the spread, including [Jim Tressel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Tressel) at [Ohio State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio_State_Buckeyes_football), [Randy Walker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randy_Walker_\(football_coach\)) at [Northwestern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwestern_Wildcats_football), [Rich Rodriguez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rich_Rodriguez) at [Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Michigan_Wolverines_football), and [Ron Zook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ron_Zook) at [Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illinois_Fighting_Illini_football). Under Tiller and his spread offense, Purdue annually had one of the top offenses in the Big Ten.[^cite_note-59]

### Danny Hope era (2009–2012)

Beginning on approximately January 7, 2008, several media outlets reported that [Danny Hope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danny_Hope) had been offered and accepted the head coaching position at Purdue where it was expected that he would replace coach Joe Tiller as part of a succession plan.[^cite_note-60] During his previous stay at Purdue, Hope was the offensive line coach for Tiller. He is credited with building the [offensive line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offensive_line) that protected [NFL](21211.md) [quarterback](25277.md) [Drew Brees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drew_Brees) and produced several NFL offensive linemen, including [All-Pro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-Pro) [Matt Light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matt_Light).[^cite_note-2_years-61]

In his first game as head coach at Purdue in 2009, the Boilermakers won, 52–31, over [Toledo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_Toledo_Rockets_football_team).[^cite_note-62] Purdue lost their next five games before upsetting No. 7 [Ohio State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_Ohio_State_Buckeyes_football_team), 26–18, at [home](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ross%E2%80%93Ade_Stadium) on October 17.[^cite_note-63] Later during the 2009 season, the Boilermakers won at [Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_Michigan_Wolverines_football_team) for the first time since 1966 with a 38–36 come-from-behind win at [The Big House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_Stadium) on November 7. It was only the third time in program history that Purdue defeated Ohio State and Michigan in the same season.[^cite_note-64] Hope's teams would miss out on bowl games in both the 2009 and 2010 seasons. However, the 2011 team was able record a 6–6 overall record and a 4–4 conference record, including a second win against Ohio State in 3 years. The team would end up going to the [Little Caesars Pizza Bowl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Caesars_Pizza_Bowl), where they defeated [Western Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Western_Michigan_Broncos_football_team) 37–32 to achieve Hope's first winning season at Purdue.[^cite_note-65] Following the season, Burke extended Hope with a two-year contract extension.[^cite_note-2_years-61]

The 2012 season was met with high expectations from fans, alumni, and Hope himself, who proclaimed that it would be his best team with many starters returning.[^cite_note-66] Moreover, with both Ohio State and Penn State serving bowl bans that year, the Boilermakers had a strong opportunity to win the Leaders division title. However, after a 3–1 non-conference start, the team would then open Big Ten play with five straight losses. Although the Boilers would eventually win their final three games, including victories over archrivals [IU](6135487.md) and [Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illinois_Fighting_Illini_football), and become bowl-eligible for the second straight year, athletic director Morgan Burke announced on November 25, 2012, that Hope would be fired.[^cite_note-67] Wide receivers coach [Patrick Higgins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Higgins_\(American_football\)) was named interim coach for the bowl game.[^cite_note-68]

### Darrell Hazell era (2013–2016)

On December 5, 2012, it was announced that [Darrell Hazell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darrell_Hazell) would leave the [Kent State Golden Flashes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kent_State_Golden_Flashes_football) to become the head coach of the Boilermakers.[^cite_note-69] The Boilermakers started the season with a 42–7 loss to the [Cincinnati Bearcats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cincinnati_Bearcats_football) at [Nippert Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nippert_Stadium).[^cite_note-70] The following week against [Indiana State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiana_State_Sycamores_football), Hazell won his first game at Purdue 20–14.[^cite_note-71] After the Boilermakers started 1–2, and [Rob Henry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rob_Henry_\(American_football\)) continuing to struggle in the team's 4th game, [Danny Etling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danny_Etling) was thrust into a game with Purdue trailing 27–10 to [Northern Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Illinois_Huskies_football).[^cite_note-72] Etling finished the game with 241 yards passing while throwing two touchdowns and two interceptions.[^cite_note-73] During the ensuing week, Etling was named the starter for the Boilermakers.[^cite_note-74] With Etling at quarterback, Hazell showed he was playing the 2013 season to gain experience for younger players. The Boilermakers finished the 2013 season 1–11, one of the worst seasons in Purdue history.[^cite_note-75] The Boilermakers went 3–9 in 2014, 2–10 in 2015, and were 3–3 in 2016 when on October 16, 2016, Hazell was fired with a record of 9–33 with the Boilermakers, including 3–24 in conference play.[^cite_note-76] He was replaced on an interim basis by wide receivers coach and recruiting coordinator [Gerad Parker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerad_Parker), who went 0–6 to end the season 3–9.[^cite_note-77]

### Jeff Brohm era (2017–2022)

On December 5, 2016, Purdue hired [Jeff Brohm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeff_Brohm) to become head coach of the football program.[^cite_note-78] Brohm came from [Western Kentucky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Kentucky_Hilltoppers_football), where he was 30–10 in 3 seasons as the team's head coach.[^cite_note-79] Brohm brought instant success to Purdue, success that the program had not experienced since head coach Danny Hope. In their game against [Michigan](18859.md), Purdue sold out [Ross Ade Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ross_Ade_Stadium) for the first time since 2008 in [Joe Tiller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Tiller)'s final game against Indiana. Brohm finished his first regular season with a 6–6 record, including the first victory over rival Indiana since 2012. Brohm also was able to earn a bowl berth for Purdue, their first since the 2012 season, where Purdue defeated Arizona in the Foster Farms Bowl, 38–35.

In Brohm's second season, Purdue enjoyed perhaps even higher success than their first season, even though the team started the year 0–3. Purdue would eventually finish 6–7, with a second consecutive bowl berth. Purdue defeated three ranked teams in 2018: No. 23 Boston College (30–13), No. 2 Ohio State (49–20), and No. 16 Iowa (38–36). Purdue suffered a lopsided 63–14 loss to Auburn in the [Music City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Music_City_Bowl) Bowl. In Brohm's third season, Purdue finished 4–8 and failed to achieve bowl eligibility. Purdue's season ended with a 44–41 loss in two overtimes to Indiana in the Old Oaken Bucket game. In Brohm's fifth season as head coach, Purdue finished the regular season with a record of 8–4, including two top 5 wins in number 2 Iowa (24–7) and number 5 (number 3 in CFP Ranking) Michigan State (40–29). Earning a berth back to the [Music City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Music_City_Bowl) Bowl, the Boilermakers beat Tennessee in overtime, 48–45, and ended the year with a 9–4 record. His 6th season lead to the team's first Big Ten West Champions, but they were defeated by #2 [Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Michigan_Wolverines_football_team) at the Big Ten Championship Game in [Indianapolis](57707.md). Purdue finished the regular season 8–4, with 2021–2022 as the first back-to-back 8–4 seasons or better since [1997](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997_Purdue_Boilermakers_football_team)–[1998](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1998_Purdue_Boilermakers_football_team). Following the Big Ten Championship Game, Brohm would leave Purdue to become the head coach at his alma mater, [Louisville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisville_Cardinals_football). Offensive Coordinator [Brian Brohm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Brohm) took over as the interim head coach for the [Citrus Bowl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_Citrus_Bowl), in which Purdue would fall to the [LSU Tigers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LSU_Tigers_football) 7–63.

### Ryan Walters era (2023–2024)

On December 13, 2022, Purdue announced that it was hiring [Ryan Walters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ryan_Walters_\(American_football\)), the defensive coordinator at [Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illinois_Fighting_Illini_football), to replace Brohm.[^cite_note-80] Purdue was Walters' first head coaching position.[^cite_note-81] In Walters' debut, the Boilermakers fell at home, 35–39, to the [Fresno State Bulldogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_Fresno_State_Bulldogs_football_team) on September 2, 2023. Purdue would go 4–8 (3–6 Big Ten) that season, tying for fourth in the Big Ten West. On heels of a 1–3 start to the 2024 season, Purdue fired offensive coordinator [Graham Harrell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_Harrell).[^cite_note-82] Pressure was mounting that Walters himself should be fired due to the team's historic poor performance throughout the season. After a 66–0 blowout loss to arch rival [Indiana](6135487.md) on November 30, Walters and most of the coaching staff were fired the next day, December 1, ending a disastrous 1–11 (0–9 Big Ten) season.[^cite_note-83] For Walters' buyout, Purdue paid him \$9.34 million.[^cite_note-84]

### Barry Odom era (2025–present)

Purdue announced that it was hiring then-current [University of Nevada, Las Vegas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Nevada,_Las_Vegas) head coach [Barry Odom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Odom) as its next head coach on December 8, 2024. With wins against [Ball State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ball_State_Cardinals_football) and [Southern Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Illinois_Salukis_football) (FCS) at the start of the 2025 season, Odom became the first Purdue head football coach to start their career 2–0 since [Cleo A. O'Donnell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleo_A._O'Donnell) in 1916.[^cite_note-85] The team then went on to lose every following game, ending the season with a record of 2–10 (0–9 Big Ten) following a 3–56 blowout loss at home to [Indiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Indiana_Hoosiers_football_team) in that year's Old Oaken Bucket game. ESPN ranked Purdue's 2025 schedule as the third toughest in the nation, behind [Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Wisconsin_Badgers_football_team) and [Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Florida_Gators_football_team), respectively.[^cite_note-86] Following the 2025 season there were substantial changes in the coaching staff. On December 8, 2025, Odom fired running back coach Lamar Conard, the last remaining staff member from the Walters era, and the offensive coach Vance Vince.[^cite_note-87] On December 18, linebackers coach and defensive coordinator Mike Scherer was fired as well.[^cite_note-88] On December 19, Purdue made a controversial announcement that it was re-hiring former defensive coordinator and linebackers coach [Kevin Kane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevin_Kane_\(American_football\)), who had served the [Minnesota Golden Gophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minnesota_Golden_Gophers_football) for the 2025 season. Kane was let go from Purdue along with most of the coaching staff in 2024 following the firing of Ryan Walters.[^cite_note-89] On December 26, Purdue announced it was hiring veteran coach [Dave Steckel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dave_Steckel_\(American_football\)) as senior defensive analyst.[^cite_note-90] Steckel briefly worked with Odom when they were both on the coaching staff at [Missouri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missouri_Tigers_football).&#x20;

## Bowl games

Purdue has participated in 21 [bowl games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowl_game) throughout its history, compiling an 11–10 record.[^cite_note-97] Purdue did not appear frequently in post-season play from [1967](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1967_NCAA_Division_I-A_football_season) to [1984](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1984_NCAA_Division_I-A_football_season), but they played well, winning 4 of 5 bowl games including four consecutive wins between 1967 and [1980](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980_NCAA_Division_I-A_football_season). When Tiller arrived in 1997, Purdue went to eight consecutive bowl games, but only won three of the eight bowl games. Tiller would lead the Boilermakers to a total of 10 bowl games in his 12 seasons as head coach.[^cite_note-98] Of those 20 bowl appearances, the Boilermakers have participated in 1 "major" Division I-A/FBS bowl games, which was part of the [BCS Bowl Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowl_Championship_Series#Bowl_games). The Boilermakers have never played in a [National Championship Game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_football_national_championships_in_NCAA_Division_I_FBS).

## Facilities

### Stuart Field (1892–1924)

Main article: [Stuart Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_Field)

[Stuart Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_Field) was dedicated on April 16, 1892,[^cite_note-oldreport-102] and named for Charles B. and William V. Stuart, two brothers who served on the university's board of trustees.[^cite_note-reamers-103] Originally a seven-acre[^cite_note-reamers-103] (2.8 ha) field with 800 seats,[^cite_note-oldreport-102] by the 1910s it was expanded to twice that area[^cite_note-reamers-103] and a [seating capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seating_capacity) of five thousand.[^cite_note-kriebel-104]

### Ross–Ade Stadium (1924–present)

Main article: [Ross–Ade Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ross%E2%80%93Ade_Stadium)



[Ross–Ade Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ross%E2%80%93Ade_Stadium) during a game in 2006

The Boilermakers have called [Ross–Ade Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ross%E2%80%93Ade_Stadium) home since 1924. It is named for [David E. Ross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_E._Ross) and [George Ade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Ade), the principal benefactors. Ross–Ade Stadium opened on November 22, 1924, with a [seating capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seating_capacity) of 13,500 and standing room for an additional 5,000 people.[^cite_note-105] A series of additions and renovations pushed the [seating capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seating_capacity) to almost 68,000 (70,000 with standing room). In 2001 [Purdue University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purdue_University) began a \$70 million renovation, which led to a reduced [seating capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seating_capacity) of 62,500. Following 2023 renovations to add seating in the south end zone, the current capacity is 61,441.[^cite_note-RossAde-1]

### Mollenkopf Athletic Center

An indoor training facility used primarily for the football team. It includes a full practice football field, extensive weight room, and offices for the football program. Also housed in Mollenkopf is the Purdue Football Hall of Glory.

## Rivalries

Purdue's major rival has always been [Indiana University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiana_University), with whom they play for the [Old Oaken Bucket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Oaken_Bucket), but during the Joe Tiller era the rivalry with [Notre Dame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notre_Dame_Fighting_Irish_football) in football has become the most heated and most competitive with Joe Tiller led teams going 5–7 vs. Notre Dame. Danny Hope picked up the [Shillelagh Trophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shillelagh_Trophy_\(Notre_Dame%E2%80%93Purdue\)) with a 24–21 loss in his first season as head coach. In addition, Purdue has a long-standing rivalry with [Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illinois_Fighting_Illini_football), with whom they play for the [Purdue Cannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purdue_Cannon) trophy.

Due to having an odd number of teams from 1993 to 2010, the Big Ten utilized a rotating system of conference games. Every school was designated two official rivals, whom they played every year. The official rivals for Purdue were [Indiana](6135487.md) and [Northwestern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwestern_Wildcats_football). However, after the [expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_NCAA_conference_realignment#Big_Ten_Conference) of the Big Ten to 12 schools, Purdue and Northwestern were placed into separate conference divisions and no longer played each other on an annual basis.

From 2011 to 2013, Purdue's new designated cross-division rival was [Iowa](963244.md). This matchup was mocked by fans of both teams. The other cross-divisional rivalries set up by the Big Ten had some history or a trophy behind the pairing, but Purdue and Iowa were left over. The respective SB Nation blogs of [Hammer & Rails](http://www.hammerandrails.com/2014/9/23/6832953/our-most-hated-rival-a-brief-history) and [Black Heart Gold Pants](http://www.blackheartgoldpants.com/football/2014/9/23/6834861/our-most-hated-rival-a-history-of-iowa-being-better-than-purdue) celebrated this rivalry between the two schools with the tongue-in-cheek reference to each other as "Our Most Hated Rivals" or simple "OMHR"

## Culture

### Marching band

Main article: [Purdue All-American Marching Band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purdue_All-American_Marching_Band)

The [Purdue All-American Marching Band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purdue_All-American_Marching_Band) is the [marching band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marching_band) of [Purdue University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purdue_University). The Purdue "All-American" Marching Band (AAMB) is the primary source of auxiliary entertainment for Purdue University football games. AAMB does many service performances for high schools, junior high schools, and elementary schools, and has been the host band of the [Indianapolis 500](159018.md) race every year the race has been held since 1927. The band has grown from an original 5 members to 373 members, making it one of the largest marching bands in the world. The two most distinctive features of the AAMB are the [World's Largest Drum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World's_Largest_Drum) and solo baton twirler the Purdue Golden Girl.

In 1886 the Purdue Student Army Training Corps produced five men who provided music for the Army trainees to listen to during their morning conditioning runs. While operating without a director until 1904, the band had started playing at Purdue football games and had grown to over 50 members. In 1904, Paul Spotts Emrick joined the band. His experience as a conductor resulted in his election as band president and director the next year. During his senior year at Purdue, the marching band, under Emrick, became the first band to break ranks and form a letter on the field—the famous Block "P".[^cite_note-115]

Emrick stayed on as director after his graduation in 1908. In 1921, Emrick commissioned Leedy to construct the World's Largest Drum, and it has been a part of the marching band ever since. In 1935, during a Purdue football game at [Northwestern University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwestern_University) the band donned lights on their uniforms while performing at halftime. With the stadium lights turned off for the performance, the band drew such awe from radio broadcaster [Ted Husing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ted_Husing); he referred to them as a "truly All-American marching band", hence the current title of the band.

### Mascot

#### Boilermaker Special

Main article: [Boilermaker Special](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boilermaker_Special)



The former official mascot of Purdue : The Boilermaker Special V (1993–2011)

The [Boilermaker Special](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boilermaker_Special) is the official [mascot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mascot) of [Purdue University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purdue_University) in [West Lafayette, Indiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Lafayette,_Indiana). It resembles a [Victorian-era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian-era) railroad [locomotive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locomotive) and is built on a truck chassis. It is operated and maintained by the student members of the Purdue Reamer Club.

[Purdue University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purdue_University) is a [land-grant university](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land-grant_university) (or Agricultural and Mechanical (A\&M) university) created through the [Morrill Act of 1862](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morrill_Act_of_1862). In the 1890s, Purdue became a leader in the research of railway technology. For many years Purdue operated the "Schenectady No. 1", and later the "Schenectady No. 2", on a dynamometer in an engineering laboratory on the [West Lafayette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Lafayette) campus. These were [4-4-0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4-4-0) type steam locomotives manufactured by the [Baldwin Locomotive Works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baldwin_Locomotive_Works) of Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. The Schenectady was a classic Victorian-era design similar in construction to the Western and Atlantic Railroad No. 3 (see [The General (locomotive)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_General_\(locomotive\)) on display at the [Southern Museum of Civil War and Locomotive History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Museum_of_Civil_War_and_Locomotive_History)). Purdue even operated its own railroad to connect the campus to a main rail line. In the 1930s the dynamometer was decommissioned and the Schenectady No. 2 was retired as the railroad industry in the United States converted from steam to diesel-electric locomotives.

Purdue did not have a mascot. In 1939, Purdue student Israel Selkowitz suggested the school adopt an official mascot to represent Purdue's engineering heritage. He originally proposed a "mechanical man". After much debate, it was decided to build a locomotive on an automobile chassis. This choice allowed the mascot to build on Purdue's engineering and railroading heritage, as well as represent the school's nickname "Boilermakers" in a meaningful way.

The "Boilermaker" nickname came about during the early years of Purdue football. There had been rumors the university enrolled burly boilermakers from the Monon Railroad shops in Lafayette, Indiana as students/football players to help beef up the scrawny football team. When a railroad operated an extra train independent of the scheduled timetable, it was known as a "special". Thus, the trains which carried Purdue's sporting teams and their fans to other cities for athletic contests were known as "Boilermaker Specials". It was a perfect match.

Financial and moral support for the first Boilermaker Special was provided by key members of the Purdue University graduating class of 1907, and members of the Purdue Reamer Club from the graduating classes of 1940 and 1941.

#### Purdue Pete

Main article: [Purdue Pete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purdue_Pete)

Purdue Pete was first designed as a logo by the University Bookstore in 1940.[^cite_note-sports-116] They would put it on their products and portray him dressed up in different clothes for the different majors. He got the Purdue part of his name from [Purdue University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purdue_University).[^cite_note-smith-117] The owners of the bookstores gave him the name "Pete", yet no one officially knows why this was chosen to be his name.[^cite_note-smith-117] He was given a physical identity in 1956 as he came out and helped the students cheer at a pep rally.[^cite_note-sports-116] Over the years, the appearance of Purdue Pete has gone under several drastic changes as well as several minor changes. His original head was made of [paper-mâché](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper-m%C3%A2ch%C3%A9), pasted onto a chicken wire frame.[^cite_note-smith-117] This was very inconvenient for the person who would be underneath because it would limit his movements, yet he was still expected to move around and do stunts.[^cite_note-smith-117] This head was changed to a giant fiberglass head where the person inside would use a harness to support it. This was unpractical due to the sheer size of it. In the 1980s, Purdue Pete acquired the appearance he is now associated with.[^cite_note-smith-117] Proposals to switch to a soft-sculpture costume were rejected in 2006 and 2011.[^cite_note-alumnus-118][^cite_note-slyder-119]

Purdue's colors are Old Gold and Black, as are its uniforms. Home uniforms are black with old gold numerals while the away uniform is white with black numerals.

Purdue's Pride sticker (given out for good performances) was the Purdue logo (locomotive). This was changed in 2006 to a sledgehammer with the slant "P" in the hammer's head (like the one wielded by mascot Purdue Pete). In the 2006 game against the [Indiana State Sycamores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiana_State_Sycamores_football), Purdue wore a throwback uniform from 1966, in honor of the 40th anniversary of the school's first Rose Bowl team. It featured a white jersey with two outer black stripes on the shoulder and one inner gold stripe. The numerals were black with no outline. The team wore gold pants with two black stripes on the sides, and the helmets were old gold with black numbers and one black stripe down the middle. The gold in the throwback uniforms was more yellow in hue than that in the regular 1997–present uniforms.



[David Hedelin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hedelin) in the all-black Purdue uniforms.

In 2002, Purdue changed from its black home jersey to an old gold jersey. The old gold uniforms had white numerals and black outline for home games, and white uniforms with old gold numbers and back outline for away games. Some complained that the numerals on the jersey were too hard to see, so in 2003 they changed to an old gold jersey with black numerals outlined in white, and white pants with two black stripes down the sides. After losing their opening game at home to Bowling Green, Tiller and the team decided to dump the gold jerseys and go back to the black uniforms. After they changed back to the black uniforms, the team came out to "[Back in Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Back_in_Black_\(song\))" by [AC/DC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AC/DC) for every home game in 2003. Since then, Purdue has stayed with the black uniforms. In one game against Wisconsin in 2006, the Boilermakers wore the black jersey with black pants. They had not sported an all-black look at home since the last game of the season in 1996 against Indiana.

In 2009, Purdue also wore the Black on Black in a night game against Notre Dame on the Purdue Blackout, and then wore them for the remainder of their home games. In 2010, the Black on Black remained the normal home uniform with the exception being the Homecoming game against Minnesota where the team donned Throwback Uniforms for the 2001 Rose Bowl team. While the original jerseys were made by Champion, the replicas were Nike branded.

In 2013, Purdue designed a new black helmet that was used during the Ohio State and Indiana games. The all black helmet had a [matte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matte_\(paint\)) finish with a decal of train tracks down the center of the helmet, with the university's official athletic logo at the end of the tracks. Also on the helmet was the phrase "One Brick Higher", the motto coach Darrell Hazell had chosen for the season.[^cite_note-121] During the 2015 season, Purdue introduced a white helmet, as well as an anthracite gray uniform.

In 2016, Purdue announced that they would have a complete re-design of their uniforms for the 2016 season, using the motto, "Focus, Fight, Finish" for the three different designs.[^cite_note-122]

## Final rankings

Purdue has finished a season ranked in the [Associated Press (AP)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AP_Poll) poll on 17 occasions.[^cite_note-Polls-123] The Boilermakers have finished ranked amongst the top 10 in college football on five occasions. Purdue attained its highest-ever ranking in the polls during the preseason of the 1968 season, when they were ranked No. 1 in the AP Poll for 6 straight weeks before they lost to No. 4 [Ohio State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio_State_Buckeyes_football) 0–13.[^cite_note-Polls-123][^cite_note-124] Since the implementation of the [Bowl Championship Series (BCS)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowl_Championship_Series) in 1998, Purdue has finished the regular season ranked one time in the final [BCS standings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowl_Championship_Series#Rankings). While the Boilermakers are 54–175–5 against opponents ranked in the AP Poll, they have an all-time record of 111–65–10 when ranked in the AP Poll themselves.[^cite_note-2012_Info-125]

### "Spoilermakers"

Purdue has knocked off the [No. 1 ranked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AP_poll) football team in college football seven times over the years—the third most of all the Division I teams in college football. Only [Notre Dame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notre_Dame_Fighting_Irish_football) and [Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_Sooners_football) have accomplished this more times.[^cite_note-126] The Boilermakers have nine wins against AP No. 1 or No. 2 teams as an unranked squad, four more than any other program in the poll era.[^cite_note-127] The first Spoilermaker game occurred in 1950 when Purdue broke Notre Dame's 39-game win streak.[^cite_note-128] In 1953, Purdue ended Michigan State's 28-game win streak with a 6–0 win.[^cite_note-129]
