The ***Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists*** is a bi-monthly, nontechnical [academic journal](324572.md), published by an organization of the same name. The organization named "Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists" is a [nonprofit organization](72487.md) concerning [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science) and global security issues resulting from accelerating [technological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technology) advances that have negative consequences for humanity. It publishes content both at a free-access website and through the journal. The organization has been publishing continuously since 1945, when it was founded by former [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project)[^cite_note-2] scientists as the *Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists of Chicago* immediately following the [atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_bombings_of_Hiroshima_and_Nagasaki). The organization is also the keeper of the symbolic [Doomsday Clock](47611.md), the time of which is announced each January.

## Background

One of the driving forces behind the creation of the *Bulletin* was the amount of public interest surrounding atomic energy and rapid technological change at the dawn of the [Atomic Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_Age). In 1945 the public interest in [atomic warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_warfare) and [weaponry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_weaponry) inspired contributors to the *Bulletin* to attempt to inform those interested about the dangers of the nuclear arms race they knew was coming and about the destruction that atomic war could bring about.[^cite_note-Bombs_Early_Light-3] To convey the particular peril posed by nuclear weapons, the *Bulletin* devised the [Doomsday Clock](47611.md) in 1947, with an original setting of seven minutes to midnight.[^cite_note-4]

The minute hand of the Clock first moved closer to midnight in response to changing world events in 1949, following the first [Soviet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) nuclear test. The Clock has been set forward and back over the years as circumstances have changed; as of 2026, it is set at 85 seconds to midnight.[^cite_note-bulletin2025-5] The Doomsday Clock is used to represent threats to humanity from a variety of sources: nuclear and other weapons of mass destruction, climate change,[^cite_note-6] and disruptive technologies.[^cite_note-7] In 2015, the *Bulletin* unveiled its Doomsday Dashboard,[^cite_note-8] an interactive infographic that illustrates some of the data the *Bulletin*'s Science and Security Board[^cite_note-9] takes into account when deciding the time of the Clock each year. As of August 2018, the *Bulletin*'s Board of Sponsors boasts 14 Nobel Laureates.[^cite_note-10]

In the 1950s, the *Bulletin* was involved in the formation of the [Pugwash Conferences on Science and World Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pugwash_Conferences_on_Science_and_World_Affairs), annual conferences of scientists concerned about nuclear proliferation, and, more broadly, the role of science in modern society.

## History

In late 1945, scientists from the University of Chicago who had been involved in the [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project) that had created the A-bomb formed a group using the name "Atomic Scientists of Chicago" and started publishing a newsletter titled *Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists of Chicago*.[^cite_note-\:0-11][^cite_note-12] The words *of Chicago* were dropped as of the seventh issue, of March 15, 1946, to reflect "the increasingly broad nature of the contents of, and the wider geographical distribution of the contributors to, the Bulletin".[^cite_note-13] The founder and first editor of the *Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists* was [biophysicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biophysics) [Eugene Rabinowitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Rabinowitch) (1901–1973). He founded the magazine, then a newsletter, with physicist [Hyman Goldsmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyman_Goldsmith?action=edit\&redlink=1). Rabinowitch was a professor of botany and biophysics at the [University of Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois_at_Urbana%E2%80%93Champaign) and was also a founding member of the Continuing Committee for the Pugwash Conferences on Science and World Affairs.[^cite_note-Atomic_Age-14] In addition to Rabinowitch and Goldsmith, contributors have included: [Morton Grodzins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morton_Grodzins), [Hans Bethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Bethe), [Anatoli Blagonravov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatoli_Blagonravov), [Max Born](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Born), [Harrison Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harrison_Brown), [Stuart Chase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_Chase), [Brock Chisholm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brock_Chisholm), [E.U. Condon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E.U._Condon?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein), [E.K. In 1949, the Educational Foundation for Nuclear Science incorporated as a not-for-profit [501(c)(3)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/501\(c\)\(3\)) organization to serve as the parent organization and fundraising mechanism of the *Bulletin*. In 2003, the board of directors voted to change the foundation's name to Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists.

## Purpose

The *Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists* began as an emergency action undertaken by scientists who saw urgent need for an immediate educational program about atomic weapons.[^cite_note-Atomic_Age,_vii-17] The intention was to educate fellow scientists about the relationship between their world of science and the world of national and international politics. A second goal was to help the American people understand what nuclear energy and its possible applications to war meant. The *Bulletin* contributors believed the atom bomb would only be the first of many dangers.[^cite_note-Atomic_Age,_vii-17] The aim of the *Bulletin* was to carry out the long, sustained effort of educating people about the realities of the scientific age.

## Doomsday Clock

Main article: [Doomsday Clock](47611.md)



The *Bulletin*'s logo of the Doomsday Clock as of January 2026.

Once the Soviet Union developed atomic weapons, the concern surrounding the world's destruction was a great fear of the scientists working on the *Bulletin.* The proximity of nuclear devastation was a popular interest and, as a result, *Bulletin* co-editor Hyman Goldsmith asked landscape artist [Martyl Langsdorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martyl_Langsdorf) to create a cover for the June 1947 magazine.[^cite_note-\:0-11] Langsdorf, who was married to Manhattan Project physicist [Alexander Langsdorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Langsdorf_Jr.), first considered using the symbol for uranium but then realized that a clock would better convey "a sense of urgency."[^cite_note-18] The resultant Doomsday Clock, which only has bullets labeling the numbers in the upper left hand corner, has been featured on the cover of the *Bulletin* many times since its creation.[^cite_note-19]

The proximity of the minute hand to midnight has been the *Bulletin* leadership's way of warning the public about manmade threats to humanity; the Clock is a metaphor, not a prediction. That is, the time on the clock is not to be interpreted as actual time. When it began in 1947, the minute hand was 7 minutes to midnight; in 1953, when the Soviet Union continued to test more and more nuclear devices, it was 2 minutes to midnight.[^cite_note-Bulletin_Staff-20] This proximity to midnight of the Doomsday Clock during the early 1950s shows the concern that the *Bulletin* contributors had about the Soviet Union and the nuclear arms race. The warnings of the *Bulletin* continued throughout the 1950s and 1960s, and the focus of the efforts shifted slightly from warning about the dangers of nuclear war to the necessity of disarmament. In 2007, the leadership began taking [anthropogenic climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropogenic_climate_change) into account in its Clock discussions. Throughout the history of the Doomsday Clock, it has moved closer to midnight, and farther away, depending upon the status of the world at that time.[^cite_note-Bulletin_Staff-20] The Clock has been getting closer to midnight since 1991, when it was set to 17 minutes to midnight, after the United States and the Soviet Union reached [an agreement on nuclear arms reductions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/START_I).

As of January 27, 2026, the Doomsday Clock stands at 85 seconds to midnight.[^cite_note-\:1-21] It is the closest approach to midnight, exceeding that of 1953, 2018, 2020, 2023, and most recently 2025. The decision to move the hand of the Clock is made by the *Bulletin*'s Science and Security Board, which meets in person twice a year, with subcommittees meeting more often; the announcement of the decision is made every January. Each November, prior to the Science and Security Board's fall discussion, the *Bulletin* hosts an annual dinner and meeting in Chicago;[^cite_note-22] both events are open to the public. Reflecting international events dangerous to humankind, the Clock has been adjusted 27 times since its inception in 1947, when it was initially set to seven minutes to midnight (11\:53pm).

## Online editions

The *Bulletin* has had a public-access website available online for some years, with a subscription magazine that comes out 6 times per year and is currently published by [Taylor & Francis Online](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_&_Francis). An e-newsletter is also available without charge by signing up via the *Bulletin* website.[^cite_note-23]

Backfiles of the subscription magazine are available in the John A. Simpson Collection.[^cite_note-24] The backfile from the first (1945) issue through the November 1998 issue of the *Bulletin* has also been made available free of charge via Google Books.[^cite_note-25]

November/December 2008 was the last print edition of the *Bulletin*, which became all-digital only that year.[^cite_note-Bulletin,_2008,_All-digital-26] [SAGE Publications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SAGE_Publications) began publishing the *Bulletin*'s subscription magazine in September 2010; [Taylor & Francis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_and_Francis) took over from Sage in January 2016.[^cite_note-27]

## Indexing

The journal is indexed in the *[Journal Citation Reports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_Citation_Reports)*, which states that the journal has a 2023 [impact factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_factor) of 1.9, ranking it 44th out of 166 journals in the category "International Relations" and 26th out of 67 journals in the category "Social Issues".[^cite_note-WoS-28]
