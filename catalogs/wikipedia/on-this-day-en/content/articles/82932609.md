The [2026 Iran war](82533582.md) has been analysed in terms of its possible military and political consequences for the [Islamic Republic of Iran](14653.md), [United States](3434750.md), [Israel](9282173.md) and other belligerents, and from international legal and geopolitical standpoints.

In the early months of the war, some analysts argued that Iran's ability to preserve the Islamic Republic, strike American bases, and disrupt the [Strait of Hormuz](29005.md) showed the limits of American military power.[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-2] Other analysts cautioned against treating the Islamic Republic's survival as a strategic victory. The US and Israel inflicted severe losses on Iran's conventional capabilities despite Iran's ability to impose costs on American allies.[^cite_note-3] [Michael Froman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Froman), president of the [Council on Foreign Relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_on_Foreign_Relations), argued after the first ceasefire that it was "simply too soon to tell" whether the war had advanced American interests, citing both American military achievements and Iran's use of the Strait as leverage.[^cite_note-4]

Since hostilities resumed in July, analysts have considered the war outcome unresolved. In August, Reuters' columnist Ron Bousso described the conflict as a stalemate in which neither side had shown a convincing strategy for breaking the deadlock.[^cite_note-5]

[Donald Trump](4848272.md)'s threats to destroy Iranian infrastructure have drawn warnings from international law experts that such actions could constitute war crimes under the [Rome Statute](68762.md),[^cite_note-6] and be actionable under [universal jurisdiction](48640.md).[^cite_note-france24-2april-7]

## Objectives

Some analysts noted the lack of a clear objective when starting the conflict.[^cite_note-8]

## Legality

Many experts argue that the war is illegal under US law because launching a war requires congressional authorization. The war is also considered to be in violation of the UN Charter's prohibition on the use of force, because the attacks fail to meet the criteria for self-defense and lack authorization from the United Nations Security Council.[^cite_note-rl-9] In response, the Trump administration claimed that previous presidents have also launched unauthorized military actions, and dismissed international law.[^cite_note-rl-9][^cite_note-JustSecurity2April-10] In [international criminal law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_criminal_law), experts have identified the attacks as a [crime of aggression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime_of_aggression),[^cite_note-guardian2march-11][^cite_note-BatemanJames-12] specifically as a [war of aggression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_aggression).[^cite_note-AFSC-20260309-13][^cite_note-OHCHR-20260312-14] The US attack also violates the [Algiers Accords (1981)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algiers_Accords_\(1981\)) in which the US agreed not to intervene politically or militarily in Iran's internal affairs. Numerous critics and analysts identified the US-Israeli attack with a more open embrace of [expansionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_expansionism_under_Donald_Trump)[^cite_note-Chaisse-2026-15][^cite_note-Chellaney-2026-16] and [imperialism by the Trump administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_imperialism#Trump_administration),[^cite_note-Tait-2026-17][^cite_note-Martin-2026-18] and a departure from the norms of the [liberal international order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_international_order).[^cite_note-bbc24march-19]

### Algiers Accords

> The United States pledges that it is and from now on will be the policy of the United States not to intervene, directly or indirectly, politically or militarily, in Iran’s internal affairs.

— [Algiers Accords (1981)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algiers_Accords_\(1981\)), General Declaration, Point I, Paragraph 1.

[^cite_note-20]

According to the Point I of the General Declaration of the [Algiers Accords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algiers_Accords_\(1981\)) between Iran and the United States, the United States pledged that it would no longer intervene in Iranian internal affairs.[^cite_note-21] Iran has sued the US in the [Iran-United States Claims Tribunal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran-United_States_Claims_Tribunal) (IUSCT) in The Hague over the 2026 war, invoking the Algiers Accords.[^cite_note-22]

### United States law

Many legal and military experts said that US actions in Iran are a type that require congressional authorization. They also noted the frequent use of the word "war" in Trump administration communication about the strikes, supporting the claim that the Trump administration had bypassed congressional war powers. Rubio said that the Trump administration had followed the [War Powers Resolution](209756.md), but questioned whether it was legally binding.[^cite_note-rl-9] Multiple American presidents and administrations have launched wars or military operations without congressional approval.[^cite_note-23] Legal experts have argued that Trump's actions in Iran can be distinguished from prior non-authorized uses of force because the "speculative pre-emptive" justification for this war is comparatively weak when measured against past domestic and international legal rationales.[^cite_note-24]

[US State Department Legal Adviser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_Adviser_of_the_Department_of_State) [Reed D. Rubinstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reed_D._Rubinstein) argued that the US strikes on Iran were justified, as they were launched "in the collective self-defense of its Israeli ally, as well as in the exercise of the United States' own inherent right of self-defense," asserting that the military campaign was not a new war but rather a continuation of the ongoing conflict deriving from "Iran's malign aggression over decades" since the [1979 Islamic Revolution](347268.md). This included the [Iranian attacks on Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Iran%E2%80%93Israel_conflict) in [April](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/April_2024_Iranian_strikes_on_Israel) and [October 2024](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/October_2024_Iranian_strikes_on_Israel), [Iranian proxy warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran_and_state-sponsored_terrorism) against the US and Israel, and [Iran's nuclear program](721807.md).[^cite_note-25] [Qatar Tribune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qatar_Tribune) expressed that the justifications for war against Iran by American officials was reminiscent of 2003 where like the argument of supposed [weapons of mass destruction (WMDs) construction by Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction), war proponents argued that [Iran was also pursuing WMDs](1757165.md) or similar weapons and that the United States needed to intervene militarily.[^cite_note-26]

On 13 March, the US Secretary of Defense [Pete Hegseth](34076202.md) stated "... [no quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_quarter), no mercy for our enemies." Aside from being "especially prohibited" in international law to "declare that no quarter will be given," it is also prohibited in the [US Department of Defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Department_of_Defense)'s own law of war manual. Hegseth's statement likely violates US War Crimes statute [18 U.S.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title_18_of_the_United_States_Code) [§ 2441](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid\:USC-2000-title18-section2441\&num=0\&edition=2000).[^cite_note-JustSecurity2April-10]

On 5 April, Trump repeatedly threatened to bomb all of Iran's bridges and power plants if it did not agree to a deal to end the war, setting a deadline of 8\:00 p.m. [EDT](496028.md) on 7 April. Trump wrote in a [Truth Social](69077351.md) post that "a whole civilization will die" if Iran refused to comply.[^cite_note-27] Lieutenant Colonel Rachel VanLandingham, a professor at [Southwestern Law School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwestern_Law_School), told *PBS News* that targeting electricity systems could affect access to clean water, many civilian facilities including hospitals, and terrify the population. VanLandingham said Trump's rhetoric could constitute a violation of US law, which prohibits "measures of intimidation against a civilian population", including efforts primarily intended to "sow terror amongst that civilian population".[^cite_note-28]

### International law

See also: [United States war crimes § 2026 Iran war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_war_crimes#2026_Iran_war), and [Israeli war crimes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israeli_war_crimes)

#### Legality of US-Israeli attacks

The joint US–Israeli attacks on Iran are widely considered illegal under international law, as they violate the [UN Charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UN_Charter)'s prohibition on the [use of force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Use_of_force_in_international_law) because they fail to meet the criteria for [self-defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-defence_in_international_law) and were not authorized by the [United Nations Security Council](31956.md).[^cite_note-sbs1march-29][^cite_note-guardian2march-11][^cite_note-BatemanJames-12] There is no recognized legal basis for preventive war or forcible [regime change](82756412.md).[^cite_note-30] The attacks have been, therefore, considered a [war of aggression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_aggression).[^cite_note-bbc24march-19][^cite_note-31][^cite_note-AFSC-20260309-13][^cite_note-OHCHR-20260312-14]

[Ben Saul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Saul), UN special rapporteur on human rights and counter-terrorism, stated that Iran had not enriched uranium to the point of building a nuclear device, that experts agreed Iran did not possess a nuclear weapon, and that the case was nowhere close to being self-defense against an imminent attack.[^cite_note-sbs1march-29] Don Rothwell, a professor of international law at the [Australian National University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_National_University), stated that the Security Council did not pass a resolution to attack Iran and had no legal basis for justifying use of force.[^cite_note-sbs1march-29] The *Law Society Journal* noted that striking Iran during active negotiations was against the principles of good faith outlined in Article 2(2) of the UN Charter.[^cite_note-32]

US President Trump had threatened to destroy all the bridges and power plants in Iran and said the country will be brought "back to the Stone Ages, where they belong," if it did not agree to a deal to end the war or reopen the Strait of Hormuz.[^cite_note-33][^cite_note-34][^cite_note-nbc1april-35] According to legal scholar [Tom Dannenbaum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Dannenbaum) at Stanford Law School, Robert Goldman, a war crimes expert at the American University Washington College of Law, and Sarah Yager, the Washington director of Human Rights Watch, targeting Iranian infrastructure, including power plants, would violate international law if it causes harm to civilians that is excessive in relation to the anticipated military advantage.[^cite_note-36] Although immediate prosecution of Trump administration officials for alleged war crimes is unlikely (similar to [Benjamin Netanyahu](56472.md) over the [Gaza war](74998519.md)), such prosecutions in principle can be brought by any country exercising [universal jurisdiction](48640.md) at any time in the future.[^cite_note-37] [Luis Moreno Ocampo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luis_Moreno_Ocampo), the founding chief prosecutor of the [ICC](14880.md), argued that threats by Donald Trump to destroy Iranian infrastructure, alongside mutual attacks on energy infrastructure by Iran and Israel, do not constitute legitimate military targets under international humanitarian law and are war crimes under the [Rome Statute](68762.md). Ocampo likened these actions to the [Russian attacks on Ukrainian energy infrastructure](71963495.md) that led to [ICC indictments](73313153.md).[^cite_note-38] [Stephen J. Rapp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Rapp), former US ambassador-at-large for war crimes issues, said that such conduct risks making the US a [rogue state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogue_state). He and two other international law experts interviewed by NBC News said that Trump's threats alone could potentially constitute a war crime.[^cite_note-nbc1april-35]

More than 100 international law experts signed an open letter in *[Just Security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Just_Security)*[^cite_note-JustSecurity2April-10] expressing "profound concern" over "serious violations of international law", namely:[^cite_note-BatemanJames-12]

- violation of the UN Charter by attacking Iran

  [^cite_note-JustSecurity2April-10]

- violations of international humanitarian law by targeting political leaders with no military role, oil and gas infrastructure (including [attack on South Pars](82720282.md)), and water [desalination](156787.md) plants (e.g. in Iran and Bahrain)

  [^cite_note-JustSecurity2April-10]

- rhetoric and threats from officials, including the threat of [denial of quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_quarter) by Secretary of Defense [Pete Hegseth](34076202.md), dismissal of rules of engagement and international law by Trump and Hegseth, and threats on energy infrastructure by Trump administration.

  [^cite_note-JustSecurity2April-10]

- the US Defense Department's systematic weakening of the protections meant to ensure compliance with international humanitarian law, including removing senior military lawyers and replacing military judge advocates general, undermining legal oversight of combat operations, as well as abolishing mechanisms designed to limit harm to civilians.

  [^cite_note-JustSecurity2April-10]

#### Legality of Iranian response

According to *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)*, under international law, Iran has the right to attack US and Israeli military targets following their attacks on Iran; however, Iranian attacks against civilians and against countries not party to the conflict are illegal.[^cite_note-guardian2march-11] The [blocking of the strait of Hormuz](82550763.md) by Iran was described by Majid Rafizadeh as a violation of the [UN Convention on the Law of the Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UN_Convention_on_the_Law_of_the_Sea) (UNCLOS).[^cite_note-39] Neither Iran nor the United States is a party to the treaty. Iran signed but did not ratify the treaty, and asserts its right to regulate the passage of foreign ships through its territorial waters. The United States treats the UNCLOS as "reflective of customary international law" despite not being a signatory. Bertina Kudrin concluded that "International maritime law, despite its many rules, is not equipped for today’s globalized world and chokepoints," due to the tensions between existing regimes of maritime law and complex developments in maritime economics since their implementation.[^cite_note-40]

> Hostilities in a chokepoint such as the Strait of Hormuz demonstrate tensions between the governing legal regimes: The transit passage legal regime assumes uninterrupted navigation through international straits, while the law of naval warfare permits interference with shipping during armed conflict. At the same time, the law of self-defense evaluates proportionality in relation to the adversary state, even though disrupting a chokepoint can impose economic costs on dozens of neutral countries that depend on the route.

— Bertina Kudrin, The Strait of Hormuz and the Limits of Maritime Law

The [US naval blockade of Iran](82937256.md) was described as legitimate under international law of naval warfare by Australian legal expert Donald Rothwell, though the UN specialized agency [International Maritime Organization](14986.md) insisted that there is "no legal basis in international law" for such action.[^cite_note-41] [Amnesty International](18947898.md) declared Iran's recruiting of child soldiers a war crime.[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43]

## Effects on Iran

### Iranian military strategy and effectiveness

See also: [Mosaic defence strategy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosaic_defence_strategy)

The quicker Iranian response relative to that of the [Twelve-Day War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve-Day_War) suggests a change in Iran's command structure.[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45][^cite_note-BBC_Tehran_response-46][^cite_note-47] [H. A. Hellyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._A._Hellyer), a Middle East expert, notes that Iran has moved beyond its initial goal of mere survival. The regime has proven its resilience and has fundamentally altered the strategic importance of the Strait of Hormuz.[^cite_note-Sabbagh-48]

According to senior adviser to the UAE president Dr. [Anwar Gargash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anwar_Gargash), Iran's strategy intended to undermine the willingness of the Gulf states to support the United States. Iran's targeting of Gulf states may also have been intended to increase energy prices globally, further putting global pressure on the US.[^cite_note-49]

[Trita Parsi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trita_Parsi) said on 19 August that Tehran believed it held the military advantage and had begun openly considering taking the offensive rather than just reacting to American attacks. By doing so, he argued, Iran would take control of the war’s scope, geography, and timing away from the US.[^cite_note-50]

[Vali Nasr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vali_Nasr) explains that Iran is aggressively escalating tensions to deny Donald Trump the ability to manage or downplay the conflict ahead of the midterm elections. By threatening to expand attacks on U.S. and Gulf targets while bolstering its leadership with hardline figures, Tehran aims to force the U.S. to immediately comply with the terms of the MOU.[^cite_note-Tait-51]

### Operation Economic Outcast

See also: [Operation Economic Fury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Economic_Fury), [Iranian economic crisis](78620967.md), [Iran internal crisis (2025–present)](80492259.md), and [Economic D-Day](84050791.md)

"Economic D-Day," or "Operation Economic Outcast" is a U.S.-led campaign of sanctions and economic isolation launched against Iran in August 2026 amid the ongoing Iran war.[^cite_note-toi-52] Various analysts expressed skepticism regarding the effectiveness of the sanctions and warned of significant global repercussions. [Daniel Fried](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Fried) characterized the measures as neither an "economic death blow" nor a game changer,[^cite_note-53] while Andrew L. Peek argued that their impact would be limited by Iran’s ability to evade maritime oil restrictions and source funds through neighbors.[^cite_note-54] The announcement coincided with the [Iranian rial](708656.md) hitting a record low, with the rial dropping to 2.05 million to the US dollar on the open market as trading began, compared with an official central bank rate of around 1.5 million rial to the dollar.[^cite_note-pbs-55] Days earlier, the [United Arab Emirates](69328.md) had announced it was suspending all trade with Iran, one of Iran's largest trading partners and biggest sources of imports.[^cite_note-pbs-55]

Jonathan Panikoff further questioned their efficacy, citing the [Gulf states](220950.md)' reluctance to isolate Iran,[^cite_note-56] the difficulty of severing China's financial ties to Tehran,[^cite_note-57] and regional doubts about U.S. policy consistency.[^cite_note-58]

Beyond effectiveness, Maia Nikoladze warned that escalating against Chinese financial institutions could trigger Chinese retaliation in critical minerals,[^cite_note-59] a sentiment echoed by [Kenneth Rogoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Rogoff), who noted that sanctions might accelerate dedollarization and prompt China to leverage its control over rare earths and pharmaceuticals.[^cite_note-\:25-60] Lesley Chavkin aligned with Rogoff, observing that such measures could drive trade toward non-dollar financial channels that remain inaccessible to the United States.[^cite_note-61]

### Strait of Hormuz

The war unexpectedly showed Iran's most important tool is neither its missile program nor its nuclear program, but the ability to disrupt the Strait of Hormuz, which quickly put pressure on US and its allies.[^cite_note-62] [International Crisis Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Crisis_Group)'s Iran Project director Ali Vaez stated, "In the attempt to try to prevent [Iran from developing a weapon of mass destruction](1757165.md), the US handed Iran a weapon of mass disruption" in the form of weaponized control over the Strait of Hormuz.[^cite_note-63] According to [Frank Gardner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Gardner_\(journalist\)), Iran has effectively "broken out of its box".[^cite_note-64]

### Yemeni Leverage

According to Gregory Brew, a senior analyst at Eurasia Group, the Houthis' success in Yemen has shifted the balance of power back in Iran's favor. Iran has managed to alter the status quo without triggering a full-scale war, which its leadership likely wants to avoid for now.[^cite_note-65]

### Internal Iranian politics

On 17 April 2026, [Abdullah Mohtadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdullah_Mohtadi) of the [Komala Party of Iranian Kurdistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Komala_Party_of_Iranian_Kurdistan) viewed the Islamic Republic as having been "\[in]sufficiently weakened" by the war for Iranian Kurdish groups to take action towards their goal of [federalism in Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federalism_in_Iran), which, according to Mohtadi, they see as necessary for democratisation.[^cite_note-Rudaw_federalism_key_future-66]

### Iranian civilians

Although the US President Trump started the war with a promise to help free the Iranians from the regime, nearly five months later, the US forces have started targeting the civilian infrastructure those same people depend on.[^cite_note-67]

### American negotiating strategy and effectiveness

During the [2026 Iran war ceasefire](82888027.md), analyst [Trita Parsi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trita_Parsi) argued that Trump had become increasingly desperate for a deal with Iran to the point of even denying ceasefire violations by Iran to maintain it. Furthermore, he suggested that Trump was looking for a "silver bullet" instead of serious negotiations with Iranian leaders.[^cite_note-68] Allison Minor, director of the Project for Middle East Integration at the Atlantic Council, argues that to end the war, President Trump must learn to "lose well" by reframing victory rather than pursuing an impossible unconditional surrender from Iran. She tells DW that this shift requires the U.S. to accept painful short-term concessions to secure its long-term interests.[^cite_note-69] According to [The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)'s diplomatic editor, [Patrick Wintour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Wintour), Tehran has crafted a long-term negotiating strategy meant to trap Donald Trump in the Iran conflict through the midterms, hoping to replicate the 1979 hostage crisis and inflict the same ruinous blow on his presidency that destroyed [Jimmy Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Carter)'s.[^cite_note-70] Ali Vaez, deputy program director for the Middle East and North Africa at the International Crisis Group, warned that if the expired memorandum of understanding is not revived, the standoff between the United States and Iran could spiral into a "forever war", adding that Washington's economic pressure would only harden Tehran’s position.[^cite_note-71]

### American geopolitical status

Several articles starting from April onward have argued that the 2026 Iran war has weakened the United States' reputation and geopolitical status, resulting in [American decline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_decline). *[The Conversation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Conversation_\(website\))* listed several reasons for why the United States has been in a weakened position, namely loss of influence in the Middle East, limiting its geopolitical involvement in other regions, the massive economic crisis from the 2026 Strait of Hormuz crisis, and the rupture of the world order leading to loss of American global power.[^cite_note-72] In the opinions column of *[The New York Times](30680.md)*, journalist [Christopher Caldwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Caldwell_\(journalist\)) stated that the war had "turned into a watershed in the decline of the American empire" because of its overextension. He argued that while the so-called "[Donroe Doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donroe_Doctrine)", practiced in execution by the [2026 United States intervention in Venezuela](82021123.md), the [Greenland crisis](82044223.md), and the [2026 Cuban crisis](82348646.md), was a "logical, even an admirable, foreign policy plan", the Iran war was illogical because it was not "defensive" for foreign policy purposes and became another trap as another "Middle Eastern military misadventure".[^cite_note-73] *[The Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Nation)* wrote that this consensus was becoming more common outside of pro-Trump partisan circles of "knee-jerk loyalty".[^cite_note-74] *[The Salt Lake Tribune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Salt_Lake_Tribune)* criticized Trump for his exaggerations of Iranian missiles and nuclear weapons being able to hit Americans and for initially predicting a quick victory where Iran would surrender and change their leadership. The article further lambasted him for his war drastically increasing gas prices and for contradicting demands including by repeatedly backing down in a "TACO" ([Trump always chickens out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trump_always_chickens_out)) process.[^cite_note-75] Neoconservative scholar [Robert Kagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Kagan) in *The Atlantic* wrote that the United States had suffered a "total defeat" that was unprecedented in its history as a result of its war in Iran, arguing that there was no way for it to return to the *status quo ante* and no way to an "American triumph" for it. He suggested that Iran would have no interest in reopening the Strait of Hormuz and that Trump was too unreliable an actor given how he previously boasted of a surprise attack like the 1941 [Attack on Pearl Harbor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attack_on_Pearl_Harbor). As a result, he continued, the war helped accelerate the world into an increasingly post-American world order, where the United States' "once-dominant position in the Gulf" will have been the first to have been lost.[^cite_note-76]

The historian [Alfred W. McCoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_W._McCoy) described the war as another "micro-military disaster" that accelerated the destruction of successive empires over the past 2,500 years.[^cite_note-77]

### American military strategy and effectiveness

Analysts argued that deliberate reductions to US intelligence capacity in the period preceding the conflict had created structural limitations on American operational effectiveness, with the rescue of the downed F-15E crew exposing the extent to which institutional erosion was constraining strategic outcomes across multiple theatres.[^cite_note-78] *[The Atlantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Atlantic)* noted the diplomatic strain between Trump and European NATO member nations, as Trump had previously not consulted them regarding the entrance to war against Iran and had since lashed out at them for not joining the war campaign against it.[^cite_note-79]

*[The Sunday Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sunday_Guardian)* referred to Trump as "coward-in-chief" for failing to use his massive military advantage to fully defeat the Islamic Republic, thus making it appear as if the US is losing against Iran despite the technical stalemate.[^cite_note-80] *The New York Times* opinion columnist [Thomas L. Friedman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_L._Friedman) wrote that the Iran war demonstrated that Trump had acted less as commander-in-chief and more as "commander-in-thief". He argued that by alienating Democrats and prioritizing an [Anti-Weaponization Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Weaponization_Fund) as part of *[Trump v. Internal Revenue Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trump_v._Internal_Revenue_Service)* to the point of condemnation by some Republicans, Trump had failed to unite his country to war against Iran. Furthermore, he continued, Trump had also alienated traditional allies of the US from the war by emphasizing expansionism against them, creating economic pressure against them, and not consulting NATO allies on the war beforehand.[^cite_note-81]

Former UK adviser [Peter Ricketts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Ricketts) argues that airstrikes cannot force Iran to yield to US demands. Tehran maintains significant leverage because it can endure military strikes and retaliate by closing the Strait of Hormuz, creating massive economic consequences for the United States.[^cite_note-Sabbagh-48]

The Iran war offered China, Russia and North Korea a rare opportunity to learn about certain new American weapons and tactics in combat, as well as how quickly the U.S. depleted its long-range Tomahawk missiles and Patriot interceptors.[^cite_note-82]

### Role of lobbies and think tanks

*[Foreign Policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Policy)* argued that the [Israel lobby in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel_lobby_in_the_United_States) held a lot of responsibility for the Iran war because of its influence in degrading relations between the United States and Iran to the point of full hostility and because it enabled Israeli aggression in the region under Israeli prime minister [Benjamin Netanyahu](56472.md).[^cite_note-83] *[The Forward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Forward)* pointed out that Israeli influence on the United States government and that of pro-Israel lobbying group [AIPAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AIPAC) (American Israel Public Affairs Committee) had considerable influence on American foreign policy, especially leading to the Iran war of 2026.[^cite_note-84] *[Jacobin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobin)* accused AIPAC of influencing US Congress representatives in voting against measures to block further US military action on Iran under Trump.[^cite_note-85] *[The Economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Economist)* observed further American public backlash against pro-Israel lobbying like from AIPAC as a result of the 2026 war, previously accelerated by the Gaza war beginning in 2023.[^cite_note-86]

The [Quincy Institute for Responsible Statecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quincy_Institute_for_Responsible_Statecraft) blamed neoconservative and pro-Israel think tanks for promoting warfare against Iran months before the 2026 war began, suggesting that they held the same level of responsibility for pushing for the [Iraq War](5043324.md) that began in 2003.[^cite_note-87] *[Al Jazeera](30204052.md)* pointed out that the neoconservative think tank [Foundation for Defense of Democracies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundation_for_Defense_of_Democracies), which includes networks of former Israeli military and intelligence officials, held heavy influence over the Trump administration's hostility towards Iran, thus setting up confrontation towards the Iran war.[^cite_note-88]

### Trump and Republican party

Vali Nasr argues that Iran is deliberately escalating tensions to prevent Donald Trump from controlling or minimizing the conflict before the midterm elections.[^cite_note-Tait-51] According to Iran analyst Farzan Sabet of the Geneva Graduate Institute, Iran is likely to escalate militarily—while avoiding full-scale war—in hopes of raising global oil prices or closing the Strait of Hormuz, betting that an unpopular conflict ahead of the U.S. midterm elections could harm President Trump and the Republican Party.[^cite_note-89] According to [Mohamad Bazzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohamad_Bazzi), director of New York University's Hagop Kevorkian Center for Near Eastern Studies, Trump began laying the groundwork for apocalyptic threats against Iran just weeks into the war, after it became clear that the United States would not achieve the swift military victory he had hoped for, while his repeated threats to "wipe out" Iran risk normalizing mass violence.[^cite_note-90]

## Effects on other states

### China

See also: [China in the 2026 Iran war](82696964.md)

The war has been described as having various benefits and drawbacks for Chinese geopolitical interests. The [BBC](19344654.md) reported that the closure of the [Strait of Hormuz](29005.md) and the war in Iran may weaken Chinese interests in countries that rely on Gulf countries' oil.[^cite_note-ub-91] US aggression in Iran played into Chinese rhetoric that frames China as "a pillar of stability in contrast to an unpredictable America." Analysts noted that China has interests both in Iran and in the Gulf states, and that Gulf states may seek closer ties with China after facing attacks from Iran because of their US-alignment. However, analysts also pointed to the loss of discounted sanctioned oil and the inflationary impact of oil scarcity as potential economic drawbacks to the war.[^cite_note-92] [Ashok Swain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashok_Swain) suggested that Trump's coercive diplomacy and expansionist actions against Iran would erode American power due to overstretching its resources in favor of China.[^cite_note-93]

[*The Diplomat*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Diplomat_\(magazine\)) argued that the United States, rather than China as US foreign policy analysts initially framed, became the revisionist power of the 2020s undermining international law and world peace, as evident by the former having started "an unprovoked war against Iran for reasons no one in the Trump administration seems to know" among many other imperialist and expansionist actions.[^cite_note-94]

Zhao Long, director of the Institute for Strategic and International Security Studies at the Shanghai Institute for International Studies, said after unveiling the "[Operation Economic Outcast](84050791.md)" against Iran that China is unlikely to accept Washington dictating its companies' trade with third countries or becoming a tool for the US's maximum pressure strategy.[^cite_note-95]

### Israel

See also: [2026 Lebanon war](82549840.md) and [Iran–Israel proxy conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Israel_proxy_conflict)

Based on the Israeli political landscape and enthusiasm for the war, [Daniel Levy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Levy_\(political_analyst\)) wrote on 30 March that its interests may be opposed to all who are seeking an end to the war. He said the Lebanon theatre of war was another campaign with no clear endgame. He concluded that "Israel will continue to encourage and provoke escalation and to undermine any negotiation or ceasefire talks, including nudging the US towards ground operations." He said Israel's actions accelerate US global decline.[^cite_note-96]

### Russia

See also: [Russia in the 2026 Iran war](82668542.md)

[The Washington Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Washington_Institute_for_Near_East_Policy) said that the war had been a setback for [Russian influence in the region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_foreign_policy_in_the_Middle_East). However, they also noted that it may distract the [United States](3434750.md) from the [war in Ukraine](70149799.md).[^cite_note-97]

## Geopolitical implications

### American expansionism

Numerous critics and analysts identified the US-Israeli attack as a more open embrace of [expansionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_expansionism_under_Donald_Trump)[^cite_note-Chaisse-2026-15][^cite_note-Chellaney-2026-16] and [imperialism by the Trump administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_imperialism#Trump_administration).[^cite_note-Tait-2026-17][^cite_note-Martin-2026-18] Similarly, Israel's confrontation with Iran and occupation of Lebanese territory and resources has been considered to be territorial and influential expansionism, especially in relation to the "[Greater Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Israel)" ideology.[^cite_note-98][^cite_note-99][^cite_note-100] The US–Israeli attack is widely considered a "[war of choice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_aggression)".[^cite_note-101] According to the founding chief prosecutor of the [International Criminal Court](14880.md) (ICC) [Luis Moreno Ocampo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luis_Moreno_Ocampo), the US war on Iran is comparable to [Russia's war in Ukraine](42085878.md), adding that the world is going from the "rules-based system" to the "rule of the man", which is "not a viable world".[^cite_note-bbc24march-19] A number of US and Israeli officials, including Hegseth,[^cite_note-102] have made [religious justifications for the war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_justifications_for_the_2026_Iran_war).[^cite_note-103][^cite_note-104]

President Trump addresses the nation assuring that the military activity in the Middle East was "nearing completion", 1 April.

[Gilbert Achcar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert_Achcar) said that the US was practicing a modernized version of [gunboat diplomacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunboat_diplomacy).[^cite_note-105] Gokay and Hamourtziadou connected the attack on Iran with a longstanding pattern of [US interventions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_interventions) in the region. They also connected the attack on Iran in the midst of negotiations to the history of United States treaty-breaking during [westward expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westward_expansion).[^cite_note-Hamourtziadou-2026-106] Saeed Shah wrote that many in the [Global South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_South) do not view the war as having the moral purpose that the US and many of its allies asserted, and that they instead see the war as a failure of diplomacy, and an act of aggression against a weaker nation. International relations experts said that the erosion of international law was making nations in the Global South feel insecure, and more likely to support a coalition against US interests.[^cite_note-107]

### Nuclear proliferation

See also: [Nuclear proliferation](22158.md)

An analyst at Eagle Intelligence Reports argued that the strikes reinforced [North Korea's nuclear deterrence calculus](106424.md#Nuclear_weapons), with Pyongyang interpreting the military action against a non-nuclear Iran as strategic validation of its own weapons programme as essential to regime survival, and that the conflict would accelerate North Korean nuclear expansion while making denuclearisation talks "almost unimaginable in the foreseeable future".[^cite_note-108]

The war took place while [non-proliferation of nuclear weapons](22158.md) was "already under significant strain." In February 2026, [New START](26709878.md) expired. Additionally, as "lessons of the [Cold War](325329.md) and the devastating consequences of nuclear weapons fade from living memory", public support to possess nuclear weapons has reportedly risen in several countries.[^cite_note-109] The war along with the histories of other countries which have given up their ambitions for nuclear weapons or the weapons themselves such as [Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction), [Libya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libya_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction) and [Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukraine_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction) may also [incentivize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incentivize) nuclear proliferation.[^cite_note-110][^cite_note-111][^cite_note-112]

## Comparisons to other conflicts

### Suez crisis

Multiple sources compared the closure of the Strait to the [Suez Crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_Crisis), suggesting that it would similarly result in a strategic failure for the US.[^cite_note-113][^cite_note-114][^cite_note-115]

### Vietnam War

*[Foreign Policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Policy)* considered the Iran war to be a major defeat for the US, one that was far bigger than that in the [Vietnam War](32611.md). The author wrote that while the Iran war looked like a typical US defeat in other conflicts, Trump's loss marked an unambiguously weaker US geopolitical position with its strategic objectives being weakened.[^cite_note-116] [Howard W. French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_W._French), the American journalist, also draws parallels between the Iran war and the Vietnam War, invoking the sage's warning: "Those who cannot remember the past are condemned to repeat it."[^cite_note-117] Admiral [James G. Stavridis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_G._Stavridis) noted that while Iran "doesn’t have a great hand of cards ... they're playing it pretty well," using a war of attrition strategy—similar to the North Vietnamese—to try and force the U.S. to walk away.[^cite_note-118]

### Iran hostage crisis

*The Conversation* suggested that similar to the 1979 to 1981 [Iran hostage crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran_hostage_crisis) that humiliated the US and drastically weakened president [Jimmy Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Carter)'s reelection chances for the [1980 United States presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980_United_States_presidential_election), Iran may have been employing similar methods of stalling the war to weaken president Donald Trump ahead of the [2026 United States elections](70357296.md).[^cite_note-119] Middle East analyst [Aaron David Miller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aaron_David_Miller), a senior fellow at the [Carnegie Endowment for International Peace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnegie_Endowment_for_International_Peace), argued that Trump was trapped in the conflict because his objectives regarding the Gulf region and the Strait of Hormuz were holding him hostage. He continued that just as Iran had once damaged Carter's reputation, Trump's was set on the same path.[^cite_note-120]

### Iran–Iraq War

See also: [Iran–Iraq War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Iraq_War)

*[ThePrint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ThePrint)* compared the 2026 Iran war to the Iran–Iraq War, suggesting that both Donald Trump and Iraqi president [Saddam Hussein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saddam_Hussein) lured themselves into believing that they achieve easy victories against Iran in warfare but found themselves stuck in quagmires as a result.[^cite_note-121]

### Iraq War

See also: [Iraq War](5043324.md) and [War in Iraq (2013–2017)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_in_Iraq_\(2013%E2%80%932017\))

*[Financial Times](136566.md)* recalled the "[you break it, you buy it](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/You_break_it,_you_buy_it)" rule used previously during the Iraq War, where the US has caused the [2026 Strait of Hormuz crisis](82550763.md) that threatened to overturn the global oil supply and hence "owned" that problem.[^cite_note-122] Contrasting the European responses to the Iran and Iraq wars, [Serge Halimi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serge_Halimi) and Pierre Rimbert wrote that "\[European] governments that once vehemently opposed the war in Iraq now limit themselves to muted protest."[^cite_note-123]

### Russo-Ukrainian War

See also: [Russo-Ukrainian war (2022–present)](70149799.md)

*[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)* wrote that both Russian president [Vladimir Putin](32817.md) and Donald Trump had gotten themselves trapped in their wars of choice because neither had been able to resolve their respective conflicts in their favors. They continued that both presidents had long built their "authoritarian strongmen" images but, as a result, surrounded themselves with sycophants who refused to push back against their respective leaders.[^cite_note-124]
