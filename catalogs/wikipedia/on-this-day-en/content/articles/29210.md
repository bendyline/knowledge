The **Sydney Swans** are an [Australian rules football](2403.md) club based in [Sydney](27862.md), New South Wales. The men's team competes in the [Australian Football League](4732785.md) (AFL), and the women's team in the [AFL Women's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AFL_Women's) (AFLW). The Swans also field a [reserves men's team](#Reserves_team) in the [Victorian Football League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_Football_League) (VFL). The Sydney Swans Academy, consisting of the club's best junior development signings, contests Division 2 of the [men's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AFL_Under-19_Championships) and [women's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AFL_Women's_Under-18_Championships) underage national championships and the [Talent League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talent_League).

The club's origins trace back to 21 March 1873, when a meeting was held at the Clarendon Hotel in [South Melbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Melbourne) to establish a junior football club, to be called the **South Melbourne Football Club**.[^cite_note-5] The club commenced playing in 1874 at its home ground, [Lakeside Oval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakeside_Stadium) in [Albert Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Park,_Victoria). Playing as South Melbourne, it participated in the [Victorian Football Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_Football_League) (VFA) competition from 1878. In 1880 the club merged with the [Albert Park Football Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Park_Football_Club_\(VFA\)), adopting the latter's red and white colours.[^cite_note-6] The club joined the breakaway [Victorian Football League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_Football_League_\(1897-1989\)) (VFL) as a founding member in 1897.&#x20;

Originally known as the "Bloods" in reference to the red colour used on players' [guernseys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guernsey_\(Australian_rules_football\)), the Swan emblem was adopted in 1933 after a journalist at the time referred to them using the moniker following a large influx of Western Australian players. In 1982, it became the first professional Australian football club to permanently relocate interstate (from [Victoria](4689460.md) to New South Wales). Initially playing in Sydney as "The Swans", it was given its current name in 1983.[^cite_note-7]

The club has a rivalry with the [Greater Western Sydney Giants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Western_Sydney_Giants), with whom they contest the [Sydney Derby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Derby_\(AFL\)). Their headquarters and training facilities are located in the [Moore Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore_Park,_New_South_Wales) sporting precinct, with offices and indoor training at the Royal Hall of Industries and outdoor sessions conducted on the adjacent [Tramway Oval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tramway_Oval) and [Sydney Cricket Ground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Cricket_Ground), the latter being the site of the club's senior men's team home matches since 1982. The Swans have won five VFL/AFL premierships including [1909](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1909_VFL_Grand_Final), [1918](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1918_VFL_Grand_Final), and [1933](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1933_VFL_Grand_Final), before experiencing a 72-year premiership drought—the longest of any team in the competition's history. This premiership drought ended with the [2005 premiership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_AFL_Grand_Final), which was later followed by another title in [2012](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_AFL_Grand_Final). Their five premierships are supplemented by fourteen grand final defeats, the most recent of which came in [2024](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_AFL_Grand_Final). According to [Roy Morgan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Morgan) statistics, the Swans are one of the most supported clubs in the AFL with more than a million fans in 2021;[^cite_note-8] it has also been the most supported club in the league for the past nineteen years consecutively, dating back to 2006.[^cite_note-9]

## History

### Origins: 1873–1876

The club's origins trace back to 21 March 1873, when a meeting was held at the Clarendon Hotel in [South Melbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Melbourne) for the purpose of establishing a junior football club, to be called the South Melbourne Football Club.[^cite_note-10]

According to club historians, it was incorporated at the Temperance Hall, Napier Street at Emerald Hill 19 June 1874.[^cite_note-11] It was first known as "Cecil Football Club" (after Cecil Street, [South Melbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Melbourne), one of the early thoroughfares),[^cite_note-12] but adopted the name "South Melbourne Football Club" four weeks later on 15 July.[^cite_note-13] The club was based at [Lake Oval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Oval) alongside the lake in Albert Park, also home of the [South Melbourne Cricket Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Melbourne_Cricket_Club).[^cite_note-Lakeside_Stadium-14][^cite_note-15]

While one of the early favourites to win, South Melbourne were a notable exclusion from the [Challenge Cup competitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Challenge_Cup_\(Australian_rules_football\)) of the 1870s with entry to this competition strictly limited to clubs playing under the [Melbourne Football Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melbourne_Football_Club)'s rules. The club's lack of adherence to the Victorian Rules (and insistence on playing by its own rules) resulted in some controversial early wins.[^cite_note-16] This, along with ability to regularly field a full senior team, may have contributed to its absence during the football season competition begun in the 1870s. Throughout this period South Melbourne, along with neighbouring Cup member club [Albert Park FC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Park_Football_Club_\(VFA\)), had experimented with [rugby football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugby_football) rules which in May 1874 had advocated strongly for their widespread adoption in Victoria;[^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18] however, this did not meet favour with the more powerful clubs in the colony.

Despite not being part of the big league of clubs, South Melbourne by the mid-1870s is recorded to have enough senior players to field two teams of twenty and played matches against non-Cup clubs during this time against nearby clubs including Fawkner Park, Elwood,[^cite_note-19] [West Melbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Melbourne_Football_Club), Southern Rifles in 1875[^cite_note-20] and Sandridge Alma,[^cite_note-21] St Kilda Alma,[^cite_note-22] Victoria Parade,[^cite_note-23] and [Williamstown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Williamstown_Football_Club) in 1876[^cite_note-24] among others. Many of this group of clubs most of which had primarily juniors had also begun to discuss starting their own cup competition.

South was one of Victoria's most prolific touring clubs. Seeking more regular senior competition in 1876 South Melbourne also went on one of its first regional tours playing against newly formed clubs at [Beechworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beechworth_Football_Club), [Blackwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackwood,_Victoria), [Taradale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taradale,_Victoria) and [Ballarat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballarat).[^cite_note-25]

### VFA era: 1877–1896

South Melbourne was a junior foundation club of the [Victorian Football Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_Football_Association) in 1877, and attained senior status in 1879.[^cite_note-26]

#### Amalgamation with Albert Park

In 1880, South Melbourne amalgamated with the nearby [Albert-park Football Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Park_Football_Club_\(VFA\)), which had a senior football history dating back to May 1867 (Albert-park had, in fact, been known as South Melbourne during its first year of existence).[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28] Following the amalgamation, the club retained the name South Melbourne, and adopted the club's now familiar red and white colours from Albert-park.[^cite_note-29] Nicknamed the "Southerners", the team was more colourfully known as the "Bloods", in reference to the bright red diagonal sash on their white jumpers[^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31] The colourful epithet the "Bloodstained Angels" was also in use. Following the 1880 amalgamation it became the strongest in metropolitan Melbourne.[^cite_note-review-32]

#### VFA success



The Bloods and the [touring British Lions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1888_British_Lions_tour_to_New_Zealand_and_Australia) in front of the grandstand at the South Melbourne Cricket Ground in 1888. South Melbourne won 7 goals to 3

Over its first decade as an amalgamated club, South Melbourne won five VFA premierships – in 1881, 1885 (undefeated) and three-in-a-row in 1888, 1889 and 1890 – and was runner-up to the provincial [Geelong Football Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geelong_Football_Club) in 1880, 1883 and 1886. The 1886 season was notable for its [4 September match](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Melbourne_vs_Geelong_\(1886_VFA_season\)) against Geelong, which generated unprecedented public interest as both clubs had entered the match undefeated.[^cite_note-33]

The club was the second Victorian club to visit New South Wales in 1883 travelling to Newcastle where it also defeated the Northern Districts League by a goal before travelling to Sydney where it defeated [Sydney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Football_Club_\(NSWAFL\)) by just a single goal in front of a large crowd at the Sydney Cricket Ground[^cite_note-34] and East Sydney Football Club by a goal in front of 600 spectators.[^cite_note-35]

In May 1889, the Swans lost narrowly to a [touring Maori team from New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_1888%E2%80%9389_New_Zealand_Native_football_team_matches).[^cite_note-36]



South Melbourne played in the first Victorian Grand Final, the [1896 VFA Grand Final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1896_VFA_Grand_Final)

At the end of the 1896 season, [Collingwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collingwood_Football_Club) and South Melbourne finished equal at the top of the VFA's premiership ladder with records of 14–3–1, requiring a [playoff match](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1896_VFA_season#Playoff_match) to determine the season's premiership; this was the first time this had occurred in VFA history.[^cite_note-rd20-37] The match took place on 3 October 1896 at the [East Melbourne Cricket Ground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Melbourne_Cricket_Ground). Collingwood won the match, six goals to five, in front of an estimated crowd of 12,000.[^cite_note-argusplayoff-38]

This grand final would be the last match South Melbourne would play in the VFA, as the following season they would be one of eight founding clubs forming the breakaway [Victorian Football League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_Football_League_\(1897%E2%80%931989\)) joining [St Kilda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Kilda_Football_Club), [Essendon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essendon_Football_Club), [Fitzroy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitzroy_Football_Club), [Melbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melbourne_Football_Club), [Geelong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geelong_Football_Club), [Carlton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlton_Football_Club) and [Collingwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collingwood_Football_Club).[^cite_note-39]

### VFL entry: 1897–1909



[1909](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1909_VFL_season) VFL team

South Melbourne was one of the original founding clubs of the [Victorian Football League](4732785.md) that was formed in [1897](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1897_VFL_season).[^cite_note-40]

### Premiership success: 1909–1945



Aerial photo of the South Melbourne cricket ground during the 1920s with a SMFC match in progress

The club had early success and won three VFL premierships in 1909, 1918 and 1933.[^cite_note-41] However, they were the subject of some off-field difficulties, and the late 1920s, the [South Melbourne Districts Football Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Melbourne_Districts_Football_Club) donated 40 [guineas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinea_\(coin\)) to South Melbourne to stop them from folding.[^cite_note-vafasmd-42] The Districts also often provided assistance payments to players when needed.[^cite_note-vafasmd-42]

In 1932, the red sash on the guernsey was replaced with a red "V".[^cite_note-43] The club was at its most successful in the 1930s, when key recruits from both Victoria and interstate led to a string of appearances in the finals, including four successive grand final appearances from 1933 to 1936, albeit with only one premiership in 1933. The collection of players recruited from interstate in 1932/1933 became known as South Melbourne's "Foreign Legion".[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45]

On grand final eve, 1935, as the Swans prepared to take on Collingwood, star full-forward Bob Pratt was clipped by a truck moments after stepping off a tram and subsequently missed the match for South. Ironically, the truck driver was a South Melbourne supporter.[^cite_note-46][^cite_note-47]



Captain [Laurie Nash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurie_Nash) marks on 26 June 1937

It was during this period that the team became known as the Swans. The nickname, which was suggested by a *[Herald and Weekly Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herald_and_Weekly_Times)* artist in 1933, was inspired by the number of Western Australians in the team (the [black swan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_swan) being the state emblem of Western Australia), and was formally adopted by the club before the following season 1934.[^cite_note-J._M._Rohan_7-48] The name stuck, in part due to the club's association with nearby [Albert Park and Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Park_and_Lake), also known for its [swans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swan) (although there are no longer any non-native white swans and only black, indigenous swans in the lake).[^cite_note-49]

After several years with only limited success, South Melbourne next reached the grand final in 1945. The match, played against Carlton, was to become known colloquially as "[the Bloodbath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1945_VFL_Grand_Final)", due to the player brawl that overshadowed the match, with a total of 9 players being reported by the umpires. Carlton won the match by 28 points, and from then on, South Melbourne struggled for many years.[^cite_note-50]

### Struggling times: 1946–1981

Following the end of the second world war, South Melbourne consistently struggled, as their traditional inner-city recruiting district largely emptied as a result of demographic shifts. The club missed the finals in 1946 and continued to fall such that by 1950 they were second-last on the ladder. They narrowly missed the finals in 1952, but from 1953 to 1969, they never finished any higher than eighth on the ladder. By the 1960s it was clear that South Melbourne's financial resources would not be capable of allowing them to compete in the growing market for country and interstate players, and their own local zone was never strong enough to compensate for this. The introduction of country [zoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoning_\(Australian_rules_football\)) failed to help, as the [Riverina Football League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riverina_Football_League) proved to be one of the least profitable zones.[^cite_note-51][^cite_note-52]



1951 VFL team

Between 1945 and 1981, South Melbourne made the finals only twice: under legendary coach [Norm Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norm_Smith), South Melbourne finished fourth in 1970, but lost the first semi-final; and, in 1977, the club finished fifth under coach Ian Stewart, but lost the elimination final. In that time, they "won" three [wooden spoons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wooden_spoon_\(award\)). Between Round 7, 1972 and Round 13, 1973, the team lost 29 consecutive games. By the end of the 1970s, South Melbourne were saddled with massive debts after struggling for such a long period of time.[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-54]

### A VFL club for Sydney



Chart of yearly ladder positions for Sydney in [VFL/AFL](4732785.md)

See also: [Australian rules football in New South Wales § History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_rules_football_in_New_South_Wales#History)

The first football clubs in [Sydney](27862.md) of any code were established in 1865 with two Australian rules football clubs named the Sydney FC and the Australian FC.[^cite_note-\:2-55] The president of Sydney FC was cricket player and administrator [Richard Driver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Driver), who between 1857 and 1865 had come into contact with cricketer and football pioneer [Tom Wills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Wills) numerous times in Sydney and Melbourne.[^cite_note-\:2-55] The public road today situated outside the western walls of the [SCG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Cricket_Ground) and [Sydney Football Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Football_Stadium_\(2022\)) is named Driver Avenue in his honour.[^cite_note-56] The two clubs in 1865 played their matches on [Hyde Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyde_Park,_Sydney) in the city.[^cite_note-\:2-55] Both clubs were defunct after just two seasons and over the remainder of the 19th century the code had a sporadic existence in Sydney.[^cite_note-\:2-55][^cite_note-57]

The VFL had been actively seeking an audience in Sydney since its first exhibition match in 1903 drew 20,000 people.[^cite_note-58] For more than three quarters of a century, it had strategically scheduled matches in Sydney and through the [Australian National Football Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_National_Football_Council) – had allocated a significant share of its marketing budget to developing the code in Sydney, showcasing interstate tournaments and encouraging its clubs to play against the state representative side. At one point, it even attempted to negotiate a [hybrid code with rugby league](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_football).[^cite_note-59] However interest in the code in Sydney remained the poorest in the country (where it was behind three other football codes). In the late 1970s, however, with increasing professionalism of the sport there was an overall increase in national interest in the VFL competition.

In 1976, Melbourne journalist [Jim Main](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Main) began to break a story that a leading Melbourne businessman who had relocated to Sydney, Mannie Bongornio, had been meeting with [Allen Aylett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allen_Aylett) about luring a VFL club to Sydney. The idea began to gain traction and in 1977, [Ron Barassi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ron_Barassi) proposed the VFL setting up a club in Sydney, which he offered to coach believing that it would help spread the code in the state.[^cite_note-60] Upon becoming league president, Aylett had the league investigate playing Sunday matches at the SCG.[^cite_note-61] The VFL scheduled 2 premiership matches for the [Sydney Cricket Ground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Cricket_Ground) in 1979. One of them, between the [previous year's grand finalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1978_VFL_Grand_Final) North Melbourne and Hawthorn drew a record 31,395 to the gates. In 1979 the financially struggling [Fitzroy Lions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitzroy_Lions) conducted a feasibility into the possibility of moving to North Sydney and a proposal was put forward, but was voted down by its board in 1980.[^cite_note-Jim_Main_2006-62] To test the market further, the VFL scheduled 4 matches for the SCG in 1980 with an average attendance of 19,000. In April 1980, the VFL stated that its market study showed there was sufficient support for a Sydney team, finding that there was an increase in television ratings in Sydney and sustained attendance at matches and that it intended to have a team in Sydney, possibly as soon as 1982.[^cite_note-63] A 1981 report by [Graham Huggins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_Huggins) concluded that there was an "untapped market in Sydney which represented an excellent opportunity for the league."[^cite_note-higgins_report-64] The report claimed that 60,000 people in Sydney had stated that they would regularly support the new club and 90% of these supporters would watch VFL on television from Sydney and 80% of these supporters had not attended rugby and 92% believed that Australian rules could become popular in Sydney.[^cite_note-65]

In 1981 the VFL had decided that it would establish an entirely new 13th VFL club in Sydney along with a possible 14th team.[^cite_note-higgins_report-64] The VFL was under strong pressure from interests in [Adelaide](1148.md) to admit a South Australian club and from the ACT for a new [Canberra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canberra) license, however VFL president [Allan Aylett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allan_Aylett) was convinced that Sydney was the most viable option.[^cite_note-66] By 1981, Canberra's [ACTAFL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACTAFL) had edged out rugby league in popularity with an increase in participation it had become the number one sport.[^cite_note-67] Under significant pressure from a rugby league junior development push and fearing the impact on its strong local competition of entry of a Sydney team made a formal bid for licence to enter a Canberra team into the VFL. With corporate backing[^cite_note-68] and strong public support including local legend [Alex Jesaulenko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alex_Jesaulenko),[^cite_note-69] the Canberra bid was confident it would be a successful expansion club.[^cite_note-70] Aylett however, determined to pursue the entry of a Sydney team, dismissed the Canberra bid publishing a scathing report on the development of football in the ACT, stating that the VFL might consider Canberra for a licence in another 10 years. Aylett's view was that Sydney offered a much bigger television audience and the most potential to add to the league's lucrative television rights. The league had also estimated that the club could initially draw support from an estimated 300,000 ex-Victorians living in and around Sydney.[^cite_note-71]

With the possibility of another club making Sydney a viable move, in 1981, the South Melbourne board, recognising the structural difficulties it faced with long-term viability and financial stability in Melbourne, decided not to miss what it saw as a strategic opportunity to capture an untapped market and save its club. The board made the decision to play all 1982 home games in Sydney. The club had been operating at a loss of at least \$150,000 for the previous five years. News of the proposal broke on 2 July 1981, after which a letter was sent to members justifying the board's reasons for making the proposal and noting that the coach and current players were in favour of the move.[^cite_note-72] On 29 July 1981, the VFL formally accepted the proposal, and paved the way for the Swans to shift to Sydney in 1982.[^cite_note-73]

Inevitably, the move caused very great internal difficulties as a large supporters' group known as Keep South at South campaigned against the move throughout the rest of 1981; and, at an [extraordinary general meeting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extraordinary_general_meeting) on 22 September, the group democratically won control of the club's board. However, the new board did not have the power to unilaterally stop the move to Sydney: under the VFL constitution, to rescind the decision that had been made on 29 July required a three-quarters majority in a vote of all twelve clubs, and at a meeting on 14 October it failed to obtain this majority.[^cite_note-74] The new board, whilst representative of most fans, lacked the support of the players, many of whom were in favour of a long-term move to Sydney; in early November, after the board promised that it would try to bring the club back to Melbourne in 1983, the players went on strike, seeking to force the new board to commit to Sydney in the long term as well as seeking payments that the cash-strapped club owed them from the previous season.[^cite_note-75] The board ended up undermining its own position when it accepted a \$400,000 loan from the VFL in late November in order to stay solvent, under the condition that it commit to Sydney for at least two years. Finally, in early December, the Keep South at South board resigned and a board in favour of the move to Sydney was installed.

### Swans move to Sydney: 1982–1984

See also: [1982 Swans season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1982_Swans_season)

In 1982, the club was still technically a Melbourne-based club which played all of its home games at the [Sydney Cricket Ground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Cricket_Ground).[^cite_note-Officially-76] Its physical "home club" was the "Southern Cross Social Club" at 120a Clovelly Road, Randwick[^cite_note-History-77] In response to the move, the club's sponsors, [Bond Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Bond) pulled out and the club was left without a major backer.[^cite_note-78] At a major launch in Sydney, Aylett vindicated the league's decision, announcing it had signed a new sponsor, Ward Transport, and that on-field success for the Swans would soon follow.[^cite_note-79] The Swans experienced success in the 1982 Escort Championships with 1,000 supporters packing out the Chevron Hotel ballroom in King Cross in response to the win, however Channel Seven did not broadcast the match in Sydney.[^cite_note-80]

The club won their first official Sydney home game against Melbourne in front of 15,764.[^cite_note-81]

On 2 June 1982, it dropped the name "South Melbourne", officially becoming "the Swans" for the rest of the season.[^cite_note-Officially-76][^cite_note-hbg15feb2017-82] The name change, however, did not endear either the Sydney media or the Sydney public, and after successive games at home, began to draw as few as 10,000. Despite just missing the finals, and some good wins at home and respectable home crowds against league leaders Carlton and Richmond, the lack of success and cold reception in Sydney led to the lustre quickly leaving the league's glamour team.[^cite_note-83] It was officially renamed the "Sydney Swans" in 1983.[^cite_note-84]

In 1983 average crowds in Sydney continued to plummet to 12,000 and Swans supporter packages dropped to as low as 100 members (well short of the 20,000 average crowds predicted by the VFL's 1980 Hennessy Report).[^cite_note-Timeline-85] Television ratings and sponsorship revenue in Sydney were also far below the league's expectations.[^cite_note-86][^cite_note-Crisis-87]

Operating at a loss well short of the VFL's predicted \$750,000 a year profit[^cite_note-Timeline-85] poor financial performance continued to drown the club in 1984, with the club flagging pay cuts to its players in order to survive.[^cite_note-88] Coach Ricky Quade resigned and caretaker coach Bob Hammond, despite showing some promise, was unable to turn the club's poor performance around.[^cite_note-89] In order to keep the club solvent during this time, the VFL began to write loans to the Swans that the club would have been unable to pay off on its own.[^cite_note-90] The Swans were the league's most reliant on sponsorship and subsidies from the VFL to stay solvent and meet player payments due to its continued poor crowds, public apathy and poor TV ratings.[^cite_note-Crisis-87]

Public support for the Swans in Sydney was so bad that by the start of the 1985 season, the VFL began to backflip and the league's administrators, having sunk large amounts of money into the club began looking to offload it.[^cite_note-91][^cite_note-92]

### Edelsten era and privatisation: 1985–1987

On 31 July 1985, for what was thought to be [\$](81215.md)6.3 million, [Geoffrey Edelsten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Edelsten) "bought" the Swans; in reality it was \$2.9 million in cash with funding and other payments spread over five years. Edelsten resigned as chairman in less than twelve months, but had already made his mark. He immediately recruited former Geelong coach [Tom Hafey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Hafey). Hafey, in turn, used his knowledge of Geelong's contracts to recruit [David Bolton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bolton_\(Australian_footballer\)), [Bernard Toohey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Toohey) and [Greg Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greg_Williams_\(Australian_footballer\)), who would all form a key part of the Sydney side, at a league-determined total fee of \$240,000 (less than the \$500,000 Geelong demanded and even the \$300,000 Sydney offered).[^cite_note-93] The likes of [Gerard Healy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerard_Healy), [Merv Neagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merv_Neagle) and [Paul Morwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Morwood) were also poached from other clubs, and failed approaches were made to [Simon Madden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Madden), [Terry Daniher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terry_Daniher), [Andrew Bews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Bews) and [Maurice Rioli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Rioli).[^cite_note-94][^cite_note-95]

During the Edelsten years, the Swans were seen by the Sydney public as a flamboyant, flashy club, typified by the style of its spearhead, [Warwick Capper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warwick_Capper), his long bright blond [mullet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mullet_\(hairstyle\)) and bright pink boots made him unmissable on the field and his pink Lamborghini, penchant for girlfriends who were fashion models and his general showy eccentricity made him notorious off the field – all somewhat [fashionable in the 1980s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980s_in_fashion). During Capper's peak years, the Swans had made successive finals appearances for the first time since relocating. His consistently spectacular aerial exploits earned him the [Mark of the Year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_of_the_Year) award in 1987 while his goalkicking efforts (amassing 103 goals in 1987) made him runner up in the Coleman Medal two years running. The Swans' successive finals appearances saw crowds during this time peak at an average of around 25,000 per game. Edelsten also introduced the "Swanettes", becoming the sole such American-style cheerleading group among VFL teams following the disbandment of Carlton's Blue Birds in 1986. The Swanettes did not get much performance time, owing to the short intervals between quarters of play in the VFL and the lack of space in which they might perform while other activities take place on the field. The Swanettes were rapidly discontinued.[^cite_note-96] During the Edelsten era, the club's owner and the private company Westec are reported to have sunk more than \$10 million in additional private capital to keep the club afloat.[^cite_note-97]

When the Southern Cross club went bankrupt in 1987, the club relocated to the newly built [Sydney Football Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Football_Stadium_\(1988\)).[^cite_note-History-77]

In 1987, the Swans scored 201 points against the [West Coast Eagles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Coast_Eagles) and the following week scored 236 points against the [Essendon Football Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essendon_Football_Club). Both games were at the SCG. The Swans remain one of only two clubs to have scored consecutive team tallies above 200 points, the only other being Geelong in 1992.[^cite_note-98] However, this was followed by several heavy losses, including defeat by Hawthorn by 99 points in the Qualifying Final and by 76 points against Melbourne in the First Semi-final.[^cite_note-99]

### Dark times: 1988–1994

The club's form was to slump in the following year. Losses were in the millions. It was obvious to most that the Swans were struggling financially, though the owners, Sydney Rules Pty Ltd a subsidiary of Powerplay International Ltd were not selling.[^cite_note-100] In early 1988 the company advised the [Australian Securities Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Securities_Exchange) to cease trading its shares as it could not continue to trade until it had offloaded the Swans.[^cite_note-101] A Canberra consortium including the [ACTAFL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACTAFL) initially proposed to buy the failed club and shift it to Canberra, however the VFL claimed this was too extreme a move. The league compromised and along with Aylett, who had denied Canberra a licence in 1981, proposed that the Swans play away games in the ACT[^cite_note-102] with a dual aim of giving the club a sustainable supporter base and helping resurrect the code in the ACT which had lost enormous ground to rugby league since the introduction of the Canberra Raiders. However the VFL blocked the move feeling that the club would lose its identity if it were to play matches in Canberra.[^cite_note-103][^cite_note-104]

By mid year the VFL had revoked the Swans licence and took over ownership of the club,[^cite_note-105] after an investigation under VFL CEO [Ross Oakley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ross_Oakley) determined that it was unable to continue operating.[^cite_note-New_era-106] However, there were no buyers. On 6 May 1988 the VFL paid Powerplay just \$10 to transfer ownership of the club in an attempt to keep it afloat until a buyer could be found.[^cite_note-107][^cite_note-108] The VFL would wait months for the club to regain financial security. The VFL had reported that it needed to find a buyer willing to pay at least \$4 million in order to make the club financially viable in the medium to long term. In the meantime, the league had secured a sponsor which helped underwrite the club until the sale.[^cite_note-109][^cite_note-110]

At the end of 1988 the VFL re-tendered the Swans licence and a group of financial backers including [Mike Willesee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mike_Willesee), Basil Sellers, Peter Weinert and Craig Kimberley, purchased the licence and bankrolled the club.[^cite_note-111][^cite_note-112]

Morale at the side plummeted as players were asked to take pay cuts.[^cite_note-113] Coach [Tom Hafey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Hafey) was sacked by the club in 1988 after a player-led rebellion at his tough training methods (unusual in the semi-professional days of that era).[^cite_note-114] The Willesee consortia appointed Col Kinnear as the new coach.[^cite_note-New_era-106]

Capper was sold to the Brisbane Bears for AUD\$400,000 in a desperate attempt to improve the club's finances. Instead, it only led to disastrous on-field performances. Instead of a 100-goal-a-season forward, Sydney's goalkicking was led by [Bernard Toohey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Toohey) (usually a defender) with 29 in 1989, then Jim West with 34 in 1990. Players left the club in droves, including Brownlow Medalist [Greg Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greg_Williams_\(Australian_footballer\)), [Bernard Toohey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Toohey) and [Barry Mitchell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Mitchell_\(footballer\)). The careers of stars such as [Dennis Carroll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dennis_Carroll), [David Bolton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bolton_\(Australian_footballer\)), [Ian Roberts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_Roberts_\(Australian_footballer\)), [Tony Morwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Morwood) and [David Murphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Murphy_\(Australian_rules_footballer\)) wound down,[^cite_note-115] while promising young players like [Jamie Lawson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamie_Lawson_\(Australian_footballer\)), [Robert Teal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Teal) and [Paul Bryce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Bryce) had their careers cut short by injury.[^cite_note-116][^cite_note-117]

Attendances consistently dropped below 10,000 when the team performed poorly between 1990 and 1994. Kinnear in 1990 urged his players rally to support the club's loyal fans and stem their mass exodus[^cite_note-118] however he was ultimately stood down in August 1991.[^cite_note-119] The nadir came with three consecutive wooden spoons in 1992, 1993 and 1994.[^cite_note-120]

In October 1992, members from the 15 AFL clubs voted on axing the struggling Swans.[^cite_note-Swans_deathbed-121] To fill the void left by the Swans, the league floated a radical proposal for Carlton or Collingwood to play all of their away games in Sydney, however it was felt that even the leagues most popular clubs wouldn't be able to draw a sufficient audience in Sydney to cover for the loss of the Swans.[^cite_note-Swans_deathbed-121] The AFL extended an offer for a Tasmanian licence which was declined,[^cite_note-Swans_deathbed-121] and received an offer from the ACTAFL to relocate the club to Canberra, however the AFL rejected this.[^cite_note-Swans_deathbed-121] There were also strong rumours that the AFL intended to merge the club with the [Brisbane Bears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brisbane_Bears) to form a combined New South Wales/Queensland team, fold altogether, or even move back to South Melbourne.[^cite_note-122] Without adequate alternatives the AFL Commission instead decided to step in and save the club, offering substantial monetary and management support, with the 15 clubs asked to cover the club's AUD\$1.2 million annual expenses including licence fee and hire of the SCG.[^cite_note-Swans_deathbed-121] With draft and salary cap concessions in the early 1990s and a series of notable recruits, the team became competitive after the early part of the decade.[^cite_note-123]



Statue of Paul Kelly at the SCG. Kelly, a New South Welshman, known as "captain courageous" he was one of Sydney's longest serving skippers captaining the side between 1993 and 2002.

During this time, the side was largely held together by two inspirational skippers, both from the [Wagga Wagga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wagga_Wagga) region of country New South Wales, [Dennis Carroll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dennis_Carroll) and later the courageous captain [Paul Kelly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Kelly_\(Australian_rules_footballer\)). However coach Gary Buckenara was sacked after 18 straight losses, managing to register just 2 wins and a draw during his term.[^cite_note-124]

Desperate to hang on, the club was keen to enlist the biggest names and identities in the AFL, and recruited [Ron Barassi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ron_Barassi) as coach[^cite_note-125] who helped save the club from extinction while serving them as coach from Round 7, 1993 to 1995. At roughly the same time, [Dermott Brereton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dermott_Brereton) was also recruited from Hawthorn on a three-year contract becoming one of the swans highest paid players,[^cite_note-126] and was considered to replace Paul Kelly as club captain.[^cite_note-127] However, Brereton's time in Sydney was marred by multiple tribunal appearances, including 6- and 7-week bans,[^cite_note-128][^cite_note-129] and, while he starred in some upset wins,[^cite_note-130][^cite_note-131] he spent more time off the field than on it, resulting in Barassi labelling the experiment as a disappointment, and he was not offered a contract extension.[^cite_note-132]

### Tony Lockett and grand final return: 1995–2001

A big coup for the club was recruitment of [St Kilda Football Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Kilda_Football_Club) champion [Tony "Plugger" Lockett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Lockett) in 1995. Lockett became a cult figure in Sydney, with an instant impact and along with the [Super League war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_League_war) in the dominant rival [rugby league](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugby_league) football code in Australia, helped the Swans to become a powerhouse Sydney icon.[^cite_note-133]

1995 would be Barassi's last year in charge. The Swans won eight games – as many as they did in the previous three seasons combined – and finished with a percentage of over 100. They were also one of only two teams to defeat the all-conquering Carlton side of that year. Captain Paul Kelly won the League's highest individual honour, the [Brownlow Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brownlow_Medal).[^cite_note-134] Barassi left an improving team, a club in a much better state than he found them.[^cite_note-135]

Former Hawthorn player Rodney Eade took over the reins in 1996 and after a slow start (they lost their first two games of the season), turned the club around into powerful force. The Swans ended the minor round on top of the premiership table with 16 wins, 5 losses and 1 draw. In the finals, the Swans won one of the most thrilling AFL preliminary finals in history after Plugger Lockett kicked a behind after the siren to win the game. The Swans lost the grand final to [North Melbourne](22106.md), which had been their first appearance in a grand final since 1945. The game was played in front of 93,102 at the [MCG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MCG).[^cite_note-136]

The Swans then made the finals for four of the next five full years that Rodney Eade was in charge. In 1998 they finished 3rd on the AFL ladder; despite beating [St Kilda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Kilda_Football_Club) in their first final the Swans were then beaten by eventual premiers [Adelaide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adelaide_Football_Club) in the semi-final at the [SCG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Cricket_Ground).

The 1999 season was a largely uneventful year for the club, the only real highlight being [Tony Lockett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Lockett) kicking his record-breaking 1300th goal against [Collingwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collingwood_Football_Club) in Round 10. The 1999 season ended with a 69-point mauling at the hands of minor premiers [Essendon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essendon_Football_Club).[^cite_note-137]

After missing the finals in [2000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2000_AFL_season), the Swans rebounded to finish 7th in [2001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001_AFL_season), but were beaten by [Hawthorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawthorn_Football_Club) by 55 points in their elimination final at [Colonial Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonial_Stadium).[^cite_note-138]

### Rebuilding and finals return: 2002–2004

Former Swans favourite son [Paul Roos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Roos_\(footballer\)) was appointed caretaker coach midway through the 2002 season, replacing Rodney Eade who was removed after Round 12. Roos won six of the remaining 10 games that year (including the last four of the season) and was installed as the permanent coach from the 2003 season onwards, despite rumours that Sydney had nearly concluded a deal with [Terry Wallace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terry_Wallace).[^cite_note-139]

Roos continued a record as a successful coach with the Swans for the eight full seasons that would follow.

A new home ground at [Stadium Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stadium_Australia) provided increased capacity over the SCG. The Swans' first game played at the stadium in Round 9, 2002 against [Essendon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essendon_Football_Club) attracted 54,169 spectators. The Sydney Swans v [Collingwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collingwood_Football_Club) match on 23 August 2003 set an attendance record for the largest crowd to watch an AFL game outside of Victoria with an official attendance of 72,393 and was the largest home and away AFL crowd at any stadium for 2003. A preliminary final against the [Brisbane Lions](5026.md) in 2003 attracted 71,019 people. The Swans lost all three of those significant matches.[^cite_note-140]

2004 saw an average year for Sydney, however one highlight was when they ended [St Kilda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Kilda_Football_Club)'s undefeated start to the season in Round 11. The match was notable for [Leo Barry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Barry)'s effort in nullifying the impact of St Kilda full-forward and eventual [Coleman Medallist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coleman_Medal) [Fraser Gehrig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraser_Gehrig), whom Barry restricted to only two possessions for the entire match.[^cite_note-141]

Sydney was able to recruit another St Kilda export in the Lockett mould, [Barry Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Hall). There were obvious parallels to the signing of Lockett (a powerful, tough forward from St Kilda with questions over his discipline and attitude), which left Hall with much to live up to. He flourished in his new surroundings and eventually became a cult figure and club leader in his own right.[^cite_note-142]

As the new century dawned, Sydney implemented a policy of giving up high order draft picks in exchange for players who struggled at other clubs. It was during this era that the Swans picked up the likes of [Paul Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Williams_\(Australian_rules_footballer\)), [Barry Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Hall), [Craig Bolton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Craig_Bolton), [Darren Jolly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darren_Jolly), [Ted Richards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ted_Richards), [Peter Everitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Everitt), [Martin Mattner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Mattner), [Rhyce Shaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhyce_Shaw), [Shane Mumford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shane_Mumford), [Ben McGlynn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_McGlynn) and [Mitch Morton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitch_Morton), amongst others, and giving up higher order draft picks meant the Swans missed out on the likes of [Daniel Motlop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Motlop), [Nick Dal Santo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_Dal_Santo), [James Kelly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Kelly_\(Australian_footballer\)), [Courtenay Dempsey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courtenay_Dempsey) and [Sam Lonergan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Lonergan) who went to [Port Adelaide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Adelaide_Football_Club), [St Kilda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Kilda_Football_Club), [Geelong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geelong_Football_Club) and the latter two to [Essendon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essendon_Football_Club) respectively. This policy is said to have paid off in the Roos era, as they implemented a strict culture of discipline at the club.

### Premiership glory: 2005

Main article: [2005 AFL Grand Final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_AFL_Grand_Final)

In 2005, the Swans came under enormous public scrutiny, even from AFL CEO [Andrew Demetriou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Demetriou), for their unorthodox, "boring" defense-oriented tactics that included tightly controlling the tempo of the game and starving the opposition of possession (in fact, seven teams that season had their lowest possession total while playing against the Swans). Swans coach [Paul Roos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Roos_\(Australian_rules_footballer\)) maintained that playing contested football was the style used by all recent Premiership-winning teams, and felt that it was ironic that the much criticised strategy proved ultimately successful.[^cite_note-143][^cite_note-144]

— [Stephen Quartermain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Quartermain)'s TV call of Leo Barry's match- and title-clinching mark on Network Ten

[^cite_note-146]

After finishing third during the regular season, the Swans lost the second qualifying final against the [West Coast Eagles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Coast_Eagles) at [Subiaco Oval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subiaco_Oval) on 2 September by 10.5 (65) to 10.9 (69). This dropped them into a semi-final against the [Geelong Cats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geelong_Football_Club) at the SCG on 9 September, and the Swans trailed the Cats 31–53 before [Nick Davis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_Davis_\(footballer\)) kicked four consecutive goals, with the last one a matter of seconds before the siren, to win the game for Sydney by 7.14 (56) to 7.11 (53). In the first preliminary final at the MCG on 16 September against [St Kilda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Kilda_Football_Club), the Swans used a seven-goal blitz in 11 minutes of the fourth quarter to overturn an 8-point deficit and overrun the Saints by 15.6 (96) to 9.11 (65).[^cite_note-147][^cite_note-148]

The Swans faced the Eagles in the [AFL Grand Final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AFL_Grand_Final) on 24 September 2005, their first Grand Final appearance since 1996. The Swans prevailed by four points, final score 8.10 (58) to West Coast's 7.12 (54). In the last few minutes, the Sydney defence held strong, with [Leo Barry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Barry) marking the ball just before the siren to stop the Eagles' final desperate shot at goal. The Premiership was the Swans' first in 72 years and their first since being based in Sydney.[^cite_note-149][^cite_note-150]

Off the field the Grand Final success instigated moves to make the club sustainable in the long term and capitalise on the success to grow the code in the state. The Greater Sydney Australian Football Foundation Limited was formed, which would later become the Sydney Swans Foundation aimed initially at raising \$5 million in funds to develop the Swans and the code in New South Wales.[^cite_note-153] The Foundation has raised millions since its inception and helped keep the Swans sustainable in Sydney.[^cite_note-154][^cite_note-155]

### Grand final loss: 2006

Main article: [2006 AFL Grand Final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_AFL_Grand_Final)

As reigning premiers, the Sydney Swans started the 2006 season slowly, losing three of their first four games, including in round one to an [Essendon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essendon_Football_Club) side that would finish near the bottom of the ladder with only three wins and a draw, and finish with the worst defensive record of any side for the season (Sydney, conversely, had the best defensive record of any side).[^cite_note-BombersvSwans2006-156][^cite_note-157]

The 2006 AFL Grand Final was contested between the Sydney Swans and West Coast Eagles at the Melbourne Cricket Ground on 30 September 2006. The West Coast Eagles avenged their 2005 Grand Final defeat by beating the Sydney Swans by one point, only the fourth one-point grand final margin in the competition's history.[^cite_note-158]

[The rivalry between the Sydney Swans and West Coast Eagles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney%E2%80%93West_Coast_AFL_rivalry) has become one of the modern rivalries.[^cite_note-159] The six games between the two sides (from the start of the 2005 finals to the first round of 2007 inclusive) were decided by a combined margin of 13 points. Four of those six games were finals and 2 grand finals.[^cite_note-160]

### Finals goal: 2007–2010

Sydney finished the 2007 home and away season in 7th place, and advanced to the finals, where they faced and were defeated by [Collingwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collingwood_Football_Club) by 38 points in the elimination final. It was their earliest exit from the finals since 2001 and was a culmination of a mostly disappointing season, as only victories against lesser teams saw them through to a fifth consecutive finals campaign.[^cite_note-161]

The conclusion of the 2007 trade saw the loss of [Adam Schneider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Schneider) and [Sean Dempster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sean_Dempster) to [St Kilda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Kilda_Football_Club), the delisting of [Simon Phillips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Phillips_\(footballer\)), Jonathan Simpkin and [Luke Vogels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luke_Vogels), and the gain of [Henry Playfair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Playfair) from [Geelong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geelong_Football_Club) and [Martin Mattner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Mattner) from [Adelaide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adelaide_Football_Club).[^cite_note-162]

The Swans spent the middle part of the 2008 season inside the top four, however a late form slump which yielded only three wins in the last nine rounds saw the Swans drop to sixth at the conclusion of the 2008 regular season. Having qualified for the finals for a sixth consecutive season, the Swans defeated [North Melbourne](22106.md) in the elimination final before losing to the [Western Bulldogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Bulldogs) the following week.[^cite_note-163]

2009 saw the club register only eight victories as they failed to reach the finals for the first time since 2002, finishing 12th with a percentage of below 100% for the first time since 1994. [Barry Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Hall), [Leo Barry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Barry), [Jared Crouch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jared_Crouch), [Michael O'Loughlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_O'Loughlin), [Amon Buchanan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amon_Buchanan) and [Darren Jolly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darren_Jolly) all departed at the conclusion of the season, with [Mark Seaby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Seaby), [Daniel Bradshaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Bradshaw) and [Shane Mumford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shane_Mumford), among others, joining the club during the trade period.[^cite_note-164]

The 2010 season saw Sydney return to the finals by virtue of a fifth-place finish at the end of the regular season. The club defeated [Carlton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlton_Football_Club) by five points in the elimination final before losing to the [Western Bulldogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Bulldogs) in the semi-finals for the second time in three seasons. The loss signalled the end of the Swans coaching career of [Paul Roos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Roos_\(Australian_rules_footballer\)) as well as that of the playing career of [Brett Kirk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brett_Kirk).[^cite_note-165]

Former [North Melbourne](22106.md) premiership-winning forward [John Longmire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Longmire) took over as coach of the Swans as part of a succession plan initiated by Paul Roos in 2009 prior to the beginning of the 2011 season.[^cite_note-166] He led the club to a seventh-place finish at the end of the regular season, therefore qualifying for the finals for the 13th time in the past 16 seasons. The Swans defeated [St Kilda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Kilda_Football_Club) in an elimination final at [Docklands Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Docklands_Stadium) before losing to [Hawthorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawthorn_Football_Club) in the semi-finals the following week.[^cite_note-167]

It was during the regular season that the Swans caused the upset of the season, defeating the star-studded [Geelong Cats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geelong_Football_Club) on its home ground, [Skilled Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kardinia_Park_\(stadium\)), where the home tenant had won its past 29 games in succession, and its past two matches at the ground by a combined margin of 336 points, in Round 23. It was the Swans' first win over the Cats since 2006 and its first win at the ground since Round 8, 1999. The Swans were also the only team to defeat the [West Coast Eagles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Coast_Eagles) at [Patersons Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subiaco_Oval) during the regular season. The Swans' victory over Geelong was overshadowed by the news that co-captain [Jarrad McVeigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jarrad_McVeigh)'s baby daughter had died in the week leading up to the match, forcing him to miss that match.[^cite_note-168]

#### 2012: Premiership year, Sydney becomes a two team town



Sydney Swans players run through the banner before the inaugural Sydney Derby on 24 March 2012.



Coach John Longmire, a New South Welshman, led Sydney to a premiership in 2012

The 2012 season began for the Swans with the inaugural Sydney Derby against AFL newcomers [Greater Western Sydney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Western_Sydney_Giants). After an even and physical first half, Sydney went on to win by 63 points. Subsequent wins over [Fremantle](312661.md), [Port Adelaide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Adelaide_Football_Club), [North Melbourne](22106.md) and [Hawthorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawthorn_Football_Club) saw the Swans sit second behind [West Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Coast_Eagles) on percentage after Round 5, but the Swans would proceed to lose three of their next four matches before embarking on a nine-match winning streak between Rounds 10 and 19 inclusive. The Swans eventually finished the regular season in third place after losing three of their final four matches, all against their fellow top-four rivals (Collingwood, Hawthorn and Geelong in Rounds 20, 22 and 23 respectively).[^cite_note-169]

The Swans defeated [Adelaide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adelaide_Football_Club) by 29 points in their qualifying final at [AAMI Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AAMI_Stadium), thus earning a week off and a home preliminary final, where they then defeated [Collingwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collingwood_Football_Club) by 26 points to qualify for their first grand final since 2006, ending an eleven-match losing streak against the Magpies in the process.



Adam Goodes served as captain between 2009 and 2012 leading the side to a premiership in 2012

In the grand final, the Swans defeated Hawthorn [by ten points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_AFL_Grand_Final) in front of 99,683 people at the MCG, with [Nick Malceski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_Malceski) kicking a snap goal with 34 seconds left to seal the Swans' fifth premiership and first since 2005. Ryan O'Keefe was named the Norm Smith Medallist and the Swans' best player in September.[^cite_note-170]



The Sydney Swans warm up before a match in 2013.

The Swans' [2013 season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2013_AFL_season) was marred by long-term injuries to many of its key players, namely [Adam Goodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Goodes), Sam Reid, [Lewis Jetta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Jetta), [Rhyce Shaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhyce_Shaw) and [Lewis Roberts-Thomson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Roberts-Thomson), among others; despite this setback, the team were still able to reach the finals for the fifteenth time in 18 seasons, reaching the preliminary finals where they were defeated by [Fremantle](312661.md) at [Patersons Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subiaco_Oval), its first loss at the venue since 2009.[^cite_note-171][^cite_note-172]

#### 2014–2016: Grand final losses



Kieren Jack, born and bred Sydneysider, served as captain between 2013 and 2016



Josh Kennedy served as captain between 2017 and 2019



Lance "Buddy" Franklin marking in 2017. Franklin spearheaded the club at full forward between 2014 and 2023, becoming the third highest goalscorer in the club's history.

The 2014 AFL season began with some difficulties for the Swans. Sydney lost their first game against [Greater Western Sydney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Western_Sydney_Giants) and then to Collingwood before becoming the first non-South Australian team to win at [Adelaide Oval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adelaide_Oval) defeating Adelaide by 63 points with Lance Franklin and Luke Parker kicking 4 goals each. After a loss to North Melbourne in Round 4, the Swans' won twelve games in a row, including victories against 2013 grand finalists Fremantle and Hawthorn, Geelong by 110 points at the SCG and then ladder leaders Port Adelaide. In Round 17, the Swans defeated Carlton to match a winning streak set three times in club history, the last of which came way back in 1935,[^cite_note-173] and eventually closed out the season with their first minor premiership in 18 years and a club record 17 wins for the season, eclipsing the previous highest of 16, which was achieved on six past occasions in 2012, 1996, 1986, 1945, 1936 and 1935. In 2014 the Swans were minor premiers, and also qualified for the [2014 AFL Grand Final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_AFL_Grand_Final). They defeated [Fremantle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fremantle_Dockers) at home in the first qualifying final in Round one of the finals series and so earned a one-week break. In the first preliminary final the Swans had a convincing win against [North Melbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Melbourne_Kangaroos), which led them to their fourth grand final in 10 years. The [2014 AFL Grand Final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_AFL_Grand_Final) was played on Saturday, 27 September 2014, in near perfect weather conditions, with Sydney seen as favourites leading up to the match. This was the first time in a finals series that former Hawk player [Lance Franklin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lance_Franklin) would play against his former team, one of very few players to have played back to back grand finals for two different teams. The Hawks dominated the game quite early and eventually defeated the Swans 11.8.(74) to 21.11.(137). The 63-point loss was Sydney's biggest ever loss in a grand final and their biggest defeat all season, meaning Hawthorn would become back to back premiers for the second time in their history.[^cite_note-174]

The Swans started the 2015 AFL season well, winning their first three, before losing their next two games against [Fremantle](312661.md), where they trailed by as many as 8 goals before half-time, and the [Western Bulldogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Bulldogs). They won their next 6 leading into the bye, including home wins against [Geelong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geelong_Football_Club) and [North Melbourne](22106.md), and an upset away win against [Hawthorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawthorn_Football_Club) in the grand final replay. The Swans lost their first game after the bye, their 3rd of the season to [Richmond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richmond_Football_Club) at the SCG, 11.11 (77) to 14.11. (95). The Swans rebounded with unconvincing wins against [Port Adelaide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Adelaide_Football_Club) and [Brisbane Lions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brisbane_Lions_Football_Club), before suffering their heaviest defeat for 17 seasons against the [Hawks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawthorn_Football_Club) by 89 points.[^cite_note-175] The following week was no better with a road trip to Perth and another loss, this time to the [Eagles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Coast_Eagles) by 52 points, the scoreline ultimately flattering the Swans. The Swans bounced back against Adelaide with a convincing win 52-point win, but lost their next game to [Geelong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geelong_Football_Club) at [Simmonds stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kardinia_Park_\(stadium\)); a close affair that Geelong blew apart in the 3rd quarter. The Swans won their final 4 games to secure a top 4 finish, against [Collingwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collingwood_Football_Club), [Greater Western Sydney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Western_Sydney_Giants), [St Kilda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Kilda_Football_Club) and [Gold Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_Coast_Suns).[^cite_note-176]

The Swans faced minor premiers [Fremantle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fremantle_Dockers) in the first qualifying final, their first finals match without Franklin, who had withdrawn from the finals due to illness.[^cite_note-177] Ultimately the Swans would go down in a low-scoring affair, effectively kicking themselves out of the game after losing [Sam Reid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Reid_\(Australian_footballer_born_1991\)) to a hamstring injury midway through the 2nd quarter.[^cite_note-178] The following week the Swans were knocked out of the finals in a one-sided contest against [North Melbourne](22106.md), struggling to score throughout the first half with the game effectively over by half-time. For the first time since 2011, the Swans failed to make a preliminary final.

The Swans' continued period of success, in which it has missed the finals only three times since 1995, has led to some criticism about a [salary cap](364837.md) concession which the club receives; the concession is in the form of an additional Cost of Living Allowance (COLA), due to the higher cost of living in Sydney compared with any other Australian city.[^cite_note-179][^cite_note-180] It was, however, announced in March 2014 that this allowance would be scrapped.[^cite_note-181] The trade ban was fought by the club before the 2015 season and a reprieve was won, with the AFL allowing the club to participate in the 2015 AFL draft. There was a catch however, with the league imposing an edict that the club could only recruit players at or below current average wage of \$340,000 (adjusted figures for 2015 was \$349,000).[^cite_note-182] During the 2015 season, with the Swans team stretched by ageing players and injuries, it had become apparent that the trade restrictions that had prevented the Swans from participating in the 2014 draft, had impacted the list. With the trade period looming, Andrew Pridham lobbied the AFL to lift the trade restrictions, labeling the ban as a restraint of trade.[^cite_note-183] In response to continued discussions between the club and league, as well as lobbying by the AFLPA,[^cite_note-184] the league further relaxed the trade restrictions for the Swans during the 2015 AFL Finals. The AFL changed the sanctions so that the Swans could replace a player that leaves the club as either a free agent, or through trade, with another player on a contract up to \$450,000 per year. This allowed the Swans to trade for [Callum Sinclair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Callum_Sinclair) in a swap deal,[^cite_note-185] as well as trade a late pick for out-of-contract defender, [Michael Talia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Talia) from the Western Bulldogs.[^cite_note-186][^cite_note-187]

The Swans started off the 2016 season with a convincing 80-point round 1 win against [Collingwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collingwood_Football_Club), with new Swans recruit [Michael Talia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Talia) suffering a long-term foot injury. They followed up the next round with a 60-point win against the Blues, with new recruit [Callum Sinclair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Callum_Sinclair) kicking 3 goals. The following week they defeated GWS by 25 points, with [Lance Franklin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lance_Franklin) kicking 4 goals. In the following match against the Crows, [Isaac Heeney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Heeney) starred with 18 touches and 4 goals in a losing side. Three more wins followed, against West Coast, Brisbane and Essendon respectively before a shock loss to Richmond in round 8 by a solitary point, after a kick after the siren. They bounced back to win against top spot North Melbourne, and the Hawks at the MCG, with Lance Franklin booting 3 goals, including a bomb from 80 metres. After a tight slog against the Suns, the Swans played the Giants once more and were defeated in the club's 100th game. They won their next game by 55 points against the Demons, in a fourth quarter breeze. After a bye in Round 14, the Sydney Swans lost their first game after, again with the last kick of the game, by 4 points. The week after was soured by a family feud involving co-captain [Kieren Jack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kieren_Jack) and his parents, after they were reportedly told by him not to come to is 200-game milestone. After the spat, Jack led the Swans to an emphatic upset victory against Geelong, booting 3 goals and gathering 24 possessions in the one-sided 38-point victory at Simonds Stadium. They then travelled back home where they faced Hawthorn and lost their 3rd match of the season by under a goal, as Buddy went goalless for the first time in the season. After an unconvincing win the following week against Carlton, the Swans went on to win their last 5 home and away games by a combined total of 349 points, giving them top spot and a home qualifying final.[^cite_note-188]

Ahead of their first final against cross-town rivals the Giants, the Swans confirmed that they would play all home finals at the SCG except for Sydney Derbies, which would be played at Stadium Australia. The final would create history, being the first Sydney Derby to be played in a final. It was also the first time that the Giants would make the finals in their fifth year. In a low-scoring first half, the Swans were very competitive, trailing by only 2 points. However, a mark not paid to Isaac Heeney midway through the third quarter turned all the momentum the Giants way, as they kicked away to win by 36 points. The Swans only kicked 2 goals after half-time with Giant [Jeremy Cameron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Cameron) outscoring them in the third quarter alone with 3 goals. They were quick to bounce back the following week, thumping the [Adelaide Crows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adelaide_Crows) by 6 goals, with Franklin and [Tom Papley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Papley) kicking 4 goals a piece, after a blistering 7 goal to 1 quarter. The story was pretty much the same in the preliminary final against the [Geelong Cats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geelong_Cats) at the MCG. The Swans kept the Cats goalless for the first quarter, and were never really challenged in their 37-point triumph. It would take them to their third grand final in five years, against the Western Bulldogs at the MCG. After leading by a scant 2-point margin at half time, the Bulldogs pulled away towards the end of the fourth quarter to hand Sydney their second grand final loss in three years.[^cite_note-189]

The Swans began the 2017 season with six straight losses, after being upset at home by Port Adelaide in the opening round, they were upset by Collingwood and Carlton, and suffered defeats to the Western Bulldogs, Greater Western Sydney (who won their first game at the SCG) and West Coast Eagles (in Perth). However, they managed to win 13 of their last 15, losing both their games to [Hawthorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawthorn_Football_Club) by 1 goal. Some of their best wins include against the reigning premiers the Bulldogs, GWS, and comeback wins against Richmond and Essendon. After becoming the first grand-finalist to lose their first six games, they have become the first team to reach the finals after starting the season 0–6. They would comprehensively defeat Essendon in their first final, before slumping to an ugly defeat against Geelong, ending their season.[^cite_note-190]

The Swans had an indifferent 2018, compounded by their struggles at home, losing 5 out of 11 games at the SCG. A lean patch of form which included upset losses to Gold Coast (for the first time ever) and Essendon (for the first time since 2011) had them looking likely to miss finals altogether; however, three out of four wins in the last four rounds was enough to see them into their ninth consecutive finals series, where they were comprehensively beaten by GWS in the elimination finals.[^cite_note-191]

The Swans' golden era of finals appearances came to an end in 2019. They missed the finals for the first time in a decade, finishing 15th on the ladder with 8 wins and 14 losses. They started the season poorly with just one win in their first seven matches, although they would briefly recover after winning five of the next seven games. Six losses on the trot ended any chance of a tenth consecutive finals appearance, but strong wins over also-rans Melbourne and St Kilda in the final two rounds ensured the season ended on a positive note.[^cite_note-192]

#### 2016–present: Pride Games

In 2015, a pre-season Pride Game was played at [Drummoyne Oval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drummoyne_Oval) between the Swans and the [Fremantle Dockers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fremantle_Dockers). From the following year, it became an annual fixture marking the club's support for the [LGBTQIA+](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGBTQIA+) community.[^cite_note-is-193][^cite_note-194] The annual Pride Games are currently only played between the Swans and St Kilda. As of November 2023 the AFL had [no plans to introduce a Pride Round](4732785.md#LGBTIQ_policy), although the [AFLW Pride Round](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AFLW_Pride_Round) was established in 2018.[^cite_note-195][^cite_note-196]

#### 2020s: Return to finals



Dane Rampe, Sydney born and raised, has been a club captain between 2019 and 2023



Callum Mills, Sydney born and raised was named sole captain in 2024

The Swans won their first match of the interrupted 2020 season against Adelaide at the Adelaide Oval by three points, but won only four more games for the season, missing the finals in consecutive seasons for the first time since 1994–95. The 16th-place finish was also the lowest in club history.[^cite_note-197]

After two seasons out of the finals, the Swans rebounded to great effect in 2021. An excellent first month of the season, which saw them upset Brisbane and reigning premiers Richmond, set up a strong home-and-away campaign. Seven wins in their final eight games saw Sydney secure sixth position with a 15–7 win-loss record, just percentage outside the top four, but their year came to a premature end in the finals after a heartbreaking one-point loss to crosstown rival Greater Western Sydney in the second elimination final. The players felt as though they had more to give, and were overcome with disappointment knowing that they had no finals win to show for it, in a season which had otherwise been a massive step in the right direction.[^cite_note-198]

In 2022, the Swans backed up their meteoric rise up the ladder with another promising start, winning five of their first six matches. This included a 30-point win against Geelong in Round 2, which involved Lance Franklin kicking his 1000th career goal, sparking one of the biggest pitch invasions in AFL history. After a mid-year slump, in which the Swans lost winnable games against Gold Coast, Port Adelaide and Essendon, they finished the home-and-away season with seven wins in a row, securing a top-4 berth for the first time since 2016.

In the 2022 finals series, the Swans beat Melbourne by 22 points at the Melbourne Cricket Ground to lock in a home preliminary final. The Swans played Collingwood in the preliminary final and won by a single point, qualifying to play in their fourth grand final in 12 years under Longmire.[^cite_note-199]

On 24 September, the Swans were defeated by Geelong by 133 points to 52 in the [2022 AFL Grand Final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_AFL_Grand_Final).

In 2023 the Swans started well winning their opening two matches and on top of the ladder.[^cite_note-200] A mid season slump saw them drop to as low as 15th[^cite_note-201] but they turned it around winning six in a row to qualify for the finals.[^cite_note-202]

In 2024, the Swans finished the season on top of the ladder with a record of 17–6. This gave them the minor premiership, their first since 2016.[^cite_note-203] They defeated GWS in the Qualifying Final and Port Adelaide in the Preliminary Final to qualify for their 7th Grand Final of the 21st century and their 5th under John Longmire. However they were defeated by the Brisbane Lions in the 2024 Grand Final 120 to 60.[^cite_note-204]

On 26 November 2024, a few months after his fourth grand final loss, John Longmire decided to announce his resignation as senior coach of Sydney Swans and hand the senior coach role to assistant coach [Dean Cox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dean_Cox).[^cite_note-205]

### Dean Cox era: 2025–present

Former [West Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Coast_Eagles), six-time [All-Australian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-Australian_team), [premiership-winning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_AFL_Grand_Final) ruckman [Dean Cox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dean_Cox) was appointed the 45th head coach of the Sydney Swans ahead of the [2025 AFL season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_AFL_season).[^cite_note-206] At a press conference in December 2024, Cox flagged improvements to defence and contested work, which he described as "really poor" in the 2024 Grand Final. He also confirmed that key defender Tom McCartin had trained as a forward during much of the preseason.[^cite_note-207] The club conducted a seven-and-a-half-hour review of the Grand Final loss, including one hour devoted to the opening minute of the match.[^cite_note-208] Cox reinforced his approach of "challenging" the playing group with back-to-back 2 km time trials in February.[^cite_note-209]

The early stages of the 2025 season were affected by an expanded injury list, including key players [Callum Mills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Callum_Mills), [Errol Gulden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Errol_Gulden), [Brodie Grundy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brodie_Grundy), [Tom Papley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Papley), [Logan McDonald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logan_McDonald), and six other players.[^cite_note-210] The Swans lost in Opening Round at the [SCG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Cricket_Ground) to [Hawthorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawthorn_Football_Club) by 20 points, with Cox acknowledging post-match that he had "gotten some things wrong."[^cite_note-211] The following week, Sydney lost at home once again, this time by 4 points to [Brisbane](5026.md) in a Grand Final rematch.[^cite_note-212]

The first win during the Cox era came in Round 2, with a 3-point victory over [Fremantle](312661.md) at [Optus Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perth_Stadium).[^cite_note-213] After a bye, the Swans defeated [North Melbourne](22106.md) by 63 points in Round 4, but lost during Gather Round to Collingwood by 31 points.[^cite_note-214][^cite_note-215] Media sources described Sydney's second-quarter performance as "unacceptable," with the team recording a pressure rating of 139 amid a run of Collingwood goals.[^cite_note-216]

Extended conceding runs continued throughout the season, including Round 7 against [Gold Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_Coast_Suns) (12 goals to none), Round 11 against [Melbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melbourne_Football_Club) (12 goals to 6), Round 12 (12 goals to none) and Round 20 against [GWS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GWS_Giants) (11 goals to none). In Round 10, the Swans contested their annual [Marn Grook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marn_Grook) Game against [Carlton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlton_Football_Club), regaining a first-quarter lead in the final term to win by 16 points.[^cite_note-217]

&#x20;Sydney suffered its largest home defeat since 1998 in Round 12, losing 90 points to [Adelaide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adelaide_Football_Club) at the SCG on 31 May.[^cite_note-218][^cite_note-219] Cox described the performance as "unacceptable and embarrassing," adding:

> "For a team that played desperate, uncompromising, ruthless football, and that (tonight) was far from it, so we need to strip it back and get to work real quick."

Fans engaged in bronx cheering during the second quarter, which commentator [Anthony Hudson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Hudson_\(commentator\)) called "Humiliating," while Adelaide's [Wayne Milera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayne_Milera) labelled the Swans "a bit of a rabble," for which the Adelaide Football Club later apologised.[^cite_note-220]

The following morning, the squad held an impromptu dawn training session at Clovelly Beach, including push-ups and sit-ups, later reported to have been initiated by the club leaders and players.[^cite_note-221][^cite_note-222] The following week Dane Rampe stated the players had "let him (Cox) down" and that Milera's comment was "not far from the truth."[^cite_note-223]

Sydney rebounded with a 44-point win over [Richmond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richmond_Football_Club) in Round 13.[^cite_note-224] After the midseason bye, key players [Errol Gulden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Errol_Gulden) and [Tom Papley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Papley) returned for a 19-point victory against [Port Adelaide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Adelaide_Football_Club) in Round 15.[^cite_note-225] The Swans won six of their final nine games, but finals hopes ended in Round 20 with a 44-point loss to GWS at [Engie Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Showground_Stadium), ending a five-match winning streak against the [Giants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GWS_Giants).[^cite_note-226]

Cox trialled a faster playing style in Round 22 in the second Grand Final rematch against [Brisbane](5026.md), during which [Isaac Heeney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Heeney) scored an equal career-best five goals. Sydney led by 20 points in the penultimate minute, but held on to win by just two as Brisbane rapidly kicked the final three goals.[^cite_note-227]

Ahead of Round 22, former [Fremantle](312661.md) captain and broadcaster [Matthew Pavlich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Pavlich) was announced as the club's new CEO, replacing [Tom Harley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Harley) who had been announced earlier in the season as the incoming Chief Operating Officer for the AFL.[^cite_note-228][^cite_note-229] Sydney's Round 21 [Pride Game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jason_Ball_\(activist\)) resulted in a 14-point win over [Essendon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essendon_Football_Club). The contest was affected by rain and marked by far the Swans' lowest home attendance of the season (20,805).[^cite_note-230] It was also the first time that pride game was contested against a team other than [St Kilda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Kilda_Football_Club), who proposed the original concept.

Member Thank You Round was celebrated in Round 23, at the final home game for the season, which ended in a 43-point loss to Geelong.[^cite_note-231] The season concluded with a 67-point win over West Coast at Optus Stadium, Cox's first time coaching against his former club.[^cite_note-232]

Sydney finished tenth overall, with a 12–11 record, a 5–6 record at home, and a percentage of 97, marking just their fifth finals absence since 2000.[^cite_note-233] [Isaac Heeney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Heeney) was selected as an All-Australian for the third time in his career, also winning a second consecutive Bob Skilton Medal.[^cite_note-234][^cite_note-235] Teammate [Brodie Grundy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brodie_Grundy) made the 44-man All-Australian squad.[^cite_note-236]

In the offseason, Sydney made several staff changes ahead of the 2026 season. Long term head of National Recruitment Manager Kinnear Beatson transitioned to the Swans Academy, replaced by John Giramondo and Chris Keane.[^cite_note-237] [2021 Premiership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_AFL_Grand_Final) winning former Melbourne Coach [Simon Goodwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Goodwin) was appointed in a new role called Director of Coaching & Performance.[^cite_note-238] Former Swans player and VFL coach [Jeremy Laidler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Laidler) returned to the club as an AFL assistant coach, alongside former Gold Coast GM of Football [Wayne Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayne_Campbell), who was appointed head of the Swans Academy.[^cite_note-239] Swans 2012 Premiership player [Nick Malceski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_Malceski) was announced as coach of the VFL team, replacing former Swans teammate Jarrad McVeigh.[^cite_note-240] In November 2025, it was announced that inaugural Swans AFLW coach [Scott Gowans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scott_Gowans), resigned from the role after a ninth-place finish.[^cite_note-241] He was replaced by Irish-born former player [Colin O'Riordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_O'Riordan), who played 34 games for the Sydney Swans, before transitioning to a role in coaching.[^cite_note-242]

During the 2025 AFL Trade Period, Sydney acquired dual [Coleman Medalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coleman_Medal) [Charlie Curnow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlie_Curnow) from [Carlton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlton_Football_Club) in exchange for [Oliver Florent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ollie_Florent) and [Will Hayward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will_Hayward), with Hayward reporting to AFL.com.au that the move "probably could've been handled better."[^cite_note-243][^cite_note-244] The Swans also successfully recruited [Jai Serong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jai_Serong) (Hawthorn) and [Malcolm Rosas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malcolm_Rosas) (Gold Coast), both of whom requested trades to Sydney.[^cite_note-245][^cite_note-246]

## Club identity

The club's on-field nickname, the Swans, was suggested by a *[Herald and Weekly Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herald_and_Weekly_Times)* artist in 1933 in reference to the number of Western Australians in the team (the [black swan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_swan) being the state emblem of Western Australia). It was formally adopted by the club before the following season of 1934.[^cite_note-J._M._Rohan_7-48][^cite_note-250] The name is also thought to refer to the population of [black swans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_swan) in the [Albert Park Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Park_Lake), near the team's original home ground, South Melbourne Cricket Ground. The [Chicago Swans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Swans) are affiliated with the club and share a similar logo.[^cite_note-251]

### Uniforms

The jumper is white with a red back and a red yoke with a silhouette of the [Sydney Opera House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Opera_House) at the point of the yoke. The Opera House design was first used at the start of the 1987 season, replacing the traditional red "V" on white design. Until 1991, the back of the jumper was white with the yoke only extending to the back of the shoulders and each side of the jumper had a red vertical stripe. The current predominantly red design appeared at the start of the 1992 season. The club's major sponsor is [QBE Insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QBE_Insurance). In 2004 the club added the initials 'SMFC' in white lettering at the back of the collar to honour the club's past as South Melbourne Football Club. The move was welcomed by Melbourne-based fans. From 2011 to 2021, the clash guernsey was a predominantly white version of the home guernsey similar to the original Opera House guernsey design, including a white back, but was rarely used, since the two Queensland clubs (the [Brisbane Lions](5026.md) and [Gold Coast Suns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_Coast_Suns)) and cross town rivals [GWS Giants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GWS_Giants) are the only clubs with which there is a clash.[^cite_note-252] As of 2023 in addition to being used in games in Victoria unless there is a clash, the red "V" is used as a clash guernsey against Gold Coast and Brisbane.

[Nike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nike,_Inc.) is the current manufacturer of the Swans' apparel.[^cite_note-253] Previous manufacturers were [Puma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puma_\(brand\)) (from mid-1990s to 2009)[^cite_note-254] and [ISC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISC_\(sportswear\)) (2010 to 2020).[^cite_note-255]

### Club song

In the 1930s, the South Melbourne Swans song was an adaptation of "[Springtime in the Rockies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Hale_Woolsey#%22When_It's_Springtime_in_the_Rockies%22)." A newspaper article from 1932 also documents that the club sang themed parodies of "[Mademoiselle from Armentières](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mademoiselle_from_Armenti%C3%A8res)" and "You Will Remember Vienna", the theme from the film *[Viennese Nights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viennese_Nights)*.[^cite_note-clubsong-257][^cite_note-inkydinky-258]

In 1951, "Cheer, Cheer Red and the White" was officially adopted. The melody originated from the "[Victory March](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victory_March_\(fight_song\))" fight song of the [Notre Dame Fighting Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notre_Dame_Fighting_Irish), which Australian audiences had heard in the 1940 film *[Knute Rockne, All American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knute_Rockne,_All_American)*.[^cite_note-clubsong-257]

The encouraging phrase "Up there, Cazaly", referring to South Melbourne player [Roy Cazaly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Cazaly), was first used by club fans in the 1920s before entering the Australian lexicon in general, especially after [World War II](32927.md).[^cite_note-259] This later inspired the 1979 [Mike Brady](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mike_Brady_\(musician\)) song "[Up There Cazaly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Up_There_Cazaly)" which is today associated with Australian rules football in general and performed at every Grand Final. In 1982, when the Swans relocated to Sydney, the official club song was changed to a rewritten version entitled "Up There for Sydney." This was used intermittently until the mid-1990s, when "Cheer, Cheer the Red and the White" was reinstated. It remains the club song to this day.[^cite_note-260]

In March 2021, the "Cheer, Cheer" lyrics were changed from "while our loyal sons are marching" to "while our loyal Swans are marching" in order to be inclusive of the AFLW team.[^cite_note-261]

### Home ground and training base

The club's original home ground was [Lakeside Oval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakeside_Stadium), when they were known as the South Melbourne Football Club from 1879 until 1981.[^cite_note-Lakeside_Stadium-14][^cite_note-262]

Since the [2016 AFL season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_AFL_season), the Swans have played all their home games at the [Sydney Cricket Ground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Cricket_Ground), a 48,000 capacity venue located in inner-east suburb of [Moore Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore_Park,_New_South_Wales). The venue has been home to Swans home games since the club's relocation to Sydney in 1982.[^cite_note-263] In the years 2002–2015, the Swans played between three and four home matches per season and most home [finals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AFL_finals_series) matches at [Stadium Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stadium_Australia) (commercially known as [ANZ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australia_and_New_Zealand_Banking_Group) Stadium), an 80,000 capacity stadium located in the west of the city. During the first five years at the ground average crowds were high, but issues with the surface as well as fan and player disengagement resulted in the club ending its association with the venue.

The club also trains on the SCG during the season and had its indoor training facilities and offices located within the stadium. During the off-season, when the ground is configured for [cricket](25675557.md), the Swans train on the adjacent [Tramway Oval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tramway_Oval) (previously known as Lakeside Oval) also located within the Moore Park precinct.[^cite_note-264][^cite_note-WorkOut-265][^cite_note-266] The oval is located less than one hundred metres from the SCG and since undergoing a redevelopment in 2018/19, has the same dimensions as the [Docklands Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Docklands_Stadium) in Melbourne. The Swans NEAFL/reserves team have played some home matches at the oval, which has grassed hills and standing areas for several hundred spectators.[^cite_note-267]

In October 2018 the club announced it would shift all offices and indoor training facilities to Moore Park's Royal Hall of Industries sometime in the early-to-mid 2020s, after announcing a \$55 million deal with the [New South Wales Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_South_Wales_Government) to redevelop the Hall.[^cite_note-268] The club pulled out of the agreement in April 2020 due to the financial implications of the [COVID-19 pandemic](62750956.md).[^cite_note-269] In August 2021, the Swans announced it had re-negotiated a lease with the government to immediately commence the project. The facility provides indoor training facilities and administration for the Swans AFL, VFL and AFLW teams.[^cite_note-270] The club began moving into the facility in December 2022.[^cite_note-271][^cite_note-272][^cite_note-273][^cite_note-274][^cite_note-275] It was formally opened by the Prime Minister and New South Wales Premier in June 2023. The facility includes an indoor training field, gym, wet recovery area, medical amenities, a retail store, commercial kitchen, media studio, auditorium, learning centre, office space and a rooftop terrace, while the newly built adjoining building includes an international standard multi-purpose indoor sports court.[^cite_note-276]

## Rivalries

### Greater Western Sydney

Main article: [Sydney Derby (AFL)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Derby_\(AFL\))

The introduction of the [GWS Giants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Western_Sydney_Giants) to the AFL in [2012](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_AFL_season) resulted in the formation of the [Sydney Derby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Derby_\(AFL\)). The Swans compete against their cross-city rivals twice every season. The best performed player from every derby match is awarded the **Brett Kirk Medal**.

Initially, the rivalry was a one-sided affair in favour of the Swans, who won 8 of the first 9 derbies. However, it has become more competitive in recent years, with the Giants winning 5 of the 7 most recent derbies. The Swans have also played the Giants in four finals matches, losing three of them.[^cite_note-277][^cite_note-278][^cite_note-279][^cite_note-280]

### West Coast Eagles

Main article: [Sydney–West Coast AFL rivalry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney%E2%80%93West_Coast_AFL_rivalry)



[Barry Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Hall) and [Darren Glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darren_Glass)

The Swans developed a famous [modern rivalry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AFL_team_rivalries) against the Perth-based [West Coast Eagles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Coast_Eagles) between 2005 and 2007, when six consecutive games between the two teams, including two qualifying finals and two grand finals, were decided by less than a goal. The rivalry was highlighted by Sydney's four-point win against West Coast in the [2005 Grand Final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_AFL_Grand_Final), and West Coast's one-point win against Sydney in the [2006 Grand Final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_AFL_Grand_Final).

### Hawthorn

The rivalry with [Hawthorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawthorn_Football_Club) has been more recent, mostly defined by two grand finals ([2012](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_AFL_Grand_Final) and [2014](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_AFL_Grand_Final)). The Swans beat Hawthorn in 2012 by 10 points to claim their fifth premiership. The rivalry grew in 2013, when Hawthorn forward Lance Franklin transferred to the Swans as a free agent on a nine-year, \$10 million deal. In 2014, the Swans finished minor premiers and were favourites to win the grand final, however were defeated by 63 points. Both teams have had close games since their grand final encounters, with their matches often finishing within single digit margins.

## Women's team



The Sydney Swans women's first home game at the [North Sydney Oval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Sydney_Oval) on 27 August 2022 set a new record crowd for women's Australian rules football in New South Wales with 8,264 in attendance.

[^cite_note-NthSyd-284]

The Sydney Swans women's team was founded for the 2016 exhibition series; however, they did not bid for AFLW entry until 2022. Following the granting of an official AFLW license, the team made its debut in [2022 AFL Women's season 7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_AFL_Women's_season_7). It was the most supported in the AFLW in 2022 (S7) with 7,757 members.[^cite_note-285] Despite finishing last in its debut season, the women's team had the 4th highest average home attendance in the league, with 3,773 playing out of the North Sydney Oval and [Henson Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henson_Park).

## Reserves team

The **Sydney reserves** are the reserves side of the club, playing in the [Victorian Football League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_Football_League) since [2021](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_VFL_season).

### History

**South Melbourne Juniors** was formed by the South Melbourne Football Club on 29 March 1900, joining the [Metropolitan Junior Football Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_Amateur_Football_Association) (MJFA).[^cite_note-293]

In [1919](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1919_VJFL_season), the [Victorian Junior Football League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_Junior_Football_League) (VFL) was formed, with the [Leopold Football Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopold_Football_Club) operating as South Melbourne's affiliate. At the end of the [1924 season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1924_VJFL_season), Leopold was replaced by the **South Melbourne Second Eighteen**.[^cite_note-294]

The side competed in the [VFL seconds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AFL_reserves) (which later became the [AFL reserves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AFL_reserves)) until the competition's demise at the end of 1999, even after the move Sydney in 1982. The team enjoyed little success in the reserves competition, with its best performances in losing grand finals in 1927, 1956, 1980 and 1995.

In 2000, the Sydney reserves – known as the Redbacks – joined the [Sydney AFL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_AFL) competition, but was so dominant in the lower competition that it withdrew prior to the finals series because the club felt the difference in standard was too greatly in favour of the Swans. Between 2001 and 2002, the Swans affiliated themselves with the [Port Melbourne Football Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Melbourne_Football_Club) in the [VFL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_Football_League), sending most of its reserves players there, while also retaining the Redbacks in the Sydney AFL as a junior development team. From 2003, the Sydney reserves recombined to a single team, which contested the higher standard [AFL Canberra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AFL_Canberra), winning four consecutive premierships between 2005 and 2008.

In 2011 the Swans reserves team joined the newly established [North East Australian Football League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_East_Australian_Football_League) with the rest of the AFL Canberra competition. The side often played as a curtain raiser to senior AFL games. The side never won a premiership, eliminated in the Eastern Conference Grand Finals in [2011](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_NEAFL_season) and [2012](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_NEAFL_season); then losing the overall NEAFL grand final five times: [2013](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2013_NEAFL_season), [2014](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_NEAFL_season), [2016](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_NEAFL_season), [2017](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_NEAFL_season) and [2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_NEAFL_season).

Following the disbandment of the NEAFL in 2020, the Sydney reserves have competed in the [Victorian Football League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_Football_League).

## Sydney Swans Academy

The **Sydney Swans Academy** consists of the club's junior development signings. It was formed in 2010 as one of two in Sydney including the [GWS Giants Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GWS_Giants_Academy) and one of four Northern Academies including the [Brisbane Lions Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brisbane_Lions_Academy) and [Gold Coast Suns Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_Coast_Suns_Academy).

72 spread staff across 9 facilities manage 550 selected underage players from age 10 up with regional hubs in [Illawarra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illawarra), [Central Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Coast_\(New_South_Wales\)), [Newcastle](52665.md), [Port Macquarie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Macquarie) and [Northern Rivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Rivers).[^cite_note-295]

The men's and women's U16 and U18 teams have contested Division 2 of the men's and women's underage championships since 2017.

The Swans Academy also joined the [Talent League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talent_League) in 2019.

## Supporter base



Sydney Swans supporters

Although a large majority of the existing [fan base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fan_base) strongly objected to the relocation of the club from South Melbourne, the Sydney Swans have built a large following in the city they now call home. Attendances and memberships in Sydney grew dramatically during the Lockett era, helped out by the [Super League War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_League_War) plaguing Rugby League.[^cite_note-305] Nevertheless, the Swans continue to have a substantial supporter base in Victoria, with attendances for Swans games in Melbourne being much higher than other non-Victorian teams. In 2024, the Sydney Swans achieved their highest home attendances in club history, with an average of 38,202 fans at each of their 11 home games at the SCG, far surpassing the previous record of 35,818 that was set back in 1997.[^cite_note-306][^cite_note-307] The club also reached a new membership record of 73,757 as of September 2024, 12.9% more than their previous record of 65,332, set one year earlier in 2023.[^cite_note-308][^cite_note-309]

[Aboriginal](2912594.md) actress and dancer [Lillian Crombie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lillian_Crombie) founded the [First Nations](12598742.md) supporters club, the Black Swans.[^cite_note-310]

According to [Roy Morgan Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Morgan_Research), the club has been the most supported club among all AFL supporters in every year since 2004.[^cite_note-311]

The club also has an official Sydney [LGBTQIA+](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGBTQIA+) supporter group, Rainbow Swans.[^cite_note-is-193]
