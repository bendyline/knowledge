**Freedom of the press** or **freedom of the media** is the fundamental principle that communication and expression through various media, including printed and electronic [media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_\(communication\)), especially [published materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publication), should be considered a right to be exercised freely.[^cite_note-1] Such freedom implies no or minimal [censorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship) or [prior restraint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prior_restraint) from government, and is often protected by laws or a provision in a [constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution). The concept of [freedom of speech](21401843.md) is often covered by the same laws as freedom of the press, thereby giving equal treatment to spoken and published expression; many countries also protect [scientific freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_freedom).

Government restrictions on freedom of the press may include [classified information](252857.md), [state secrets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_secrets), punishment for [libel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libel), punishment for violation of [copyright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright), [privacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privacy), or judicial orders. Where freedom of the press is lacking, governments may require pre-publication approval, or punish distribution of documents critical of the government or certain political perspectives. Jurisdictions with high levels of transparency are subject to "[sunshine laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunshine_laws)" or [freedom of information legislation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_information_legislation) that allow citizens broad access to government-held information.

Freedom of the press was formally established in Great Britain with the [lapse of the Licensing of the Press Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute_of_Anne#Lapse_of_the_Licensing_of_the_Press_Act_1662) in 1695. Sweden was the first country in the world to adopt a legally protected freedom of the press with the [Freedom of the Press Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Freedom_of_the_Press_Act) of 1766, although it did not become inscribed in the constitution due to opposition from the nobility.[^cite_note-\:4-2] The [United Nations](31769.md)' 1948 [Universal Declaration of Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Declaration_of_Human_Rights) states: "Everyone has the right to freedom of opinion and expression; this right includes freedom to hold opinions without interference, and to seek, receive, and impart information and ideas through any media regardless of frontiers".[^cite_note-3]

## Relationship to self-publishing

Freedom of the press is not construed as an absence of interference by outside entities, such as a government or religious organization, but rather as a right for authors to have their works published by other people.[^cite_note-\:0-4] This idea was famously summarized by the 20th-century American [journalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journalist), [A. J. Liebling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._J._Liebling), who wrote, "Freedom of the press is guaranteed only to those who own one". Freedom of the press gives the printer or publisher exclusive control over what the publisher chooses to publish, including the right to refuse to print anything for any reason.[^cite_note-\:0-4] If the author cannot reach a voluntary agreement with a publisher to produce the author's work, then the author must turn to [self-publishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-publishing).

## Status of press freedom worldwide



*[Cumhuriyet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumhuriyet)*'s former editor-in-chief [Can Dündar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Can_D%C3%BCndar) receiving the 2015 [Reporters Without Borders Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reporters_Without_Borders_Prize). Shortly after, he was arrested.

Beyond legal definitions, several [non-governmental organizations](46539.md) use other criteria to judge the level of press freedom worldwide. Some create subjective lists, while others are based on quantitative data:

- [Reporters Without Borders](526054.md) (RWB) (French: Reporters sans frontières) (RSF) considers the number of journalists murdered, expelled, or harassed, the existence of a [state monopoly](20803507.md) on TV and radio, as well as the existence of [censorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship) and [self-censorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-censorship) in the media, and the overall independence of media as well as the difficulties that foreign reporters may face to rank countries in levels of press freedom.

- The [Committee to Protect Journalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_to_Protect_Journalists) (CPJ) systematically tracks the number of journalists killed and imprisoned in reprisal for their work. It says it uses the tools of [journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journalism) to help journalists by tracking press freedom issues through independent research, fact-finding missions, and a network of foreign correspondents, including local working journalists in countries worldwide. CPJ shares information on breaking cases with other press freedom organizations worldwide through the [International Freedom of Expression Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Freedom_of_Expression_Exchange), a global network of more than 119 free expression organizations. CPJ also tracks impunity in cases of journalist murders. CPJ staff applies strict criteria for each case; researchers independently investigate and verify the circumstances behind each death or imprisonment.

- [Freedom House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_House) studies the more general political and economic environments of each nation in order to determine whether relationships of dependence exist that limit in practice the level of press freedom that might exist in theory. Panels of experts assess the press freedom score and draft each country summary according to a weighted scoring system that analyzes the political, economic, legal and safety situation for journalists based on a 100-point scale. It then categorizes countries as having a free, partly free, or not free press.

### Democratic states

A free and independent press has been theorized to be a key mechanism of a functioning, healthy [democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy).[^cite_note-\:3-12] In the absence of [censorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship), journalism exists as a [watchdog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watchdog_journalism) of private and government action, providing information to maintain an informed citizenry of voters.[^cite_note-\:3-12] In this perspective, "government efforts to influence published or broadcasted news content, either via media control or by inducing [self-censorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-censorship), represent a threat to the access of important and necessary information to the public and affect the quality of democracy".[^cite_note-13] An independent press "serves to increase political knowledge, participation, and [voter turnout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voter_turnout)",[^cite_note-\:3-12] acting as an essential driver of civic participation.

## History

### Europe

Main article: [Media freedom in the European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_freedom_in_the_European_Union)

Central, Northern, and Western Europe have a long tradition of freedom of speech, including freedom of the press, which yet exists in the 18th century and in the 19th century.[^cite_note-20]

German Enlightenment writer [Christoph Martin Wieland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christoph_Martin_Wieland) argued in 1788 that "press freedom is the ultimate bulwark of humanity" and that restrictions beyond general criminal law would lead to its "gradual erosion", emphasizing that "science, literature, and the printing press... belong to no single nation, but to all mankind".[^cite_note-21]

After World War II, [Hugh Baillie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Baillie), the president of the [United Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Press_International) wire service based in the U.S., promoted freedom of news dissemination. In 1944, he called for an open system of news sources and transmission, and a minimum of government regulation of the news. His proposals were aired at the Geneva Conference on Freedom of Information in 1948 but were blocked by the Soviets and the French.[^cite_note-22]

Since 1950, the [European Convention on Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Convention_on_Human_Rights) includes "Article 10" related to Freedom of expression which applies to [Member states of the Council of Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Member_states_of_the_Council_of_Europe).

Media freedom is a [fundamental right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_rights) that applies to all [member states](10890716.md) of the [European Union](9317.md) and its [citizens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EU_citizens), as defined in the [EU Charter of Fundamental Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EU_Charter_of_Fundamental_Rights) (since 2000) as well as the European Convention on Human Rights (since 1950).[^cite_note-eprs-23]<sup>: 1 </sup> Within the [EU enlargement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EU_enlargement) process, guaranteeing media freedom is named a "key indicator of a country's readiness to become part of the EU".[^cite_note-24]

#### United Kingdom

According to the *[New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Times)*, "Britain has a long tradition of a free, inquisitive press", but "\[u]nlike the United States, Britain has no constitutional guarantee of press freedom".[^cite_note-25] Freedom of the press was established in Great Britain in 1695, with [Alan Rusbridger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Rusbridger), former editor of *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)*, stating: "When people talk about licensing journalists or newspapers the instinct should be to refer them to history. Read about how licensing of the press in Britain was abolished in 1695. Remember how the freedoms won here became a model for much of the rest of the world, and be conscious of how the world still watches us to see how we protect those freedoms".[^cite_note-26]



First page of [John Milton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Milton)'s 1644 edition of *[Areopagitica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Areopagitica)*

Until 1694, Great Britain had an elaborate system of [licensing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Licensing); the most recent was seen in the [Licensing of the Press Act, 1662](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Licensing_of_the_Press_Act_1662). No publication was allowed without the accompaniment of a government-granted license. Fifty years earlier, at a time of [civil war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Civil_War), [John Milton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Milton) wrote his [pamphlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pamphlet) *[Areopagitica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Areopagitica)* (1644).[^cite_note-Sanders-27] In this work Milton argued forcefully against this form of government censorship and parodied the idea, writing "when as debtors and delinquents may walk abroad without a keeper, but inoffensive books must not stir forth without a visible jailer in their title". Although at the time it did little to halt the practice of licensing, it would be viewed later a significant milestone as one of the most eloquent defenses of [press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_media) freedom.[^cite_note-Sanders-27]

Milton's central argument was that the individual is capable of using reason and distinguishing right from wrong, and good from bad. In order to be able to exercise this ration right, the individual must have unlimited access to the ideas of his fellow men in "a free and open encounter" Milton's writings developed the concept of the open [marketplace of ideas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marketplace_of_ideas), the idea that when people argue against each other, good arguments will prevail. One form of speech that was widely restricted in Great Britain was [seditious libel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seditious_libel), and laws were in place that made criticizing the government a crime. The king was above public criticism and statements critical of the government were forbidden, according to the English court of the [Star Chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Chamber). The truth was not a defense to seditious libel because the goal was to prevent and punish all condemnation of the government.

Locke contributed to the [lapse of the Licensing Act in 1695](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute_of_Anne#Lapse_of_the_Licensing_of_the_Press_Act_1662), whereupon the press needed no license. Still, some libels were tried throughout the 18th century, until "the Society of the Bill of Rights" led by [John Horne Tooke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Horne_Tooke) and [John Wilkes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wilkes) organized a campaign to publish Parliamentary Debates. This culminated in three defeats of the Crown in the 1770 cases of Almon, Miller and [Woodfall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Sampson_Woodfall), who all had published one of the [Letters of Junius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letters_of_Junius), and the unsuccessful arrest of [John Wheble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wheble) in 1771. Thereafter the Crown was much more careful in the application of [libel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libel); for example, in the aftermath of the [Peterloo Massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peterloo_Massacre), [Burdett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Burdett) was convicted, whereas by contrast, the [Junius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junius_\(writer\)) affair was over a [satire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satire) and sarcasm about the non-lethal conduct and policies of the government.

In Britain's [American colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirteen_Colonies), the first editors discovered their readers enjoyed it when they criticised the local governor; the governors discovered they could shut down the newspapers. The most dramatic confrontation came in New York in 1734, where the governor brought [John Peter Zenger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Peter_Zenger) to trial for criminal libel after the publication of satirical attacks. The defense lawyers argued that according to English common law, the truth was a valid defense against libel. The jury acquitted Zenger, who became the iconic American hero for freedom of the press. The result was an emerging tension between the media and the government. By the mid-1760s, there were 24 weekly newspapers in the 13 colonies, and the satirical attack on the government became common features in American newspapers.[^cite_note-28]

In the [Victorian era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_era), the press became more influential than it had been previously, to the dismay of some readers. [Thomas Carlyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Carlyle), in his essay "[Signs of the Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_and_Miscellaneous_Essays)" (1829), said that the "true [Church of England](5955.md), at this moment, lies in the Editors of its Newspapers. These preach to the people daily, weekly; admonishing kings themselves; advising peace or war, with an authority which only the first [Reformers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant_Reformers), and a long-past class of [Popes](23056.md), were possessed of". Similarly, [Charles Dickens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Dickens), in his *[Pickwick Papers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pickwick_Papers)* (1837), caricatured the newspapers as but the "chosen organ and representative" of either the [Whigs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whigs_\(British_political_party\)) or the [Tories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tories_\(UK\)), and that they were "essentially and indispensably necessary" to the parties' operations.[^cite_note-29]

[John Stuart Mill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Stuart_Mill) in 1869 in his book *[On Liberty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Liberty)* approached the problem of authority versus liberty from the viewpoint of a 19th-century [utilitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utilitarian): The individual has the right of expressing himself so long as he does not harm other individuals. The good society is one in which the greatest number of persons enjoy the greatest possible amount of happiness. Applying these general principles of liberty to freedom of expression, Mill states that if we silence an opinion, we may silence the truth. The individual freedom of expression is therefore essential to the well-being of society. Mill wrote:

If all mankind minus one, were of one opinion, and one, and only one person were of the contrary opinion, mankind would be no more justified in silencing that one person, than he, if he had the power, would be justified in silencing mankind.

[^cite_note-30]

The December 1817 Trials of writer and satirist [William Hone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Hone) for publishing three political pamphlets is considered a landmark in the fight for a free press.

#### Denmark–Norway

Between 4 September 1770 and 7 October 1771 the kingdom of [Denmark–Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denmark%E2%80%93Norway) had the most unrestricted freedom of press of any country in Europe. This occurred during the regime of [Johann Friedrich Struensee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Friedrich_Struensee), whose second act was to abolish the old censorship laws. However, due to the great amount of mostly anonymous pamphlets published that was critical and often slanderous towards Struensee's own regime, he reinstated some restrictions regarding the freedom of press a year later, 7 October 1771.[^cite_note-31]

#### Italy

See also: [Censorship in Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship_in_Italy)



The Statute was adopted as the constitution of the Kingdom of Italy, granting freedom of the press.

After the [Italian unification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_unification) in 1861, the [Albertine Statute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albertine_Statute) of 1848 was adopted as the constitution of the [Kingdom of Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Italy). The Statute granted the freedom of the press with some restrictions in case of abuses and in religious matters, as stated in Article 28:[^cite_note-32]

> The press shall be free, but the law may suppress abuses of this freedom. However, Bibles, catechisms, liturgical and prayer books shall not be printed without the prior permission of the Bishop.

After the [abolition of the monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_constitutional_referendum,_1946) in 1946 and the [abrogation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repeal) of the Statute in 1948, the [Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Italy) of the [Republic of Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Italy) guarantees the freedom of the press, as stated in Article 21, Paragraphs 2 and 3:[^cite_note-33]

> The press may not be subjected to any authorisation or censorship. Seizure may be permitted only by judicial order stating the reason and only for offences expressly determined by the law on the press or in case of violation of the obligation to identify the persons responsible for such offences.

The Constitution allows the [warrantless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warrant_\(law\)) [confiscation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confiscation) of [periodicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodicals) in cases of absolute urgency, when the [Judiciary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judiciary_of_Italy) cannot timely intervene, on the condition that a [judicial validation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judicial_review) must be obtained within 24 hours. Article 21 also gives restrictions against those publications considered offensive by [public morality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_morality), as stated in Paragraph 6:

> Publications, performances, and other exhibits offensive to public morality shall be prohibited. Measures of preventive and repressive measure against such violations shall be established by law.

#### Nazi Germany (1933–1945)



[Joseph Goebbels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Goebbels)' [Ministry of Public Enlightenment and Propaganda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Public_Enlightenment_and_Propaganda) was a driving force of suppressing freedom of the press in Nazi Germany.

In 1933, freedom of the press was suppressed in [Nazi Germany](21212.md) by the [Reichstag Fire Decree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reichstag_Fire_Decree) of President [Paul von Hindenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_von_Hindenburg), just as [Adolf Hitler](2731583.md) was coming to power. Hitler suppressed freedom of the press through [Joseph Goebbels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Goebbels)' [Ministry of Public Enlightenment and Propaganda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Public_Enlightenment_and_Propaganda).[^cite_note-34] The Ministry acted as a central control point for all media, issuing orders as to what stories could be run and what stories would be suppressed. Anyone involved in the film industry, from directors to the lowliest assistant, had to sign an oath of loyalty to the [Nazi Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Party) due to the opinion-changing power Goebbels perceived movies to have; Goebbels himself maintained some personal control over every single film made in Nazi Europe. Journalists who crossed the Propaganda Ministry were routinely imprisoned.

#### Sweden

One of the world's first freedom of the press acts was introduced in Sweden in 1766 ([Swedish Freedom of the Press Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Freedom_of_the_Press_Act)), due in part to [classical liberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_liberal) member of parliament, [Ostrobothnian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ostrobothnia_\(historical_province\)) priest, [Anders Chydenius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anders_Chydenius).[^cite_note-\:4-2][^cite_note-35][^cite_note-36][^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38] However, the ordinance had been a long time in the making. The first proposal to abolish censorship was presented to the Riksdag as early as 1727 by Anders Bachmanson (later ennobled as Nordencrantz), a member of the burgher estate.[^cite_note-39]

Excepted and liable to prosecution in the 1766 act was only vocal opposition to the [king](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King) and the [Church of Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Sweden). The act was largely rolled back after [King Gustav](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_III_of_Sweden)'s coup d'état in 1772, restored after the overthrowing of his son, [Gustav IV of Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_IV_of_Sweden) in 1809, and fully recognized with the abolition of the king's prerogative to cancel licenses in the 1840s.

#### Romania

Until 1989, Romania was part of the [communist bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_Bloc) as the [Socialist Republic of Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_Republic_of_Romania). The communist regime heavily restricted freedom of the press and other civil liberties. [Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_Free_Europe/Radio_Liberty), though available to the Romanian citizenry, was highly illegal and severe repercussions for existed for listening. Directly following the [Romanian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_Revolution), post-communist corruption was largely the subject of investigative reports. At the same time, widespread violence against journalists began. During the [June 1990 Mineriad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/June_1990_Mineriad), a series of protests against the [National Salvation Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Salvation_Front_\(Romania\)), counter-demonstrators assaulted reporting journalists.[^cite_note-47]

In 1992, President [Ion Iliescu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion_Iliescu) had a nervous meltdown when called journalist [Paul Pârvu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_P%C3%A2rvu?action=edit\&redlink=1) asked him if he felt guilt over Romanian deaths during the revolution.[^cite_note-48] During the exchange, Iliescu referred to Pârvu as an "animal". Modern, major media outlets were founded during the mid-1990s, such as [Antena 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antena_1_\(Romania\)) in 1994 and [ProTV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pro_TV) in 1995. In 1999, the editor of a *Ora*, a local newspaper, Tiberiu Patru, was arrested[^cite_note-ifex-49] before being able to publish an investigation of corruption in [Dolj County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolj_County) under. In response, *Ora* moved its newsroom in front of the National Theater of Craiova to protest Patru's arrest.

The 2000s saw the creation of many new media outlets across television, radio, and the traditional press. In 2023, [Reporters Without Borders](526054.md) identified safety as a concern for Romanian journalists.[^cite_note-50]

According to the reports of the RSF in 2007, the [freedom of the press in Iran](4297092.md) ranked 166 among 169 states. These websites were both Palestinian and Arabic and were identified to have been publishing material that "threaten national security and civil peace". Quds News Network, among the blocked sites, stated that the move reflected the Palestinian Authority's repression of the press.[^cite_note-78]

In 2023, nearly 75% of journalists killed worldwide were Palestinians who had died in [Israel](9282173.md)’s [war in Gaza](74998519.md).[^cite_note-79] According to the [Committee to Protect Journalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_to_Protect_Journalists), Israel was the second worst country in the world for allowing the murderers of journalists to go unpunished.[^cite_note-80] [UNESCO](21786641.md) awarded its 2024 World Press Freedom Prize to the Palestinian journalists of [Gaza](12047.md).[^cite_note-81]

#### China

Main article: [Freedom of the press in China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_the_press_in_China)

Critics argue that the [Chinese Communist Party](7175.md) has failed to live up to its promises about the freedom of the [mainland Chinese media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_of_China). [Freedom House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_House) consistently ranks China as 'Not Free'[^cite_note-82] in its annual press freedom survey, including the 2014 report. PRC journalist [He Qinglian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/He_Qinglian) says that the PRC's media are controlled by directives from the Communist Party's propaganda department and are subjected to intense monitoring which threatens punishment for violators, rather than to pre-publication censorship. In 2008, [ITV News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITV_News) reporter John Ray was arrested while covering a 'Free Tibet' protest.[^cite_note-83] International media coverage of Tibetan protests only a few months before the Beijing Olympics in 2008 triggered a strong reaction inside China. Chinese media practitioners took the opportunity to argue with propaganda authorities for more media freedom: one journalist asked, 'If not even Chinese journalists are allowed to report about the problems in Tibet, how can foreign journalists know about the Chinese perspective about the events?' Foreign journalists also reported that their access to certain websites, including those of human rights organizations, was restricted.[^cite_note-84]

[International Olympic Committee](15147.md) president [Jacques Rogge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Rogge) stated at the end of the 2008 Olympic Games that "The regulations \[governing foreign media freedom during the Olympics] might not be perfect but they are a sea-change compared to the situation before. We hope that they will continue".[^cite_note-85] The Foreign Correspondents Club of China (FCCC) issued a statement during the Olympics that 'despite welcome progress in terms of accessibility and the number of press conferences within the Olympic facilities, the FCCC has been alarmed at the use of violence, intimidation and harassment outside. The club has confirmed more than 30 cases of reporting interference since the formal opening of the Olympic media centre on 25 July, and is checking at least 20 other reported incidents.[^cite_note-86]

Since the Chinese state continues to exert a considerable amount of control over media, public support for domestic reporting has come as a surprise to many observers. Not much is known about the extent to which the Chinese citizenry believe the official statements of the CPC, nor about which media sources they perceive as credible and why. So far, research on the media in China has focused on the changing relationship between media outlets and the state during the reform era. Nor is much known about how China's changing media environment has affected the government's ability to persuade media audiences. Research on political trust reveals that exposure to the media correlates positively with support for the government in some instances, and negatively in others. The research has been cited as evidence that the Chinese public believes propaganda transmitted to them through the news media, but also that they disbelieve it.

In 2012 the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights urged the Chinese government to lift restrictions on media access to the region and allow independent and impartial monitors to visit and assess conditions in Tibet. The Chinese government did not change its position.[^cite_note-87]

#### Pakistan

Main article: [Freedom of the press in Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_the_press_in_Pakistan)

Article 19 of the constitution of the Pakistan states that: "Every citizen shall have the right to freedom of speech and expression, and there shall be freedom of the press, subject to any reasonable restrictions imposed by law in the interest of the glory of Islam or the integrity, security or defense of Pakistan or any part thereof, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, or in relation to contempt of court, commission of or incitement to an offence".[^cite_note-88] Since independence the electronic media in Pakistan remained dominated by the state-run Pakistan Television and Pakistan Broadcasting Corporations. Ironically, press freedom in Pakistan flourished for the first time in 2002 during Gen. [Pervez Musharraf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pervez_Musharraf)'s era.[^cite_note-89]

To a large extent the media enjoys freedom of expression in spite of political pressure and direct bans sometimes administered by political stake holders. Political pressure on media is mostly done indirectly. One tool widely used by the government is to cut off 'unfriendly' media from governmental advertising. Using draconian laws, the government has also banned or officially silenced popular television channels. The [Pakistan Electronic Media Regulatory Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan_Electronic_Media_Regulatory_Authority) (PEMRA) has been used to silence the broadcast media by either suspending licenses or by simply threatening to do so. In addition, media is also threatened by non-state actors involved in the current conflict.

In its 2018 [Press Freedom Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Press_Freedom_Index), [Reporters Without Borders](526054.md) ranked Pakistan number 139 out of 180 countries based on freedom of the press. The report implied considerable improvement in the freedom of press compared to the preceding years.[^cite_note-90]

#### Malaysia

[The press in Malaysia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_the_press_in_Malaysia) is controlled and journalists cannot have a conversation about certain things. For instance, a British reporter in Malaysia was arrested after she reported on the [1Malaysia Development Berhad scandal](47550643.md) and published details of the alleged transfer of \$681 million from 1MDB to bank accounts held by [Najib Razak](523641.md).[^cite_note-91]

#### Singapore

[Singapore's media environment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_media_in_Singapore) is considered to be controlled by the government.[^cite_note-92][^cite_note-93]

#### Saudi Arabia



Saudi journalist Jamal Khashoggi was a journalist and critic but was murdered by the Saudi Government.

[^cite_note-washingtonpost.com-94]

[Saudi Arabia](349303.md) does not tolerate dissidents and it can impose penalties on such people. Saudi Arabia is also responsible for executing [Saudi American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saudi_Americans) journalist, [Jamal Khashoggi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamal_Khashoggi) in 2018. As he entered a Saudi embassy in Turkey, a group of Saudi assassins killed him.[^cite_note-washingtonpost.com-94]

#### India

Main article: [Freedom of the press in India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_the_press_in_India)

The [Indian Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Constitution), while not mentioning the word "press", provides for *"the right to freedom of speech and expression"* (Article 19(1) a). However, this right is subject to restrictions under sub clause, whereby this freedom can be restricted for reasons of "[sovereignty](28791.md) and integrity of India, the security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, preserving decency, preserving morality, in relation to contempt, court, [defamation](28661.md), or incitement to an offense". Laws such as the [Official Secrets Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Official_Secrets_Act_\(India\)) and [Prevention of Terrorist Activities Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prevention_of_Terrorist_Activities_Act)[^cite_note-pota-95] (PoTA) have been used to limit press freedom. Under PoTA, person could be detained for up to six months for being in contact with a terrorist or terrorist group. PoTA was repealed in 2006, but the Official Secrets Act 1923 continues.

For the first half-century of independence, media control by the state was the major constraint on press freedom. [Indira Gandhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indira_Gandhi) famously stated in 1975 that [All India Radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_India_Radio) is "a Government organ, it is going to remain a Government organ..."[^cite_note-96] With the liberalization starting in the 1990s, private control of media has burgeoned, leading to increasing independence and greater scrutiny of government.

It ranks poorly at 142nd[^cite_note-97] rank out of 180 listed countries in the [Press Freedom Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Press_Freedom_Index) 2021 released by [Reporters Without Borders](526054.md) (RSF).[^cite_note-\:1-98] Analytically India's press freedom, as could be deduced by the [Press Freedom Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Press_Freedom_Index), has constantly reduced since 2002, when it culminated in terms of apparent freedom, achieving a rank of 80 among the reported countries. In 2018, India's freedom of press ranking declined two placed to 138. In explaining the decline, RSF cited growing intolerance from [Hindu nationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_nationalist) supporters of Indian Prime Minister [Narendra Modi](444222.md), and the murders of journalists such as [Gauri Lankesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauri_Lankesh).[^cite_note-99][^cite_note-100][^cite_note-101]

#### Bangladesh

Main article: [Freedom of the press in Bangladesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_the_press_in_Bangladesh)

[Bangladeshi media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_of_Bangladesh) is reportedly following self-censorship due to the controversial Information and Communication Technology (ICT) Act. Under this act, 25 journalists and several hundred bloggers and Facebook users are reportedly prosecuted in Bangladesh in 2017.[^cite_note-102]

Bangladesh ranks poorly at 146th rank out of 180 listed countries in the [Press Freedom Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Press_Freedom_Index) 2018 released by [Reporters Without Borders](526054.md) (RWB).[^cite_note-\:1-98] Bangladeshi media has faced many problems in 2018. The country's most popular online newspaper [bdnews24.com](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bdnews24.com) was blocked for a few hours on 18 June 2018, by Bangladesh's regulatory authority. Another newspaper *[The Daily Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Daily_Star_\(Bangladesh\))*'s website was blocked for 22 hours on 2 June 2018, after it had published a report about a victim of an extrajudicial execution in the southeastern city of [Cox's Bazar](2271204.md).[^cite_note-103]

During the [road-safety protests in 2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Bangladesh_road-safety_protests), Bangladeshi government switched off 3G and 4G mobile data and also arrested a photographer named [Shahidul Alam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shahidul_Alam) under ICT act, after he had given an interview with [Al Jazeera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Jazeera_Arabic).[^cite_note-104]

#### Japan

In Japan's system, the Japanese government excludes foreign media outlets and freelance journalists from briefings, giving the [kisha clubs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kisha_clubs) (journalists mainly from Japan's mainstream media) the right to use primary sources. Also, the elite group of journalists provides a mechanism whereby politicians threaten to block journalists from briefings if their report is critical of the government. The clubs are considered to be one of the reasons why Japan came bottom among the G7 nations and was ranked 68th in the world in the 2023 World Press Freedom Index.[^cite_note-105]

## Implications of new technologies

Many of the traditional means of delivering information are being slowly superseded by the increasing pace of modern technological advance. Almost every conventional mode of media and information dissemination has a modern counterpart that offers significant potential advantages to journalists seeking to maintain and enhance their freedom of speech. A few simple examples of such phenomena include:

- [Satellite television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_television) versus [terrestrial television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrestrial_television): Whilst terrestrial television is relatively easy to manage and manipulate, satellite television is much more difficult to control as journalistic content can easily be broadcast from other jurisdictions beyond the control of individual governments. An example of this in the Middle East is the satellite broadcaster [Al Jazeera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Jazeera_Arabic). This Arabic-language media channel operates out of [Qatar](8238258.md), whose government is relatively liberal compared to many of its neighboring states. As such, its views and content are often problematic to a number of governments in the region and beyond. However, because of the increased affordability and miniaturisation of satellite technology (e.g., dishes and receivers) it is simply not practicable for most states to control popular access to the channel.

- Internet-based publishing (e.g., [blogging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blog), [social media](5897742.md)) vs. traditional [publishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publishing): Traditional magazines and newspapers rely on physical resources (e.g., offices, printing presses) that can easily be targeted and forced to close down. Internet-based publishing systems can be run using ubiquitous and inexpensive equipment and can operate from any global jurisdiction. Nations and organisations are increasingly resorting to legal measures to take control of online publications, using national security, anti-terror measures and [copyright laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DMCA_takedown) to issue takedown notices and restrict opposition speech.

  [^cite_note-108]

- Internet, anonymity software and [strong cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_cryptography): In addition to Internet-based publishing, the Internet (in combination with anonymity software such as [Tor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tor_\(network\)) and [cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptography)) allows for [sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Source_\(journalism\)) to remain anonymous and sustain confidentiality while delivering information to or [securely communicating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secure_communication) with journalists anywhere in the world in an instant (e.g. [SecureDrop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SecureDrop), [WikiLeaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WikiLeaks)).

- [Voice over Internet protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VOIP) (VOIP) vs. conventional [telephony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephony): Although conventional telephony systems are easily tapped and recorded, modern VOIP technology can employ low-cost strong cryptography to evade surveillance. As VOIP and similar technologies become more widespread they are likely to make the effective monitoring of journalists (and their contacts and activities) a very difficult task for governments.

Governments are responding to the challenges posed by new media technologies by deploying increasingly sophisticated technology of their own (a notable example being China's attempts to impose control through a state-run [internet service provider](100245.md) that controls access to the Internet).
