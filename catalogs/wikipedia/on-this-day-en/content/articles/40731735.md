The **Indigenous peoples in Bolivia** or **Native Bolivians** ([Spanish](26825.md): *Bolivianos Nativos*) are [Bolivians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolivians) who have predominantly or total [Amerindian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_in_South_America) ancestry. They constitute anywhere from 38.7% to 62.05% of [Bolivia](3462.md)'s population,[^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6] depending on different estimates, and they belong to 36 recognized ethnic groups. [Aymara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aymara_people) and [Quechua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quechua_people) are the largest groups.[^cite_note-iwgia-7] The geography of Bolivia includes the [Andes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andes), the [Gran Chaco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gran_Chaco), the [Yungas](31357358.md), the [Chiquitania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiquitania) and the [Amazon rainforest](48139.md).

An additional 30–68% of the population is mestizo, having mixed European and Indigenous ancestry.[^cite_note-cia-8]

## Rights



The [Gateway of the Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gateway_of_the_Sun) at [Tiwanaku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiwanaku).

In 1991, the Bolivian government signed the [Indigenous and Tribal Peoples Convention, 1989](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_and_Tribal_Peoples_Convention,_1989), a major binding international convention protecting [Indigenous rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_rights). On 7 November 2007, the government passed Law No. 3760 which approved of [UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declaration_on_the_Rights_of_Indigenous_Peoples).[^cite_note-iwgia-7]

In 1993, the Law of Constitutional Reform recognized Indigenous rights.[^cite_note-\:2-12]

## Social protests and political mobilization

### Revolution of 1952

Historically Indigenous people in Bolivia suffered many years of marginalization and a lack of representation.[^cite_note-\:2-12] However, the late 20th century saw a surge of political and social mobilization in Indigenous communities.[^cite_note-\:2-12] The 1952 [Bolivian National Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolivian_National_Revolution) that liberated Bolivians and gave Indigenous peoples citizenship still gave little political representation to Indigenous communities.[^cite_note-\:2-12] It was in the 1960s and 1970s that social movements such as the [Kataraista](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katarismo) movement began to also include Indigenous concerns.[^cite_note-\:2-12] The Katarista movement, consisting of the Aymara communities of La Paz and the [Altiplano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altiplano), attempted to mobilize the Indigenous community and pursue an Indigenous political identity through mainstream politics and life.[^cite_note-\:4-13] Although the Katarista movement failed to create a national political party, the movement influenced many peasant unions such as the Confederación Sindical Única de Trabajadores Campesinos de Bolivia ([Unified Syndical Confederation of Peasant Workers of Bolivia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unified_Syndical_Confederation_of_Peasant_Workers_of_Bolivia)).[^cite_note-\:2-12] The Katarista movement of the 1970s and 1980s died out by the end of the decade; however, many of the same concerns rose again in the 1990s.[^cite_note-\:2-12]

### Social movement: 1990s and 2000s

The 1990s saw a large surge of political mobilization for Indigenous communities.[^cite_note-\:2-12] President [Sánchez de Lozada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%A1nchez_de_Lozada) passed reforms such as the 1993 Law of Constitutional Reform to acknowledge Indigenous rights in Bolivian culture and society. However, many of these reforms fell short as the government continued to pass destructive environmental and anti-indigenous rules and regulations.[^cite_note-\:2-12] A year after the 1993 Law of Constitutional Reform passed recognizing Indigenous rights, the 1994 Law of Popular Participation decentralized political structures, giving municipal and local governments more political autonomy.[^cite_note-\:2-12] Two years later the 1996 Electoral Law greater expanded Indigenous political rights as the national congress transitioned into a hybrid proportional system, increasing the number of Indigenous representatives.[^cite_note-\:2-12]

Environmental injustice became a polarizing issue as many Indigenous communities protested against government-backed privatization and eradication of natural resources and landscapes.[^cite_note-\:5-14] [Coca leaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coca_leaf) production is an important sector of the Bolivian economy and culture, especially for [*campesinos*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peasant#Latin_American_farmers) and Indigenous peoples.[^cite_note-\:6-15] The eradication of coca production, highly supported by the U.S. and its [war on drugs](1181646.md) and the Bolivian government spurred heavy protests by the Indigenous community.[^cite_note-\:5-14] One of the main leaders of the coca leaf movement, [Evo Morales](284163.md), became a vocal opponent against state efforts to eradicate coca. The coca leaf tensions began in the region of Chapare in 2000 and became violent as protests against police officials and residents began. During this time protestors organized road blockades, and traffic stops to protest low prices.[^cite_note-16] Coca leaf producers continued to resist the government's policies on production further devaluing the peso and seized control of the peasant confederation (Confederación Sindical Única de Trabajadores Campesinos de Bolivia).[^cite_note-\:0-17] With Evo Morales' leadership, the *cocaceleros* were able to form coalitions with other social groups and eventually create a political party, the [Movement Towards Socialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Movement_Towards_Socialism_\(Bolivia\)) (MAS).[^cite_note-\:6-15]

Similarly, the 2000 [Water War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cochabamba_Water_War) bought these protests to national attention.[^cite_note-\:7-18] The Water War began in the city of [Cochabamba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cochabamba), where the private company [Bechtel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bechtel) began to increase rates for water after the government contracted out to [privatize Cochabamba's water system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_privatization_in_Bolivia).[^cite_note-\:7-18] When Cochabamba's residents realized they could not afford to pay for this resource, they began to protest in alliance with urban workers, rural peasants and students.[^cite_note-\:0-17] The mass protest resulted in a state of emergency as clashes against the police and protestors became more violent.[^cite_note-\:4-13] The protests were largely successful and resulted in the reversal of the privatization.[^cite_note-\:4-13]

Additionally in 2003, as reliance on natural resources in Bolivia's economy grew, resistance came from Bolivia's Indigenous community in the form of the Gas Wars.[^cite_note-19] This conflict, which grew from the Water Wars, united coca farmers, unions and citizens to protest the sale of Bolivia's gas reserves to the United States through the port of Chile.[^cite_note-\:7-18] Again, Indigenous peoples participated alongside miners, teachers and ordinary citizens through road blockades and the disruption of traffic.[^cite_note-\:7-18] Political protests for social and economic reforms have been a consistent method for Indigenous mobilization and inclusion in the political process.[^cite_note-\:7-18] They have concluded in successful results and created a platform for Indigenous rights. The protest movements soon paved the way for legal and political changes and representation.

**Indigenous march in 2011**

In 2011 Bolivian Indigenous activists started a long protest march from the Amazon plains to the country's capital, against a government plan to build a 306 km (190 mi) highway through a national park in Indigenous territory.[^cite_note-20]

The subcentral [Isiboro Sécure National Park and Indigenous Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isiboro_S%C3%A9cure_National_Park_and_Indigenous_Territory) (TIPNIS), the [Confederation of Indigenous Peoples of Bolivia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederation_of_Indigenous_Peoples_of_Bolivia) (CIDOB), and the highland Indigenous confederation [National Council of Ayllus and Markas of Qullasuyu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Council_of_Ayllus_and_Markas_of_Qullasuyu) (CONAMAQ)—supported by other indigenous and environmental groups—organized a march from Trinidad, Beni, to the national capital La Paz in opposition to the project, beginning on 15 August 2011.[^cite_note-21]

"One of the latest tactics deployed by governments to bypass Indigenous contestation is to consult non-\[local] Indigenous communities. This happened to communities in the case of the road project through Bolivia's Isiboro Sécure National Park and Indigenous Territory (TIPNIS)." International pressure built up after Evo Morales' government violently repressed a large Indigenous march against a road project in "the massacre of Chaparina".[^cite_note-22]

This led to the Chaparina Massacre—on 25 September 2011, national police brutally repressed Indigenous marchers protesting the construction of a government-proposed highway through the TIPNIS Indigenous territory and national park.[^cite_note-23]

## Evo Morales and the plurinational state



[Evo Morales](284163.md) served as Bolivia's first indigenous [president](188806.md).

One of the biggest successes for Bolivia's Indigenous community was the election of [Evo Morales](284163.md), former leader of the *[cocaleros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocalero)* and Bolivia's first Indigenous [president](188806.md).[^cite_note-\:1-24] President Morales attempted to establish a [plurinational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plurinationalism) and postcolonial state to expand the collective rights of the Indigenous community.[^cite_note-\:8-25] The [2009 constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Bolivia) recognized the presence of the different communities that reside in Bolivia and gave Indigenous peoples the right of [self-governance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-governance) and autonomy over their [ancestral territories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancestral_domain).[^cite_note-\:8-25] Expanding on the Constitution, the 2010 Framework Law of Autonomies and Decentralization outlined the legal rules and procedures that Indigenous communities must take to receive autonomy.[^cite_note-\:1-24] Through these decentralization efforts, Bolivia became the first plurinational state in South America.[^cite_note-26] However, many Indigenous communities claim that the process to receive autonomy is inefficient and lengthy.[^cite_note-\:8-25] Along with Indigenous concerns, there are internal issues and competing interests between Bolivia's restrictive legal framework, liberal policies and the concept of Indigenous self-governance.[^cite_note-\:1-24] Nonetheless, the addition of subautonomies in Bolivia's government has made strides in including Indigenous communities in the political process.

## Achievements



Men of the Original Indigenous Territory of Raqaypampa.

In 2015 Bolivians made history again by selecting the first Indigenous president of the [Supreme Court of Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Justice_\(Bolivia\)), Justice Pastor [Cristina Mamani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cristina_Mamani?action=edit\&redlink=1). Mamani is a lawyer from the Bolivian highlands from the Aymara community.[^cite_note-Bolivia's_New_Faces_of_Justice-27] She won the election with the most votes.[^cite_note-Bolivia's_New_Faces_of_Justice-27] The Supreme Court of Justice is made up of nine members and nine alternative justices, each representing the nine departments in Bolivia.[^cite_note-Bolivia_-_The_Judiciary-28] The justices are elected in popular nonpartisan elections with terms of six years.[^cite_note-Bolivia_-_The_Judiciary-28]
