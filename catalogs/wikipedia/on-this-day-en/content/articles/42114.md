***Salmonella*** is a [genus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genus) of [rod-shaped](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacillus_\(shape\)) (bacillus), [gram-negative bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gram-negative_bacteria) of the [family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_\(taxonomy\)) [Enterobacteriaceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enterobacteriaceae). The two known [species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Species) of *Salmonella* are *[Salmonella enterica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmonella_enterica)* and *[Salmonella bongori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmonella_bongori)*. *S. enterica* is the [type species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_species) and is further divided into six [subspecies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subspecies)[^cite_note-Su-2] that include over 2,650 [serotypes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serotype).[^cite_note-3] *Salmonella* was named after [Daniel Elmer Salmon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Elmer_Salmon) (1850–1914), an American [veterinary surgeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veterinary_surgeon).

*Salmonella* species are non-[spore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endospore)-forming, predominantly [motile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motility) [enterobacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enterobacteriaceae) with cell diameters between about 0.7 and 1.5 [μm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micrometre), lengths from 2 to 5 μm, and peritrichous [flagella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flagella) (all around the cell body, allowing them to move).[^cite_note-Fabrega2013-4] They are [chemotrophs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemotrophs), obtaining their energy from [oxidation and reduction reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox), using organic sources. They are also [facultative anaerobes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facultative_aerobic_organism), capable of generating [adenosine triphosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate) with [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen) ("aerobically") when it is available, or using other [electron acceptors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_acceptor) or [fermentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermentation) ("anaerobically") when oxygen is not available.[^cite_note-Fabrega2013-4]

*Salmonella* species are [intracellular pathogens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intracellular_pathogen),[^cite_note-ReferenceB-5] of which certain serotypes cause illness such as [salmonellosis](1046292.md). Most infections are due to the ingestion of [food contaminated](531611.md) by feces. Typhoidal *Salmonella* serotypes can cause [foodborne illness](531611.md) as well as [typhoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhoid) and [paratyphoid fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paratyphoid_fever) and can be transferred only between humans.[^cite_note-6] Typhoid fever is caused by typhoidal *Salmonella* entering the bloodstream then spreading throughout the body, invading organs, and secreting [endotoxins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endotoxin) (the septic form). This infection can lead to life-threatening [hypovolemic shock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypovolemic_shock) and [septic shock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Septic_shock) and requires [intensive care](6332859.md) including [antibiotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibiotic).

Nontyphoidal *Salmonella* serotypes are [zoonotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoonotic) and can be transferred from animals and between humans. They usually invade only the [gastrointestinal tract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastrointestinal_tract) and cause salmonellosis, the symptoms of which can be resolved without antibiotics. However, in [sub-Saharan Africa](27067.md), nontyphoidal *Salmonella* can be invasive and cause paratyphoid fever, which requires immediate antibiotic treatment.[^cite_note-Sherris-7]

## Taxonomy

See also: [Salmonella enterica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmonella_enterica)

See also: [Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmonella_enterica_subsp._enterica)

The genus *[Salmonella](42114.md)* is part of the family of Enterobacteriaceae. Its taxonomy has been revised and has the potential to confuse. The genus comprises two species, *S. bongori* and *S. enterica*, the latter of which is divided into six subspecies: *S. e. enterica*, *S. e. salamae*, *S. e. arizonae*, *S. e. diarizonae*, *S. e. houtenae*, and *S. e. indica*.[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9] The taxonomic group contains more than 2500 [serotypes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serotype) (also serovars) defined on the basis of the somatic O ([lipopolysaccharide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipopolysaccharide)) and flagellar H antigens (the [Kauffman–White classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kauffman%E2%80%93White_classification)). The full name of a serotype is given as, for example, *Salmonella enterica* subsp. *enterica* serotype Typhimurium, but can be abbreviated to *Salmonella* Typhimurium. Further differentiation of strains to assist [clinical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinical_research) and [epidemiological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epidemiology) investigation may be achieved by [antibiotic sensitivity testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibiotic_sensitivity_testing) and by other [molecular biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_biology) techniques such as [pulsed-field gel electrophoresis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulsed-field_gel_electrophoresis), [multilocus sequence typing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multilocus_sequence_typing), and, increasingly, [whole genome sequencing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whole_genome_sequencing). Historically, salmonellae have been clinically categorized as invasive (typhoidal) or non-invasive (nontyphoidal salmonellae) based on host preference and disease manifestations in humans.[^cite_note-10]

## History

*Salmonella* was first visualized in 1880 by [Karl Eberth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Joseph_Eberth) in the [Peyer's patches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peyer's_patch) and [spleens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spleen) of typhoid patients.[^cite_note-11] Four years later, [Georg Theodor Gaffky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Theodor_August_Gaffky) was able to grow the pathogen in pure culture.[^cite_note-12] A year after that, medical research scientist [Theobald Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theobald_Smith) discovered what would be later known as *[Salmonella enterica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmonella_enterica)* (var. Choleraesuis). At the time, Smith was working as a research laboratory assistant in the Veterinary Division of the [United States Department of Agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Agriculture). The division was under the administration of [Daniel Elmer Salmon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Elmer_Salmon), a veterinary pathologist.[^cite_note-FDA-13] Initially, *Salmonella* Choleraesuis was thought to be the causative agent of [hog cholera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_swine_fever), so Salmon and Smith named it "Hog-cholera bacillus". The name *Salmonella* was not used until 1900, when [Joseph Leon Lignières](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Leon_Ligni%C3%A8res) proposed that the pathogen discovered by Salmon's group be called *Salmonella* in his honor.[^cite_note-Deadly_Diseases-14]<sup>: 16 </sup>

In the late 1930s, Australian [bacteriologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteriologist) [Nancy Atkinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nancy_Atkinson) established a salmonella typing laboratory – one of only three in the world at the time – at the [Government of South Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_South_Australia)'s [Laboratory of Pathology and Bacteriology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SA_Pathology) in [Adelaide](1148.md) (later the Institute of Medical and Veterinary Science). It was here that Atkinson described multiple new strains of salmonella, including *Salmonella* Adelaide, which was isolated in 1943. Atkinson published her work on salmonellas in 1957.[^cite_note-15]

## Serotyping

Serotyping is done by mixing cells with antibodies for a particular antigen. It can give some idea about risk. A 2014 study showed that *S.* Reading is very common among young [turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey_\(bird\)) samples, but it is not a significant contributor to human salmonellosis.[^cite_note-\:0-16] Serotyping can assist in identifying the source of contamination by matching serotypes in people with serotypes in the suspected source of infection.[^cite_note-\:1-17] Appropriate prophylactic treatment can be identified from the known antibiotic resistance of the serotype.[^cite_note-\:2-18]

Newer methods of "serotyping" include xMAP and [real-time PCR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-time_PCR), two methods based on DNA sequences instead of antibody reactions. These methods can be potentially faster, thanks to advances in sequencing technology. These "molecular serotyping" systems actually perform [genotyping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genotyping_by_sequencing) of the genes that determine surface antigens.[^cite_note-19][^cite_note-20]

## Detection, culture, and growth conditions



US [Food and Drug Administration](11632.md) scientist tests for presence of *Salmonella*

Most subspecies of *Salmonella* produce [hydrogen sulfide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_sulfide),[^cite_note-BarretClark-21] which can readily be detected by growing them on [media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Growth_medium) containing [ferrous sulfate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferrous_sulfate), such as is used in the [triple sugar iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TSI_slant) test. Most isolates exist in two phases, a motile phase and a non-motile phase. Cultures that are nonmotile upon primary culture may be switched to the motile phase using a [Craigie tube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Craigie_tube) or ditch plate.[^cite_note-Phase-22] [RVS broth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rappaport_Vassiliadis_soya_peptone_broth) can be used to enrich for *Salmonella* species for detection in a clinical sample.[^cite_note-Atlas-23]

*Salmonella* can also be detected and subtyped using [multiplex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplex_polymerase_chain_reaction)[^cite_note-24] or [real-time polymerase chain reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-time_polymerase_chain_reaction) (qPCR)[^cite_note-25] from extracted *Salmonella* DNA.

Mathematical models of *Salmonella* growth kinetics have been developed for chicken, pork, tomatoes, and melons.[^cite_note-26][^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28][^cite_note-29][^cite_note-30] *Salmonella* reproduce asexually with a cell division interval of 40 minutes.[^cite_note-Deadly_Diseases-14][^cite_note-\:0-16][^cite_note-\:1-17][^cite_note-\:2-18]

*Salmonella* species lead predominantly host-associated lifestyles, but the bacteria were found to be able to persist in a bathroom setting for weeks following contamination, and are frequently isolated from water sources, which act as bacterial reservoirs and may help to facilitate transmission between hosts.[^cite_note-31] *Salmonella* is notorious for its ability to survive desiccation and can persist for years in dry environments and foods.[^cite_note-32]

The bacteria are not destroyed by freezing,[^cite_note-33][^cite_note-34] but [UV light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraviolet_radiation) and heat accelerate their destruction. They perish after being heated to 55 °C (131 °F) for 90 min, or to 60 °C (140 °F) for 12 min,[^cite_note-35] although if inoculated in high fat, high liquid substances like peanut butter, they gain heat resistance and can survive up to 90 °C (194 °F) for 30 min.[^cite_note-36] To protect against *Salmonella* infection, heating food to an internal temperature of 75 °C (167 °F) is recommended.[^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38]

*Salmonella* species can be found in the digestive tracts of humans and animals, especially reptiles. *Salmonella* on the skin of reptiles or amphibians can be passed to people who handle the animals.[^cite_note-39] Food and water can also be contaminated with the bacteria if they come in contact with the feces of infected people or animals.[^cite_note-Barbara2003-40]

## Nomenclature

See also: [Salmonella enterica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmonella_enterica) and [Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmonella_enterica_subsp._enterica)

Initially, each *Salmonella* "species" was named according to clinical consideration, for example *Salmonella typhi-murium* (mouse-typhoid), *S. cholerae-suis* (pig-cholera). After host specificity was recognized not to exist for many species, new strains received species names according to the location at which the new strain was isolated.[^cite_note-41] These "species" are grouped into "subgenra" using biochemical characteristics by Kauffmann, forming four groups, I through IV.[^cite_note-Minor87-42]

In 1987, Le Minor and Popoff used molecular findings to argue that *Salmonella* consisted of only one species, *[S. enterica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmonella_enterica)*, turning former "species" names into [serotypes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serotypes). The species contains seven DNA groups, five of which correspond to Kauffmann's (III is split in two).[^cite_note-Minor87-42] In 1989, Reeves *et al.* proposed that the DNA group V should remain its own species, resurrecting the name *[S. bongori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmonella_bongori)*.[^cite_note-43] The current (by 2005) nomenclature has thus taken shape, with six recognised subspecies under *S. enterica*: *enterica* (I), *salamae* (II), *arizonae* (IIIa), *diarizonae* (IIIb), *houtenae* (IV), and *indica* (VI).[^cite_note-\:3-44][^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46][^cite_note-47]

The serotype or serovar is a classification of *Salmonella* based on antigens that the organism presents. The [Kauffman–White classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kauffman%E2%80%93White_classification) scheme differentiates serological varieties from each other, with the serotypes/serovars capitalized but not italicized: an example is *Salmonella* Typhimurium. This nomenclature can be combined with the species and subspecies, resulting in the full name *Salmonella enterica* subsp. *enterica* serovar Typhimurium. More modern approaches for typing and subtyping *Salmonella* include DNA-based methods such as [pulsed field gel electrophoresis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulsed_field_gel_electrophoresis), [multiple-loci VNTR analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_Loci_VNTR_Analysis), [multilocus sequence typing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multilocus_sequence_typing), and multiplex-[PCR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymerase_chain_reaction)-based methods. They largely agree with the Kauffman-White results, allowing old names to be reused.[^cite_note-PorwollikS-48][^cite_note-Achtman2012-49]

Many specialists in infectious disease are unfamiliar with the current *Salmonella* nomenclature, with journals needing to emphasize the correct taxonomy and orthography.[^cite_note-50]

### Non-*Salmonella*

In 2005, a third species, *Salmonella subterranea*, was proposed, but according to the [World Health Organization](33583.md), the bacterium reported does not belong in the genus *Salmonella*.[^cite_note-Pasteur-55] In 2016, *S. subterranea* was proposed to be assigned to *[Atlantibacter subterranea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantibacter_subterranea?action=edit\&redlink=1)*,[^cite_note-56] but LPSN rejects it as an [invalid publication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valid_publication), as it was made outside of IJSB and IJSEM.[^cite_note-57] [GTDB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GTDB) and NCBI agree with the 2016 reassignment.[^cite_note-58][^cite_note-59]

## Pathogenicity

*Salmonella* species are facultative [intracellular pathogens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intracellular_pathogen).[^cite_note-ReferenceB-5] *Salmonella* can invade different cell types, including [epithelial cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epithelial_cells), [M cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microfold_cell), [macrophages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macrophage), and [dendritic cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dendritic_cell).[^cite_note-60] As [facultative anaerobic organism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facultative_anaerobic_organism), *Salmonella* uses oxygen to make [adenosine triphosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate) (ATP) in aerobic environments (i.e., when oxygen is available). However, in anaerobic environments (i.e., when oxygen is not available) *Salmonella* can produce ATP by [anaerobic respiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaerobic_respiration) — that is, by using, instead of oxygen, one of four possible electron acceptors at the end of the electron transport chain: [sulfate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfate), [nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrate), [sulfur](27127.md), or [fumarate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fumarate) (all of which are less efficient acceptors than oxygen) — or by [fermentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermentation).[^cite_note-61]

Most infections are due to ingestion of food contaminated by animal feces, or by human feces (for example, from the hands of a food-service worker at a commercial eatery). *Salmonella* serotypes can be divided into two main groups—typhoidal and nontyphoidal. Typhoidal serotypes include *Salmonella* Typhi and *Salmonella* Paratyphi A, which are adapted to humans and do not occur in other animals. Nontyphoidal serotypes are more common, and usually cause self-limiting [gastrointestinal disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastrointestinal_disease). They can infect a range of animals, and are [zoonotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoonotic), meaning they can be transferred between humans and other animals.[^cite_note-62][^cite_note-63]

*Salmonella* pathogenicity and host interaction has been studied extensively since the 2010s. Most of the important virulent genes of *Salmonella* are encoded in five pathogenicity islands—the so-called *Salmonella* pathogenicity islands (SPIs). These are chromosomal encoded and make a significant contribution to bacterial-host interaction. More traits, like plasmids, flagella or [biofilm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biofilm)-related proteins, can contribute in the infection. SPIs are regulated by complex and fine-tuned regulatory networks that allow the gene expression only in the presence of the right environmental stresses.[^cite_note-Fabrega2013-4]

Molecular modeling and active site analysis of SdiA homolog, a putative quorum sensor for Salmonella typhimurium pathogenicity, reveals the specific binding patterns of AHL transcriptional regulators.[^cite_note-64] It is also known that Salmonella plasmid virulence gene spvB enhances bacterial virulence by inhibiting autophagy.[^cite_note-65]

## Typhoidal *Salmonella*

See also: [Typhoid fever](31596.md) and [Paratyphoid fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paratyphoid_fever)

Typhoid fever is caused by *Salmonella* serotypes which are strictly adapted to humans or higher primates—these include [*Salmonella* Typhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmonella_enterica_subsp._enterica#Serovars), Paratyphi A, Paratyphi B, and Paratyphi C. In the systemic form of the disease, salmonellae pass through the lymphatic system of the intestine into the blood of the patients (typhoid form) and are carried to various organs (liver, spleen, kidneys) to form secondary foci (septic form). Endotoxins first act on the vascular and nervous apparatus, resulting in increased permeability and decreased tone of the vessels, upset of thermal regulation, and vomiting and diarrhoea. In severe forms of the disease, enough liquid and electrolytes are lost to upset the water-salt metabolism, decrease the circulating blood volume and arterial pressure, and cause [hypovolemic shock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypovolemia). [Septic shock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Septicaemia) may also develop. Shock of mixed character (with signs of both hypovolemic and septic shock) is more common in severe [salmonellosis](1046292.md). [Oliguria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliguria) and [azotemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azotemia) may develop in severe cases as a result of renal involvement due to [hypoxia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypoxia_\(medical\)) and [toxemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteremia).

## Nontyphoidal *Salmonella*

See also: [Salmonellosis](1046292.md)

### Gastroenteritis

Infection with nontyphoidal serotypes of *Salmonella* generally results in gastroenteritis or what is commonly called [food poisoning](531611.md). Experimental infections of healthy adults suggest that Infection usually occurs when a person ingests a high concentration of the bacteria[^cite_note-66] but studies of outbreaks from contaminated food suggests that much lower doses can result in infection[^cite_note-67]. Infants, young children and the elderly are likely to be more susceptible to infection, easily achieved by ingesting a small number of bacteria, although direct evidence of this is not available. In infants, infection through inhalation of bacteria-laden dust is possible.[^cite_note-68]

The organisms enter through the digestive tract. Infection is initiated after Salmonella reach the gastrointestinal tract. Gastric acidity is responsible for the destruction of the majority of ingested bacteria, but *Salmonella* has evolved a degree of tolerance to acidic environments that allows a subset of ingested bacteria to survive.[^cite_note-69] Some of the microorganisms are killed in the stomach, while the surviving ones enter the small intestine, invade cells of the epithelium and multiply within the cells in tissues. Salmonella triggers a strong immune response through a number of mechanisms that leads to inflammatory diarrhoea typical of gastroenteritis[^cite_note-70]. The inflammatory response also results in changes to the good bacteria resident in the gut lumen, that favours outgrowth of the *Salmonella* at this location[^cite_note-71]. Consequently, a combination of high numbers of *Salmonella* in the faeces and diarrhoea contributes to transmission via contamination of the environment.&#x20;

About 2,000 serotypes of nontyphoidal *Salmonella* are known, which may be responsible for as many as 1.4 million illnesses in the United States each year. People who are at risk for severe illness include infants, elderly, organ-transplant recipients, and the immunocompromised.[^cite_note-Barbara2003-40]

### Disseminated disease (or invasive nontyphoidal *Salmonella* disease, iNTS)



Child deaths from invasive non typhoidal salmonella

While, in developed countries, non-typhoidal serotypes present mostly as gastrointestinal disease, in sub-Saharan Africa, these serotypes can create a major problem in bloodstream infections, and are the most commonly isolated bacteria from the blood of those presenting with fever. Bloodstream infections caused by nontyphoidal salmonellae in Africa are normally associated with co-morbidities including Malaria, HIV and malnutrition, and were reported in 2012 to have a [case fatality rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_fatality_rate) of 20–25%. Most cases of invasive nontyphoidal *Salmonella* infection (iNTS) are caused by *Salmonella enterica* Typhimurium or *Salmonella enterica* Enteritidis. A new form of *Salmonella* Typhimurium (ST313, lineage I) emerged in the southeast of the African continent 75 years ago, followed by a second wave (ST313, lineage II) which came out of central Africa 18 years later[^cite_note-72]. This second wave of iNTS possibly originated in the [Congo Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congo_Basin), was characterized by changes in its genome sequence called genome degradation that is typical of host-adapted and host restricted serotypes such as *Salmonella enterica* serotype Typhi. Spread of the second wave in the early part of the 21st century was coincided with acquisition of a gene that made it resistant to the antibiotic [chloramphenicol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloramphenicol)[^cite_note-73]. This created the need to use expensive antimicrobial drugs in areas of Africa that were very poor, making treatment difficult. The increased prevalence of iNTS in sub-Saharan Africa compared to other regions is thought to be due to the large proportion of the African population with some degree of immune suppression or impairment due to the burden of [HIV](14170.md), [malaria](20423.md), and malnutrition, especially in children. The genetic makeup of iNTS is evolving into a more typhoid-like bacterium, able to efficiently spread around the human body. Symptoms are reported to be diverse, including fever, [hepatosplenomegaly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepatosplenomegaly), and respiratory symptoms, often with an absence of gastrointestinal symptoms.[^cite_note-74]

### Epidemiology

Due to being considered sporadic, between 60% and 80% of salmonella infections cases go undiagnosed.[^cite_note-75] In March 2010, data analysis was completed to estimate an [incidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incidence_\(epidemiology\)) rate of 1140 per 100,000 person-years. In the same analysis, 93.8 million cases of [gastroenteritis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastroenteritis) were due to salmonella infections. At the 5th percentile the estimated amount was 61.8 million cases and at the 95th percentile the estimated amount was 131.6 million cases. The estimated number of deaths due to salmonella was approximately 155,000 deaths.[^cite_note-76] In 2014, in countries such as Bulgaria and Portugal, children under 4 were 32 and 82 times more likely, respectively, to have a salmonella infection.[^cite_note-77] Those who are most susceptible to infection are: children, pregnant women, elderly people, and those with deficient immune systems.[^cite_note-78]

Risk factors for Salmonella infections include a variety of foods. Meats such as chicken and pork have the possibility to be contaminated. A variety of vegetables and sprouts may also have salmonella. Lastly, a variety of processed foods such as chicken nuggets and pot pies may also contain this bacteria.[^cite_note-79]

Successful forms of prevention come from existing entities such as the [FDA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FDA), [United States Department of Agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Agriculture), and the [Food Safety and Inspection Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_Safety_and_Inspection_Service). All of these organizations create standards and inspections to ensure public safety in the [U.S.](3434750.md) For example, the [FSIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FSIS) agency working with the USDA has a Salmonella Action Plan in place. Recently, it received a two-year plan update in February 2016. Their accomplishments and strategies to reduce Salmonella infection are presented in the plans.[^cite_note-80] The [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention](6811.md) also provides valuable information on preventative care, such has how to safely handle raw foods, and the correct way to store these products. In the [European Union](9317.md), the [European Food Safety Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Food_Safety_Authority) created preventative measures through risk management and risk assessment. From 2005 to 2009, the EFSA placed an approach to reduce exposure to *Salmonella*. Their approach included risk assessment and risk management of poultry, which resulted in a reduction of infection cases by one half.[^cite_note-81] In [Latin America](18524.md) an orally administered vaccine for Salmonella in poultry developed by Dr. Sherry Layton has been introduced which prevents the bacteria from contaminating the birds.[^cite_note-82]

A *Salmonella* Typhimurium outbreak in 2022 was linked to chocolate produced in Belgium, leading to the country temporarily halting Kinder chocolate production.[^cite_note-83][^cite_note-84]

In Germany, food-borne infections must be reported.[^cite_note-85] From 1990 to 2016, the number of officially recorded cases decreased from about 200,000 to about 13,000 cases.[^cite_note-86] In the United States, about 1,200,000 cases of *Salmonella* infection are estimated to occur each year.[^cite_note-87] A World Health Organization study estimated that 21,650,974 cases of typhoid fever occurred in 2000, 216,510 of which resulted in death, along with 5,412,744 cases of paratyphoid fever.[^cite_note-burden-88]

## Molecular mechanisms of infection

The mechanisms of infection differ between typhoidal and nontyphoidal serotypes, owing to their different targets in the body and the different symptoms that they cause. Both groups must enter by crossing the barrier created by the intestinal cell wall, but once they have passed this barrier, they use different strategies to cause infection.

### Switch to virulence

While travelling to their target tissue in the gastrointestinal tract, *Salmonella* is exposed to stomach acid, to the detergent-like activity of bile in the intestine, to decreasing oxygen supply, to the competing normal gut flora, and finally to antimicrobial peptides present on the surface of the cells lining the intestinal wall. All of these form stresses that *Salmonella* can sense and reacts against, and they form [virulence factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virulence_factor) and as such regulate the switch from their normal growth in the intestine into [virulence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virulence).[^cite_note-89]

The switch to virulence gives access to a replication [niche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niche_differentiation) inside the host (such as humans), and can be summarised into several stages:

1. Approach, in which they travel towards a host cell via intestinal [peristalsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peristalsis) and through active swimming via the [flagella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flagellum), penetrate the mucus barrier, and locate themselves close to the [epithelium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epithelium) lining the intestine,

2. Adhesion, in which they adhere to a host cell using bacterial [adhesins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacterial_adhesin) and a [type III secretion system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_III_secretion_system),

3. Invasion, in which *Salmonella* enter the host cell (see variant mechanisms below),

4. Replication, in which the bacterium may reproduce inside the host cell,

5. Spread, in which the bacterium can spread to other organs via cells in the blood (if it succeeded in avoiding the immune defence). Alternatively, bacteria can go back towards the intestine, re-seeding the intestinal population.

6. Re-invasion (a *secondary infection*, if now at a systemic site) and further replication.

### Mechanisms of entry

Nontyphoidal serotypes preferentially enter [M cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microfold_cell) on the intestinal wall by bacterial-mediated [endocytosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endocytosis), a process associated with intestinal inflammation and diarrhoea. They are also able to disrupt [tight junctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tight_junction) between the cells of the intestinal wall, impairing the cells' ability to stop the flow of [ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ions), water, and immune cells into and out of the intestine. The combination of the inflammation caused by bacterial-mediated endocytosis and the disruption of tight junctions is thought to contribute significantly to the induction of diarrhoea.[^cite_note-Haraga-90]

Salmonellae are also able to breach the intestinal barrier via [phagocytosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phagocytosis) and trafficking by [CD18](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CD18)-positive immune cells, which may be a mechanism key to typhoidal *Salmonella* infection. This is thought to be a more stealthy way of passing the intestinal barrier, and may, therefore, contribute to the fact that lower numbers of typhoidal *Salmonella* are required for infection than nontyphoidal *Salmonella*.[^cite_note-Haraga-90] *Salmonella* cells are able to enter [macrophages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macrophage) via [macropinocytosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macropinosome).[^cite_note-91] Typhoidal serotypes can use this to achieve dissemination throughout the body via the [mononuclear phagocyte system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mononuclear_phagocyte_system), a network of connective tissue that contains immune cells, and surrounds tissue associated with the immune system throughout the body.[^cite_note-Haraga-90]

Much of the success of *Salmonella* in causing infection is attributed to two [type III secretion systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_III_secretion_system) (T3SS) which are expressed at different times during the infection. The T3SS-1 enables the injection of bacterial effectors within the host cytosol. These T3SS-1 effectors stimulate the formation of membrane ruffles allowing the uptake of *Salmonella* by [nonphagocytic cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phagocytic_cell). *Salmonella* further resides within a membrane-bound compartment called the *Salmonella*-Containing Vacuole (SCV). The acidification of the SCV leads to the expression of the T3SS-2. The secretion of T3SS-2 effectors by *Salmonella* is required for its efficient survival in the host cytosol and establishment of systemic disease.[^cite_note-Haraga-90] In addition, both T3SS are involved in the colonization of the intestine, induction of intestinal inflammatory responses and diarrhea. These systems contain many genes which must work cooperatively to achieve infection.

The AvrA toxin injected by the SPI1 type III secretion system of *S.* Typhimurium works to inhibit the [innate immune system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Innate_immune_system) by virtue of its [serine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serine)/[threonine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threonine) [acetyltransferase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetyltransferase) activity, and requires binding to [eukaryotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eukaryotic_cell) target cell [phytic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phytic_acid) (IP6).[^cite_note-PMID20430892-92] This leaves the host more susceptible to infection.[^cite_note-93]

### Clinical symptoms

Salmonellosis is known to be able to cause [back pain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Back_pain) or [spondylosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spondylosis). It can manifest as five clinical patterns: gastrointestinal tract infection, enteric fever, bacteremia, local infection, and the chronic reservoir state. The initial symptoms are nonspecific fever, weakness, and myalgia among others. In the bacteremia state, it can spread to any parts of the body and this induces localized infection or it forms abscesses. The forms of localized *Salmonella* infections are arthritis, urinary tract infection, infection of the central nervous system, bone infection, soft tissue infection, etc.[^cite_note-salspond-94] Infection may remain as the latent form for a long time, and when the function of [reticular endothelial cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mononuclear_phagocyte_system) is deteriorated, it may become activated and consequently, it may secondarily induce spreading infection in the bone several months or several years after acute salmonellosis.[^cite_note-salspond-94]

A 2018 [Imperial College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_College_London) study also shows how salmonella disrupt specific arms of the [immune system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immune_system) (e.g. 3 of 5 [NF-kappaB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NF-kappaB) proteins) using a family of [zinc metalloproteinase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinc_metalloproteinase) effectors, leaving others untouched.[^cite_note-95] Salmonella thyroid abscess has also been reported.[^cite_note-96]

## Resistance to oxidative burst

A hallmark of *Salmonella* pathogenesis is the ability of the bacterium to survive and proliferate within [phagocytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phagocyte). Phagocytes produce DNA-damaging agents such as [nitric oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitric_oxide) and oxygen [radicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_\(chemistry\)) as a defense against pathogens. Thus, *Salmonella* species must face attack by molecules that challenge genome integrity. Buchmeie&#x72;*&#x20;et al.*[^cite_note-Buchmeier-97] showed that mutants of *S. enterica* lacking RecA or RecBC protein function are highly sensitive to oxidative compounds synthesized by macrophages, and furthermore these findings indicate that successful systemic infection by *S. enterica* requires RecA- and RecBC-mediated recombinational repair of DNA damage.[^cite_note-Buchmeier-97][^cite_note-pmid11751841-98]

## Host adaptation

*S. enterica*, through some of its serotypes such as Typhimurium and Enteritidis, shows signs that it has the ability to infect several different mammalian host species, while other serotypes, such as Typhi, seem to be restricted to only a few hosts.[^cite_note-99] Two ways that *Salmonella* serotypes have [adapted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Host_adaptation) to their hosts are by the loss of genetic material, and mutation. In more complex mammalian species, [immune systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immune_system), which include pathogen specific immune responses, target serovars of *Salmonella* by binding antibodies to structures such as flagella. Thus *Salmonella* that has lost the genetic material which codes for a flagellum to form can evade a host's [immune system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immune_system).[^cite_note-100] *mgtC* [leader RNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_prime_untranslated_region) from bacteria virulence gene (mgtCBR operon) decreases flagellin production during infection by directly base pairing with mRNAs of the *fljB* gene encoding flagellin and promotes degradation.[^cite_note-101] In the study by Kisela *et al.*, more pathogenic serovars of *S. enterica* were found to have certain adhesins in common that have developed out of convergent evolution.[^cite_note-102] This means that, as these strains of *Salmonella* have been exposed to similar conditions such as immune systems, similar structures evolved separately to negate these similar, more advanced defenses in hosts. Although many questions remain about how *Salmonella* has evolved into so many different types, *Salmonella* may have evolved through several phases. For example, as Baumler *et al.* have suggested, *Salmonella* most likely evolved through [horizontal gene transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_gene_transfer), and through the formation of new serovars due to additional [pathogenicity islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathogenicity_island), and through an approximation of its ancestry.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-103] So, *Salmonella* could have evolved into its many different serotypes by gaining genetic information from different pathogenic bacteria. The presence of several [pathogenicity islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathogenicity_island) in the genome of different serotypes has lent credence to this theory.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-103]

*Salmonella* sv. Newport shows signs of adaptation to a plant-colonization lifestyle, which may play a role in its disproportionate association with food-borne illness linked to produce. A variety of functions selected for during sv. Newport persistence in tomatoes have been reported to be similar to those selected for in sv. Typhimurium from animal hosts.[^cite_note-Moraes_MH_2018-104] The *papA* gene, which is unique to sv. Newport, contributes to the strain's fitness in tomatoes, and has homologs in the genomes of other Enterobacteriaceae that are able to colonize plant and animal hosts.[^cite_note-Moraes_MH_2018-104]

## Research

In addition to their importance as pathogens, nontyphoidal Salmonella species such as *S. enterica* serovar Typhimurium are commonly used as [homologues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_\(biology\)) of typhoid species. Many findings are transferable and it attenuates the danger for the researcher in case of contamination, but is also limited. For example, it is not possible to study specific typhoidal toxins using this model.[^cite_note-105] However, strong research tools such as the commonly used mouse intestine [gastroenteritis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastroenteritis) model build upon the use of *Salmonella* Typhimurium.[^cite_note-106]

For [genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetics), *S.* Typhimurium has been instrumental in the development of genetic tools that led to an understanding of fundamental bacterial physiology. These developments were enabled by the discovery of the first generalized transducing phage P22[^cite_note-107] in *S*. Typhimurium, that allowed quick and easy [genetic editing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_editing). In turn, this made fine structure genetic analysis possible. The large number of mutants led to a revision of genetic nomenclature for bacteria.[^cite_note-108] Many of the uses of transposons as genetic tools, including transposon delivery, mutagenesis, and construction of chromosome rearrangements, were also developed in *S*. Typhimurium. These genetic tools also led to a simple test for carcinogens, the Ames test.[^cite_note-109]

As a natural alternative to traditional antimicrobials, phages are being recognised as highly effective control agents for Salmonella and other foodborne bacteria.[^cite_note-110]

## Ancient DNA

*S. enterica* genomes have been reconstructed from up to 6,500 year old human remains across Western Eurasia, which provides evidence for geographic widespread infections with systemic *S. enterica* during prehistory, and a possible role of the Neolithization process in the evolution of host adaptation.[^cite_note-111][^cite_note-112] Additional reconstructed genomes from colonial Mexico suggest *S. enterica* as the cause of *[cocoliztli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocoliztli)*, an epidemic in 16th-century [New Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Spain).[^cite_note-113]
