The **Acropolis of Athens** ([Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language): Ἡ Ἀκρόπολις τῶν Ἀθηνῶν, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Ancient_Greek): *_Hē Akropolis tōn Athēnōn_*; [Modern Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Greek): Η Ακρόπολη των Αθηνών, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Greek): *I Akrópoli ton Athinón*) is an ancient [citadel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citadel) located on a rocky [outcrop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outcrop) above the city of [Athens](1216.md), Greece, and contains the remains of several [ancient buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_architecture) of great architectural and historical significance, the most famous being the [Parthenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenon). The word *[Acropolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acropolis)* is from [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language)&#x20;*&#x20;*&#x1F04;κρο&#x3BD;*&#x20;(akron)* 'highest point, extremity' and&#x20;*&#x20;*&#x3C0;όλι&#x3C2;*&#x20;(polis)* 'city'.[^cite_note-1] The term acropolis is generic and there are many other acropoleis in [Greece](12108.md). During ancient times the Acropolis of Athens was also more properly known as **Cecropia** (Κεκροπία), after the legendary serpent-man [Cecrops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecrops_I), the supposed first Athenian king.

While there is evidence that the hill was inhabited as early as the 4th millennium BC, it was [Pericles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pericles) (c. 495–429 BC) in the fifth century BC who coordinated the construction of the buildings whose present remains are the site's most important ones, including the Parthenon, the [Propylaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propylaia_\(Acropolis_of_Athens\)), the [Erechtheion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erechtheion) and the [Temple of Athena Nike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Athena_Nike).[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3] The Parthenon and the other buildings were seriously damaged during the 1687 siege by the [Venetians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Venice) during the [Morean War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morean_War) when gunpowder being stored by the Turkish rulers in the Parthenon was hit by a Venetian bombardment and exploded.[^cite_note-4]

### Early settlement

The Acropolis is located on a flattish-topped rock that rises 150 m (490 ft) above sea level in the city of [Athens](1216.md), with a surface area of about 3 ha (7.4 acres). While the earliest artifacts date to the Middle [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic) era, there have been documented habitations in [Attica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attica) from the Early Neolithic period (6th millennium BC).

There can be little doubt that a [Mycenaean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycenaean_Greece) [megaron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megaron) palace stood upon the hill during the late [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegean_civilization). Nothing of this structure survives except, probably, a single limestone column base and pieces of several sandstone steps.[^cite_note-Castleden-5] Soon after the palace was constructed, a massive [Cyclopean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclopean) circuit wall was built, 760 m (2,490 ft) long, up to 10 m (32 ft 10 in) high, and ranging 3.5 m (11 ft 6 in) to 6 m (19 ft 8 in) thick. From the end of the [Late Helladic IIIB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helladic_chronology) (1300–1200 BC) on,[^cite_note-6] this wall would serve as the main defense for the acropolis until the 5th century.[^cite_note-7] The wall consisted of two [parapets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parapet) built with large stone blocks and cemented with an earth mortar called *[emplekton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Core-and-veneer)* (Greek: ἔμπλεκτον).[^cite_note-8] The wall uses typical Mycenaean conventions in that it followed the natural contour of the terrain and its gate, which was towards the south, was arranged obliquely, with a parapet and tower overhanging the incomers' right-hand side, thus facilitating defense. There were two lesser approaches up the hill on its north side, consisting of steep, narrow flights of steps cut in the rock. [Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer) is assumed to refer to this fortification when he mentions the "strong-built house of [Erechtheus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erechtheus)" (*[Odyssey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odyssey)* 7.81). At some time before the 13th century BC, an earthquake caused a fissure near the northeastern edge of the Acropolis. This fissure extended some 35 m (115 ft) to a bed of soft marl in which a well was dug.[^cite_note-9] An elaborate set of stairs was built and the well served as an invaluable, protected source of drinking water during times of siege for some portion of the [Mycenaean period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycenaean_Greece).[^cite_note-10]

### Archaic Acropolis

"Temple of Athena Polias" redirects here. For the temple in Priene, see [Temple of Athena Polias (Priene)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Athena_Polias_\(Priene\)).



Primitive Acropolis with the Pelargicon and the Old Temple of Athena.



Elevation view of a proposed reconstruction of the Old Temple of Athena. Built around 525 BC, it stood between the Parthenon and the [Erechtheum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erechtheum). Fragments of the sculptures in its pediments are in the Acropolis Museum.

Little is known about the architectural appearance of the Acropolis before the [Archaic era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaic_Greece). During the 7th and the 6th centuries BC, the site was controlled by [Kylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylon_\(ancient_Athenian\)) during the failed Kylonian revolt,[^cite_note-Pomeroy1999-11] and twice by [Peisistratos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peisistratos); each of these was an attempt directed at seizing political power by *[coups d'état](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coup)*. Apart from the [Hekatompedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hekatompedon_temple) mentioned later, Peisistratos also built an entry gate or [propylaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propylaea).[^cite_note-12] Nevertheless, it seems that a nine-gate wall, the [Enneapylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enneapylon),[^cite_note-13] had been built around the acropolis hill and incorporated the biggest water spring, the [Clepsydra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klepsydra_\(Akropolis\)), at the northwestern foot.

A temple to [Athena Polias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athena), the [tutelary deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tutelary_deity) of the city, was erected between 570 and 550 BC. This [Doric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doric_order) limestone building, from which many relics survive, is referred to as the Hekatompedon (Greek for "hundred–footed"), Ur-Parthenon (German for "original Parthenon" or "primitive Parthenon"), H–Architecture or Bluebeard temple, after the pedimental three-bodied man-serpent sculpture, whose beards were painted dark blue. Whether this temple replaced an older one or just a sacred precinct or altar is not known. Probably, the Hekatompedon was built where the Parthenon now stands.[^cite_note-14]



[Destruction of the Acropolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_destruction_of_Athens) by the armies of [Xerxes I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerxes_I), during the [Second Persian invasion of Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Persian_invasion_of_Greece), 480–479 BC

Between 529 and 520 BC yet another temple was built by the [Pisistratids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisistratus), the [Old Temple of Athena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Temple_of_Athena), usually referred to as the Arkhaios Neōs (ἀρχαῖος νεώς, "ancient temple"). This temple of Athena Polias was built upon the [Dörpfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_D%C3%B6rpfeld) foundations,[^cite_note-15] between the [Erechtheion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erechtheion) and the still-standing Parthenon. The Arkhaios Neōs was destroyed as part of the [Achaemenid destruction of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_destruction_of_Athens) during the [Second Persian invasion of Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Persian_invasion_of_Greece) during 480–479 BC; however, the temple was probably reconstructed during 454 BC, since the treasury of the [Delian League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delian_League) was transferred in its [opisthodomos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opisthodomos). The temple may have been burnt down during 406/405 BC as [Xenophon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenophon) mentions that the old temple of Athena was set afire. [Pausanias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pausanias_\(geographer\)) does not mention it in his 2nd century AD *Description of Greece*.[^cite_note-16]

Around 500 BC the Hekatompedon was dismantled to make place for a new grander building, the [Older Parthenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Older_Parthenon) (often referred to as the Pre-Parthenon or Early Parthenon). For this reason, Athenians decided to stop the construction of the [Olympieion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Olympian_Zeus,_Athens) temple which was connoted with the tyrant Peisistratos and his sons, and, instead, used the [Piraeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piraeus) limestone destined for the Olympieion to build the Older Parthenon. To accommodate the new temple, the south part of the summit was cleared, made level by adding some 8,000 two-ton blocks of limestone, a foundation 11 m (36 ft) deep at some points, and the rest was filled with soil kept in place by the retaining wall. However, after the victorious [Battle of Marathon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Marathon) in 490 BC, the plan was revised and marble was used instead. The limestone phase of the building is referred to as Pre-Parthenon I and the marble phase as Pre-Parthenon II. In 485 BC, construction stalled to save resources as [Xerxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerxes_I_of_Persia) became king of Persia, and war seemed imminent.[^cite_note-17]

The Older Parthenon was still under construction when the Persians invaded and sacked the city in 480 BC. The building was burned and looted, along with the Ancient Temple and practically everything else on the rock.[^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19] After the Persian crisis had subsided, the Athenians incorporated many architectural parts of the unfinished temple (unfluted column drums, triglyphs, metopes, etc.) into the newly built northern [curtain wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtain_wall_\(fortification\)) of the Acropolis, where they served as a prominent "war memorial" and can still be seen today. The devastated site was cleared of debris. Statuary, cult objects, religious offerings, and unsalvageable architectural members were buried ceremoniously in several deeply dug pits on the hill, serving conveniently as a fill for the artificial plateau created around the Classical Parthenon. This "[Persian debris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perserschutt)" was the richest archaeological deposit excavated on the Acropolis by 1890.[^cite_note-20]

### The Periclean building program

After winning at [Eurymedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Eurymedon) during 468 BC, [Cimon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cimon) and [Themistocles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Themistocles) ordered the reconstruction of the southern and northern walls of the Acropolis. Most of the major temples, including the [Parthenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenon), were rebuilt by order of [Pericles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pericles) during the so-called [Golden Age of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Pericles) (460–430 BC). [Phidias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phidias), an Athenian sculptor, and [Ictinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ictinus) and [Callicrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Callicrates), two famous architects, were responsible for the reconstruction.[^cite_note-21]



The [Parthenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenon), as seen from the north-west in 1978

During 437 BC, [Mnesicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mnesicles) started building the [Propylaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propylaea_\(Acropolis_of_Athens\)), a monumental gate at the western end of the Acropolis with [Doric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doric_order) columns of [Pentelic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentelic) marble, built partly upon the old Propylaea of Peisistratos.[^cite_note-22] These colonnades were almost finished during 432 BC and had two wings, the northern one decorated with paintings by [Polygnotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygnotus).[^cite_note-McCulloch1841-23] About the same time, south of the Propylaea, building started on the small Ionic [Temple of Athena Nike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Athena_Nike) in Pentelic [marble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marble) with [tetrastyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrastyle) porches, preserving the essentials of Greek temple design. After an interruption caused by the [Peloponnesian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peloponnesian_War), the temple was finished during the time of [Nicias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicias)' peace, between 421 BC and 409 BC.[^cite_note-Mark1993-24]



The [Erechtheion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erechtheion), viewed from the south-west, looking across the remains of the Old Temple in 2015

Construction of the elegant temple of [Erechtheion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erechtheion) in Pentelic marble (421–406 BC) was by a complex plan which took account of the extremely uneven ground and the need to circumvent several shrines in the area. The entrance, facing east, is lined with six Ionic columns. Unusually, the temple has two porches, one on the northwest corner borne by Ionic columns, the other, to the southwest, supported by huge female figures or [caryatids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caryatid). The eastern part of the temple was dedicated to [Athena Polias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athena_Polias), while the western part, serving the cult of the archaic king [Poseidon-Erechtheus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erechtheus), housed the altars of [Hephaestus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hephaestus) and Voutos, brother of Erechtheus. Little is known about the original plan of the interior, which was destroyed by fire during the first century BC and has been rebuilt several times.[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26]

During the same period, a combination of sacred precincts including the temples of Athena Polias, [Poseidon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poseidon), [Erechtheus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erechtheus), [Cecrops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecrops_I), [Herse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herse), [Pandrosos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pandrosos) and [Aglauros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aglauros), with its Kore Porch (Porch of the Maidens) or Caryatids' Balcony was begun.[^cite_note-27] Between the temple of Athena Nike and the Parthenon, there was the [Sanctuary of Artemis Brauronia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brauroneion) (or the Brauroneion), the goddess represented as a bear and worshipped in the [deme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deme) of Brauron. According to Pausanias, a wooden statue or *[xoanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xoanon)* of the goddess and a statue of [Artemis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis) made by [Praxiteles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Praxiteles) during the 4th century BC were both in the sanctuary.[^cite_note-28]



[The Propylaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propylaea_\(Acropolis_of_Athens\)) in 2005

Behind the Propylaea, Phidias' gigantic bronze statue of [Athena Promachos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athena_Promachos) ("Athena who fights in the front line"), built between 450 BC and 448 BC, dominated. The base was 1.50 m (4 ft 11 in) high, while the total height of the statue was 9 m (30 ft). The goddess held a lance, the gilt tip of which could be seen as a reflection by crews on ships rounding Cape [Sounion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sounion), and a giant shield on the left side, decorated by [Mys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mys_\(artist\)?action=edit\&redlink=1) with images of the fight between the [Centaurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centaur) and the [Lapiths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapiths).[^cite_note-29] Other monuments that have left almost nothing visible to the present day are the [Chalkotheke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalkotheke), the [Pandroseion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pandroseion), [Pandion's sanctuary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pandion's_sanctuary), Athena's altar, Zeus Polieus's sanctuary and, from Roman times, the circular [Temple of Roma and Augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Roma_and_Augustus).[^cite_note-30]

### Hellenistic and Roman Period



3-D model of the Acropolis in 165 AD (click to rotate)

During the Hellenistic and Roman periods, many of the existing buildings in the area of the Acropolis were repaired to remedy damage from age and occasionally war.[^cite_note-autogenerated54-31] Monuments to foreign kings were erected, notably those of the [Attalid kings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attalid) of Pergamon Attalos II (in front of the NW corner of the Parthenon), and Eumenes II, in front of the Propylaea. These were rededicated during the early [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Athens) to Augustus or Claudius (uncertain) and [Agrippa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Vipsanius_Agrippa), respectively.[^cite_note-32] Eumenes was also responsible for constructing a [stoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoa_of_Eumenes) on the south slope, similar to that of [Attalos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoa_of_Attalos) in the [agora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenian_agora) below.[^cite_note-33]

During the [Julio-Claudian period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julio-Claudian_dynasty), the Temple of Roma and Augustus, a small, round edifice about 23 meters from the Parthenon, was to be the last significant ancient construction on the summit of the rock.[^cite_note-34] Around the same time, on the north slope, in a [cave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cave_Sanctuaries_of_the_Akropolis) next to the one dedicated to [Pan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_\(god\)) since the Classical period, a sanctuary was founded where the [archons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archons#Ancient_Greece) dedicated to [Apollo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo) on assuming office.[^cite_note-35] During 161 AD, on the south slope, the Roman [Herodes Atticus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodes_Atticus) built his grand amphitheater or [odeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odeon_of_Herodes_Atticus). It was [destroyed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sack_of_Athens_\(267_AD\)) by the invading [Herulians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herulian) a century later but was reconstructed during the 1950s.[^cite_note-Steves2011-36]

During the 3rd century, under threat from a Herulian invasion, repairs were made to the Acropolis walls, and the [Beulé Gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beul%C3%A9_Gate) was constructed to restrict entrance in front of the Propylaea, thus returning the Acropolis to use as a fortress.[^cite_note-autogenerated54-31]

### Byzantine, Latin, and Ottoman Period



Depiction of the Venetian siege of the Acropolis of Athens during 1687.

During the [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine) period, the Parthenon was used as a church dedicated to the [Virgin Mary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgin_Mary).[^cite_note-37] During the Latin [Duchy of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Athens), the Acropolis functioned as the city's administrative center, with the Parthenon as its cathedral, and the Propylaea as part of the ducal palace.[^cite_note-38] A large tower was added, the [Frankopyrgos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankish_Tower_\(Acropolis_of_Athens\)) (Frankish Tower), demolished during the 19th century.[^cite_note-Neils2005-39]

After the [Ottoman conquest of Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Greece), the Propylaea were used as the garrison headquarters of the Turkish army,[^cite_note-40] the Parthenon was converted into a [mosque](19894.md) and the [Erechtheum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erechtheum) was turned into the [governor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governor)'s private [harem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harem). The buildings of the Acropolis suffered significant damage during the 1687 siege by the Venetians in the [Morean War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morean_War). The Parthenon, which was being used as a gunpowder [magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magazine_\(artillery\)), was hit by [artillery](2508.md) and damaged severely.[^cite_note-41]



1842 [daguerreotype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daguerreotype) by [Joseph-Philibert Girault de Prangey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph-Philibert_Girault_de_Prangey) (earliest known photo of the site)



Idealized reconstruction of the Acropolis and [Areios Pagos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Areios_Pagos) in Athens, [Leo von Klenze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_von_Klenze), 1846.

During subsequent years, the Acropolis was a site of bustling human activity with many Byzantine, Frankish, and Ottoman structures. The dominant feature during the Ottoman period was a [mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenon_mosque) inside the Parthenon, complete with a minaret.

The Acropolis was besieged thrice during the [Greek War of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_War_of_Independence)—two sieges from the Greeks [in 1821–1822](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_the_Acropolis_\(1821%E2%80%931822\)) and one from the Ottomans [in 1826–1827](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_the_Acropolis_\(1826%E2%80%931827\)). A new bulwark named after [Odysseas Androutsos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odysseas_Androutsos) was built by the Greeks between 1822 and 1825 to protect the recently rediscovered [Klepsydra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klepsydra_\(Akropolis\)) spring, which became the sole fresh water supply of the fortress.

### Independent Greece



The Acropolis (photo using albumen silver print) by 19th century photographer [Francis Frith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Frith)

After independence, most features that dated from the Byzantine, Frankish, and Ottoman periods were cleared from the site in an attempt to restore the monument to its original form, "cleansed" of all later additions.[^cite_note-42] The [Parthenon mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenon_mosque) was demolished in 1843, and the [Frankish Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankish_Tower_\(Acropolis_of_Athens\)) in 1875. German [Neoclassicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoclassicism) architect [Leo von Klenze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_von_Klenze) was responsible for the restoration of the Acropolis in the 19th century, according to German historian Wolf Seidl, as described in his book *Bavarians in Greece*.[^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44]

Some antiquities from the Acropolis were exhibited in the [old Acropolis Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Acropolis_Museum), which was built in the second half of the 19th century.[^cite_note-45]

At the beginning of the [Axis occupation of Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_occupation_of_Greece) in 1941, German soldiers raised the Nazi [German War Flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reichskriegsflagge) over the Acropolis. It would be taken down by [Manolis Glezos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manolis_Glezos) and [Apostolos Santas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostolos_Santas) in one of the first acts of resistance. In 1944 Greek Prime Minister [Georgios Papandreou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgios_Papandreou) arrived on the Acropolis to celebrate liberation from the Nazis.

## Archaeological remains



Remains of the [Theatre of Dionysus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_Dionysus) as of 2007. View from the west.

The entrance to the Acropolis was a monumental gateway termed the Propylaea. To the south of the entrance is the tiny Temple of Athena Nike. At the centre of the Acropolis is the Parthenon or Temple of Athena Parthenos (Athena the Virgin). East of the entrance and north of the Parthenon is the temple known as the Erechtheum. South of the platform that forms the top of the Acropolis there are also the remains of the ancient, though often remodelled, [Theatre of Dionysus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_Dionysus). A few hundred metres away, there is the now partially reconstructed [Odeon of Herodes Atticus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odeon_of_Herodes_Atticus).[^cite_note-46]

Many of the valuable ancient artifacts are situated in the [Acropolis Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acropolis_Museum), which resides on the southern slope of the same rock, 280 metres from the Parthenon.[^cite_note-47]

## The Acropolis Restoration Project



The Acropolis of Athens as seen from the Pnyx in October 2025 with the scaffolding on the west façade of the Parthenon removed after 15 years.

The Acropolis Restoration Project began in 1975 to reverse the decay of centuries of attrition, pollution, destruction from military actions, and misguided past restorations. The project included the collection and identification of all stone fragments, even small ones, from the Acropolis and its slopes, and the attempt was made to restore as much as possible using reassembled original material ([anastylosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anastylosis)), with new marble from [Mount Pentelicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Pentelicus) used sparingly. All restoration was made using [titanium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titanium) dowels and is designed to be completely reversible, in case future experts decide to change things. A combination of cutting-edge modern technology and extensive research and reinvention of ancient techniques were used.[^cite_note-umich-48][^cite_note-Home-49][^cite_note-50]

The Parthenon colonnades, largely destroyed by Venetian bombardment during the 17th century, were restored, with many wrongly assembled columns now properly placed. The roof and floor of the Propylaea were partly restored, with sections of the roof made of new marble and decorated with blue and gold inserts, as in the original.[^cite_note-umich-48] Restoration of the Temple of Athena Nike was completed in 2010.[^cite_note-51]

A total of 2,675 tons of architectural members were restored, with 686 stones reassembled from fragments of the originals, 905 patched with new marble, and 186 parts made entirely of new marble. A total of 530 cubic meters of new Pentelic marble were used.[^cite_note-52]

In 2021, the addition of new [reinforced concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforced_concrete) paths to the site to improve accessibility caused controversy among archaeologists.[^cite_note-53]

## Cultural significance



View of the Acropolis at dusk from Mount Lycabettus in 2023

Every four years, the Athenians had a festival called the Great [Panathenaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panathenaea) that rivaled the [Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Olympic_Games) in popularity. During the festival, a procession (believed to be depicted on the Parthenon frieze) traveled through the city via the [Panathenaic Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panathenaic_Way) and culminated on the Acropolis. There, a new robe of woven wool (*[peplos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peplos)*) was placed on either the statue of Athena Polias in the Erechtheum (during the annual Lesser Panathenaea) or the statue of [Athena Parthenos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athena_Parthenos) in the Parthenon (during the Great Panathenaea, held every four years).[^cite_note-54]

Within the later tradition of [Western civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Civilization) and [Classical revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classicism), the Acropolis, from at least the mid-18th century on, has often been invoked as a critical symbol of the Greek legacy and of the glories of [Classical Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Greece).

Most of the artifacts from the temple are housed today in the Acropolis Museum at the foot of the ancient rock.

## Geology

The Acropolis is a [klippe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klippe) consisting of two lithostratigraphic units: the Athens [schist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schist) and the overlying Acropolis [limestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limestone).[^cite_note-55][^cite_note-56] The Athens schist is a soft reddish rock dating from the late [Cretaceous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cretaceous) period. The original sediments were deposited in a river delta approximately 72 million years ago. The Acropolis limestone dates from the late [Jurassic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurassic) period, predating the underlying Athens schist by about 30 million years. The Acropolis limestone was thrust over the Athens schist by compressional [tectonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tectonic) forces, forming a [nappe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nappe) or overthrust sheet. Erosion of the limestone nappe led to the eventual detachment of the Acropolis, forming the present-day feature. Where the Athens schist and the limestone meet there are springs and karstic caves.

The limestone that the Acropolis is built upon is unstable because of the erosion and tectonic shifts that the region is prone to. This instability may cause rock slides that cause damage to the historic site. Various measures have been implemented to protect the site, including retaining walls, drainage systems, and rock bolts. These measures work to counter the natural processes that threaten the historic site.[^cite_note-57]

Many of the hills in the Athens region were formed by the erosion of the same nappe as the Acropolis. These include the hills of [Lykabettos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Lycabettus), [Areopagus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Areopagus), and [Mouseion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mouseion_Hill).

The marble used for the buildings of the Acropolis was sourced from the quarries of [Mount Pentelicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Pentelicus), a mountain to the northeast of the city.
