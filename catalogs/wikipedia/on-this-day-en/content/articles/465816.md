# Flag carrier

This article is about preferential transport companies. For other uses, see [Flag carrier (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_carrier_\(disambiguation\)).

"National airline" redirects here. For a list of airlines named National, see [National Airlines (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Airlines_\(disambiguation\)).



An [Airbus A350](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbus_A350) of [Malaysia Airlines](153162.md), the flag carrier of Malaysia, with the [Malaysian flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysian_flag) depicted at the front of the aircraft and across the fuselage.

A **flag carrier** is a [transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport) company, such as an [airline](1942.md) or [shipping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shipping) company, that, being locally registered in a given [sovereign state](23604120.md), enjoys preferential rights or privileges accorded by that government for international operations.

Historically, the term was used to refer to [airlines owned by the government of their home country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_government-owned_airlines) and associated with the national identity of that country.[^cite_note-1] Such an airline may also be known as a *national airline* or a *national carrier*, although this can have different legal meanings in some countries. Today, it is any international airline with a strong connection to its home country or that represents its home country internationally, regardless of whether it is government-owned.[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3]

Flag carriers may also be known as such due to laws requiring aircraft or ships to display the state [flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag) of the country of their [registry](6162348.md).[^cite_note-4] For example, under the law of the United States, a *U.S. flag air carrier* is any airline that holds a certificate under Section 401 of the [Federal Aviation Act of 1958](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Aviation_Act_of_1958) (i.e., any U.S.-based airline operating internationally),[^cite_note-5] and any ship registered in the United States is known as a *U.S. flag vessel*.[^cite_note-6]

## Background



A [Douglas DC-4](518813.md) owned and operated by [El Al](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Al), the flag carrier of [Israel](9282173.md), in 1948

The term "flag carrier" is a legacy of the early days of commercial aviation when governments often took the lead by establishing [state-owned airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_government-owned_airlines) because of the high capital costs of running them. However, not all such airlines were government-owned; [Pan Am](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_Am), [TWA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TWA), [Cathay Pacific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathay_Pacific), [Union de Transports Aériens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_de_Transports_A%C3%A9riens), [Canadian Pacific Air Lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Pacific_Air_Lines) and [Olympic Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Airlines) were all privately owned, but were considered to be flag carriers[^cite_note-7] as they were the "main national airline"[^cite_note-8] and often a sign of their country's presence abroad.[^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10]

The heavily regulated aviation industry also meant aviation rights are often negotiated between governments, denying airlines access to an open market. These [Bilateral Air Transport Agreements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilateral_Air_Transport_Agreement) similar to the [Bermuda I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bermuda_Agreement) and [Bermuda II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bermuda_II) agreements specify rights awardable only to locally registered airlines, forcing some governments to jump-start airlines to avoid being disadvantaged in the face of foreign competition. Some countries also establish flag carriers such as [Israel](9282173.md)'s [El Al](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Al)[^cite_note-11] or [Lebanon](17771.md)'s [Middle East Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East_Airlines)[^cite_note-12] for [nationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalist) reasons or to aid the country's economy, particularly in the area of tourism.[^cite_note-13]

In many cases, governments would directly assist in the growth of their flag carriers typically through subsidies and other fiscal incentives. The establishment of competitors in the form of other locally registered airlines may be prohibited or heavily regulated to avoid direct competition.[^cite_note-14] Even where privately run airlines may be allowed to be established, the flag carriers may still be accorded priority, especially in the apportionment of aviation rights to local or international markets.[^cite_note-15]

Near the end of the twentieth century, many of these airlines have been [corporatized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporatized) as a [public company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_company) or a [state-owned enterprise](204768.md), while others have been completely [privatized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privatized).[^cite_note-16] The aviation industry has also been gradually [deregulated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airline_deregulation) and liberalized,[^cite_note-17] permitting greater [freedoms of the air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedoms_of_the_air) particularly in the [United States](3434750.md) and in the [European Union](9317.md) with the signing of the [Open Skies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EU%E2%80%93US_Open_Skies_Agreement) agreement.[^cite_note-18] One of the features of such agreements is the right of a country to designate multiple airlines to serve international routes with the result that there is no single "flag carrier".[^cite_note-19]
