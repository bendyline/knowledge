This article is about minor roads in an urban setting. For longer distance inter-urban parallel roads, see [Local–express lanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local%E2%80%93express_lanes).

&#x20;\
&#x20;A **frontage road** (also known as an **access road**, **outer road**, **service road**, **feeder road**, or **parallel road**) is a [local road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_road) running parallel to a higher-speed, [limited-access road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limited-access_road). Where parallel high-speed roads are provided as part of a major highway, these are also known as [local lanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local-express_lanes). Sometimes a similar arrangement is used for city roads; for example, the collector portion of [Commonwealth Avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_Avenue_\(Boston\)) in Boston, U.S., is known as a **carriage road**.

A frontage road is often used to provide access to private driveways, shops, houses, industries or farms that would otherwise be cut off by a limited-access road. This can prevent the commercial disruption of an [urban area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_area) that the freeway traverses or allow commercial development of bordering property.

## Advantages



A [two-way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-way_street) residential frontage road *(left)* parallel to a busy major highway

There are several advantages to using frontage roads. One advantage is to separate local traffic from through traffic. When frontage roads are lacking in an urban area, the highway is used as a local road, reducing speeds and increasing congestion. Another advantage is to provide an alternative when the highway is closed or obstructed.&#x20;

The existence of a frontage road can also be a catalyst for development, and property values along the highway often increase after the construction of frontage roads.[^cite_note-1]

Freeflowing frontage road

There are also some disadvantages to using frontage roads. When frontage roads are used without controlling the access to the primary road, at every intersection where an intersecting road runs across the primary, the number of conflict points increases one fold for each frontage road, since each frontage road is itself another intersection. A highway with frontage roads can be difficult for pedestrians to cross, for a variety of reasons including, but not limited to when neither the primary road nor the crossing is elevated, or gaps in traffic are few and the intervals between those gaps is long. Such examples include:

- [US 190](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_190) in [East Baton Rouge Parish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Baton_Rouge_Parish), [Louisiana](18130.md);

- [LA 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_Highway_1) in [West Baton Rouge Parish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Baton_Rouge_Parish), Louisiana;

- Palatine Road in [Cook County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cook_County), [Illinois](14849.md);

- The northeast edge of [Spur 503](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spur_503) in [Denison, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denison,_Texas);

- The Southeast edge of [U.S. Route 69](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Route_69) in [McAlester, Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McAlester,_Oklahoma).

A complex example is [US 77](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_77)/Commerce ([34°10′49″N 97°08′35″W  /  34.1802°N 97.1430°W  / 34.1802; -97.1430](https://geohack.toolforge.org/geohack.php?pagename=Frontage_road\&params=34.1802_N_97.143_W_)) in [Ardmore, Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ardmore,_Oklahoma), particularly at the Grand Avenue intersection. Right turns from the central carriageways are not allowed; a slip ramp must be taken to the [two-way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-way_street) frontage road, where the turning traffic must yield to the through traffic. Only then can a vehicle make a right turn from the signal on the frontage road.

Furthermore, frontage roads can increase [urban sprawl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_sprawl). Land along highways is made open for development, allowing shopping centers and other buildings to sprawl.

Cost can also be a disadvantage, as building a highway with frontage roads can be more expensive than building a highway alone.

## Local–express lane system

Main article: [Local–express lanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local%E2%80%93express_lanes)

A different alternative to the concept of frontage roads in urban freeways is the local–express system, which is designed to handle closely spaced interchange ramps without disrupting through traffic. Unlike frontage roads, the local lanes are typically high-speed fully controlled-access lanes, conforming to freeway requirements. These local lanes will run along the outside of the inner express lanes.

The outer lanes may also be known as a collector/distributor road where [slip ramps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slip_road) provide access to and from the inner mainline lanes. This distinction is usually made when the outer lanes are only present by an interchange and not the full length of the highway.

For even more capacity, frontage roads may feed into and from freeway local lanes although this is less common.

A *frontage lane* is a paved path that is used for the transportation and travel from one street to another. The difference is that typically a frontage road will follow along the side of a highway, whereas a frontage lane is a short connection between two different roads. Frontage lanes, closely related to a frontage road, are common in metropolitan areas and in small rural towns. Frontage lanes are technically not classified as roads due to their purpose as a bridge from one road to another, and due to the architectural standards that they are not as wide as a standard road, or used as commonly as a standard road, street, or [avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avenue_\(landscape\)).

A *backage road* is a similar concept to a frontage road, but lies on the back side of the land parcels that abut the controlled access's right of way. Like the frontage road, it serves mainly to provide access to those parcels as an alternative to a frontage road. Regardless of which direction the businesses face, the difference is that backage roads will be sandwiched between businesses and be separated from the freeway, whereas frontage roads will be right beside a freeway.

Some make a distinction between frontage roads and *parallel roads*. Frontage roads may more commonly refer to the one-way roads alongside a freeway, whereas parallel roads more commonly refer to the two-way roads running alongside a freeway.

## Examples

### Argentina

In [Argentina](18951905.md), especially around [Buenos Aires](255919.md), frontage roads known as **colectoras** can be found next to freeways. Examples include [Avenida General Paz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avenida_General_Paz), [Ruta 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Route_8_\(Argentina\)), and [Ruta 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Route_9_\(Argentina\)) coming into Buenos Aires.

### Canada

#### Alberta

Service or frontage roads feature in many parts of the highway network in [Alberta](717.md), and are common along freeway-grade or [twinned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twinning_\(roads\)) segments of major provincial highways such as Highways [1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberta_Highway_1), [2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberta_Highway_2) and [16](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberta_Highway_16), as well as some sections of major two-lane highways. Frontage roads are implemented along provincial highways as part of new construction, upgrading and twinning projects to ensure businesses or landowners along the highway right-of-way retain access to properties which would have direct access to the highway extinguished otherwise by the province.

In cities such as [Edmonton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmonton) and [Calgary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calgary), frontage road schemes are implemented along freeways, expressways and some high-speed suburban arterial roads. Within Edmonton, one-way frontage roads run along parts of [Whitemud Drive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitemud_Drive) and the upgraded [Yellowhead Trail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellowhead_Trail) for access to surface streets and businesses, and many arterial roads within the inner suburbs often have frontage roads to provide business and residential access away from faster through traffic. Access is usually through crossing streets, interchanges, or occasionally, ramps or direct connectors. In other urban areas, frontage roads are commonly built along a provincial highway through suburban areas to spur commercial development.

#### British Columbia

Bi-directional frontage roads exist both on the North and South sides of the [Highway 1](633679.md) through [Abbotsford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbotsford,_British_Columbia).

#### Ontario

A freeway with a significant remaining network of service roads is the [Queen Elizabeth Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Elizabeth_Way) (QEW). However, most of the slip ramps between [St. Catharines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Catharines,_Ontario) and [Mississauga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississauga) were removed during major reconstruction in the 1970s and 1990s. Service roads are no longer able to directly access the QEW; they have been rerouted to intersections with other major roads which have interchanges with the QEW. Nonetheless, the service roads are positioned too close to the QEW to easily widen the freeway unless all the private properties along the service road are bought out. This would be unlikely in the current political environment.

The only remaining slip ramps connecting to service roads are on the QEW running through [St. Catharines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Catharines,_Ontario). These dangerous low-standard ramps (due to lack of acceleration/deceleration lanes) are due to be replaced in a planned extensive reconstruction of the QEW that is currently underway. Similar service roads and slip ramps exist along [Highway 401](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontario_Highway_401) through [Oshawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oshawa,_Ontario), but like through St. Catharines, these are also in the process of being replaced with modern ramps.

[Highway 427](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontario_Highway_427) had its service roads replaced with a [collector-express](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collector-express) system in the 1970s. However, it has several [RIRO access](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RIRO_access) onramps and offramps to serve residential traffic in addition to its standard [parclo interchanges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parclo_interchange) with major [arterials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arterial_road).

A short section of [Highway 400](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontario_Highway_400) has the service roads of Davis Road (southbound) and Wist Road (northbound) from South Canal Bank Road to [Canal Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_numbered_roads_in_Simcoe_County) in [King Township](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King,_Ontario) and [Bradford West Gwillimbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bradford_West_Gwillimbury). There is a [RIRO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-in/right-out) interchange to the service roads, signed as to Canal Road, to access the farms of the [Holland Marsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holland_Marsh) and the town of Bradford West Gwillimbury.

Although not considered a service road, [Lake Shore Boulevard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Shore_Boulevard) in downtown Toronto has several slip ramps that weave in and out of the parallel [Gardiner Expressway](314947.md), in a similar fashion as a service road.

#### Quebec

Many autoroutes in [Greater Montreal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Montreal) (including the [A-40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quebec_Autoroute_40), [A-520](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quebec_Autoroute_520), [A-13](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quebec_Autoroute_13), [A-15](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quebec_Autoroute_15) and [A-25](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quebec_Autoroute_25)) maintain networks of frontage roads along at least some of their lengths as they pass through urban/developing areas.

### India



Service Lane on Bangalore elevated tollway on Tumkur Road, Bangalore.

In India, frontage roads or Service lanes (sometimes called नल्ला "Nullah" in Hindi) exist on most high density [dual carriageway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_carriageway) roads and dual carriageway highways. On Access controlled Expressways like the [Yamuna Expressway](19787093.md), the frontage roads remain separate from the main carriageway throughout the road's length. Retrofitted and previously non-access controlled roads, such as most [National Highways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Highway_\(India\)), only have service lanes on stretches where fly-overs (overpasses) are built over junctions or through towns.

### Mexico

In [Guadalajara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guadalajara,_Mexico), the López Mateos, Vallarta and Mariano Otero avenues (the latter in the stretch between López Mateos to Niños Héroes) are two-lane avenues surrounded by two [one-way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-way_traffic) frontage roads. Lázaro Cárdenas Expressway is similar, but with three lanes in both the central road and the frontage roads. Because these frontage roads are considered as part of the avenue itself, the central road is known locally as the "central lanes", whereas the frontage roads are known as "lateral lanes". Turns are always forbidden in the central lanes; drivers wishing to make a turn must leave the central lanes and make the turn from the lateral lanes.

### Netherlands

Frontage roads are common in the Netherlands and detailed in the Dutch national **design manual for bicycle traffic** as per pages 121 and 127[^cite_note-2] where they are referred to as **parallel roads.** In the Netherlands, engineers have used frontage roads to benefit cyclists as well as automobiles. Because frontage roads only carry local traffic, the speed on these roads is low (their speed limit is 30 km/h), making them an ideal environment for bicyclists. Because the speed and volume is so low, no additional treatments are needed to make a service road a safe bike facility. In the Netherlands, service roads are often linked together with bike paths to help create a comprehensive bicycle route, with the bike path links serving as barriers to through motor traffic. Since service roads serve a dual purpose, they are an inexpensive way to create routes in cycling network, compared to cycletracks or stand-alone bike paths. Extensive amounts of information on frontage roads can be found on Northeastern's webpage.[^cite_note-3]

### Mainland China

In the [People's Republic of China](5405.md) [mainland](55079.md), roads running next to [expressways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expressways_of_China), taking outgoing traffic and feeding incoming traffic, are referred to as **service roads** ([Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplified_Chinese_characters): 辅路; [pinyin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinyin): *Fǔlù*) or **auxiliary roads** ([Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplified_Chinese_characters): 辅道; [pinyin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinyin): *Fǔdào*). Where expressways cross larger urban areas, such frontage roads may run next to the expressway itself. Much of the Beijing portion of the [Jingkai Expressway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jingkai_Expressway), for example, has, in fact, [China National Highway 106](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China_National_Highway_106) acting as a split-direction frontage road. Many newer urban highways are entirely elevated, with parallel access roads running beneath the entire length.

### Philippines

The service roads begin at [Sales Interchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sales_Interchange) at [Pasay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasay) up until [Alabang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alabang) exit in [Muntinlupa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muntinlupa).

Outside the expressways, some of the wider major roads in [Metro Manila](72340.md) feature service roads for business access. [Roxas Boulevard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roxas_Boulevard) features a service road to the east, running from [Kalaw Avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalaw_Avenue) in [Ermita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ermita), [Manila](184334.md) to C. Rivera Street in Pasay. [Quezon Avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quezon_Avenue) in [Quezon City](284223.md) runs an eastbound one-way service road from [Timog Avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timog_Avenue) and [East Avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Avenue_\(Quezon_City\)) which crosses [EDSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EDSA). ### Hong Kong

Frontage roads exist both in city and along major expressways between new towns. [Gloucester Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gloucester_Road,_Hong_Kong) has frontage road running parallel of it from east to west. ### United States

**Alaska**

Though [Alaska](624.md) has very few roads that are built to freeway standard, a couple of the highways that are do have frontage roads; notably along the [Seward Highway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seward_Highway) ([Alaska Route 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaska_Route_1)) with Homer Drive running south (from Tudor Road to Dimond Boulevard) and Brayton Drive running north (from DeArmoun Road to Tudor Road); and the [Minnesota Drive Expressway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minnesota_Drive_Expressway) (from West 100th Ave to Dimond Boulevard) in South Anchorage. #### Arizona

Frontage roads are not very common in Arizona but do exist along certain freeways.

In metropolitan Phoenix, the state's first freeway, [Interstate 17](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_17) has a frontage road (Black Canyon Highway); some sections of the frontage road was reduced to a single lane in the 1990s when I-17 was widened. Several freeways overbuilt existing arterials, which were converted to frontage roads: Price Road (Tempe), Pima Road (Scottsdale) and Beardsley Road (north Phoenix) on the [Loop 101](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arizona_State_Route_101), as well as 59th Avenue on the [Loop 202](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arizona_State_Route_202) Ed Pastor (South Mountain) Freeway. In Tucson, I-10 has a two-lane, one-way frontage road, and in between Casa Grande and Tucson, a two-lane, [two-way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-way_street) frontage road.

#### California

#### Connecticut

Parts of the [Edens Expressway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_94_in_Illinois), the [Dan Ryan Expressway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Ryan_Expressway), the [Eisenhower Expressway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eisenhower_Expressway), and the [Kennedy Expressway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kennedy_Expressway) use frontage roads. [Interstate 69](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_69) between [Indianapolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indianapolis,_Indiana) and [Bloomington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloomington,_Indiana) was built directly over [State Road 37](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiana_State_Road_37) between the two cities. #### Massachusetts

Service roads are relatively uncommon in much of New England, and in Boston in particular, largely due to resistance to expressway construction, which necessitated scaled-back rights of way. Still, some unique examples of the type exist in the [Rose Fitzgerald Kennedy Greenway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rose_Fitzgerald_Kennedy_Greenway) Surface Road, Cross street, and [Atlantic Avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_Avenue_\(Boston\)) in downtown Boston. As a result of the [Big Dig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Dig), the carriageways of these streets were re-aligned to function as a [two-way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-way_street) service road system through downtown Boston with the Rose Kennedy Greenway park system as their 'median', and the expressway underground. In this special case of a service road, the subterranean I-93 Central Artery expressway is not visible from the surface, but accessible through access ramps into the tunnel system. Just south of downtown, I-93 also includes a short section at-grade service road between exits 16 in South Boston and the I-90 interchange south of Chinatown, in a more typical arrangement of the concept.

Typical service roads also exist along the eight-lane freeway section of [Massachusetts Route 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Route_2) through [Arlington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arlington,_Massachusetts) and [Belmont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belmont,_Massachusetts) (two near northwestern suburbs of Boston) and [United States Route 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Route_1) in [Lynnfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynnfield,_Massachusetts).

In [Fall River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_River,_Massachusetts), from 1965 to 2023, Davol Street served as the frontage road for [Massachusetts Route 79](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Route_79) between "Broadway Extended", Anawan and Pocasset Streets, Central Street, and [Interstate 195](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_195_\(Rhode_Island%E2%80%94Massachusetts\)?action=edit\&redlink=1) in the south, to Brightman Street in the north. For most of this stretch, Davol Street is split, with the Western Fall River Expressway (Route 79) running between a [one-way pair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-way_pair) of northbound North Davol Street and southbound South Davol Street. Until its closure in 2011, the [Brightman Street Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brightman_Street_Bridge) carried [U.S. Route 6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Route_6_in_Massachusetts) and [Route 138](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Route_138) between [Somerset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somerset,_Massachusetts) and Fall River. At the eastern approach to the bridge, US 6 and MA 138 connected to MA 79, Brightman Street, and both sides of Davol Street at a complex interchange (to and from MA 79)/intersection (to and from US 6/MA 138, Brightman Street, and both sides of Davol Street). North of this interchange/intersection, both sides of Davol Street become the exit ramp from MA 79 southbound and entrance ramp to MA 79 northbound.&#x20;

After the [Veterans Memorial Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veterans_Memorial_Bridge_\(Bristol_County,_Massachusetts\)) replaced the Brightman Street Bridge in 2011, this relocated US 6 and MA 138 onto a taller, expressway-like bridge with a large modified trumpet-style interchange with MA 79 and both sides of Davol Street. A new [U-turn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U-turn) ramp from North Davol Street to South Davol Street and MA 79 southbound was added, along with a bike path along the bridge from Somerset and then south to Brightman Street. Brightman Street itself could only be accessed from North Davol Street; traffic from Brightman Street had to use local side streets to reach North Davol Street to access either direction of MA 79, US 6, and MA 138.&#x20;

In 2015 and 2016, the Fall River Viaduct carrying MA 79 to its terminus at MA 138 and I-195 was demolished. When reconstruction was complete in 2016, Routes 79 and 138 were merged onto one boulevard-style Davol Street roadway with signaled intersections (at Central Street, Anawan and Pocasset Streets, and a new complex intersection with connections to I-195, Millikan Boulevard, and Ponta Delgada Boulevard/Water Street) from a partially-reconstructed partial interchange just south of Brownell Street.&#x20;

By spring 2023, the [Massachusetts Department of Transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Department_of_Transportation) (MassDOT) had closed and started to remove some of the Route 79 expressway between the partial interchange from just south of Brownell Street at the southern end and the Veterans Memorial Bridge interchange at the northern end. All Route 79 traffic has been directed onto both sides of the Davol Street frontage road south of the Veterans Memorial Bridge interchange. Part of the 2011 Veterans Memorial Bridge interchange was closed or modified as well, most notably the ramp from US 6 eastbound/MA 138 southbound on the bridge to MA 79 southbound and the aforementioned U-turn ramp from North Davol Street to South Davol Street and Route 79 southbound (a temporary U-turn to the south has been built in its place for use during the construction period).&#x20;

During re-construction of Davol Street as a boulevard, a temporary roadway will be built at-grade on the site of the former elevated expressway, with all traffic using it until the reconstructed halves of Davol Street are finished. Afterwards, the temporary roadway will be removed, with the land being slated for redevelopment, and all Route 79/138 traffic will return to North and South Davol Streets. By the completion of the project, the removal of the expressway will eliminate the frontage road status of North and South Davol Streets.

North and South Davol Streets carry US 6 from the Veterans Memorial Bridge (and before that, the Brightman Street Bridge) to President Avenue, where it turns east to cut through the city.&#x20;

Between the mid-1960s and 2023, the status of Route 138 between "Broadway Extended" and either bridge to Somerset has been up for debate. Signs dating back to, and possibly prior to, the construction of the Fall River Viaduct (demolished 2015-2016) indicate that this stretch of Davol Street carries Route 138. Most signs from the mid-1960s indicate that MA 138 joins Route 79 from its southern end and leaves it at the ramps to either bridge to Somerset. However, some signs along President Avenue (US 6) and South Davol Street (and formerly also near the pre-2015 site of the split of Davol Street into the one-way pair) indicated that Route 138 still used the one-way pair of North and South Davol Streets. The elimination of the Route 79 expressway largely will end this decades-long debate, as North and South Davol Streets will carry all traffic (US 6 and Routes 79 and 138) from Central Street in the south to the Veterans Memorial Bridge in the north, where the Davol Street halves will re-join and serve as the transition to a new, permanent "southern end" of the truncated Western Fall River Expressway.

#### Michigan

Frontage roads are also common in [Metro Detroit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metro_Detroit), where they are usually referred to as "service drives." As in Texas, they typically run one-way with frequent slip ramps to and from the limited-access roadway, with [Texas U-turns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_U-turn) at or near many intersections. Unlike Texas, there is usually little commercial development situated along the frontage road itself (see [example](https://maps.google.com/?ie=UTF8\&om=1\&z=17\&ll=42.408138,-83.218442\&spn=0.004484,0.005268\&t=k)); the road serves to provide access to the freeway from existing residential streets and commercial surface thoroughfares. Also unlike in many locales in urban Texas, where an exit ramp may actually precede the entrance ramp for the previous interchange to facilitate access to businesses situated directly on the frontage road (in effect, the two interchanges overlap along the frontage road), Michigan slip ramps to and from frontage roads are generally positioned as they normally would be in the absence of the frontage road. Motorists entering and exiting the freeway are not sharing the frontage road simultaneously to as large a degree, reducing weaving. Access to the frontage road between exits is provided by turnarounds and frequent bridging, generally every 1/2 mile, between exits.

[Michigan left hand turns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_Left) are also quite common at surface street-frontage road intersections, with dedicated turnaround lanes (similar to the Texas U-turn) built over the freeway on separate bridges approximately 100 meters from the main intersection and bridging.[^cite_note-4]

With the exceptions of [Interstate 275](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I-275_\(Michigan\)) and the freeway portion of [M-53](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M-53_\(Michigan_highway\)), every Metro Detroit freeway has a frontage road along it for at least a portion of its length. Several other freeways outside Metro Detroit use these as well.

There are two other cities in Michigan where frontage roads running more than one mile in length outside of Metro Detroit can be found. There are frontage roads along [Interstate 496](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_496) and [U.S. Route 127](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Route_127) in Greater [Lansing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lansing) and along [Interstate 475](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_475_\(Michigan\)) in Downtown [Flint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flint,_Michigan). Outside the cities, [US-23](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Route_23_\(Michigan\)) has them from [Ann Arbor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ann_Arbor,_Michigan) to [Fenton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fenton,_Michigan), while [US-127](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Route_127_\(Michigan\)) has them from [Leslie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leslie,_Michigan) to [Mason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mason,_Michigan). New freeway construction in Michigan has not included frontage roads since the completion of [Interstate 696](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_696), most of which was constructed along the rights of way of major surface arteries, in 1989. Michigan does not build frontage roads in rural areas.

#### New York

One-way service roads on either sides of highways are relatively common in New York City and the surrounding areas. Due to the high [urban density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_density), this design allows rapid access on and off the highway while also providing a viable alternate route in the case of accidents and traffic. In the borough of Queens, the [Van Wyck Expressway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Wyck_Expressway) has this system implemented for most of its length. On Long Island, the [Long Island Expressway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Island_Expressway) (Interstate 495), has one-way service roads on each side of the expressway for most of its length from the [Queens–Midtown Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queens%E2%80%93Midtown_Tunnel) to [Riverhead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riverhead_\(town\),_New_York).

Upstate, frontage roads are less common. [New York State Route 104](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_State_Route_104) has frontage roads along its limited access portions in [Irondequoit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irondequoit,_New_York) and [Webster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Webster,_New_York), and the [Southern Tier Expressway (I-86)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_86_\(Pennsylvania%E2%80%93New_York\)) in [Horseheads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horseheads,_New_York) has two short frontage roads (Brickyard Lane and Fairport Lane) during its [elevated expressway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevated_highway) portion. These stretches of service roads have signalized intersections with main roadways, as well as [RIRO-style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-in/right-out) intersections with tertiary roadways and larger commercial properties.

#### North and South Carolina

Service roads can be found alongside interstate highways in [North Carolina](21650.md) and [South Carolina](27956.md). Some of these roads have houses facing the highways which they parallel. They may also have highway service, as most of them are located near [interchanges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interchange_\(road\)). Most service roads in the Carolinas do not have ramps leading to and from their respective highways; rather, as mentioned before, most are located near interchanges, which allows people to exit the highway and go around to the frontage road if needed. Those service roads are also commonly used as roads for farms and their products.

Significant stretches of frontage roads are found in and around [Akron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akron,_Ohio); [Cleveland](5951.md), where ones without prior names are called "marginal roads"; [Cincinnati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cincinnati); and [Downtown Youngstown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downtown_Youngstown).

#### Texas



A frontage road for [Texas State Highway 183](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_State_Highway_183) (Airport Freeway) in [Irving, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irving,_Texas)

In 2007, the [Texas state comptroller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Comptroller_of_Public_Accounts) stated the state had built over 6,500 miles (10,500 km) of frontage roads. This was far more than any other state. The state government's tendency to construct frontage roads alongside major highways has been historically credited to chief highway engineer Dewitt Greer (1940–1968). He began building frontage roads as a measure to reduce right-of-way acquisition costs by ensuring access to new highways for affected landowners; otherwise, the state would have needed to pay them a higher price for cutting off access to their land.[^cite_note-Babineck-5]

Most Texas [freeways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeway) have service roads on both sides. In urban and suburban areas, the traffic typically travels one-way, in the direction of the adjacent freeway. Most other areas have [two-way traffic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-way_street), but as an area urbanizes, the frontage road is often converted to one-way traffic (2 lanes). In cases of freeway congestion or shutdown, the frontage road provides an instant detour, subject to delays at each stop sign or stoplight at cross streets.

Where two new Texas freeways meet, especially on the edge of major metropolitan areas, the state will often first build the junctions for the one-way frontage roads—that is, four at-grade intersections—followed by an overpass where one freeway crosses over another. This requires motorists who desire to switch freeways to exit to the adjacent frontage road, turn at an at-grade intersection onto the frontage road for the other freeway, and then merge into the other freeway. As traffic increases at the at-grade intersections, the state slowly adds direct ramps between freeways for the most in-demand traffic movements, thereby reducing such inconvenience for motorists.

Over 80% of [Houston](13774.md) freeways have service roads,[^cite_note-6] which locals typically call feeders. Many service roads in urban and suburban areas of Texas have the convenience of [Texas U-turns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_U-turn), as a left lane curving under an overpass, allowing drivers to avoid stopping for traffic lights when making a U-turn.

Service roads are often built as part of a multi-phase plan to construct new limited-access highways. They initially serve as a highway with access to local business before the freeway is constructed years later. After the completion of the freeway, frontage roads serve as a major thoroughfare for local activity, such as with the [Katy Freeway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katy_Freeway) project (I-10) in [Greater Houston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Houston).[^cite_note-7] In several cases, a long-range plan has called for a future freeway, but the design has either changed or the project was canceled before completion.[^cite_note-8]

Nicknames for frontage roads vary within the state of Texas. In Houston and [East Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Texas), they are called feeders. Dallas and [Fort Worth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Worth,_Texas) area residents call their frontage roads "service roads", and "access roads" is the predominant term used in [San Antonio](53848.md).[^cite_note-9] Most signs reference "Frontage Road" despite local regional vernacular (there are signs in Houston that use the term "feeder").

In Houston, the free sections of [Beltway 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_State_Highway_Beltway_8), [SH 249](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_State_Highway_249) and [FM 1093](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farm_to_Market_Road_1093), not part of the Sam Houston Tollway, Tomball Tollway, and Fort Bend Westpark Tollway (respectively) are composed of frontage roads.

In 2002, the [Texas Department of Transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Department_of_Transportation) proposed to discontinue building frontage roads on new freeways, citing studies that suggest frontage roads increase congestion. However, this proposal was widely ridiculed and criticized and was dropped later the same year.[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11]

The [Stemmons Freeway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stemmons_Freeway) in [Dallas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dallas,_Texas) illustrates the practicability of the frontage road: the [real estate developer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_estate_developer) John Stemmons offered free land to the Texas Highway commission in which to build a freeway ([Interstate 35E](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_35E_\(Texas\))) on the condition that the [state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Department_of_Transportation) build the freeway with frontage roads that would give access to undeveloped [property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Property) that he owned along the freeway corridor. The state was able to reduce its costs (largely the cost of land acquisition) of building the freeway, and didn't need to acquire and demolish developed property; the developer profited from development along the freeway. [San Antonio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Antonio,_Texas) developer Charles Martin Wender used the same tactic for his Westover Hills development, offering free land through the middle of his property for [SH 151](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_State_Highway_151) as well as paying half the costs for the initial frontage road construction. Following Wender's lead, several neighboring landowners also donated right-of-way for the route.
