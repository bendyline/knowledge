An **Earth observation satellite** or **Earth remote sensing satellite** is a [satellite](27683.md) used or designed for [Earth observation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_observation) (EO) from [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit), including [spy satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spy_satellite) and similar ones intended for non-military uses such as [environmental monitoring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_monitoring), [meteorology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteorology), [cartography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartography) and others. The most common type are **Earth imaging satellites**, that take [satellite images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_image), analogous to [aerial photographs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerial_photograph); some EO satellites may perform [remote sensing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remote_sensing) without forming pictures, such as in [GNSS radio occultation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GNSS_radio_occultation).

The first occurrence of satellite remote sensing can be dated to the launch of the first artificial satellite, [Sputnik 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sputnik_1), by the Soviet Union on October 4, 1957.[^cite_note-Tatem-1] Sputnik 1 sent back radio signals, which scientists used to study the [ionosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionosphere).[^cite_note-Kuznetsov-2]

The United States Army Ballistic Missile Agency launched the first American satellite, [Explorer 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explorer_1), for NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory on January 31, 1958. The information sent back from its radiation detector led to the discovery of the Earth's [Van Allen radiation belts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Allen_radiation_belt).[^cite_note-Allen-3] The [TIROS-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TIROS-1) spacecraft, launched on April 1, 1960, as part of NASA's [Television Infrared Observation Satellite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_Infrared_Observation_Satellite) (TIROS) program, sent back the first television footage of weather patterns to be taken from space.[^cite_note-Tatem-1]

In 2008, more than 150 Earth observation satellites were in orbit, recording data with both passive and active sensors and acquiring more than 10 terabits of data daily.[^cite_note-Tatem-1] By 2021, that total had grown to over 950, with the largest number of satellites operated by US-based company [Planet Labs](41200593.md).[^cite_note-4]

Most [Earth](9228.md) observation satellites carry instruments that should be operated at a relatively low altitude. Most orbit at altitudes above 500 to 600 kilometers (310 to 370 mi). Lower orbits have significant [air-drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drag_\(physics\)), which makes frequent orbit [reboost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reboost) maneuvers necessary. The Earth observation satellites [ERS-1, ERS-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Remote-Sensing_Satellite) and [Envisat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Envisat) of [European Space Agency](10363.md) as well as the [MetOp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MetOp) spacecraft of [EUMETSAT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EUMETSAT) are all operated at altitudes of about 800 km (500 mi). The [Proba-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PROBA), [Proba-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proba-2) and [SMOS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_Moisture_and_Ocean_Salinity_satellite) spacecraft of European Space Agency are observing the Earth from an altitude of about 700 km (430 mi). The Earth observation satellites of UAE, [DubaiSat-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DubaiSat-1) & [DubaiSat-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DubaiSat-2) are also placed in [Low Earth orbits](47568.md) (LEO) orbits and providing [satellite imagery](1184557.md) of various parts of the Earth.[^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6]

To get global coverage with a low orbit, a [polar orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_orbit) is used. A low orbit will have an orbital period of about 100 minutes and the Earth will rotate around its polar axis about 25° between successive orbits. The [ground track](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_track) moves towards the west 25° each orbit, allowing a different section of the globe to be scanned with each orbit. Most are in [Sun-synchronous orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun-synchronous_orbit).

A [geostationary orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geostationary_orbit), at 36,000 km (22,000 mi), allows a satellite to hover over a constant spot on the earth since the orbital period at this altitude is 24 hours. This allows uninterrupted coverage of more than 1/3 of the Earth per satellite, so three satellites, spaced 120° apart, can cover the whole Earth. This type of orbit is mainly used for [meteorological satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weather_satellite).

## History

See also: [Remote sensing § History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remote_sensing#History)



Lednickie Lake (Poland) seen by the American reconnaissance satellite [CORONA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CORONA_\(satellite\)) 98, 1965

[Herman Potočnik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Poto%C4%8Dnik) explored the idea of using orbiting spacecraft for detailed peaceful and military observation of the ground in his 1928 book, *The Problem of Space Travel*. He described how the special conditions of space could be useful for scientific experiments. The book described [geostationary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geostationary) satellites (first put forward by [Konstantin Tsiolkovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konstantin_Tsiolkovsky)) and discussed communication between them and the ground using radio, but fell short of the idea of using satellites for mass broadcasting and as telecommunications relays.[^cite_note-7]

The onset of the [Cold War](325329.md) prompted the rapid development of [Satellite launch systems](1306157.md) and camera technology capable of sufficient Earth observation to garner intelligence on enemy military infrastructure and evaluate nuclear posture.[^cite_note-8] Following the U-2 incident in 1960, which highlighted the risks of aerial spying, the U.S. accelerated surveillance satellite programs like [CORONA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CORONA_\(satellite\)). Satellites largely replaced aircraft overflights for surveillance after 1960.[^cite_note-9]

## Applications

### Weather

Main article: [Weather satellite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weather_satellite)

See also: [Satellite temperature measurements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_temperature_measurements)



[GOES-8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GOES-8), a [United States](3434750.md) weather satellite

A weather satellite is a type of [satellite](27683.md) that is primarily used to monitor the [weather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weather) and [climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate) of the [Earth](9228.md).[^cite_note-NESDIS-10] These meteorological satellites, however, see more than [clouds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud) and cloud systems. City lights, [fires](11145.md), effects of [pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollution), [auroras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auroral_light), [sand and dust storms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_storm), [snow](28191.md) cover, [ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice) mapping, boundaries of [ocean currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_current), [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) flows, etc., are other types of environmental information collected using weather satellites.

Weather satellite images helped in monitoring the volcanic ash cloud from [Mount St. Helens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_St._Helens) and activity from other volcanoes such as [Mount Etna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Etna).[^cite_note-11] Smoke from fires in the western United States such as [Colorado](5399.md) and [Utah](31716.md) have also been monitored.

### Environmental monitoring



Composite satellite image of the Earth, showing its entire surface in [equirectangular projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equirectangular_projection)

Other environmental satellites can assist [environmental monitoring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_monitoring) by detecting changes in the Earth's vegetation, atmospheric trace gas content, sea state, ocean color, and ice fields. By monitoring vegetation changes over time, droughts can be monitored by comparing the current vegetation state to its long term average.[^cite_note-12] For example, the 2002 oil spill off the northwest coast of [Spain](26667.md) was watched carefully by the European [ENVISAT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Envisat), which, though not a weather satellite, flies an instrument (ASAR) which can see changes in the sea surface. Anthropogenic emissions can be monitored by evaluating data of tropospheric NO<sub>2</sub> and SO<sub>2</sub>.

These types of satellites are almost always in [Sun-synchronous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun-synchronous_orbit) and ["frozen"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frozen_orbit) orbits. A Sun-synchronous orbit passes over each spot on the ground at the same time of day, so that observations from each pass can be more easily compared, since the Sun is in the same spot in each observation. A ["frozen"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frozen_orbit) orbit is the closest possible orbit to a circular orbit that is undisturbed by the [oblateness of the Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geopotential_model), gravitational attraction from the Sun and Moon, [solar radiation pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_radiation_pressure), and [air drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_drag).

### Mapping

Terrain can be mapped from space with the use of satellites, such as [Radarsat-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radarsat-1)[^cite_note-13] and [TerraSAR-X](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TerraSAR-X).

## International regulations



[RapidEye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RapidEye) *Earth exploration-satellite system* in action around the Earth

According to the [International Telecommunication Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Telecommunication_Union) (ITU), **Earth exploration-satellite service** (also: **Earth exploration-satellite radiocommunication service**) is – according to *Article 1.51* of the [ITU Radio Regulations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITU_Radio_Regulations) (RR)[^cite_note-14] – defined as:

> A [radiocommunication service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiocommunication_service) between [earth stations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_station) and one or more [space stations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_space_station), which may include links between space stations, in which:
>
> - information relating to the characteristics of the Earth and its natural phenomena, including data relating to the state of the environment, is obtained from passive or [active sensors](25676.md) on [satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellites);
>
> - similar information is collected from airborne or Earth-based platforms;
>
> - such information may be distributed to earth stations within the system concerned;
>
> - platform interrogation may be included.
>
> &#x20;This service may also include feeder links necessary for its operation.

### Classification

- Earth exploration-satellite service&#x20;

  - [Meteorological-satellite service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteorological-satellite_service) (article 1.52)

### Frequency allocation

The allocation of radio frequencies is provided according to *Article 5* of the ITU Radio Regulations (edition 2012).[^cite_note-15]

In order to improve harmonisation in spectrum utilisation, the majority of service-allocations stipulated in this document were incorporated in national Tables of Frequency Allocations and Utilisations which is with-in the responsibility of the appropriate national administration. The allocation might be primary, secondary, exclusive, and shared.

- primary allocation: is indicated by writing in capital letters (see example below)

- secondary allocation: is indicated by small letters

- exclusive or shared utilization: is within the responsibility of administrations

However, military usage, in bands where there is civil usage, will be in accordance with the ITU Radio Regulations.
