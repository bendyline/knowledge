**Ireland** ([Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_language): *[Éire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89ire)* [\[ˈeːɾʲə\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Irish) [//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/c/ca/Ga-%C3%89ire.oga/Ga-%C3%89ire.oga.mp3](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/c/ca/Ga-%C3%89ire.oga/Ga-%C3%89ire.oga.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup>), [also known](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_the_Irish_state) as the **Republic of Ireland** (*Poblacht na hÉireann*),[^cite_note-nomenclature-12] is a country in [Northwestern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwestern_Europe). It consists of 26 of the [32 counties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counties_of_Ireland) of the island of [Ireland](147575.md), with a population of about 5.4 million.[^cite_note-Pop.Ireland-5] Its [capital](181337.md) and largest city is [Dublin](8504.md), on the eastern side of the island, with a population of over 1.5 million.[^cite_note-Pop.Ireland-5] The [sovereign state](23604120.md) shares its only land border with [Northern Ireland](21265.md), which is [part of the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countries_of_the_United_Kingdom). It is otherwise surrounded by the [Atlantic Ocean](698.md), with the [Celtic Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_Sea) to the south, [St George's Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_George%27s_Channel) to the south-east and the [Irish Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Sea) to the east. It is a [unitary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_state), [parliamentary republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_republic).[^cite_note-13] The legislature, the [Oireachtas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oireachtas), consists of a [lower house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_house), [Dáil Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A1il_%C3%89ireann); an [upper house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_house), [Seanad Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seanad_%C3%89ireann); and an elected [president](24676.md) (*Uachtarán*) who serves as the largely ceremonial [head of state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_of_state), but with some important powers and duties. The [head of government](37570.md) is the [Taoiseach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoiseach) (prime minister, lit. 'chief'), elected by the Dáil and appointed by the president, who appoints other government ministers.&#x20;

The [Irish Free State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Free_State) was created with [Dominion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominion) status in 1922, following the [Anglo-Irish Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Irish_Treaty). In 1937, a [new constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Ireland) was adopted, in which the state was named "Ireland" and effectively became a republic, with an elected [non-executive president](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_of_state#Non-executive_model). It was officially declared a republic in 1949, following [The Republic of Ireland Act 1948](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Republic_of_Ireland_Act_1948). Ireland became a member of the [United Nations](31769.md) in 1955. It joined the [European Communities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Communities) (EC), the predecessor of the [European Union](9317.md) (EU), in 1973. The state had no formal relations with Northern Ireland for most of the 20th century, but the 1980s and 1990s saw the British and Irish governments working with Northern Irish parties to resolve the conflict that had become known as [the Troubles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Troubles). Since the signing of the [Good Friday Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_Friday_Agreement) in 1998, the [Irish government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Ireland) and [Northern Irish government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Executive) have co-operated on a number of policy areas under the [North/South Ministerial Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North/South_Ministerial_Council) created by the Agreement.&#x20;

Ireland is a [developed country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developed_country) with a [quality of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quality_of_life) ranked sixth in the world by the 2024 [Human Development Index Report adjusted for inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_inequality-adjusted_Human_Development_Index).[^cite_note-14] It also ranks highly in [healthcare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Healthcare_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland), [economic freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_economic_freedom) and [freedom of the press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Press_Freedom_Index).[^cite_note-15][^cite_note-16] According to the [Global Peace Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Peace_Index), Ireland was the second most peaceful country worldwide in 2024.[^cite_note-peace2024-17]&#x20;

It is a member of the EU and a founding member of the [Council of Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Europe) and the [OECD](33853117.md). The Irish government has followed a policy of [military neutrality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutral_country) through non-alignment since before [World War II](32927.md), and the country is consequently not a member of [NATO](21133.md),[^cite_note-18] although it is a member of the [Partnership for Peace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partnership_for_Peace) and [certain aspects of PESCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permanent_Structured_Cooperation#Neutral_states). [Ireland's economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_the_Republic_of_Ireland) is advanced,[^cite_note-19] with one of Europe's major financial hubs being centred on Dublin. It ranks among the top five wealthiest countries in the world in terms of both [GDP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_\(nominal\)_per_capita) and [GNI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GNI_\(nominal\)_per_capita) per capita.[^cite_note-20][^cite_note-21][^cite_note-22][^cite_note-23] After joining the EC, the country's government enacted a series of liberal economic policies that helped to boost economic growth between 1995 and 2007, a time now often referred to as the [Celtic Tiger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_Tiger) period. A recession and reversal in growth then followed during the [Great Recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Recession), which was exacerbated by the bursting of the [Irish property bubble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_property_bubble).[^cite_note-24] The Great Recession lasted until 2014, and was followed by a new period of strong economic growth.[^cite_note-25]&#x20;

## Name

Main article: [Names of the Irish state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_the_Irish_state)

The names *Ireland* and *Éire* both derive from [Old Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Irish) *[Ériu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89riu)*, which in turn comes from [Proto-Celtic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Celtic) *[ɸīweriyū](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Reconstruction\:Proto-Celtic/%C9%B8%C4%ABweriy%C5%AB)* meaning "fertile soil". The [Classical Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Latin) name for the island of Ireland, *[Ivernia](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Ivernia#Latin)*, also comes from this same root,[^cite_note-26][^cite_note-27] and it remains a popular, poetic synonym for Ireland to this day (usually respelled "*[Hibernia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hibernia)*", [under the influence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphological_reanalysis) of *[hibernum](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/hibernum#Latin)*, the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language) word for "[winter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter)").&#x20;

The state created in 1922, comprising 26 of the 32 [counties of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counties_of_Ireland), was "styled and known as the Irish Free State" (*Saorstát Éireann*).[^cite_note-28] The [Constitution of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Ireland), adopted in 1937, says that "the name of the State is *Éire*, or, in the English language, Ireland". Section 2 of the [Republic of Ireland Act 1948](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Ireland_Act_1948) states, "It is hereby declared that the description of the State shall be the Republic of Ireland." The 1948 Act does not *name* the state "Republic of Ireland", because to have done so would have put it in conflict with the Constitution.[^cite_note-29]&#x20;

The government of the United Kingdom used the name "Eire" (without the [diacritic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diacritic)) and, from 1949, "Republic of Ireland", for the state.[^cite_note-30] It was not until the 1998 [Good Friday Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_Friday_Agreement), when the state dropped its [claim to Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireland%E2%80%93United_Kingdom_relations#Articles_2_and_3_and_the_name_Ireland), that it began calling the state "Ireland".[^cite_note-31][^cite_note-Daly_2007-32]&#x20;

The state is also informally called "the Republic", "Southern Ireland" or "the South";[^cite_note-33] especially when distinguishing the state from the island or when discussing [Northern Ireland](21265.md) ("the North"). [Irish republicans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_republicans) reserve the name "Ireland" for the whole island[^cite_note-Daly_2007-32] and often refer to the state as "the Free State", "the 26 Counties",[^cite_note-Daly_2007-32][^cite_note-34] or "the South of Ireland".[^cite_note-35] This is a "response to the [partitionist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partitionism) view \[...] that Ireland stops at the border".[^cite_note-36]&#x20;

## History

Main article: [History of the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Republic_of_Ireland)

For the history of the entire island, see [History of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Ireland).

### Home-rule movement

Main article: [Irish Home Rule movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Home_Rule_movement)



[Charles Stewart Parnell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Stewart_Parnell) (1846–1891) addressing a meeting. The [Irish Parliamentary Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Parliamentary_Party) was formed in 1882 by Parnell.

From the [Act of Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Act_of_Union_1800) on 1 January 1801, until 6 December 1922, the island of Ireland was part of the [United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_of_Great_Britain_and_Ireland). During the [Great Famine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Famine_\(Ireland\)), from 1845 to 1849, the island's population of over 8 million fell by 30%. One million Irish died of starvation and disease and another 1.5 million emigrated, mostly to the United States.[^cite_note-37] This set the pattern of emigration for the century to come, resulting in constant population decline up to the 1960s.[^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40]&#x20;

From 1874, and particularly under [Charles Stewart Parnell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Stewart_Parnell) from 1880, the [Irish Parliamentary Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Parliamentary_Party) gained prominence. This was firstly through widespread agrarian agitation via the [Irish Land League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Land_League), which won [land reforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_reforms) for tenants in the form of the [Irish Land Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Land_Acts), and secondly through its attempts to achieve [Home Rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Home_Rule_Movement), via two unsuccessful bills which would have granted Ireland limited national autonomy. These led to "grass-roots" control of national affairs, under the [Local Government Act 1898](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_Government_\(Ireland\)_Act_1898), that had been in the hands of landlord-dominated [grand juries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_juries) of the [Protestant Ascendancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant_Ascendancy).&#x20;

Home Rule seemed certain when the [Parliament Act 1911](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_Act_1911) abolished the veto of the [House of Lords](13658.md), and [John Redmond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Redmond) secured the [Third Home Rule Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_Rule_Act_1914) in 1914. However, the [Unionist movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unionism_in_Ireland) had been growing since 1886 among Irish Protestants after the introduction of the first home rule bill, fearing discrimination and loss of economic and social privileges if [Irish Catholics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Catholics) achieved real political power. During the late 19th and early 20th centuries, unionism was particularly strong in parts of [Ulster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster), where industrialisation was more common in contrast to the more agrarian rest of the island, and where the Protestant population was more prominent, with a majority in four counties.[^cite_note-41] Under the leadership of the Dublin-born [Sir Edward Carson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Carson) of the [Irish Unionist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Unionist_Party) and the Ulsterman [Sir James Craig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Craig,_1st_Viscount_Craigavon) of the [Ulster Unionist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_Unionist_Party), unionists became strongly militant, forming [Ulster Volunteers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_Volunteers) in order to oppose "the Coercion of Ulster".[^cite_note-42] After the Home Rule Bill passed parliament in May 1914, to avoid rebellion with Ulster, the British Prime Minister [H. H. Asquith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._H._Asquith) introduced an [Amending Bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Parliamentary_Party#Home_Rule_succeeds) reluctantly conceded to by the Irish Party leadership. This provided for the temporary exclusion of Ulster from the workings of the bill for a trial period of six years, with an as yet undecided new set of measures to be introduced for the area to be temporarily excluded.&#x20;

### Revolution and steps to independence



Damage in Dublin city centre following the 1916 [Easter Rising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Rising)

Though it received the [Royal Assent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Assent) and was placed on the statute books in 1914, the implementation of the [Third Home Rule Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_Rule_Act_1914) was suspended until after the [First World War](4764461.md) which defused the threat of civil war in Ireland. With the hope of ensuring the implementation of the Act at the end of the war through [Ireland's engagement in the war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireland_and_World_War_I), Redmond and the Irish [National Volunteers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Volunteers) supported the UK and its [Allies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_I). 175,000 men joined [Irish regiments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_regiment) of the [10th (Irish)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/10th_\(Irish\)_Division_\(United_Kingdom\)) and [16th (Irish)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/16th_\(Irish\)_Division) divisions of the [New British Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitchener%27s_Army), while Unionists joined the [36th (Ulster)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/36th_\(Ulster\)_Division) divisions.[^cite_note-43]&#x20;

The remainder of the [Irish Volunteers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Volunteers), who refused Redmond and opposed any support of the UK, launched an armed insurrection against British rule in the 1916 [Easter Rising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Rising), together with the [Irish Citizen Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Citizen_Army) and other militant Irish nationalist groups. This commenced on 24 April 1916 with the [Proclamation of the Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proclamation_of_the_Irish_Republic). After a week of heavy fighting, primarily in Dublin, the surviving rebels were forced to surrender their positions. The majority were imprisoned, with fifteen of the prisoners (including most of the leaders and all of the Proclamation signatories) executed for taking part in armed rebellion and waging war against the British Crown, court-martialed under the [Defence of the Realm Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defence_of_the_Realm_Act_1914). This included [Patrick Pearse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Pearse), the Commander-in-Chief of the Irish rebel forces, and [James Connolly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Connolly), socialist and founder of the [Industrial Workers of the World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Workers_of_the_World) union and both the Irish and Scottish Labour movements. The Rising, and the executions and arrests, together with the [Conscription Crisis of 1918](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conscription_Crisis_of_1918), had the effect of changing public opinion in Ireland against the British Government.[^cite_note-44]&#x20;

In January 1919, after the December [1918 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1918_United_Kingdom_general_election_in_Ireland), 73 of Ireland's 105 [Members of Parliament](3776360.md) (MPs) elected were [Sinn Féin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Sinn_F%C3%A9in) members who were elected on a platform of [abstentionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstentionism) from the [British House of Commons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_House_of_Commons). In January 1919, they set up an Irish parliament called [Dáil Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A1il_%C3%89ireann_\(Irish_Republic\)). This [First Dáil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_D%C3%A1il) issued a [declaration of independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declaration_of_independence) and proclaimed an [Irish Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Republic). The declaration was mainly a restatement of the [1916 Proclamation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proclamation_of_the_Irish_Republic) with the additional provision that Ireland was no longer a part of the United Kingdom. The Irish Republic's [Ministry of Dáil Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_D%C3%A1il_%C3%89ireann) sent a delegation under [Ceann Comhairle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceann_Comhairle) (Head of Council, or Speaker, of the Dáil) [Seán T. O'Kelly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Se%C3%A1n_T._O%27Kelly) to the [Paris Peace Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Peace_Conference,_1919) of 1919, but it was not admitted.&#x20;



[Leinster House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leinster_House), Dublin. In 1922 a new parliament called the [Oireachtas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oireachtas_of_the_Irish_Free_State) was established, of which [Dáil Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A1il_%C3%89ireann) became the [lower house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_house).

After the [War of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_War_of_Independence) and truce called in July 1921, representatives of the [British government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_government) and the five Irish treaty delegates, led by [Arthur Griffith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Griffith), [Robert Barton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Barton) and [Michael Collins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Collins_\(Irish_leader\)), negotiated the [Anglo-Irish Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Irish_Treaty) in London from 11 October to 6 December 1921. The Irish delegates set up headquarters at [Hans Place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Place) in [Knightsbridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knightsbridge), and it was here in private discussions that the decision was taken on 5 December to recommend the treaty to Dáil Éireann. On 7 January 1922, the [Second Dáil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_D%C3%A1il) [ratified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Irish_Treaty_D%C3%A1il_vote) the Treaty by 64 votes to 57.[^cite_note-45]&#x20;

In accordance with the treaty, on 6 December 1922 the entire island of Ireland became a self-governing [Dominion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominion) called the Irish Free State (*Saorstát Éireann*). Under the [Constitution of the Irish Free State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_the_Irish_Free_State), the [Parliament of Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_Northern_Ireland) had the option to leave the Irish Free State one month later and return to the United Kingdom. During the intervening period, the powers of the [Parliament of the Irish Free State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_the_Irish_Free_State) and [Executive Council of the Irish Free State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Council_of_the_Irish_Free_State) did not extend to Northern Ireland. Northern Ireland exercised its right under the treaty to leave the new Dominion and rejoined the United Kingdom on 8 December 1922. It did so by making an address to the King requesting, "that the powers of the Parliament and Government of the Irish Free State shall no longer extend to Northern Ireland."[^cite_note-46] The Irish Free State was a [constitutional monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_monarchy) [sharing a monarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_union) with the United Kingdom and other Dominions of the [British Commonwealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_of_Nations). The country had a [governor-general](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governor-General_of_the_Irish_Free_State) (representing the monarch), a [bicameral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicameralism) parliament, a cabinet called the "Executive Council", and a prime minister called the [President of the Executive Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_Executive_Council_of_the_Irish_Free_State).&#x20;

### Irish Civil War

Main article: [Irish Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Civil_War)



[Éamon de Valera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89amon_de_Valera), Irish political leader, pictured outside Ennis Courthouse in 1917. He would later be involved in introducing the 1937 [Constitution of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Ireland).

The Irish Civil War (June 1922 – May 1923) was the consequence of the ratification of the Anglo-Irish Treaty and the creation of the Irish Free State.[^cite_note-47] Anti-treaty forces, led by [Éamon de Valera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89amon_de_Valera), objected to the fact that acceptance of the treaty abolished the [Irish Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Republic) of 1919 to which they had sworn loyalty, arguing in the face of public support for the settlement that the "people have no right to do wrong".[^cite_note-48] They objected most to the fact that the state would remain part of the [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire) and that members of the [Free State Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oireachtas_of_the_Irish_Free_State) would have to swear what the anti-treaty side saw as an oath of fidelity to the British king. Pro-treaty forces, led by [Michael Collins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Collins_\(Irish_leader\)), argued that the treaty gave "not the ultimate freedom that all nations aspire to and develop, but the freedom to achieve it".[^cite_note-49]&#x20;

At the start of the war, the [Irish Republican Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Republican_Army_\(1919%E2%80%931922\)) (IRA) split into two opposing camps: a pro-treaty IRA and an [anti-treaty IRA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Republican_Army_\(1922%E2%80%931969\)). The pro-treaty IRA disbanded and joined the new [National Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Army_\(Ireland\)). However, because the anti-treaty IRA lacked an effective command structure and because of the pro-treaty forces' defensive tactics throughout the war, Michael Collins and his pro-treaty forces were able to build up an army with many tens of thousands of World War I veterans from the 1922 disbanded [Irish regiments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_regiments#Irish_regiments_of_the_British_Army) of the British Army, capable of overwhelming the anti-treatyists. British supplies of artillery, aircraft, machine-guns and ammunition boosted pro-treaty forces, and the threat of a return of Crown forces to the Free State removed any doubts about the necessity of enforcing the treaty. Lack of public support for the anti-treaty forces (often called the Irregulars) and the determination of the government to overcome the Irregulars contributed significantly to their defeat.&#x20;

### Constitution of Ireland 1937



[The Constitution Committee meeting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_the_Irish_Free_State) at the [Shelbourne Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shelbourne_Hotel), Dublin

Following a national plebiscite in July 1937, the new [Constitution of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Ireland) (*Bunreacht na hÉireann*) [came into force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adoption_of_the_Constitution_of_Ireland) on 29 December 1937.[^cite_note-50] This replaced the [Constitution of the Irish Free State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_the_Irish_Free_State) and declared that the name of the state is *Éire*, or "Ireland" in the English language.[^cite_note-51] While [Articles 2 and 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Articles_2_and_3_of_the_Constitution_of_Ireland) of the Constitution defined the national territory to be the whole island, they also confined the state's jurisdiction to the area that had been the Irish Free State. The former Irish Free State government had abolished the Office of [Governor-General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governor-General_of_the_Irish_Free_State) in December 1936. Although the constitution established the office of [President of Ireland](24676.md), the question over whether Ireland was a republic remained open. Diplomats were accredited to the king, but the president exercised all internal functions of a head of state.[^cite_note-Daly_2007-32] For instance, the President gave assent to new laws with his own authority, without reference to King [George VI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_VI) who was only an "organ", that was provided for by statute law.&#x20;

[Ireland remained neutral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_neutrality_during_World_War_II) during World War II, a period it described as [The Emergency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Emergency_\(Ireland\)).[^cite_note-52] Ireland's [Dominion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominion) status was terminated with the passage of [The Republic of Ireland Act 1948](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Republic_of_Ireland_Act_1948), which came into force on 18 April 1949 and declared that the state was a republic.[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-54] At the time, a declaration of a republic terminated Commonwealth membership. This rule was changed 10 days after Ireland declared itself a republic, with the [London Declaration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Declaration) of 28 April 1949. Ireland did not reapply when the rules were altered to permit republics to join. Later, the [Crown of Ireland Act 1542](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_of_Ireland_Act_1542) was repealed in Ireland by the [Statute Law Revision (Pre-Union Irish Statutes) Act 1962](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute_Law_Revision_\(Pre-Union_Irish_Statutes\)_Act_1962).[^cite_note-55]&#x20;

### Recent history



In 1973 Ireland joined the [European Economic Community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Economic_Community) along with the United Kingdom and Denmark. The country signed the [Lisbon Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisbon_Treaty) in 2007.

Ireland became a member of the [United Nations](31769.md) in December 1955, after having been denied membership because of its [neutral stance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_neutrality_during_World_War_II) during the Second World War and not supporting the [Allied cause](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II).[^cite_note-56] At the time, joining the UN involved a commitment to using force to deter aggression by one state against another if the UN thought it was necessary.[^cite_note-57]&#x20;

Interest towards membership of the [European Communities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Communities) (EC) developed in Ireland during the 1950s, with consideration also given to membership of the [European Free Trade Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Free_Trade_Area). As the United Kingdom intended on EC membership, Ireland applied for membership in July 1961 due to the substantial economic linkages with the United Kingdom. The founding EC members remained sceptical regarding Ireland's economic capacity, neutrality, and unattractive [protectionist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protectionist) policy.[^cite_note-58] Many Irish economists and politicians realised that economic policy reform was necessary. The prospect of EC membership became doubtful in 1963 when French President General [Charles de Gaulle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_de_Gaulle) stated that France opposed Britain's accession, thereby halting negotiations with other candidate countries. In 1969 his successor, [Georges Pompidou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Pompidou), was not opposed to British and Irish membership. Negotiations began and in 1972 the [Treaty of Accession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Accession_1972) was signed. A [referendum was held later that year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Amendment_of_the_Constitution_of_Ireland) which confirmed Ireland's entry into the bloc, and it finally joined the EC as a member state on 1 January 1973.[^cite_note-59]&#x20;

The economic crisis of the late 1970s was fuelled by the [Fianna Fáil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fianna_F%C3%A1il) government's budget, the abolition of the car tax, excessive borrowing, and global economic instability including the [1979 oil crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1979_oil_crisis).[^cite_note-60] There were significant policy changes from 1989 onwards, with economic reform, tax cuts, welfare reform, an increase in competition, and a ban on borrowing to fund current spending. This policy began in 1989–1992 by the Fianna Fáil/[Progressive Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_Democrats) government, and continued by the subsequent Fianna Fáil/[Labour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_Party_\(Ireland\)) government and [Fine Gael](11252.md)/Labour/[Democratic Left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Left_\(Ireland\)) government. Ireland became one of the world's fastest growing economies by the late 1990s in what was known as the [Celtic Tiger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_Tiger) period, which lasted until the [Great Recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Recession). Since 2014, Ireland has experienced increased economic activity.[^cite_note-61]&#x20;

In the Northern Ireland question, the British and Irish governments started to seek a peaceful resolution to the violent conflict involving many [paramilitaries](146730.md) and the [British Army](4887.md) in Northern Ireland known as "[The Troubles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Troubles)". A peace settlement for Northern Ireland, known as the [Good Friday Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_Friday_Agreement), was approved in 1998 in referendums north and south of the border. As part of the peace settlement, the territorial claim to Northern Ireland in [Articles 2 and 3 of the Constitution of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Articles_2_and_3_of_the_Constitution_of_Ireland) was removed by referendum. In its white paper on [Brexit](41688778.md), the United Kingdom government reiterated its commitment to the Good Friday Agreement. With regard to Northern Ireland's status, it said that the UK Government's "clearly-stated preference is to retain Northern Ireland's current constitutional position: as part of the UK, but with strong links to Ireland".[^cite_note-62]&#x20;

## Geography

Main article: [Geography of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Ireland)



The [Cliffs of Moher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cliffs_of_Moher) on the Atlantic coast



[MacGillycuddy's Reeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacGillycuddy%27s_Reeks), Ireland's highest mountain range

The state extends over an area of about five-sixths (70,273 km<sup>2</sup> or 27,133 sq mi) of the island of [Ireland](147575.md) (84,421 km<sup>2</sup> or 32,595 sq mi), with [Northern Ireland](21265.md) constituting the remainder. The island is bounded to the north and west by the Atlantic Ocean and to the northeast by the [North Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Channel_\(Great_Britain_and_Ireland\)). To the east, the [Irish Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Sea) connects to the Atlantic Ocean via [St George's Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_George%27s_Channel) and the [Celtic Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_Sea) to the southwest.&#x20;

The western landscape mostly consists of rugged cliffs, hills and mountains. The central lowlands are extensively covered with glacial deposits of clay and sand, as well as significant areas of [bogland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogland) and several lakes. The highest point is [Carrauntoohil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrauntoohil) (1,038.6 m or 3,407 ft), located in the [MacGillycuddy's Reeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacGillycuddy%27s_Reeks) mountain range in the southwest. [River Shannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Shannon), which traverses the central lowlands, is the longest river in Ireland at 386 kilometres or 240 miles in length. The west coast is more rugged than the east, with numerous islands, [peninsulas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peninsula), [headlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Headland) and [bays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bay).&#x20;

Ireland is one of the least forested countries in Europe.[^cite_note-63] Until the end of the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), the land was heavily forested. Native species include [deciduous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deciduous) trees such as [oak](39696.md), [ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraxinus_excelsior), [hazel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corylus_avellana), [birch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birch), [alder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alnus_glutinosa), [willow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willow), [aspen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Populus_tremula), [elm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wych_elm), [rowan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorbus_aucuparia) and [hawthorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crataegus_monogyna), as well as [evergreen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evergreen) trees such [Scots pine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scots_pine), [yew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxus_baccata), [holly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilex_aquifolium) and [strawberry trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arbutus_unedo).[^cite_note-64] The growth of [blanket bog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blanket_bog) and the extensive clearing of woodland for farming are believed to be the main causes of [deforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation).[^cite_note-65] Today, only about 10% of Ireland is woodland,[^cite_note-forest-66] most of which is non-native [conifer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conifer) [plantations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plantation), and only 2% of which is native woodland.[^cite_note-67][^cite_note-68] The average woodland cover in European countries is over 33%.[^cite_note-forest-66] According to *[Coillte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coillte)*, a state-owned forestry business, the country's climate gives Ireland one of the fastest growth rates for forests in Europe.[^cite_note-69] [Hedgerows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedgerows), which are traditionally used to define land boundaries, are an important substitute for woodland habitat, providing refuge for native wild flora and a wide range of insect, bird and mammal species.[^cite_note-70] It is home to two terrestrial ecoregions: [Celtic broadleaf forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_broadleaf_forests) and [North Atlantic moist mixed forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Atlantic_moist_mixed_forests).[^cite_note-71]&#x20;

[Agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture) accounts for about 64% of the total land area.[^cite_note-72] This has resulted in limited land to preserve natural habitats, in particular for larger wild mammals with greater territorial requirements.[^cite_note-73] The long history of agricultural production coupled with modern agricultural methods, such as [pesticide](48340.md) and [fertiliser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertiliser) use, has placed pressure on [biodiversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity).[^cite_note-CIA-74]&#x20;

### Climate

Main article: [Climate of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_of_Ireland)



[Wicklow Mountains National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wicklow_Mountains_National_Park)

The [Atlantic Ocean](698.md) and the warming influence of the [Gulf Stream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_Stream) affect weather patterns in Ireland.[^cite_note-climate-75] Temperatures differ regionally, with central and eastern areas tending to be more extreme. Due to a [temperate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperate_climate) [oceanic climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanic_climate), temperatures typically range from a winter minimum of −5 °C (23 °F) to a summer maximum of 26 °C (79 °F).[^cite_note-76] The highest temperature recorded in Ireland was 33.3 °C (91.9 °F) on 26 June 1887 at [Kilkenny Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilkenny_Castle) in Kilkenny, while the lowest temperature recorded was −19.1 °C (−2.4 °F) at [Markree Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markree_Castle) in Sligo.[^cite_note-77] Rainfall is more prevalent during winter months and less so during the early months of summer. Southwestern areas experience the most rainfall as a result of south westerly winds, while [Dublin](8504.md) receives the least. Sunshine duration is highest in the southeast of the country.[^cite_note-climate-75] The far north and west are two of the windiest regions in Europe, with great potential for [wind energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_energy) generation.[^cite_note-78]&#x20;

Ireland normally gets between 1100 and 1600 hours of sunshine each year, most areas averaging between 3.25 and 3.75 hours a day. The sunniest months are May and June, which average between 5 and 6.5 hours per day over most of the country. The extreme southeast gets most sunshine, averaging over 7 hours a day in early summer. December is the dullest month, with an average daily sunshine ranging from about 1 hour in the north to almost 2 hours in the extreme southeast. The sunniest summer in the 100 years from 1881 to 1980 was 1887, according to measurements made at the Phoenix Park in Dublin; 1980 was the dullest.[^cite_note-79]&#x20;

## Government and politics

Main articles: [Politics of the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics_of_the_Republic_of_Ireland) and [Elections in the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elections_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland)

Catherine Connolly

[Catherine Connolly](25296931.md)\
[President](24676.md)

Micheál Martin

[Micheál Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miche%C3%A1l_Martin)\
[Taoiseach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoiseach)

Ireland is a constitutional republic with a [parliamentary system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_system) of government. The [Oireachtas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oireachtas) is the [bicameral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicameral) national parliament composed of the [President of Ireland](24676.md) and the two Houses of the Oireachtas: [Dáil Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A1il_%C3%89ireann) (House of Representatives) and [Seanad Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seanad_%C3%89ireann) (Senate).[^cite_note-80] [Áras an Uachtaráin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%81ras_an_Uachtar%C3%A1in) is the [official residence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Official_residence) of the President of Ireland, while the houses of the Oireachtas meet at [Leinster House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leinster_House) in [Dublin](8504.md).&#x20;

The President serves as [head of state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_of_state), is elected for a seven-year term, and may be re-elected once. Restrictive [nomination rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomination_rules) for presidential candidates resulted in 12.9% [spoilt votes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoilt_vote) during the [2025 Irish presidential election](75903783.md).[^cite_note-p963-81] The President is primarily a [figurehead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Figurehead_\(metaphor\)), but is entrusted with certain constitutional powers with the advice of the [Council of State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_State_\(Ireland\)). The office has absolute discretion in some areas, such as referring a bill to the Supreme Court for a judgment on its constitutionality.[^cite_note-82] [Catherine Connolly](25296931.md) became the tenth President of Ireland on 11 November 2025.[^cite_note-83]&#x20;

The [Taoiseach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoiseach) (Prime Minister) serves as the [head of government](37570.md) and is appointed by the President upon the nomination of the Dáil. Most Taoisigh have served as the leader of the political party that gains the most seats in national elections. It has become customary for [coalitions](6036.md) to form a government, as there has not been a single-party government since 1989.[^cite_note-84]&#x20;

The Dáil has 174 members ([Teachtaí Dála](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teachta_D%C3%A1la)) elected to represent multi-seat [constituencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A1il_constituencies) under the system of [proportional representation](23298.md) by means of the [single transferable vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_transferable_vote). The Seanad is composed of sixty members, with eleven [nominated by](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nominated_members_of_Seanad_%C3%89ireann) the Taoiseach, six elected by two [university constituencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_constituencies), and 43 elected by public representatives from panels of candidates established on a vocational basis.&#x20;

The [government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Ireland) is constitutionally limited to fifteen members. No more than two members can be selected from the Seanad, and the Taoiseach, [Tánaiste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C3%A1naiste) (deputy prime minister) and [Minister for Finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Finance_\(Ireland\)) must be members of the Dáil. The Dáil must be dissolved within five years of its first meeting following the previous election,[^cite_note-85] and a general election for members of the Dáil must take place no later than thirty days after the dissolution. In accordance with the [Constitution of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Ireland), parliamentary elections must be held at least every seven years, though a lower limit may be set by statute law. The current government is a coalition of [Fianna Fáil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fianna_F%C3%A1il), [Fine Gael](11252.md), and some [independents](61259001.md) with [Micheál Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miche%C3%A1l_Martin) of Fianna Fáil as Taoiseach and [Simon Harris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Harris) of Fine Gael as Tánaiste. Opposition parties in the current Dáil are [Sinn Féin](28175.md), the [Labour Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_Party_\(Ireland\)), [People Before Profit–Solidarity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People_Before_Profit%E2%80%93Solidarity), [Social Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_Democrats_\(Ireland\)), [Aontú](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aont%C3%BA), [The Green Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Party_\(Ireland\)) as well as a number of independents.&#x20;

Ireland has been a [member state of the European Union](10890716.md) since 1973. Citizens of the United Kingdom can freely enter the country without a passport due to the [Common Travel Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Travel_Area), which is a passport-free zone comprising the islands of Ireland, Great Britain, the [Isle of Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isle_of_Man) and the [Channel Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_Islands). However, some identification is required at airports and seaports.&#x20;

The [Regimes of the World index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V-Dem_Democracy_Indices#Regimes_of_the_World) classified Ireland as a [liberal democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_democracy) in year 2024.[^cite_note-j496-86] [Corruption in the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corruption_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland) includes [clientelism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clientelism) and [pork barrel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pork_barrel) politics.[^cite_note-j247-87]&#x20;

### Local government

Main article: [Local government in the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_government_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland)



[Government Buildings Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Buildings)



[Dáil Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A1il_%C3%89ireann), [lower house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_house) Chamber

The [Local Government (Ireland) Act 1898](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_Government_\(Ireland\)_Act_1898) was the founding statute of the present system of local government, while the [Twentieth Amendment to the constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twentieth_Amendment_of_the_Constitution_of_Ireland) of 1999 provided for its constitutional recognition. The twenty-six traditional [counties of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counties_of_Ireland) are the basis of the local government areas, with the traditional counties of [Cork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Cork), [Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Dublin) and [Galway](92632.md) containing two or more local government areas. The [Local Government Act 2001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_Government_Act_2001), as amended by the [Local Government Reform Act 2014](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_Government_Reform_Act_2014),[^cite_note-LocalGovReform2014-88] provides for a system of [thirty-one local authorities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_government_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland) – twenty-six county councils, two city and county councils, and three city councils.[^cite_note-LocalGovReform2014-88] Counties (with the exception of the three counties in Dublin) are divided into [municipal districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Municipal_district). A second local government tier of [town councils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Town_council_\(Ireland\)) was abolished in 2014.&#x20;

### Law

Main articles: [Law of the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_the_Republic_of_Ireland), [Courts of the Republic of Ireland](670507.md), and [Law enforcement in the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_enforcement_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland)



The [Four Courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Courts), completed in 1802, is the principal building for civil courts.

Ireland has a [common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law) [legal system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_system) with a written constitution that provides for a [parliamentary democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_democracy). The court system consists of the [Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Ireland), the [Court of Appeal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_Appeal_\(Ireland\)), the [High Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Court_\(Ireland\)), the [Circuit Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circuit_Court_\(Ireland\)) and the [District Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/District_Court_\(Ireland\)), all of which apply the [Irish law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_the_Republic_of_Ireland) and hear both civil and criminal matters. Trials for serious offences must usually be held before a [jury](44720.md). The High Court, Court of Appeal and the Supreme Court have authority, by means of [judicial review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judicial_review_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland), to determine the compatibility of laws and activities of other institutions of the state with the constitution and the law. Except in exceptional circumstances, court hearings must occur in public.[^cite_note-89][^cite_note-90]&#x20;



The [Criminal Courts of Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_Courts_of_Justice,_Dublin) is the principal building for criminal courts.

The [Garda Síochána](383207.md) (*lit.* Guardians of the Peace), more commonly referred to as the Gardaí, is the state's civilian police force. The force is responsible for all aspects of civil policing, both in terms of territory and infrastructure. It is headed by the Garda Commissioner, who is appointed by the Government. Most uniformed members do not routinely carry [firearms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firearms_legislation_in_Ireland). Standard policing is traditionally carried out by uniformed officers equipped only with a [baton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baton_\(law_enforcement\)) and [pepper spray](157302.md).[^cite_note-91]&#x20;

The [Military Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Police_Corps_\(Ireland\)) is the corps of the [Irish Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Army) responsible for the provision of policing service personnel and providing a military police presence to forces while on exercise and deployment. In wartime, additional tasks include the provision of a traffic control organisation to allow rapid movement of military formations to their mission areas. Other wartime roles include control of [prisoners of war](25008.md) and refugees.[^cite_note-92]&#x20;

Ireland's [citizenship laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_nationality_law) relate to "the island of Ireland", including islands and seas, thereby extending them to [Northern Ireland](21265.md), which is part of the United Kingdom. Therefore, anyone born in Northern Ireland who meets the requirements for being an Irish citizen, such as birth on the island of Ireland to an Irish or British citizen parent or a parent who is entitled to live in Northern Ireland or the Republic without restriction on their residency,[^cite_note-93] may exercise an entitlement to Irish citizenship, such as an [Irish passport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_passport).[^cite_note-94]&#x20;

In 2024, Ireland was second only to [Iceland](14531.md) in the list of the world's most peaceful countries.[^cite_note-peace2024-17]&#x20;

### Foreign relations

Main article: [Foreign relations of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_Ireland)

See also: [Ireland–NATO relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireland%E2%80%93NATO_relations)



Taoiseach [Micheál Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miche%C3%A1l_Martin) and [US President](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_President) [Donald Trump](4848272.md), at the [White House](33057.md), [Washington, D.C.](108956.md) in March 2025

Foreign relations are substantially influenced by membership of the European Union, although bilateral relations with the United Kingdom and United States are also important.[^cite_note-95] It held the [Presidency of the Council of the European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidency_of_the_Council_of_the_European_Union) on six occasions, most recently from January to June 2013.[^cite_note-96]&#x20;

Ireland tends towards independence in foreign policy; thus the country is not a member of [NATO](21133.md) and has a [longstanding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Emergency_\(Ireland\)#Neutrality) policy of military neutrality. This policy has led to the [Irish Defence Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Defence_Forces) contributing to peace-keeping missions with the United Nations since 1960, including during the [Congo Crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congo_Crisis) and subsequently in [Cyprus](5593.md), [Lebanon](17771.md) and [Bosnia and Herzegovina](3463.md).[^cite_note-97]&#x20;

Despite [Irish neutrality during World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_neutrality_during_World_War_II), Ireland had more than 50,000 [participants in the war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Participants_in_World_War_II#Ireland) through enlistment in the British armed forces. During the [Cold War](325329.md), Irish military policy, while ostensibly neutral, was biased towards NATO.[^cite_note-98] During the [Cuban Missile Crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_Missile_Crisis), [Seán Lemass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Se%C3%A1n_Lemass) authorised the search of Cuban and Czechoslovak aircraft passing through Shannon and passed the information to the [CIA](5183633.md).[^cite_note-99] Ireland's air facilities were used by the United States military for the delivery of military personnel involved in the [2003 invasion of Iraq](201936.md) through [Shannon Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_Airport). The airport had previously been used for the [United States invasion of Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_invasion_of_Afghanistan) in 2001, as well as the [First Gulf War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_War).[^cite_note-100]&#x20;

Since 1999, Ireland has been a member of NATO's [Partnership for Peace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partnership_for_Peace) (PfP) program and NATO's [Euro-Atlantic Partnership Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro-Atlantic_Partnership_Council) (EAPC), which is aimed at creating trust between NATO and other states in Europe and the former Soviet Union.[^cite_note-101][^cite_note-102]&#x20;

### Military

Main article: [Defence Forces (Ireland)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defence_Forces_\(Ireland\))

See also: [Irish neutrality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_neutrality)



[Irish Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Army) soldiers as part of [Kosovo Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kosovo_Force), 2010

Ireland is a [neutral country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutral_country),[^cite_note-103] and has "triple-lock" rules governing the participation of Irish troops in conflict zones, whereby approval must be given by the UN, the [Dáil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A1il) and Government.[^cite_note-104] Accordingly, its military role is limited to national self-defence and participation in [United Nations peacekeeping](15652027.md).&#x20;

The Irish Defence Forces (*Óglaigh na hÉireann*) are made up of the [Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Army), [Naval Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Naval_Service), [Air Corps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Air_Corps) and [Reserve Defence Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reserve_Defence_Forces). It is small but well equipped, with almost 10,000 full-time military personnel and over 2,000 in reserve.[^cite_note-105][^cite_note-106] Daily deployments of the Defence Forces cover [aid to civil power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_aid_to_the_civil_power) operations, protection and patrol of Irish territorial waters and [EEZ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EEZ) by the Irish Naval Service, and UN, EU and [PfP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partnership_for_Peace) peace-keeping missions. By 1996, over 40,000 Irish service personnel had served in international UN peacekeeping missions.[^cite_note-107]&#x20;

The Irish Air Corps is the air component of the Defence Forces and operates sixteen fixed wing aircraft and eight helicopters. The Irish Naval Service is Ireland's navy, and operates six [patrol ships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrol_ship), and smaller numbers of inflatable boats and training vessels, and has armed boarding parties capable of seizing a ship and a special unit of [frogmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frogman). The military includes the Reserve Defence Forces ([Army Reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Army_Reserve_\(Ireland\)) and [Naval Service Reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_Service_Reserve)) for part-time reservists. Ireland's special forces include the [Army Ranger Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Army_Ranger_Wing), which trains and operates with international special operations units. The President is the formal Supreme Commander of the Defence Forces, but in practice these Forces answer to the Government via the [Minister for Defence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Defence_\(Ireland\)).[^cite_note-108]&#x20;

In 2017, Ireland signed the United Nations [Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_on_the_Prohibition_of_Nuclear_Weapons).[^cite_note-109]&#x20;

## Economy

Main article: [Economy of the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_the_Republic_of_Ireland)

Ireland is an open economy and ranks first for "high-value" [foreign direct investment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_direct_investment) (FDI) flows.[^cite_note-110] Ireland ranks 5th of 187 ([IMF](15251.md)) and 6th of 175 ([World Bank](36752.md)) in [GDP per capita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_\(nominal\)_per_capita) as well as ranking in the top ten for [GNI per capita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GNI_\(nominal\)_per_capita). An alternative metric, known as [modified gross national income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modified_gross_national_income) (GNI), was created by the [Central Statistics Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Statistics_Office_\(Ireland\)) and is used by the Irish government to give a view of activity in the domestic economy after stripping out large multinational export movements which can often relate to intangible assets.[^cite_note-111] This is particularly relevant in Ireland's economy, as GDP disproportionately includes income from non-Irish owned companies, which often flows out of Ireland.[^cite_note-112] Foreign multinationals are the main driver of Ireland's economy, employing a quarter of the private sector workforce,[^cite_note-113] and paying 80% of Irish corporate taxes.[^cite_note-114][^cite_note-115][^cite_note-116] 14 of Ireland's top 20 firms (by 2017 turnover) are US-based multinationals[^cite_note-itimes-117] and 80% of foreign multinationals in Ireland are from the US.[^cite_note-118][^cite_note-119][^cite_note-itimes-117]&#x20;



Ireland is part of the EU (dark blue & purple) and [eurozone](184391.md) (dark blue).

Ireland adopted the euro currency in 2002 along with eleven other [EU member states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Member_states_of_the_European_Union).[^cite_note-CIA-74][^cite_note-120]&#x20;

Following the [Great Recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Recession) and the bursting of the [Irish property bubble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_property_bubble), the country officially exited recession in 2010, driven by a growth in exports from US multinationals in Ireland.[^cite_note-121] However, due to a rise in the cost of public borrowing due to government guarantees of private banking debt, the Irish government accepted an €85 billion programme of assistance from the EU, [International Monetary Fund](15251.md) (IMF) and bilateral loans from the United Kingdom, Sweden and Denmark.[^cite_note-122] Following three years of contraction, the economy grew by 0.7% in 2011 and 0.9% in 2012.[^cite_note-123]&#x20;

The [median equivalised disposable income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Median_income) was \$31,392 (PPP) per year in 2021.[^cite_note-y148-124] The unemployment rate was 14.7% in 2012, including 18.5% among recent immigrants.[^cite_note-125] In March 2016 the [unemployment rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unemployment_rate) was reported by the [CSO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Statistics_Office_\(Ireland\)) to be 8.6%, down from a peak unemployment rate of 15.1% in February 2012.[^cite_note-126] In addition to unemployment, net emigration from Ireland between 2008 and 2013 totalled 120,100,[^cite_note-Financial_Times-127] or some 2.6% of the total population according to the [Census of Ireland 2011](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Census_of_Ireland_2011). One-third of the emigrants were aged between 15 and 24.[^cite_note-Financial_Times-127] By November 2022, unemployment had fallen back to 4.4%.[^cite_note-128]&#x20;

Ireland exited its EU-IMF bailout programme on 15 December 2013.[^cite_note-129] Having implemented budget cuts, reforms and sold assets, Ireland was again able to access debt markets. Since then, Ireland has been able to sell long term bonds at record rates.[^cite_note-130] However, the stabilisation of the Irish credit bubble required a large transfer of debt from the private sector balance sheet (highest OECD leverage), to the public sector balance sheet (almost unleveraged, pre-crisis), via Irish bank bailouts and public deficit spending.[^cite_note-131][^cite_note-132] The transfer of this debt means that Ireland, in 2017, still has one of the highest levels of both public sector indebtedness, and private sector indebtedness, in the EU-28/OECD.[^cite_note-133][^cite_note-134][^cite_note-135][^cite_note-136][^cite_note-137][^cite_note-138]&#x20;

Ireland became one of the main destinations for US pharmaceutical [corporate tax inversions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporation_tax_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland#Corporate_tax_inversions) from 2009 to 2016.[^cite_note-139][^cite_note-140] The country has also become the largest foreign location for large US technology multinationals (i.e. Apple, Google, Microsoft, Facebook), which delivered a GDP growth rate of 26.3% (and GNP growth rate of 18.7%) in 2015. This growth was subsequently shown to be due to Apple restructuring its "[double Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_Irish)" subsidiary (Apple Sales International, currently under threat of a [€13bn EU "illegal state aid" fine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EU_illegal_State_aid_case_against_Apple_in_Ireland) for preferential tax treatment).&#x20;

### Taxation policy

The transformation of Ireland's tax policy started with the creation of a 10% low-tax [special economic zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_economic_zone), called the [International Financial Services Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Financial_Services_Centre,_Dublin) (IFSC), in 1987.[^cite_note-141] In 2003, the IFSC was expanded nationwide with the reduction of Irish corporation tax from 32% to 12.5%.[^cite_note-142][^cite_note-143] This accelerated the later stages of Ireland's transition from a predominantly agricultural economy into a [knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge_economy) and [service economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Service_economy) focused on attracting mainly US multinationals from high-tech, life sciences, and financial services industries seeking to avail of Ireland's low corporation tax rates and favourable [corporate tax system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporation_tax_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland).&#x20;

The multinational tax planning schemes corporations use in Ireland have materially affected Irish economic statistics. This reached a climax with what [Paul Krugman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Krugman) termed [leprechaun economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leprechaun_economics) with double digit GDP growth rates in 2015 relating largely to Apple restructuring its Irish subsidiaries. The [Central Bank of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Bank_of_Ireland) introduced additional statistics based on a modified gross national income, to remove these distortions.[^cite_note-144][^cite_note-145][^cite_note-146][^cite_note-147][^cite_note-148]&#x20;

From the creation of the [IFSC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Financial_Services_Centre,_Dublin), the country experienced strong and sustained economic growth which fuelled a dramatic rise in Irish consumer borrowing and spending, and Irish construction and investment, which became known as the [Celtic Tiger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_Tiger) period.[^cite_note-aw-149][^cite_note-150] By 2007, Ireland had the highest private sector debt in the OECD with a household debt-to-disposable income ratio of 190%. Global capital markets, which had financed Ireland's build-up of debt in the Celtic Tiger period by enabling Irish banks to borrow in excess of the domestic deposit base to over 180% at peak,[^cite_note-151] withdrew support in the [2008 financial crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_financial_crisis). Their withdrawal from the over-borrowed Irish credit system would precipitate a deep Irish property correction which then led to the [Post-2008 Irish banking crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-2008_Irish_banking_crisis).[^cite_note-152][^cite_note-aw-149]&#x20;

The country's successful low-tax economy opens it to accusations of [Ireland being a corporate haven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireland_as_a_tax_haven).[^cite_note-153][^cite_note-154][^cite_note-155][^cite_note-156][^cite_note-157] A 2017 study ranks Ireland as the 5th largest global [Conduit OFC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conduit_and_Sink_OFCs), which legally route funds to [tax havens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_havens). The EU's 2018 Digital Sales Tax (DST)[^cite_note-158] and the desire for a [Common Consolidated Corporate Tax Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Consolidated_Corporate_Tax_Base)[^cite_note-159] was seen partially as an attempt to restrict "[multinational tax schemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporation_tax_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland#Multinational_tax_schemes).[^cite_note-160][^cite_note-161][^cite_note-162]&#x20;

### Trade



The [International Financial Services Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Financial_Services_Centre,_Dublin) in Dublin

Although [multinational corporations](214491.md) dominate Ireland's export sector, exports from other sources also contribute significantly to the national income and particularly to employment and local economies outside of the major cities. The activities of multinational companies based in Ireland have made it one of the largest exporters of pharmaceutical agents, medical devices and software-related goods and services in the world. Ireland's exports also relate to the activities of large Irish companies (such as [Ryanair](262878.md), [Kerry Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerry_Group), [Smurfit Kappa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smurfit_Kappa)) and increasingly Irish based aircraft lessors such as [Avolon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avolon).&#x20;

The country exports mineral resources including zinc and lead concentrates and also has significant deposits of [gypsum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gypsum) and smaller quantities of copper, [aluminium oxide](141888.md), silver, gold, [barite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barite), and [dolomite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolomite_\(mineral\)).[^cite_note-CIA-74]&#x20;

[Tourism in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism_in_Ireland) contributes about 4% of GDP and is a significant source of employment.&#x20;

Other goods exports include agri-food such as cattle, beef, sheep and dairy products.&#x20;

Ireland's major imports include data processing equipment, chemicals, petroleum and petroleum products, textiles, and clothing. [Financial services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_services) provided by multinational corporations based at the [Irish Financial Services Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Financial_Services_Centre) also contribute to Irish exports.&#x20;

The EU is by far the country's largest trading partner, accounting for 57.9% of exports and 60.7% of imports. Prior to [Brexit](41688778.md), the United Kingdom was the most important trading partner within the EU, accounting for 15.4% of exports and 32.1% of imports. Outside the EU, the United States accounted for 23.2% of exports and 14.1% of imports in 2010.[^cite_note-CSO-163]&#x20;

### Energy

Main article: [Energy in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_in_Ireland)



A wind farm in [County Wexford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Wexford)

[ESB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ESB_Electric_Ireland), [Bord Gáis Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bord_G%C3%A1is_Energy) and [Airtricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airtricity) are the three main electricity and gas suppliers in Ireland. There are 19.82 billion cubic metres of proven reserves of gas.[^cite_note-CIA-74][^cite_note-164] Natural gas extraction previously occurred at the [Kinsale Head](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinsale_Head_gas_field) until its exhaustion. The [Corrib gas field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corrib_gas_field) was due to come on stream in 2013/14. In 2012, the [Barryroe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barryroe) field was confirmed to have up to 1.6 billion barrels of oil in reserve, with between 160 and 600 million recoverable.[^cite_note-165] That could provide for Ireland's entire energy needs for up to 13 years, when it is developed in 2015/16.&#x20;

There have been significant efforts to increase the use of renewable and sustainable forms of energy in Ireland, particularly in [wind power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_power_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland), with 3,000 MegaWatts[^cite_note-166] of [wind farms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_farm) being constructed, some for the purpose of export.[^cite_note-167] The [Sustainable Energy Authority of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_Energy_Authority_of_Ireland) (SEAI) has estimated that 6.5% of Ireland's 2011 energy requirements were produced by renewable sources.[^cite_note-168] The SEAI has also reported an increase in energy efficiency in Ireland with a 28% reduction in carbon emissions per house from 2005 to 2013.[^cite_note-169]&#x20;

In 2021, Ireland was the 24th largest wind energy producer in the world and the 3rd ranked in 2020 on a per capita basis.[^cite_note-170]&#x20;

### Transport

Main articles: [Transport in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_in_Ireland), [Rail transport in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_transport_in_Ireland), and [Roads in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roads_in_Ireland)

The country's three main [international airports](1519705.md) at [Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Airport), [Shannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_Airport) and [Cork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cork_Airport) serve many European and intercontinental routes with scheduled and [chartered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_charter) flights. The London-to-Dublin air route is the ninth-busiest international air route in the world, and also the busiest international air route in Europe, with 14,500 flights between the two in 2017.[^cite_note-second-busiest-171][^cite_note-172] In 2015, 4.5 million people took the route, at that time the world's second-busiest.[^cite_note-second-busiest-171] [Aer Lingus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aer_Lingus) is the flag carrier of Ireland, although [Ryanair](262878.md) is the country's largest airline. Ryanair is Europe's largest low-cost carrier,[^cite_note-173] the second-largest in terms of passenger numbers, and the world's largest in terms of international passenger numbers.[^cite_note-174]&#x20;



[Iarnród Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iarnr%C3%B3d_%C3%89ireann) trains at [Heuston station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Heuston_railway_station)

Railway services are provided by [Iarnród Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iarnr%C3%B3d_%C3%89ireann) (Irish Rail), which operates all internal [intercity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/InterCity_\(Irish_Rail\)), [commuter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commuter) and [freight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freight) railway services in the country. Dublin is the centre of the network with two main stations, [Heuston station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Heuston_railway_station) and [Connolly station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Connolly_railway_station), linking to the country's cities and main towns. The [Enterprise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enterprise_\(train\)) service, which runs jointly with [Northern Ireland Railways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Railways), connects Dublin and [Belfast](5046.md). The whole of Ireland's mainline network operates on track with a [gauge of 5 ft 3 in (1,600 mm)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Track_gauge_in_Ireland), which is unique in Europe and has resulted in distinct rolling stock designs. Dublin's public transport network includes the [DART](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Area_Rapid_Transit), [Luas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luas), [Dublin Bus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Bus), and [Dublinbikes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublinbikes).[^cite_note-175]&#x20;

[Motorways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motorways_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland), [national primary roads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_primary_road) and [national secondary roads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_secondary_road) are managed by [Transport Infrastructure Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_Infrastructure_Ireland), while [regional roads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_road_\(Ireland\)) and [local roads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_roads_in_Ireland) are managed by the local authorities in each of their respective areas. The road network is primarily focused on the capital, but motorways connect it to other major Irish cities including Cork, Limerick, Waterford and Galway.[^cite_note-176]&#x20;

Dublin is served by major infrastructure such as the [East-Link](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East-Link_\(Dublin\)) and [West-Link](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West-Link) toll-bridges, as well as the [Dublin Port Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Port_Tunnel). The [Jack Lynch Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Lynch_Tunnel), under the [River Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Lee_\(Ireland\)) in Cork, and the [Limerick Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limerick_Tunnel), under the [River Shannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Shannon), were two major projects outside Dublin.[^cite_note-177]&#x20;

## Demographics

Main article: [Demographics of the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographics_of_the_Republic_of_Ireland)

See also: [Historical population of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_population_of_Ireland)



Population of Ireland since 1951

Genetic research suggests that the earliest settlers migrated from [Iberia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberia) following the most recent [ice age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternary_glaciation).[^cite_note-178] After the [Mesolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesolithic), [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic) and [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age), migrants introduced a [Celtic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celt) language and culture. Migrants from the two latter eras still represent the genetic heritage of most [Irish people](775859.md).[^cite_note-179][^cite_note-180] [Gaelic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaels) tradition expanded and became the dominant form over time. Irish people are a combination of Gaelic, [Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Germanic_peoples), [Anglo-Norman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Normans), French, and British ancestry.&#x20;

The population of Ireland was recorded as 5,149,139 in 2022, an increase of 8% since 2016.[^cite_note-CSO_2022_population-181] In 2011, Ireland had the highest birth rate in the European Union (16 births per 1,000 of population).[^cite_note-182] In 2014, 36.3% of births were to unmarried women.[^cite_note-183] Annual population growth rates exceeded 2% during the 2002–2006 intercensal period, which was attributed to high rates of [natural increase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_increase) and immigration.[^cite_note-184] This rate declined somewhat during the subsequent 2006–2011 intercensal period, with an average annual percentage change of 1.6%. The [total fertility rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_fertility_rate) (TFR) in 2017 was estimated at 1.80 children born per woman, below the replacement rate of 2.1, it remains considerably below the high of 4.2 children born per woman in 1850.[^cite_note-185] In 2018 the [median age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Median_age) of the Irish population was 37.1 years.[^cite_note-186]&#x20;

At the time of the 2022 census, the number of non-Irish nationals was recorded at 631,785. This represents an 8% increase from the [2016 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_census_of_Ireland) figure of 535,475.[^cite_note-CSO_2022_population-181] The five largest sources of non-Irish nationals were Poland (93,680), the UK (83,347), India (45,449), Romania (43,323), Lithuania (31,177), and Latvia (27,338) respectively. The non-Irish nationalities which saw the largest increase from 2016 were India (+33,984), Romania (+14,137), Brazil (+13,698), Ukraine (+10,006). The non-Irish nationalities which saw the largest decrease from 2016 were Poland (−28,835), UK (−19,766), Lithuania (−5,375), Latvia (−1,633), and Slovakia (−1,117).[^cite_note-187]&#x20;

### Languages

Main articles: [Languages of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Ireland), [Irish language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_language), [Hiberno-English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiberno-English), and [Mid Ulster English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid_Ulster_English)



Percentage of population speaking Irish daily (outside the education system) in the [2011 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_census_of_Ireland)

The Irish Constitution describes [Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_language) as the "national language" and the "first official language", but [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language), the "second official language", dominates. In the [2016 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_census_of_Ireland), about 1.75 million people (40% of the population) said they were able to speak Irish but, of those, under 74,000 spoke it on a daily basis.[^cite_note-189] Irish is spoken as a community language only in a small number of rural areas mostly in the west and south of the country, collectively known as the [Gaeltacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaeltacht). Except in Gaeltacht regions, road signs are usually bilingual.[^cite_note-190] Most public notices and print media are in English only. While the state is officially bilingual, citizens can often struggle to access state services in Irish and most government publications are not available in both languages, even though citizens have the right to deal with the state in Irish. Irish language media include the TV channel [TG4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TG4), the radio station [RTÉ Raidió na Gaeltachta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT%C3%89_Raidi%C3%B3_na_Gaeltachta) and online newspaper [Tuairisc.ie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuairisc.ie). In the [Irish Defence Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Defence_Forces), all foot and arms drill commands are given in the Irish language.&#x20;

As a result of immigration, [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_language) is the most widely spoken language in Ireland after English, with Irish as the third most spoken.[^cite_note-191] Several other Central European languages (namely Czech, Hungarian and Slovak), as well as [Baltic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltic_languages) (Lithuanian and Latvian) are also spoken on a day-to-day basis. Other languages spoken in Ireland include [Shelta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shelta), spoken by Irish Travellers, and a dialect of [Scots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_Scots_dialects) is spoken by some [Ulster Scots people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_Scots_people) in Donegal.[^cite_note-192] Most secondary school students choose to learn one or two foreign languages. Languages available for the Junior Certificate and the Leaving Certificate include French, German, Italian and Spanish; Leaving Certificate students can also study Arabic, Japanese and Russian. Some secondary schools also offer [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek), [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language) and [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin). The study of Irish is generally compulsory for Leaving Certificate students, but some may qualify for an exemption in some circumstances, such as learning difficulties or entering the country after age 11.[^cite_note-193]&#x20;

Religious freedom is constitutionally provided for in Ireland, and the country's constitution has been [secular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secular_state) since 1973. [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_Ireland) is the predominant religion, and while Ireland remains a predominantly Catholic country, the percentage of the population who identified as Catholic on the census has declined significantly in the early 21st century, from 84.2 percent in the [2011 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_census_of_Ireland) to 69.1 percent in 2022.[^cite_note-194][^cite_note-195] Other results from the [2016 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_census_of_Ireland) were: 4.2% [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism), 1.3% Orthodox, 1.3% Muslim, and 9.8% having no religion.[^cite_note-196] According to a [Georgetown University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgetown_University) study, before 2000 the country had one of the highest rates of regular [mass](5684685.md) attendance in the [Western world](21208200.md).[^cite_note-197] While daily attendance was 13% in 2006, there was a reduction in weekly attendance from 81% in 1990 to 48% in 2006; the decline was reported as stabilising, though.[^cite_note-198] In 2011, it was reported that weekly mass attendance in Dublin was 18% and even lower among younger generations.[^cite_note-199]&#x20;



[St Mary's Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Mary%27s_Cathedral,_Dublin) is the seat of the Catholic Church in [Dublin](8504.md).



[St Patrick's Cathedral, Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Patrick%27s_Cathedral,_Dublin), is the national Cathedral of the [Church of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Ireland).

The [Church of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Ireland), adhered to by 2.7% of the population, is the second largest Christian denomination. Membership declined throughout the twentieth century but experienced an increase early in the 21st century alongside other Christian denominations. Other significant Protestant denominations are the [Presbyterian Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyterian_Church_in_Ireland) and [Methodist Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodist_Church_in_Ireland). Immigration has contributed to a growth in [Hindu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland) and Muslim populations. In percentage terms, as of the 2006 census, [Orthodox Christianity](10186.md) and [Islam](6037917.md) were the fastest growing religions with increases of 100% and 70% respectively.[^cite_note-200] Ireland has had a small [Jewish community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_community) since at least the 17th century, growing substantially with [Lithuanian](17675.md) and [Eastern European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Europe) immigration from the 1880s, when many settled in Dublin's [Portobello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portobello,_Dublin) area, historically known as "Little Jerusalem." [^cite_note-201][^cite_note-202][^cite_note-203]&#x20;

Ireland's patron saints are [Patrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Patrick), [Bridget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brigid_of_Kildare), and [Columba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columba); Saint Patrick is commonly recognised as the primary patron saint.[^cite_note-204] [Saint Patrick's Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Patrick%27s_Day) is celebrated on 17 March in Ireland and abroad as the Irish national day with parades and other celebrations.&#x20;

As with other traditionally Catholic European states, such as Spain and Italy,[^cite_note-205] Ireland underwent a period of legal secularisation in the late twentieth century. In 1972, the article of the Constitution naming specific religious groups was deleted by the [Fifth Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth_Amendment_of_the_Constitution_of_Ireland) in a referendum. Article 44 remains in the Constitution: "The State acknowledges that the homage of public worship is due to Almighty God. It shall hold His Name in reverence, and shall respect and honour religion." The article also establishes freedom of religion, prohibits endowment of any religion, prohibits the state from religious discrimination, and requires the state to treat religious and non-religious schools in a non-prejudicial manner.&#x20;

Although most [schools in Ireland are patronised by religious organisations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denominational_education_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland), government policy has been to "transfer" some schools to non-denominational or multi-denominational patronage,[^cite_note-206][^cite_note-207] and a secularist trend is occurring among younger generations.[^cite_note-208]&#x20;

### Education

Main article: [Education in the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland)



The Long Room at the [Library of Trinity College Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_Trinity_College_Dublin)

These paragraphs are an excerpt from [Education in the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Education_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland\&action=edit)]

[Education in the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland) is a [primary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_education), [secondary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_education) and [higher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_education) (often known as "third-level" or [tertiary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertiary_education)) education. In recent years, [further education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Further_education) has grown immensely, and Ireland now has one of the highest percentages of higher education completion in the EU, at 55% of working age adults, and 63% of adults aged 25–34 (as of 2023).[^cite_note-Education_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland_CSO2024-209] Growth in the [economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_the_Republic_of_Ireland) since the 1960s has driven much of the change in the education system. For universities there are student service fees (up to a maximum of €2,500), which students are required to pay on registration, to cover examinations, insurance and registration costs, although grants are available for low-income students.[^cite_note-210]&#x20;

Students must go to schools from ages 5 to 16 or until they have completed three years of second-level of education. [^cite_note-211] Under the [Constitution of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Ireland), parents are not obliged "in violation of their conscience and lawful preference to send their children to schools established by the State, or to any particular type of school designated by the State."[^cite_note-212] However, the parental right to homeschool their child has met legal contests over minimum standards in the absence of constitutional provision for State-defined educational standards.&#x20;

While [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language) is the primary [medium of instruction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medium_of_instruction) at all levels in most schools across the state, in [Gaelscoileanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelscoil) (Irish-language schools), Irish is the primary medium of instruction at all levels and English is taught as a second language. The Irish language remains a core subject taught in all public schools, with exemptions given to individual pupils on grounds of significant periods lived abroad, learning difficulties and other similar and/or valid reasons.&#x20;

The typical secondary school consists of First to Third Year (with the Junior Certificate at the end of Third), Transition Year (if offered and taken by the student), and Fifth and Sixth Year (with the Leaving Certificate at the end of Sixth Year).&#x20;

The vast majority of students complete secondary education, with only 4% of students failing to complete the Leaving Certificate, the second lowest percentage in the EU, where the average is 10%.[^cite_note-Education_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland_CSO2024-209]&#x20;

Ireland's secondary students rank above average in terms of academic performance in both the OECD and EU; having reading literacy, mathematical literacy and [scientific literacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_literacy) test scores better than average. Ireland has the highest percentage in the OECD of students at who are highly proficient at reading, at 32%.[^cite_note-213]&#x20;

### Healthcare

Main article: [Healthcare in the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Healthcare_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland)



[RCSI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RCSI) Disease and Research Centre at [Beaumont Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaumont_Hospital,_Dublin) in [Dublin](8504.md)

Healthcare in Ireland is provided by both public and private healthcare providers.[^cite_note-214] The [Minister for Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Health_\(Ireland\)) has responsibility for setting overall health service policy. Every resident of Ireland is entitled to receive health care through the public health care system, which is managed by the [Health Service Executive](4017657.md) and funded by general taxation. A person may be required to pay a subsidised fee for certain health care received; this depends on income, age, illness or disability. All maternity services are provided free of charge and children up to the age of 6 months. Emergency care is provided to patients who present to a hospital emergency department. However, visitors to emergency departments in non-emergency situations who are not referred by their [GP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_practitioner) may incur a fee of €100. In some circumstances this fee is not payable or may be waived.[^cite_note-215]&#x20;

Anyone holding a [European Health Insurance Card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Health_Insurance_Card) is entitled to free maintenance and treatment in public beds in Health Service Executive and voluntary hospitals. Outpatient services are also provided for free. However, the majority of patients on median incomes or above are required to pay subsidised hospital charges. Private health insurance is available to the population for those who want to avail of it.&#x20;

The average [life expectancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_expectancy) in Ireland in 2021 was 82.4 years ([OECD list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_life_expectancy#OECD_\(2022\))), with 80.5 years for men and 84.3 years for women.[^cite_note-216][^cite_note-217] It has the highest birth rate in the EU (16.8 births per 1,000 inhabitants, compared to an EU average of 10.7)[^cite_note-218] and a very low infant mortality rate (3.5 per 1,000 [live births](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_birth_\(human\))). The Irish healthcare system ranked 13th out of 34 European countries in 2012 according to the European Health Consumer Index produced by [Health Consumer Powerhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_Consumer_Powerhouse).[^cite_note-219] The same report ranked the Irish healthcare system as having the 8th best health outcomes but only the 21st most accessible system in Europe.&#x20;

## Culture

Main article: [Culture of Ireland](991296.md)

Ireland's culture was for centuries predominantly [Gaelic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_Ireland), and it remains one of the six principal [Celtic nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_nations). Following the [Anglo-Norman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Normans) invasion in the 12th century, and gradual British conquest and colonisation beginning in the 16th century, Ireland became influenced by English and Scottish culture. Subsequently, Irish culture, though distinct in many aspects, shares characteristics with the rest of the [Anglosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglosphere), [Catholic Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Europe), and other Celtic regions. The [Irish diaspora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_diaspora), one of the world's largest and most dispersed, has contributed to the globalisation of Irish culture, producing many prominent figures in art, music, and science.&#x20;

### Literature and drama

Main articles: [Irish literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_literature), [Irish short stories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_short_stories), [Irish poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_poetry), and [Irish theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_theatre)



[Jonathan Swift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Swift) (1667–1745)

Ireland has made a significant contribution to world literature in both the English and Irish languages. Modern [Irish fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_fiction) began with the publishing of the 1726 novel *[Gulliver's Travels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulliver%27s_Travels)* by [Jonathan Swift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Swift). Other writers of importance [during the 18th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/18th_century_in_literature) and their most notable works include [Laurence Sterne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurence_Sterne) with the publication of *[The Life and Opinions of Tristram Shandy, Gentleman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Life_and_Opinions_of_Tristram_Shandy,_Gentleman)* and [Oliver Goldsmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Goldsmith)'s *[The Vicar of Wakefield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Vicar_of_Wakefield)*. Numerous [Irish novelists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Irish_novelists) emerged during the 19th century, including [Maria Edgeworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Edgeworth), [John Banim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Banim), [Gerald Griffin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerald_Griffin), [Charles Kickham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Kickham), [William Carleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Carleton), [George Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_A._Moore), and [Somerville and Ross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somerville_and_Ross). [Bram Stoker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bram_Stoker) is best known as the author of the 1897 novel *[Dracula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dracula)*.&#x20;

[James Joyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joyce) (1882–1941) published his most famous work *[Ulysses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulysses_\(novel\))* in 1922, which is an interpretation of the *[Odyssey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odyssey)* set in Dublin. [Edith Somerville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edith_Anna_Somerville) continued writing after the death of her partner [Martin Ross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Violet_Florence_Martin) in 1915. Dublin's [Annie M. P. Smithson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annie_M._P._Smithson) was one of several authors catering for fans of romantic fiction in the 1920s and 1930s. After the Second World War, popular novels were published by, among others, Brian O'Nolan, who published as [Flann O'Brien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flann_O%27Brien), [Elizabeth Bowen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Bowen), and [Kate O'Brien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kate_O%27Brien_\(novelist\)). During the final decades of the 20th century, [Edna O'Brien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edna_O%27Brien), [John McGahern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_McGahern), [Maeve Binchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maeve_Binchy), [Joseph O'Connor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_O%27Connor), [Roddy Doyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roddy_Doyle), [Colm Tóibín](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colm_T%C3%B3ib%C3%ADn), and [John Banville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Banville) came to the fore as novelists.&#x20;



[W. B. Yeats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._B._Yeats) (1865–1939)

[Patricia Lynch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patricia_Lynch) was a prolific children's author in the 20th century, while [Eoin Colfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eoin_Colfer)'s works were [NYT Best Sellers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Times_Best_Seller) in this genre in the early 21st century.[^cite_note-220] In the genre of the short story, which is a form favoured by many Irish writers, the most prominent figures include [Seán Ó Faoláin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Se%C3%A1n_%C3%93_Faol%C3%A1in), [Frank O'Connor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_O%27Connor) and [William Trevor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Trevor). Well known Irish poets include [Patrick Kavanagh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Kavanagh), [Thomas McCarthy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_McCarthy_\(poet\)), [Dermot Bolger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dermot_Bolger), and [Nobel Prize in Literature](23385442.md) laureates [William Butler Yeats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._B._Yeats) and [Seamus Heaney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seamus_Heaney) (born in Northern Ireland but resided in Dublin). Prominent writers in the Irish language are [Pádraic Ó Conaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C3%A1draic_%C3%93_Conaire), [Máirtín Ó Cadhain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%A1irt%C3%ADn_%C3%93_Cadhain), [Séamus Ó Grianna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%A9amus_%C3%93_Grianna), and [Nuala Ní Dhomhnaill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuala_N%C3%AD_Dhomhnaill).&#x20;

The history of [Irish theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_theatre) begins with the expansion of the English administration in Dublin during the early 17th century, and since then, Ireland has significantly contributed to English drama. In its early history, theatrical productions in Ireland tended to serve political purposes, but as more theatres opened and the popular audience grew, a more diverse range of entertainments were staged. Many Dublin-based theatres developed links with their London equivalents, and British productions frequently found their way to the Irish stage. However, most Irish playwrights went abroad to establish themselves. In the 18th century, [Oliver Goldsmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Goldsmith) and [Richard Brinsley Sheridan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Brinsley_Sheridan) were two of the most successful playwrights on the London stage at that time. At the beginning of the 20th century, theatre companies dedicated to the staging of Irish plays and the development of writers, directors and performers began to emerge, which allowed many Irish playwrights to learn their trade and establish their reputations in Ireland rather than in Britain or the United States. Following in the tradition of acclaimed practitioners, principally [Oscar Wilde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Wilde), Literature Nobel Prize laureates [George Bernard Shaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Bernard_Shaw) (1925) and [Samuel Beckett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Beckett) (1969), playwrights such as [Seán O'Casey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Se%C3%A1n_O%27Casey), [Brian Friel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Friel), [Sebastian Barry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sebastian_Barry), [Brendan Behan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brendan_Behan), [Conor McPherson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conor_McPherson) and [Billy Roche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billy_Roche) have gained popular success.[^cite_note-221] Other Irish playwrights of the 20th century include [Denis Johnston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denis_Johnston), [Thomas Kilroy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Kilroy), [Tom Murphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Murphy_\(playwright\)), [Hugh Leonard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Leonard), [Frank McGuinness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_McGuinness), and [John B. Keane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_B._Keane).&#x20;

### Music and dance

Main articles: [Irish music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_music) and [Irish dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_dance)

Enya

[Enya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enya); Ireland's best–selling solo artist

[^cite_note-222]

U2

[U2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U2); best–selling Irish group in history

[^cite_note-223]

[Irish traditional music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_traditional_music) has remained vibrant, despite globalising cultural forces, and retains many traditional aspects. It has influenced various music genres, such as American country and roots music, and to some extent modern rock. It has occasionally been blended with styles such as rock and roll and punk rock. Ireland has also produced many internationally known artists in other genres, such as rock, pop, jazz, and blues. Ireland's best selling musical act is the rock band [U2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U2), who have sold 170 million copies of their albums worldwide since their formation in 1976.[^cite_note-224]&#x20;

There are a number of classical music ensembles around the country, such as the [RTÉ Performing Groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT%C3%89_Performing_Groups).[^cite_note-225] Ireland also has two opera organisations: [Irish National Opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_National_Opera) in Dublin, and the annual [Wexford Opera Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wexford_Festival_Opera), which promotes lesser-known operas, takes place during October and November.&#x20;

Ireland has participated in the [Eurovision Song Contest](9954.md) since 1965.[^cite_note-226] Its first win was in 1970, when [Dana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dana_Rosemary_Scallon) won with *[All Kinds of Everything](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_Kinds_of_Everything)*.[^cite_note-227] It has subsequently won the competition [six more times](3014113.md),[^cite_note-228][^cite_note-229] the highest number of wins by any competing country. The phenomenon *[Riverdance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riverdance)* originated as an interval performance during the [1994 contest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1994_Eurovision_Song_Contest).[^cite_note-230]&#x20;

Irish dance can broadly be divided into [social dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_dance) and [performance dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Performance_dance). Irish social dance can be divided into *céilí* and set dancing. Irish [set dances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_dance) are [quadrilles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrilles), danced by 4 couples arranged in a square, while [céilí dances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceili_dance) are danced by varied [formations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formation_dance) of couples of 2 to 16 people. There are also many stylistic differences between these two forms. Irish social dance is a living tradition, and variations in particular dances are found across the country. In some places dances are deliberately modified and new dances are choreographed. Performance dance is traditionally referred to as [stepdance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Step_dance). [Irish stepdance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_stepdance), popularised by the show *[Riverdance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riverdance)*, is notable for its rapid leg movements, with the body and arms being kept largely stationary. The solo stepdance is generally characterised by a controlled but not rigid upper body, straight arms, and quick, precise movements of the feet. The solo dances can either be in "soft shoe" or "hard shoe".&#x20;

### Architecture

Main article: [Architecture of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture_of_Ireland)



The [Dublin Custom House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Custom_House) is a [neoclassical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoclassical_architecture) building from the late 18th century.



[Powerscourt Estate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powerscourt_Estate), near [Enniskerry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enniskerry) in [County Wicklow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Wicklow), was built in the 18th century.

Ireland has a wealth of structures,[^cite_note-231] surviving from the [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic) period, such as *[Brú na Bóinne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Br%C3%BA_na_B%C3%B3inne)*, [Poulnabrone dolmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poulnabrone_dolmen), [Castlestrange stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castlestrange_stone), [Turoe stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turoe_stone), and [Drombeg stone circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drombeg_stone_circle).[^cite_note-232] As Ireland was never a part of the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire), ancient architecture in [Greco-Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Roman) style is non-existent, in contrast to most of Western Europe. The country instead had an extended period of [Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age) architecture.[^cite_note-233] The [Irish round tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_round_tower) originated during the [Early Medieval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Medieval) period and several of these structure are still extant throughout the country.&#x20;

Christianity introduced simple [monastic houses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_monastic_houses_in_Ireland), such as [Clonmacnoise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clonmacnoise), [Skellig Michael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skellig_Michael) and [Scattery Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scattery_Island_Cathedral_and_Monastery). A stylistic similarity has been remarked between these [double monasteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_monastery) and those of the [Copts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copts) of Egypt.[^cite_note-234] Gaelic kings and aristocrats occupied [ringforts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ringfort) or *[crannógs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crann%C3%B3g)*.[^cite_note-vikperiod-235] Church reforms during the 12th century via the [Cistercians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cistercians) stimulated further continental influence, with the [Romanesque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanesque_architecture) styled [Mellifont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mellifont_Abbey), [Boyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boyle_Abbey) and [Tintern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tintern_Abbey_\(County_Wexford\)) abbeys.[^cite_note-236] Gaelic settlement had been limited to the Monastic proto-towns, such as [Kells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kells,_County_Meath), where the current street pattern preserves the original circular settlement outline to some extent.[^cite_note-237] Significant urban settlements only developed following the period of Viking invasions.[^cite_note-vikperiod-235] The major Hiberno-Norse [Longphorts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longphort) were located on the coast, but with minor inland fluvial settlements, such as the eponymous [Longford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longford).&#x20;



The ruins of [Monasterboice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monasterboice) in [County Louth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Louth) are of early Christian settlements.

Castles were built by the [Anglo-Normans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Normans) during the late 12th century, such as [Dublin Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Castle) and [Kilkenny Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilkenny_Castle),[^cite_note-238] and the concept of the planned walled trading town was introduced, which gained legal status and several rights by grant of a [Charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charter) under [Feudalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feudalism). These charters specifically governed the design of these towns.[^cite_note-239] Two significant waves of planned town formation followed, the first being the 16th- and 17th-century plantation towns, which were used as a mechanism for the [Tudor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tudor_dynasty) English kings to suppress local insurgency, followed by 18th-century landlord towns.[^cite_note-240] Surviving Norman founded planned towns include [Drogheda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drogheda) and [Youghal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Youghal); plantation towns include [Portlaoise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portlaoise) and [Portarlington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portarlington,_County_Laois); well-preserved 18th-century planned towns include [Westport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westport,_County_Mayo) and [Ballinasloe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballinasloe). These episodes of planned settlement account for many of the present-day towns throughout the country.&#x20;



Brick architecture of multi-storey buildings in [Merrion Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merrion_Square), Dublin

[Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_architecture) cathedrals, such as [St Patrick's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Patrick%27s_Cathedral,_Dublin), were also introduced by the Normans.[^cite_note-241] [Franciscans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franciscan) were dominant in directing the abbeys by the Late Middle Ages, while elegant tower houses, such as [Bunratty Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunratty_Castle), were built by the Gaelic and Norman aristocracy. Many religious buildings were ruined with the [dissolution of the monasteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_monasteries).[^cite_note-242]&#x20;

After the [Battle of the Boyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Boyne) in the late 1690s and into the 1700s, [palladianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palladianism) and interior [rococo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rococo), particularly in [country houses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Country_houses), expanded throughout Ireland under continental influence as well as under the influence of one of Ireland's first specifically trained architects, [Edward Lovett Pearce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Lovett_Pearce), with the [Houses of Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Houses_of_Parliament) being the most significant.[^cite_note-greenwood-243]&#x20;

With the erection of buildings such as [The Custom House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Custom_House), [Four Courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Courts), [General Post Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Post_Office_\(Dublin\)) and [King's Inns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King%27s_Inns), the [neoclassical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoclassical_architecture) and [Georgian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_architecture) styles flourished, especially in [Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_Dublin).[^cite_note-greenwood-243] Georgian townhouses produced streets of singular distinction, particularly in [Dublin](8504.md), [Limerick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limerick) and [Cork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cork_\(city\)). Following [Catholic emancipation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_emancipation), cathedrals and churches influenced by the French [Gothic Revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_Revival_architecture) emerged, such as [St Colman's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cobh_Cathedral) and [St Finbarre's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Finbarre%27s_Cathedral).[^cite_note-greenwood-243] Ireland has long been associated with vernacular [thatched roof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thatched_roof) cottages, though these are nowadays in decline.[^cite_note-244]&#x20;



[Capital Dock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_Dock) in [Dublin](8504.md) is the tallest building in the Republic of Ireland.

Beginning with the American designed [art deco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_deco) church at [Turner's Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turners_Cross,_Cork), [Cork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cork_\(city\)) in 1927, Irish architecture followed the international trend towards modern and sleek building styles since the 20th century.[^cite_note-245] Other developments include the regeneration of [Ballymun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballymun) and an urban extension of Dublin at [Adamstown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adamstown,_Dublin).[^cite_note-246] Since the establishment of the [Dublin Docklands Development Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Docklands_Development_Authority) in 1997, the [Dublin Docklands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Docklands) area underwent large-scale redevelopment, which included the construction of the [Convention Centre Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convention_Centre_Dublin) and [Grand Canal Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canal_Theatre).[^cite_note-247] Completed in 2018, [Capital Dock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_Dock) in Dublin is the tallest building in the Republic of Ireland achieving 79 metres (259 feet) in height (the [Obel Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obel_Tower) in [Belfast](5046.md), [Northern Ireland](21265.md) being the tallest in Ireland). The [Royal Institute of the Architects of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Institute_of_the_Architects_of_Ireland) regulates the practice of architecture in the state.[^cite_note-248]&#x20;

### Media

Main article: [Media of the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_of_the_Republic_of_Ireland)

[Raidió Teilifís Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raidi%C3%B3_Teilif%C3%ADs_%C3%89ireann) (RTÉ) is Ireland's [public service broadcaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_service_broadcaster), funded by a [television licence fee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_licence_fee) and advertising.[^cite_note-249] RTÉ operates two national television channels, [RTÉ One](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT%C3%89_One) and [RTÉ Two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT%C3%89_Two). The other independent national television channels are [Virgin Media One](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgin_Media_One), [Virgin Media Two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgin_Media_Two), [Virgin Media Three](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgin_Media_Three), [Virgin Media Four](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgin_Media_Four) and [TG4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TG4), the latter of which is a public service broadcaster for speakers of the Irish language. All these channels are available on [Saorview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saorview), the national [free-to-air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free-to-air) [digital terrestrial television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_terrestrial_television) service.[^cite_note-250] Additional channels included in the service are [RTÉ News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT%C3%89_News_\(TV_channel\)), [RTÉjr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT%C3%89jr), and RTÉ One +1. Subscription-based television providers operating in Ireland include [Virgin Media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgin_Media_Ireland) and [Sky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky_Ireland).&#x20;

The [BBC](19344654.md)'s [Northern Irish division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Northern_Ireland) is widely available in Ireland. [BBC One Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_One_Northern_Ireland) and [BBC Two Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Two_Northern_Ireland) are available in pay television providers including Virgin and Sky as well as via [signal overspill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_overspill) by [Freeview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeview_\(UK\)) in border counties.&#x20;

Supported by the [Irish Film Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Film_Board), the Irish film industry grew significantly since the 1990s, with the promotion of indigenous films as well as the attraction of international productions like *[Braveheart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braveheart)* and *[Saving Private Ryan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saving_Private_Ryan)*.[^cite_note-Media_Landscape-251]&#x20;

A large number of regional and local radio stations are available countrywide. A survey showed that a consistent 85% of adults listen to a mixture of national, regional and local stations on a daily basis.[^cite_note-252] [RTÉ Radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT%C3%89_Radio) operates four national stations, [Radio 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT%C3%89_Radio_1), [2fm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT%C3%89_2fm), [Lyric fm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT%C3%89_lyric_fm), and [RnaG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT%C3%89_Raidi%C3%B3_na_Gaeltachta). [Censorship in the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland) is mainly managed by the [Censorship of Publications Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship_of_Publications_Board_\(Ireland\)), [Coimisiún na Meán](73295318.md) and [Irish Film Classification Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Film_Classification_Office).&#x20;

Ireland has a traditionally competitive print media, which is divided into daily national newspapers and weekly regional newspapers, as well as national Sunday editions. The strength of the British press is a unique feature of the Irish print media scene, with the availability of a wide selection of British published newspapers and magazines.[^cite_note-Media_Landscape-251]&#x20;

[Eurostat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurostat) reported that 82% of Irish households had Internet access in 2013 compared to the [EU](9317.md) average of 79% but only 67% had broadband access.[^cite_note-253]&#x20;

### Cuisine

See also: [Irish cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_cuisine) and [List of Irish dishes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Irish_dishes)



A [pint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pint) of [Guinness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinness)

Irish cuisine was traditionally based on meat and dairy products, supplemented with vegetables and seafood. Examples of popular Irish cuisine include [boxty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boxty), [colcannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colcannon), [coddle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coddle), [stew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_stew), and [bacon and cabbage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacon_and_cabbage). Ireland is known for the [full Irish breakfast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_breakfast), which involves a fried or grilled meal generally consisting of rashers, egg, sausage, white and black pudding, and fried tomato. Apart from the influence by European and international dishes, there has been an emergence of a new Irish cuisine based on traditional ingredients handled in new ways.[^cite_note-irelandlogue-254] This cuisine is based on fresh vegetables, fish, oysters, mussels and other shellfish, and the wide range of hand-made cheeses that are now being produced across the country. Shellfish have increased in popularity, especially due to the high quality shellfish available from the [country's coastline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Irish_counties_by_coastline). The most popular fish include [salmon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmon) and [cod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cod). Traditional breads include [soda bread](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soda_bread) and [wheaten bread](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheaten_bread). [Barmbrack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barmbrack) is a [yeasted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeast) [bread](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bread) with added [sultanas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultana_\(grape\)) and [raisins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raisin), traditionally eaten on [Halloween](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halloween).[^cite_note-255]&#x20;

Popular everyday beverages among the Irish include [tea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_breakfast_tea) and [coffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_coffee). Alcoholic drinks associated with Ireland include [Poitín](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poit%C3%ADn) and the world-famous [Guinness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinness), which is a [dry stout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dry_stout) that originated in the brewery of [Arthur Guinness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Guinness) at [St. James's Gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._James%27s_Gate) in Dublin. [Irish whiskey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_whiskey) is also popular throughout the country and comes in various forms, including single malt, single grain, and blended whiskey.[^cite_note-irelandlogue-254]&#x20;

### Sports

Main article: [Sport in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sport_in_Ireland)



[Croke Park](322713.md) stadium is the headquarters of the [Gaelic Athletic Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_Athletic_Association).

[Gaelic football](37688.md) and [hurling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurling) are the traditional sports of Ireland as well as popular [spectator sports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectator_sports).[^cite_note-256] They are administered by the [Gaelic Athletics Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_Athletics_Association) on an [all-Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-Ireland) basis. Other [Gaelic games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_games) organised by the association include [Gaelic handball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_handball) and [rounders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rounders).[^cite_note-257] [Association football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_football_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland) (soccer) is the third most popular spectator sport and has the highest level of participation.[^cite_note-258] Although the [League of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Ireland) is the national league, the English [Premier League](11250.md) is the most popular among the public.[^cite_note-259] The [Republic of Ireland national football team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Ireland_national_football_team) plays at international level and is administered by the [Football Association of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Football_Association_of_Ireland).[^cite_note-260]&#x20;

The [Irish Rugby Football Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Rugby_Football_Union) is the governing body of [rugby union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugby_union_in_Ireland), which is played at local and international levels on an all-Ireland basis, and has produced players such as [Brian O'Driscoll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_O%27Driscoll) and [Ronan O'Gara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronan_O%27Gara), who were on the team that won the [Grand Slam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Slam_\(rugby_union\)) in [2009](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_Six_Nations_Championship).[^cite_note-261] The success of the [Irish Cricket Team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Cricket_Team) in the [2007 Cricket World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_Cricket_World_Cup) has led to an increase in the popularity of [cricket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cricket_in_Ireland), which is also administered on an all-Ireland basis by [Cricket Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cricket_Ireland).[^cite_note-262] Ireland are one of [the twelve Test playing members](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_International_Cricket_Council_members#Full_Members) of the [International Cricket Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Cricket_Council), having been granted [Test status](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Test_cricket) in 2017. [Professional domestic matches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cricket_in_Ireland#Domestic_cricket) are played between the major cricket unions of [Leinster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leinster_Cricket_Union), [Munster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munster_Cricket_Union), [Northern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Cricket_Union_of_Ireland), and [North West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_West_Cricket_Union). [Netball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netball) is represented by the [Ireland national netball team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Ireland_national_netball_team).&#x20;

[Golf](19568112.md) is another popular sport in Ireland, with over 300 courses countrywide.[^cite_note-263] The country has produced several internationally successful golfers, such as [Pádraig Harrington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C3%A1draig_Harrington), [Shane Lowry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shane_Lowry_\(golfer\)) and [Paul McGinley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_McGinley). [Horse racing](74711.md) has a large presence, with influential breeding and racing operations in the country. Racing takes place at courses at [The Curragh Racecourse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Curragh_Racecourse) in [County Kildare](94784.md), [Leopardstown Racecourse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopardstown_Racecourse) just outside Dublin, and [Galway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galway). Ireland has produced champion horses such as [Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_\(horse\)), [Montjeu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montjeu), and [Sea the Stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_the_Stars). [Boxing](4243.md) is Ireland's most successful sport at an Olympic level. Administered by the [Irish Athletic Boxing Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Athletic_Boxing_Association) on an all-Ireland basis, it has gained in popularity as a result of the international success of boxers such as [Bernard Dunne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Dunne), [Andy Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andy_Lee_\(boxer\)) and [Katie Taylor](12426364.md).&#x20;

Some of Ireland's highest performers in [athletics](43719.md) have competed at the [Olympic Games](22576.md), such as [Eamonn Coghlan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eamonn_Coghlan) and [Sonia O'Sullivan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonia_O%27Sullivan). The annual [Dublin Marathon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Marathon) and [Dublin Women's Mini Marathon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Women%27s_Mini_Marathon) are two of the most popular athletics events in the country.[^cite_note-264] [Rugby league](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugby_league) is represented by the [Ireland national rugby league team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireland_national_rugby_league_team) and administered by [Rugby League Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugby_League_Ireland) (who are full member of the [Rugby League European Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugby_League_European_Federation)) on an all-Ireland basis. The team compete in the [European Cup (rugby league)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Cup_\(rugby_league\)) and the [Rugby League World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugby_League_World_Cup). Ireland reached the quarter-finals of the [2000 Rugby League World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2000_Rugby_League_World_Cup) as well as reaching the semi-finals in the [2008 Rugby League World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Rugby_League_World_Cup).[^cite_note-265] The [Irish Elite League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Elite_League) is a domestic competition for rugby league teams in Ireland.[^cite_note-266]&#x20;

While [Australian rules football in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_rules_football_in_Ireland) has a limited following, a series of [International rules football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_rules_football) games (constituting a hybrid of the Australian and Gaelic football codes) takes place annually between teams representing Ireland and Australia.[^cite_note-267] [Baseball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baseball_in_Ireland) and [basketball](3921.md) are also emerging sports in Ireland, both of which have an international team representing the island of Ireland. Other sports which retain a following in Ireland include [cycling](857311.md), [greyhound racing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greyhound_racing), [horse riding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horse_riding), and [motorsport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motorsport).&#x20;

### Society

See also: [Abortion in the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abortion_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland) and [LGBT rights in the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGBT_rights_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland)

Ireland [ranks fifth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Gender_Gap_Report) in the world in terms of [gender equality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_equality).[^cite_note-268] In 2011, Ireland was ranked the most [charitable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charity_\(practice\)) country in Europe, and second most charitable in the world.[^cite_note-269] Contraception was controlled in Ireland until 1979, however, the receding influence of the [Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_Ireland) has led to an increasingly [secularised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secularism) society.[^cite_note-270] A constitutional ban on [divorce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divorce) was lifted following a [referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifteenth_Amendment_of_the_Constitution_of_Ireland) in 1995. Divorce rates in Ireland are very low compared to European Union averages (0.7 divorced people per 1,000 population in 2011) while the marriage rate in Ireland is slightly above the European Union average (4.6 marriages per 1,000 population per year in 2012). [Abortion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abortion_in_the_Republic_of_Ireland) had been banned throughout the period of the Irish state, first through provisions of the [Offences Against the Person Act 1861](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offences_Against_the_Person_Act_1861) and later by the [Protection of Life During Pregnancy Act 2013](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protection_of_Life_During_Pregnancy_Act_2013). The right to life of the unborn was protected in the constitution by the [Eighth Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eighth_Amendment_of_the_Constitution_of_Ireland) in 1983; this provision was removed following a [referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirty-sixth_Amendment_of_the_Constitution_of_Ireland), and replaced it with a provision allowing legislation to regulate the termination of pregnancy. The [Health (Regulation of Termination of Pregnancy) Act 2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_\(Regulation_of_Termination_of_Pregnancy\)_Act_2018) passed later that year provided for abortion generally during the first 12 weeks of pregnancy, and in specified circumstances after that date.[^cite_note-271]&#x20;

[Capital punishment](5902.md) is constitutionally banned in Ireland, while discrimination based on age, gender, sexual orientation, marital or familial status, religion, race or membership of the travelling community is illegal. The legislation which outlawed homosexual acts was repealed in 1993.[^cite_note-272][^cite_note-273] The [Civil Partnership and Certain Rights and Obligations of Cohabitants Act 2010](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Partnership_and_Certain_Rights_and_Obligations_of_Cohabitants_Act_2010) permitted civil partnerships between same-sex couples.[^cite_note-274][^cite_note-275][^cite_note-276] The [Children and Family Relationships Act 2015](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Children_and_Family_Relationships_Act_2015) allowed for adoption rights for couples other than married couples, including civil partners and cohabitants, and provided for [donor-assisted human reproduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third-party_reproduction); however, significant sections of the Act have yet to be [commenced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coming_into_force).[^cite_note-277] Following a [referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirty-fourth_Amendment_of_the_Constitution_of_Ireland) held on 23 May 2015, Ireland became the eighteenth country to [provide in law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marriage_Act_2015) for [same-sex marriage](92656.md), and the first to do so by popular vote.[^cite_note-278]&#x20;

Ireland became the first country in the world to introduce an environmental levy for [plastic shopping bags](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_shopping_bag) in 2002 and a public [smoking ban](386818.md) in 2004. [Recycling in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recycling_in_Ireland) is carried out extensively, and Ireland has the second highest rate of packaging recycling in the [European Union](9317.md). It was the first country in Europe to ban [incandescent lightbulbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banning_of_incandescent_lightbulbs) in 2008 and the first EU country to ban in-store tobacco advertising and product display in 2009.[^cite_note-279][^cite_note-280] In 2015, Ireland became the second country in the world to introduce [plain cigarette packaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plain_cigarette_packaging).[^cite_note-281] Despite the above measures to discourage tobacco use, smoking rates in Ireland remain at approximately 15.4% as of 2020.[^cite_note-282]&#x20;

### State symbols

Further information: [Symbols of the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbols_of_the_Republic_of_Ireland)



The [seal of the president of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seal_of_the_president_of_Ireland), incorporating a harp

The state shares many symbols with the [island of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Island_of_Ireland). These include the colours green and [blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Patrick%27s_Blue), animals such as the [Irish Wolfhound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Wolfhound) and [stags](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stag), structures such as [round towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_round_tower) and [celtic crosses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_cross), and designs such as [Celtic knots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_knot) and [spirals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triple_spiral). The [shamrock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shamrock), a type of [clover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clover), has been a national symbol of Ireland since the 17th century when it became customary to wear it as a symbol on [St. Patrick's Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Patrick%27s_Day). These symbols are used by state institutions as well as private bodies in the Republic of Ireland.&#x20;

The [flag of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_Ireland) is a [tricolour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tricolour_\(flag\)) of green, white and orange. The flag originates with the [Young Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_Ireland) movement of the mid-19th century but was not popularised until its use during the [Easter Rising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Rising) of 1916.[^cite_note-283] The colours represent the [Gaelic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_Ireland) tradition (green) and the followers of [William of Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_III_of_England) in Ireland (orange), with white representing the aspiration for peace between them.[^cite_note-284] It was adopted as the flag of the Irish Free State in 1922 and continues to be used as the sole flag and ensign of the state. A [naval jack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_jack), a green flag with a yellow harp, is set out in Defence Forces Regulations and flown from the bows of warships in addition to the national flag in limited circumstances (e.g. when a ship is not underway). It is based on the unofficial [Green Ensign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Ensign) of Ireland used in the 18th and 19th centuries and the traditional green flag of Ireland dating from the 16th century.&#x20;

Like the national flag, the national anthem, *[Amhrán na bhFiann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amhr%C3%A1n_na_bhFiann)* (English: A Soldier's Song), has its roots in the Easter Rising, when the song was sung by the rebels. Although originally published in English in 1912,[^cite_note-sherry-285] the song was translated into Irish in 1923 and the Irish-language version is more commonly sung today.[^cite_note-sherry-285] The song was officially adopted as the anthem of the Irish Free State in 1926 and continues as the national anthem of the state.[^cite_note-286] The first four bars of the chorus followed by the last five comprise the [presidential salute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honors_music).&#x20;

The [arms of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arms_of_Ireland) originate as the arms of the monarchs of Ireland and was recorded as the arms of the King of Ireland in the 12th century. From the [union of the crowns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_of_the_crowns) of [England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_England), [Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Scotland) and [Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Ireland) in 1603, they have appeared [quartered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartering_\(heraldry\)) on the [royal coat of arms of the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_coat_of_arms_of_the_United_Kingdom). Today, they are the personal arms of the president of Ireland whilst he or she is in office and are flown as the [presidential standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidential_Standard_\(Ireland\)). The harp symbol is used extensively by the state to mark official documents, Irish coinage and on the [seal of the president of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seal_of_the_president_of_Ireland).&#x20;
