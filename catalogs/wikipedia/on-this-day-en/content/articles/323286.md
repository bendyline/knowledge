**Beit She'an** ([Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language): בֵּית שְׁאָן *[Bēṯ Šəʾān](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/6/61/He-Bet_Shean.ogg/He-Bet_Shean.ogg.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup>*), also known as **Beisan** ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language): بيسان *[Bisān](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/8/82/Ar-Bisan.ogg/Ar-Bisan.ogg.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup>*),[^cite_note-Shahin-2] or **Beth-shean**, is a city in the [Northern District](324281.md) of [Israel](9282173.md). The town lies at the [Beit She'an Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beit_She'an_Valley) about 120 m (394 feet) below sea level. In 2022, 98% of the population was Jewish and 2% was counted as other.[^cite_note-3]

Beit She'an is believed to be one of the oldest cities in the region. It has played an important role in history due to its geographical location at the junction of the [Jordan River Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_River) and the [Jezreel Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jezreel_Valley). Beth She'an's ancient [tell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tell_\(archaeology\)) contains remains beginning in the [Chalcolithic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcolithic). When [Canaan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canaan) came under [Imperial Egyptian rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Kingdom_of_Egypt) in the [Late Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Bronze_Age_collapse), Beth She'an served as a major Egyptian administrative center.[^cite_note-\:0-4] The city came under [Israelite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israelites) rule in the [monarchic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarchic_period). It probably fell under [Philistine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philistines) control during the time of [Saul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saul), when, according to the [Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible), his body was displayed there along with his sons.[^cite_note-\:02-5]

During the [Hellenistic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period), the settlement was known as **Scythopolis** ([Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek): *Σκυθόπολις*). After the region came under [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire) rule, Scythopolis gained imperial free status and was the leading city of the [Decapolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decapolis). A multi-cultural metropolis under [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire) rule, it served as the capital of the province of [Palaestina Secunda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palaestina_Secunda), and had a mixed population of [Christians](18337522.md), [Jews](25955086.md), [Samaritans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaritans) and [pagans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paganism). After the [Arab conquest of the Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_conquest_of_the_Levant), and following a series of devastating earthquakes (most notably in [749](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/749_Galilee_earthquake)), the city lost its prominence, and became a medium-sized country town.[^cite_note-\:0-4][^cite_note-6]

The population of the town was completely changed from 1948 to 1950. It had been entirely [Muslim](19541.md) and Christian, designated to be part of the Jewish state in the 1947 [United Nations Partition Plan for Palestine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Partition_Plan_for_Palestine), and was captured by the [Haganah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haganah) in May 1948. The battle over the town during [Operation Gideon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Gideon) caused most of its inhabitants to flee, and the remainder were expelled.[^cite_note-Golan-7] The town was then resettled by Jewish immigrants.[^cite_note-Golan-7] Today, Beit She'an serves as a regional centre for the towns in the Beit She'an Valley. The ancient city ruins are now protected within the [Beit She'an National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_parks_and_nature_reserves_of_Israel). The town is located near the [Jordan River Crossing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_River_Crossing), one of three crossing points between Israel and [Jordan](7515964.md).

## Geography



Ancient ruins of Beit She'an

Beit She'an's location has always been strategically significant, due to its position at the junction of the Jordan River Valley and the [Jezreel Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jezreel_Valley), essentially controlling access from [Jordan](7515964.md) and the inland to the coast, as well as from [Jerusalem](16043.md) and [Jericho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jericho) to the [Galilee](12639.md).

Beit She'an is situated on [Highway 90](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highway_90_\(Israel%E2%80%93Palestine\)), the north–south road which runs the length of the eastern edge of Israel and the West Bank. The city stretches over an area of 7 square kilometers with a substantial national park in the north of the city. Beit She'an has a population of 20,000.[^cite_note-8]

Today the town is under the administration of the [Emek HaMa'ayanot Regional Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emek_HaMaayanot_Regional_Council).

## Climate

Beit She'an has a hot [semi-arid climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-arid_climate), with mild, rainy winters and very hot, dry summers. Beit She'an is one of the hottest cities in Israel, due to its location in [Beit She'an valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beit_She'an_Valley), which is part of the [Jordan Valley](12105764.md). The annual precipitation is 305 mm, making it the driest city in Northern Israel. The cause of the relative aridity is the location in the rain shadow of the [Samaria mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaria) and [Mount Gilboa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Gilboa), which blocks some of the rain from reaching the area.

## History of archaeological exploration

The [University of Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania) carried out excavations of ancient Beit She'an in 1921–1933. Relics from the [Egyptian period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canaanites#Late_Bronze_Age_\(1550%E2%80%931200_BC\)) were discovered, most of them now exhibited in the [Rockefeller Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockefeller_Museum) in [Jerusalem](16043.md). Some are in the [University of Pennsylvania Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania_Museum) in [Philadelphia](50585.md).[^cite_note-9] Excavations at the site were resumed by the [Hebrew University of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_University_of_Jerusalem) in 1983 and then again from 1989 to 1996 under the direction of [Amihai Mazar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amihai_Mazar).[^cite_note-10] The excavations have revealed no less than 18 successive ancient towns.[^cite_note-Upenn-11][^cite_note-Stanford-12]

## History

### Neolithic and Chalcolithic Ages

In 1933, archaeologist G.M. FitzGerald, under the auspices of the [University of Pennsylvania Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania_Museum), carried out a "deep cut" on Tell el-Hisn ("castle hill"), the large [tell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tell_\(archaeology\)), or mound, of Beth She'an, in order to determine the earliest occupation of the site. His results suggest that settlement began in the [Late Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Neolithic) or [Early Chalcolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Chalcolithic) periods (sixth to fifth millennia BC.)[^cite_note-13] Occupation continued intermittently throughout the Late Neolithic and Chalcolithic periods, with a likely gap during the Late Chalcolithic period (ca. 4000–3300 BC).[^cite_note-Braun,_p.61-64-14]

### Early Bronze Age



House of the Egyptian governor on Tell el-Husn

Settlement seems to have resumed at the beginning of the [Early Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Bronze_Age) IB (c. 3300–3050/3000 BCE) and continues throughout this period, is then missing during the Early Bronze Age II (c. 3050/3000–2720 BCE), and then resumes in the Early Bronze Age IIIA (c. 2720–2500 BCE).[^cite_note-Braun,_p.61-64-14]

During Early Bronze IIIB (c. 2500–2350 BCE), IVA (c. 2350–2150 BCE), IVB (c. 2150–2020 BCE) there is a possible occupation gap due to drier climate conditions and periods of drought in the transitional phases.

### Middle Bronze Age

A large cemetery on the northern mound was in use from the Bronze Age to [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine) times.[^cite_note-15] [Canaanite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canaan) graves dating from 2000 to 1600 BCE were discovered there in 1926.[^cite_note-16]

The Middle Bronze I (c. 2020–1820 BCE) has a possible occupation gap.

The Middle Bronze Age II (c. 1820–1550 BCE) in attested in Area R as Strata R-5–R-3.[^cite_note-17]

### Late Bronze Age

The Late Bronze Age IA (Stratum R-2).[^cite_note-18] The Late Bronze Age IB–IIA (Stratum R-1).[^cite_note-19]

#### Egyptian period

During the three hundred years of rule by the [New Kingdom of Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Kingdom_of_Egypt), the population of Beit She'an appears to have been primarily Egyptian administrative officials and military personnel.

**Level IX** (LB IIA). After the [conquest of Beit She'an by Pharaoh Thutmose III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Megiddo_\(15th_century_BC\)) in the 15th century BCE, as recorded in an inscription at [Karnak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karnak),[^cite_note-20] the small town on the summit of the mound became the center of the Egyptian administration of the region.[^cite_note-21] The Egyptian newcomers changed the organization of the town and left a great deal of material culture behind. A large Canaanite temple (39 m (128 ft) in length) excavated by the [University of Pennsylvania Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania_Museum) (Penn Museum) may date from about the same period as [Thutmose III](155730.md)'s conquest, though the Hebrew University excavations suggest that it dates to a later period.[^cite_note-22] Artifacts of potential cultic significance were found around the temple. Based on [an Egyptian stele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beisan_steles) found at the place, the temple was dedicated to the god Mekal.[^cite_note-23] The [Hebrew University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_University) excavations determined that this temple was built on the site of an earlier one.[^cite_note-24]



Lion and Lioness, 14th century BC

One of the most important finds near the temple is the Lion and Lioness (or a dog[^cite_note-ReferenceA-25]) stela, currently in the [Israel Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel_Museum) in Jerusalem, which depicts the two playing.[^cite_note-26]

**Level VIII**. Amarna period

**Level VII** (LB IIB). The town was completely rebuilt, following a new layout, during the [19th dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/19th_dynasty).[^cite_note-27] The Penn Museum excavations uncovered [two important stelae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beisan_steles) from the period of [Seti I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seti_I) and a monument of [Ramesses II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramesses_II).[^cite_note-28] One of those steles is particularly interesting because it testifies to the presence [Habiru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habiru), which Seti I protected from an Asiatic tribe. While [William F. Albright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_F._Albright) identified the Habiru as "Hebrews"[^cite_note-29] that identification has been challenged by newer scholarship. Pottery was produced locally, but some was made to mimic Egyptian forms.[^cite_note-rehov.org-30] Other Canaanite goods existed alongside Egyptian imports, or locally made Egyptian-style objects.[^cite_note-31]

During the 20th Dynasty, invasions of the "[Sea Peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_Peoples)" upset Egypt's control over the [Eastern Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Mediterranean). Though the exact circumstances are unclear, the entire site of Beit She'an was destroyed by fire around 1150 BC. The Egyptians did not attempt to rebuild their administrative center and finally lost control of the region. The [20th Dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/20th_Dynasty) saw the construction of large administrative buildings in Beit She'an, including "Building 1500", a small palace for the Egyptian governor.[^cite_note-32]

Over 50 clay anthropoid coffins were found at the site mainly from the 13th and 12th centuries BC. Most are in the typical Egyptian style but some are of a "grotesque" type linked to the Aegean which caused earlier archaeologists to suggest they were of the "sea peoples" which pharaoh Ramses III claimed to have resettled in the region.[^cite_note-33]

**Level VI** (LB IIB). Stratum S-4. Egyptian garrison (early 20th Dyn. Ramesses III). Sherds of Mycenaean IIIC pottery.[^cite_note-34]

#### Iron IB

An [Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age) IB (c. 1080–950, cf. [21st Dynasty of Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twenty-first_Dynasty_of_Egypt)) Canaanite city was constructed on the site of the Egyptian center shortly after its destruction.[^cite_note-36]

The [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible) identifies Beit She'an as where the bodies of [King Saul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Saul) and three of his sons were hung by the [Philistines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philistines) after the [Battle of Gilboa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saul).[^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39] The Philistines prevailed and Saul died in battle together with three of his sons, [Jonathan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_\(1_Samuel\)), [Abinadab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abinadab) and [Malchishua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malchishua), ([1 Samuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1_Samuel) and [1 Chronicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1_Chronicles), [1 Samuel 31; 1 Chronicles 10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Samuel+31;+1+Chronicles+10\&version=NRSV)). [1 Samuel 31\:10](https://bible.oremus.org/?passage=1%20Samuel%2031\:10\&version=nrsv) states that "the victorious Philistines hung the body of King Saul on the walls of Beit She'an". Later, the people of [Jabesh-Gilead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jabesh-Gilead) took the remnants, carried them into their city, and burned them. No archeological evidence was found of a Philistine occupation of Beit She'an, but it is possible the force only passed there.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-25]

[1 Kings 4\:12](https://bible.oremus.org/?passage=1%20Kings%204\:12\&version=nrsv) refers to Beit She'an as part of the kingdom of [Solomon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomon), though the historical accuracy of this list is debated.[^cite_note-40] Archaeomagnetic dates suggest that the first Israelite urban settlement was established either during the Solomonic period or in the pre-Omride phase of the early [kingdom of Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Israel_\(Samaria\)) at the latest, and that it was probably destroyed around 935–900 BC.[^cite_note-41]

##### Assyrian period

In 732 BCE, the [Assyrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Assyrian_Empire) conquest of the [northern kingdom of Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Israel_\(Samaria\)) under [Tiglath-Pileser III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiglath-Pileser_III) brought about the destruction of Beit She'an by fire.[^cite_note-rehov.org-30] (see also Megiddo).

Minimal reoccupation occurred until the [Hellenistic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period).[^cite_note-rehov.org-30]

### Classical Age

#### Hellenistic period



Map of the [Decapolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decapolis) showing the location of Beit She'an, here called by its Greek name, Scythopolis

The [Hellenistic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period) saw the reoccupation of the site of Beit She'an under the new name "Scythopolis" ([Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek): Σκυθόπολις),[^cite_note-42] meaning "City of the Scythians", possibly named after the [Scythian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scythians) mercenaries who settled there as veterans.[^cite_note-Avi-Yonah_1962_pp._123–134-43]

Little is known about the Hellenistic city, but during the 3rd century BCE a large temple was constructed on the tell.[^cite_note-44] It is unknown which deity was worshipped there, but the temple continued to be used during Roman times. Graves dating from the Hellenistic period are simple, singular [rock-cut tombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock-cut_tomb).[^cite_note-Rowe_49-45] From 301 to 198 BCE the area was under the control of the [Ptolemies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemies), and Beit She'an is mentioned in 3rd–2nd century BCE written sources describing the [Syrian Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Wars) between the Ptolemaic and [Seleucid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucid) dynasties. In 198 BCE the Seleucids finally conquered the region.

#### Roman period

In 63 BCE, [Pompey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompey) made [Judea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judea) a part of the [Roman Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republic). Beit She'an was refounded and rebuilt by [Gabinius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aulus_Gabinius).[^cite_note-Rowe_46-46] The town center shifted from the summit of the mound, or tell, to its slopes. Scythopolis prospered and became the leading city of the [Decapolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decapolis), the only one west of the Jordan River.[^cite_note-47]

The city flourished under the "[Pax Romana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pax_Romana)", as evidenced by high-level [urban planning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_planning) and extensive construction, including the best preserved Roman theatre of ancient [Samaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaria), as well as a [hippodrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippodrome), a [cardo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardo) and other trademarks of the Roman influence. [Mount Gilboa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Gilboa), 7 km (4 mi) away, provided dark [basalt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basalt) blocks, as well as water (via an aqueduct) to the town. Beit She'an is said to have sided with the Romans during the early phase of the [First Jewish–Roman War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Jewish%E2%80%93Roman_War) in 66 CE.[^cite_note-Rowe_46-46] Excavations have focused less on the Roman period ruins, so not much is known about this period. The Penn. University Museum excavation of the northern cemetery, however, did uncover significant finds. The Roman period tombs are of the *[loculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loculus_\(architecture\))* type: a rectangular rock-cut spacious chamber with smaller chambers (*loculi*) cut into its side.[^cite_note-Rowe_49-45] Bodies were placed directly in the *loculi*, or inside sarcophagi which were placed in the *loculi*. A [sarcophagus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarcophagus) with an inscription identifying its occupant in Greek as "Antiochus, the son of Phallion", may have held the cousin of [Herod the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herod_the_Great).[^cite_note-Rowe_49-45] One of the most interesting Roman grave finds was a [bronze incense shovel](http://www.penn.museum/collections/object/297803) with the handle in the form of an animal leg, or hoof, now in the [University of Pennsylvania Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania_Museum).[^cite_note-48]

### Middle Ages

#### Byzantine period



Mosaic from 5th–7th century synagogue in northern Beit Shean, possibly Samaritan, [Israel Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel_Museum)

[^cite_note-49]

Copious archaeological remains were found dating to the [Byzantine period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Palestine) (324 – 636) and were excavated by the [Penn Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penn_Museum) from 1921 to 1923. A rotunda church was constructed on top of the [tell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tell_\(archaeology\)), and a wall enclosed the entire city.[^cite_note-Rowe_50-50] Textual sources mention several other churches in the town.[^cite_note-Rowe_50-50] Scythopolis was primarily Christian, as attested to by many churches, but evidence of Jewish habitation and a [Samaritan synagogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaritanism) indicate established communities of these minorities. The pagan temples in the city center went out of use, but the [nymphaeum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nymphaeum) and the [thermae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermae) were restored, and a Sigma-shaped complex was added.[^cite_note-51] Many of the buildings of Scythopolis were damaged in the [363 Galilee earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/363_Galilee_earthquake). In 409, [Syria Palaestina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria_Palaestina) was divided and Scythopolis became the capital of the northern region of [Palaestina Secunda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palaestina_Secunda).[^cite_note-Rowe_45-52] and the [See of Scythopolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scythopolis_\(see\)) was the [Metropolitan archdiocese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_bishop).

Dedicatory inscriptions indicate a preference for donations to religious buildings. Many colourful [mosaics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosaic) were preserved, such as that featuring the [zodiac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zodiac) in the Lady Mary's Monastery, or the one with a [temple menorah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_menorah) and the phrase *shalom* in the House of Leontius [synagogue](48559.md). The Samaritan synagogue's mosaic was unique in its [aniconic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aniconic) abstention from human or animal images, instead using floral and geometrical motifs. Elaborate decorations were also found in the settlement's many luxurious villas, and in the 6th century especially, the city reached its maximum size of 40,000 and spread beyond its period city walls.[^cite_note-Rowe_45-52]

The Byzantine-era portion of the northern cemetery was excavated in 1926. The tombs from this period consisted of small rock-cut halls with vaulted graves on three sides.[^cite_note-Rowe_52-53] A great variety of objects were found in the tombs, including [terracotta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terracotta) figurines possibly depicting the [Virgin and Child](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgin_and_Child), many terracotta lamps, glass mirrors, bells, tools, knives, finger rings, iron keys, glass beads, bone hairpins, and many other items.[^cite_note-Rowe_52-53]

Influential Christian personalities who lived or passed through Scythopolis are [Procopius of Scythopolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procopius_of_Scythopolis) (died July 7, 303), [Cyril of Scythopolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyril_of_Scythopolis) (ca. 525–559), [Epiphanius of Salamis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epiphanius_of_Salamis) (c. 310/320–403) and [Joseph of Tiberias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_of_Tiberias) (c. 285 – c. 356) who met there around the year 355.

#### Early Muslim period

Main article: [Jund al-Urdunn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jund_al-Urdunn)



[Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic) inscription in stone and gold-glass mosaic dated to 738 from the Bazaar Gate of then Bisan, now displayed in the Israel Museum

In 634, Byzantine forces were defeated by the [Rashidun army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashidun_army) under the [Rashid caliph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashidun_Caliphate) [Umar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umar), and the city reverted to its Semitic name, being named Baysan (Bisan) in Arabic. The day of victory came to be known in Arabic as *Yawm Baysan* "day of Baysan."[^cite_note-Shahin-2] The city was not damaged; newly arrived Muslims lived alongside the Christian population until the 8th century. The town formed one of the districts of [Jund al-Urdunn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jund_al-Urdunn), itself a part of the [Bilad al-Sham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilad_al-Sham), until the Crusades.[^cite_note-54]

The city declined; structures were built in the streets, narrowing them to mere [alleys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alley), and makeshift shops were opened among the colonnades. The city reached a low point in the 8th century, witnessed by the removal of [marble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marble) for producing [lime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium_oxide), the blocking off of the main street, and the conversion of a main plaza into a cemetery.[^cite_note-JVL-55]

However, some recently discovered counter-evidence may be offered to this picture of decline. In common with state-directed building work carried out in other towns and cities in the region during the 720s,[^cite_note-56] Baysan's commercial infrastructure was refurbished: its main colonnaded market street, once thought to date to the sixth century, is now known—based on a mosaic inscription—to be a redesign dating from the time of the Umayyad caliph [Hisham ibn Abd al-Malik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hisham_ibn_Abd_al-Malik) (r. 724–43).[^cite_note-57] [Al-Bakri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Bakri) of [al-Andalus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Andalus) noted that the wine produced there was delicious.[^cite_note-Shahin-2]

On January 18, 749, Umayyad Baysan was devastated by a [catastrophic earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/749_Galilee_earthquake). A few residential neighborhoods grew among the ruins, probably established by the survivors, but the city never recovered its magnificence. The city center moved to the southern hill, where the [Crusaders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusaders) later built a castle.[^cite_note-Death-58]

Jerusalemite historian [al-Muqaddasi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Muqaddasi) visited Baysan in 985, during the [Abbasid Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Caliphate) and wrote that it was "on the river, with plentiful palm trees, and water, though somewhat heavy (brackish.)" He further noted that Baysan was notable for its [indigo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigo), [rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice), [dates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Date_palm), and grape syrup known as *dibs*.[^cite_note-59] Its principal [mosque](19894.md) was situated in the center of its marketplace.[^cite_note-60]

#### Crusader period



Crusader castle with moat and inner tower.

In the [Crusader period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusades), the Lordship of Bessan was occupied by [Tancred, Prince of Galilee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tancred,_Prince_of_Galilee) in 1099; it was never part of the [Principality of Galilee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principality_of_Galilee), despite its location, but became a royal domain of the [Kingdom of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Jerusalem) in 1101, probably until around 1120. According to the [Lignages d'Outremer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lignages_d'Outremer), the first Crusader lord of Bessan once it became part of the Kingdom of Jerusalem was Adam, a younger son of Robert III de Béthune, [peer of Flanders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_of_Flanders) and head of the [House of Béthune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_B%C3%A9thune). His descendants were known by the family name *de Bessan*.

It occasionally passed back under royal control until new lords were created. The town became part of the [Belvoir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belvoir_Castle_\(Israel\)) [fiefdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusader_states).[^cite_note-61]

A small Crusader fortress surrounded by a moat was built in the area southeast of the Roman theatre, where the diminished town had relocated after the 749 earthquake.[^cite_note-Death-58] The fortress was destroyed by [Saladin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saladin) in 1183.[^cite_note-Negev-62]

During the 1260 [Battle of Ain Jalut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Ain_Jalut), retreating Mongol forces passed near the town, but did not enter it.

### Modern Ages

#### Mamluk period



Plan of Beisan-Scythopolis from the 1871–1877 [PEF Survey of Palestine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PEF_Survey_of_Palestine)

Under [Mamluk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamluk_Sultanate) rule, Beit She'an was the principal town in the district of [Damascus](8914.md) and a relay station for the [postal service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barid) between [Damascus](8914.md) and [Cairo](6293.md). It was also the capital of [sugar cane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_cane) processing for the region. Jisr al-Maqtu'a, "the truncated/cut-off bridge", a bridge consisting of a single arch spanning 25 ft (7.6 m) and hung 50 ft (15 m) above a stream, was built during that period.[^cite_note-Shahin164-63]

#### Ottoman period



Ottoman Saraya

During this period, the inhabitants of Baysan were mainly Muslim, but there were some [Jews](25955086.md). The 14th century [topographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topographer) [Ishtori Haparchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ishtori_Haparchi) settled there and completed his work "[Bulb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulb) and Flower" ([Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language): כפתור ופרח, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Hebrew): *Kaftor vaFeraḥ*) in 1322, the first Hebrew book on the geography of the [Land of Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_of_Israel).[^cite_note-64][^cite_note-Britannica-65]

During the 400 years of [Ottoman rule](22278.md), Baysan lost its regional importance. During the reign of Sultan [Abdul Hamid II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdul_Hamid_II) when the [Jezreel Valley railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jezreel_Valley_railway), which was part of the [Haifa](55606.md) to [Damascus](8914.md) extension of the [Hejaz railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hejaz_railway) was constructed, a limited revival took place. The local peasant population was largely impoverished by the Ottoman feudal land system, which leased tracts of land to tenants and collected taxes from them for their use.[^cite_note-Shahin-2]

The Swiss–German traveler [Johann Ludwig Burckhardt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Ludwig_Burckhardt) described Beisan in 1812: "The present village of Bysan contains seventy or eighty houses; its inhabitants are in a miserable condition, from being exposed to the depredations of the [Bedouins](61770.md) of [the Ghor](12105764.md), to whom they also pay a heavy tribute."[^cite_note-Burckhardt1822-66]

In 1870/1871, an Ottoman census listed the village in the [nahiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nahiya) (sub-district) of Shafa al-Shamali.[^cite_note-67]

In the early 20th century, though still a tiny and obscure village, Beisan was known for its plentiful water supply, fertile soil, and producing olives, grapes, figs, almonds, apricots, and apples.[^cite_note-Shahin-2]

#### British Mandate period





Detailed map of the town in the 1930s from the [Survey of Palestine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survey_of_Palestine)

Under the Mandate, the city was the center of the [District of Baysan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/District_of_Baysan). According to a [census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1922_census_of_Palestine) conducted in 1922 by the [British Mandate authorities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandate_for_Palestine), Beit She'an (Baisan) had a population of 1,941, consisting of 1,687 Muslims, 41 Jews and 213 Christians.[^cite_note-Census1922-68]

In 1934, [Lawrence of Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_of_Arabia) noted that "Bisan is now a purely Arab village," where "very fine views of the river can be had from the housetops." He further noted that "many nomad and [Bedouin](61770.md) encampments, distinguished by their black tents, were scattered about the riverine plain, their flocks and herds grazing round them."[^cite_note-Shahin-2] Beisan was home to a mainly [Mizrahi Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mizrahi_Jews) community of 95 until 1936, when the [1936–1939 Arab revolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1936%E2%80%931939_Arab_revolt_in_Palestine) saw Beisan serve as a center of Arab attacks on Jews in Palestine.[^cite_note-Britannica-65][^cite_note-Haaretz-69][^cite_note-70] In 1938, after learning of the murder of his close friend and Jewish leader Haim Sturmann, [Orde Wingate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orde_Wingate) led his men on an offensive in the Arab section of Baysan, the rebels’ suspected base.[^cite_note-Azure-71]

According to population surveys conducted in [British Mandate Palestine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandatory_Palestine), Beisan consisted of 5,080 Muslim Arabs out of a population of 5,540 (92% of the population), with the remainder being listed as Christians.[^cite_note-Domino-72] In 1945, the surrounding [District of Baysan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/District_of_Baysan) consisted of 16,660 Muslims (67%), 7,590 Jews (30%), and 680 Christians (3%); and Arabs owned 44% of land, Jews owned 34%, and 22% constituted public lands. The [1947 UN Partition Plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1947_UN_Partition_Plan) allocated Beisan and most of its district to the proposed [Jewish state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_state).[^cite_note-Shahin-2][^cite_note-73][^cite_note-74]

#### 1948 Palestine war

Further information: [1948 Palestine war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1948_Palestine_war) and [1948 Palestinian expulsion and flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1948_Palestinian_expulsion_and_flight)

Jewish forces and local Bedouins first clashed during the [1947–1948 civil war in Mandatory Palestine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1947%E2%80%931948_civil_war_in_Mandatory_Palestine) in February and March 1948, part of [Operation Gideon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Gideon),[^cite_note-Shahin-2] itself part of [Plan Dalet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plan_Dalet).[^cite_note-75] [Joseph Weitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Weitz), a leading [Yishuv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yishuv) figure, wrote in his diary on May 4, 1948, that, "The Beit Shean Valley is the gate for our state in the Galilee...\[I]ts clearing is the need of the hour."[^cite_note-Shahin-2]

Beisan, then an Arab village, fell to the Jewish militias three days before the end of the Mandate. After [Israel's Declaration of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel's_Declaration_of_Independence) in May 1948, during intense shelling by Syrian border units, followed by the recapture of the valley by the [Haganah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haganah), the Arab inhabitants [fled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1948_Palestinian_exodus) across the Jordan River.[^cite_note-76] The property and buildings abandoned after the conflict were then held by the State of Israel.[^cite_note-Shahin-2] Most [Arab Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Christian) relocated to [Nazareth](21702.md).

Historian [Saleh Abdel Jawad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saleh_Abdel_Jawad) claims that Beisan experienced a massacre via aerial bombardment by Israeli forces that was "entirely without military justification" and which was used as "a tool of expulsion".

#### State of Israel



War monument, [Al Mansfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Mansfeld) and [Munio Weinraub](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munio_Weinraub), 1960



A neighborhood in Beit She'an

A [ma'abarah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ma'abarot) (immigration camp) inhabited mainly by North African Jewish [olim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olim) (immigrants)[^cite_note-77] was erected in Beit She'an, and it later became a [development town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Development_town).

From 1969, Beit She'an was a target for [Katyusha rockets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katyusha_rocket_launcher) and [mortar](324499.md) attacks from Jordan.[^cite_note-78] In the [1974 Beit She'an attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1974_Beit_She'an_attack), militants of the [Popular Democratic Front for the Liberation of Palestine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Front_for_the_Liberation_of_Palestine#Formation_as_the_PDFLP), took over an apartment building and murdered a family of four.[^cite_note-Haaretz-69]

In 1999, Beit She'an was incorporated as a city.[^cite_note-7wonders-79] Geographically, it lies in the middle of the [Emek HaMaayanot Regional Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emek_HaMaayanot_Regional_Council), formerly the Beit She'an Valley Regional Council.[^cite_note-Foeme-80]

Since June 2000, Beit She'an has been included in the "Tentative List of World Heritage Places" of the [UNESCO](21786641.md).[^cite_note-81]

During the [Second Intifada](71717.md), in the [2002 Beit She'an attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2002_Beit_She'an_attack), six Israelis were killed and over 30 were injured by two [Palestinian militants](2646043.md), who opened fire and threw grenades at a polling station in the center of Bet She'an where party members were voting in the [Likud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Likud) primary.

## Seismicity

Beit She'an is located above the [Dead Sea Transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_Sea_Transform) (a [fault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fault_\(geology\)) system that forms the [transform boundary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transform_fault) between the [African Plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Plate) to the west and the [Arabian Plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabian_Plate) to the east) and is one of the cities in Israel most at risk to [earthquakes](10106.md) (along with [Kiryat Shmona](323369.md), [Safed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safed), [Tiberias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiberias), and [Eilat](66162.md)).[^cite_note-82] Historically, the city was destroyed in the [Golan earthquake of 749](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golan_earthquake_of_749).

## Economy



Beit She'an municipality



Beit She'an park



[Bank Leumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_Leumi) branch in Beit She'an

Beit She'an is a center of cotton-growing, and many of residents are employed in the cotton fields of the surrounding [kibbutzim](106200.md). Other local industries include a textile mill and clothing factory.[^cite_note-Britannica-65]

### Tourism

Ancient Beit She'an, one of the most spectacular Roman and Byzantine sites in Israel, is a major tourist attraction.[^cite_note-Mfa-86]

When the ancient city of Beit She'an was opened to the public in the 1990s and turned into a national park, tourism became a major sector of the economy.[^cite_note-87]

New railway station, Beit She'an

Beit She'an had a railway station that opened in 1904 on the [Jezreel Valley railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jezreel_Valley_railway) which was an extension of the [Hejaz railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hejaz_railway). This station closed together with the rest of the Jezreel Valley railway in 1948.

In 2011–2016 the valley railway was rebuilt and the new [Beit She'an railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beit_She'an_railway_station),[^cite_note-88] located at the same site as the historical station was opened. Passenger service offered at the station connects the city to Afula, Haifa and destinations in between. In addition to passenger service, the station also includes a freight rail terminal.

## Sports

The local football club, [Hapoel Beit She'an](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hapoel_Beit_She'an_F.C.) spent several seasons in the top division in the 1990s, but folded in 2006 after several relegations. Maccabi Beit She'an currently plays in [Liga Bet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liga_Bet).

## Voting Patterns

In the [2022 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Israeli_legislative_election), 90 percent of voters in Beit She'an voted for the parties of the right-wing coalition led by [Benjamin Netanyahu](56472.md), while 8 percent voted for the parties of the center-left coalition led by [Yair Lapid](2448072.md) and 0.1 percent voted for the Arab parties.[^cite_note-89]
