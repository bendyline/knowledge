**British Sikhs** number over 535,000 people and account for 0.8% of the British population as of 2021, forming the United Kingdom's [fourth-largest religious group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_the_United_Kingdom). According to the [2021 United Kingdom census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_United_Kingdom_census), British Sikhs numbered 535,517, with 520,092 in England, 10,988 in Scotland, 4,048 in Wales, and 389 in Northern Ireland.[^cite_note-ons.gov.uk-4][^cite_note-2022census_Scot-2][^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6] The largest Sikh populations in the [United Kingdom](31717.md) are in the [West Midlands](351076.md) and [Greater London](45367389.md).

### Sikh gurus and their associates

In 1708 during the [final days](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joti_Jot) of [Guru Gobind Singh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guru_Gobind_Singh) in [Nanded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanded), the [Mughal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire) [emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_emperors_of_the_Mughal_Empire) [Bahadur Shah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahadur_Shah_I) is said to have dispatched an English surgeon by the name of Dr Cole to dress the ailing guru's wounds.[^cite_note-\:0-19][^cite_note-20][^cite_note-21] The Guru paid the English surgeon 10 gold coins per day for his services.[^cite_note-\:0-19] The English surgeon attending the Guru is mentioned in an account of the passing of Guru Gobind Singh attributed to Koer Singh.[^cite_note-22]

During the execution of [Banda Singh Bahadur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banda_Singh_Bahadur) at Delhi in 1716, two Englishmen, named John Surman and [Edward Stephenson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Stephenson_\(colonial_administrator\)) of the [East India Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_India_Company), were witnesses.[^cite_note-\:1-23][^cite_note-\:2-24] An account of the execution of Banda was written by the two men to [Robert Hedges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Hedges_\(colonial_administrator\)), dated to 10 March 1716.[^cite_note-\:1-23][^cite_note-\:2-24]

### Sikh Empire

See also: [Sikhism and the British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhism_and_the_British_Empire)



Duleep Singh, the last [Maharaja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maharaja) of the [Sikh Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikh_Empire) and the first Sikh in England, on the Lower Terrace of [Osborne House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osborne_House) on 28 August 1854.

Sikhs and Britain have a long and storied history. Decades before the last Sikh King, [Duleep Singh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duleep_Singh), visited Britain in the middle of the 19th century, there had been Anglo-Sikh contact as far back as the 1800s in the Punjab with his father [Maharaja Ranjit Singh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranjit_Singh). Since then, even though this relationship has changed in nature many times, both communities have left a strong permanent influence on each other. For instance, in such varied parts of British society as food, language, political systems, soldiering and of course cricket, the British-Sikh relationship has given rise to many new facets of modern British and Indian society.[^cite_note-25]

### Migration of Sikhs to Britain

The first Sikh to settle permanently in Britain was [Maharaja Duleep Singh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duleep_Singh) (1838–1893), the last Sikh Emperor of the Imperial Sukerchakia Dynasty, from 1844 to 1849. He arrived in England in the year 1854, having been exiled from his kingdom by the East India Company. His mother, [Empress Jind Kaur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jind_Kaur) (1817–1863), arrived in 1860 at Kensington in [Victorian London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/19th-century_London) and settled permanently, after being at war with Britain for an extended period of time until the fall of the Sikh Dynasty in 1849. She was given permission by Parliament to settle on English soil.

The first Sikh settlers started migrating from the Punjab in 1911, when the first Sikh Gurdwara was opened in London. During the start of the [First](4764461.md) and [Second World Wars](32927.md) respectively, there was already an established Sikh presence in many parts of England. Britain's first south Asian immigrants after the war were Pakistani Muslims and Punjabi Sikhs from the Jullundur Doab. They tended to settle in midland towns such as [Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham) and [Leeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leeds), as well as in the London borough of [Southall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southall).

### Present

In 2019, [Seema Malhotra MP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seema_Malhotra) set up the first debate in Parliament to discuss the positive contribution of the Sikh community over the last 70 years.[^cite_note-26]

Despite the existence of advocacy organisations like the British Sikh Report, there is very little systematic research on British Sikhs. The only major [academic work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikh_studies) that is a comprehensive, systematic, and thorough history of the community is by Gurharpal Singh and Darshan S. Tatla, *Sikhs in Britain: The Making of a Community* (Zed, 2006). This work needs updating in light of the impending Census 2021 to reflect changes in the community's profile.

## Demography

According to the [2021 United Kingdom census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_United_Kingdom_census), Sikhs in England and Wales numbered 524,140, or 0.9% of the population.[^cite_note-ons.gov.uk-4]

### Place of birth

According to the 2017 British Sikh Report which surveys the community in the UK, 71% were born in England, followed by 15% in [India](14533.md), 8% in [East Africa](260987.md), 2% in Scotland, and 1% in [Afghanistan](737.md).[^cite_note-27]

### Politics



Sikhs meet with PM [David Cameron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Cameron)

There have been sixteen Sikh MPs and eight Sikh peers in the UK's history.[^cite_note-30] The first Sikh MP, [Piara Singh Khabra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piara_Singh_Khabra), was elected in [1992](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1992_United_Kingdom_general_election) from [Ealing Southall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ealing_Southall_\(UK_Parliament_constituency\)) and the first Sikh peer was [Tarsem King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarsem_King,_Baron_King_of_West_Bromwich) of [West Bromwich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Bromwich), appointed in 1999. Currently, there are seven Sikh peers and eleven Sikh MPs; Sikhs make up 0.89% of the [House of Lords](13658.md) and 1.69% of the [House of Commons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_House_of_Commons), while making up 0.8% of the British population.

The seven incumbent Sikh [peers](13658.md) include four [Conservatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Party_\(UK\)) ([Baron Rana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baron_Rana) of Malone in [Antrim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Antrim),[^cite_note-31][^cite_note-32] [Baroness Verma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baroness_Verma) of [Leicester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leicester), [Baron Suri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baron_Suri) of [Ealing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ealing), and [Baron Ranger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baron_Ranger) of [Mayfair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayfair)), two [crossbenchers](361590.md#United_Kingdom) ([Baron Singh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indarjit_Singh) of [Wimbledon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wimbledon,_London) and [Baron Ranger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kulveer_Ranger,_Baron_Ranger_of_Northwood) of Northwood) and one Labour member ([Baron Sahota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baron_Sahota) of [Telford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telford)).

[A 2023 poll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Sikh_Report) indicated that 43% of British Sikhs would vote for Labour, 20% Conservative and 4% each for the Liberal Democrats and Greens in [the next general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_Kingdom_general_election).[^cite_note-33]

### Education

65% percent of British Sikhs have a [graduate level qualification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graduate_diploma) or above. Sikhs in the 20–34 age group have the highest level of graduates (55%) within the Sikh community. The highest level of postgraduate qualifications of [Master's degrees](170346.md) (22%) is in the 35–49 age group. Eight percent of Sikhs aged 65 and over have a [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy). The split of formal education between women and men is roughly equal, with slightly more women holding a university degree or equivalent (48% of women, 42% of men).[^cite_note-36]

### Employment

The most popular employment sectors for British Sikhs include: [Healthcare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_care_in_the_United_Kingdom) (10%), [IT and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_technology) (8%), [Teaching and Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_in_the_United_Kingdom) (9%), [Accountancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accounting) and [Financial Management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_Management) (7%), indicating that Sikhs tend to favour professional and technical employment sectors over others. Healthcare is a popular sector for all age groups. Teaching and Education is more common in the 35–49 and the 50–64 age groups than others, whereas accountancy and financial management is more popular with the 20–34 age group (9%) compared with 6% respectively for both the 35–49 and the 50–64 age groups. The top career choices for Sikh women are Healthcare (14%) and Teaching and Education (15%). Healthcare is also a joint second most popular choice for Sikh men along with Accountancy and Financial Management, the most popular sector being IT and Technology (13%).[^cite_note-37]

### Integration

British Sikhs have been praised as an example of positive cultural integration in the United Kingdom, many having achieved success due to a strong cultural work ethic combined with an emphasis on the importance of the family.[^cite_note-38]

### Wealth

#### Home ownership

The 2021 census for England and Wales recorded 75.3% of Sikhs either owning their home with a mortgage (45.7%) or outright (29.6%). 18.9% rent privately or live rent free and the remaining 5.7% live in [social housing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_housing_in_the_United_Kingdom). Across religious groups, Sikhs held the highest rates of home ownership (compared to 62.8% of the wider population) and were the least likely to live in social housing (compared to 17.1% of the wider population).[^cite_note-39]

Home ownership is very high amongst British Sikhs with the [British Sikh Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Sikh_Report) in 2014 recording 87% of households owning at least a portion of their home. 30% of British Sikh households own their homes outright and only 9% rent their properties. Only 1% of British Sikhs claim [Housing Benefit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Housing_in_the_United_Kingdom). This represents the highest level of private home ownership rate over any other community in the UK. In addition, half of all British Sikh families (49%) own more than one property in the UK, with a similar number (50%) owning at least one property in India, apparently indicating that property ownership is used as a top means of building assets for the future. 6% of British Sikhs own property elsewhere in Europe.[^cite_note-40]

#### Income

Relative to the national average income at approximately £40,000 before tax (according to the *British Sikh Report*), it found that Sikh households tend to be affluent. Two in every three British Sikh households (66%) have pre-tax incomes in excess of £40,000, and over a third (34%) have an income in excess of £80,000, giving a value for the Sikh Pound of 7.63 billion.[^cite_note-41]

However, this data appears to be contradicted by research carried out by the *[Joseph Rowntree Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Rowntree_Foundation)*, which actually states Sikhs have the second highest poverty rate in the UK, with 27% of British Sikhs living below the poverty line; this is in comparison to 18% of the population as a whole.[^cite_note-42]

About one in three British Sikh families (34%) own a business in the UK.[^cite_note-43]

### Charitable giving and volunteering



Sikhs distributing langar (free community kitchen) in London

Performing Seva (selfless service) is a basic tenet of Sikhism, and Sikhs are also expected to share at least 10 per cent of their earnings with those less fortunate and for good causes (Dasvandh).

64% percent of British Sikhs engage in some volunteering work, and 40% give between one and five hours per week on voluntary activities, including [Seva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seva_Bharati) at their [Gurdwara](482849.md), whilst more than 2% spend over 25 hours on such activities, spending an average of about 200 hours per year on voluntary activities. 93% percent claim to donate some money to charity every month, with 50% donating between £1 and £20 every month, and 7% donating more than £100 per month. It is estimated that Sikhs in Britain donate around £380 per year to charity on average. Taken as a whole, Sikhs in the UK are estimated to donate about £125 million to charity per annum and spend over 65 million hours each year on voluntary activities.[^cite_note-44]

### Care of the elderly

Sikhs prefer to live in extended family households as they grow older (61% of males and 52% of females). The second highest preference is in their own home (44% males and 41% females) and the third preference is in a [retirement village](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retirement_community) (31% females and 24% males).[^cite_note-45]

According to the [2021 United Kingdom census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_United_Kingdom_census), Sikhs in England and Wales enumerated 524,140, or 0.9% of the population[^cite_note-ons.gov.uk-4] Northern Ireland recorded a population of 389, or 0.02% of the population.[^cite_note-MS-B21-49] The equivalent census was recorded a year later in Scotland with a population of 10,988, making up 0.2% of the population.[^cite_note-Scot_22-50]

In the 2021 census for England and Wales, the main places of birth were the United Kingdom at 299,104 people (57.1% of the total Sikh population), [South Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Asians_in_the_United_Kingdom) at 192,485 (36.7%), South and Eastern Africa at 17,659 (3.4%) and other parts of Europe at 8,277 (1.6%). The largest ethnic group within the 'other ethnic group' category were Sikhs, with 76,535 using the write-in response option to declare they held Sikh ethnicity. A further 22,814 self-declared that Sikh was their ethnicity within the 'other Asian' category.[^cite_note-62] 426,230 people in England and Wales identified as Sikh through the religion question alone, 1,725 through the ethnic group question alone and 97,910 through both questions together.[^cite_note-63]

According to the 2017 British Sikh Report which surveys the community in the UK, 71% were born in England, followed by 15% in India, 8% in East Africa, 2% in Scotland, and 1% in Afghanistan.[^cite_note-64]

## Exemptions in British law for Sikhs

Sikhs are exempt from a few British laws on account of religious reasons. For example, men wearing a [Dastar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dastar) (turban) may ride a motorcycle without a helmet, and are permitted to wear their [Kirpan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirpan) as religious dress rather than as an offensive weapon in certain situations. In February 2010, Sir [Mota Singh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mota_Singh), Britain's first Asian judge, criticised the banning of the Kirpan in public places such as schools.[^cite_note-82] The tenth and final guru, [Guru Gobind Singh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guru_Gobind_Singh) formally included the [Kirpan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirpan) as a mandatory article of faith for all baptised Sikhs,[^cite_note-amritsar.com-83] making it a duty for Sikhs to be able to defend the needy and suppressed, and to defend righteousness and the freedom of expression.

## Controversies

### Attitudes towards women

In November 2021, according to a report by Sikh Women’s Aid (SWA) organisation, about 70% of the women in the UK Sikh community have suffered [domestic abuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestic_abuse). Over a third of the respondents had suffered [child sexual abuse](18721790.md). Most of the victims knew their abusers and about half of the incidents took place in the home.[^cite_note-89][^cite_note-90]

### Census data

In 2018, some Sikh organisations requested the ONS ([Office for National Statistics](324029.md)) to include an ethnic [tick box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Checkbox) for Sikhs, creating an ongoing dispute between various Sikh organisations. The ONS rejected the request.[^cite_note-91] The ONS rejected the demand in their published paper.[^cite_note-92]

### Inter-faith marriages

Holding an *[Anand Karaj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anand_Karaj)* wedding ceremony between a Sikh and a non-Sikh has become a contentious issue. In 2016, [armed police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armed_police) arrested [scores of protesters at Gurdwara Sahib in Leamington Spa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gurdwara_Sahib_Leamington_and_Warwick#Conflict), which *[The Telegraph](23797577.md)* claims "has a history of tensions over mixed marriages".[^cite_note-93] [Sikh Youth UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Sikh_Youth_Federation), which was behind the protest, blamed "a rogue Gurdwara committee creating discord".[^cite_note-94]

One Sikh journalist called the issue a "deepening schism"[^cite_note-95] while another expressed dismay at the protesters' use of masks, and the way their actions allowed the *[kirpan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirpan)* (ceremonial dagger) to be seen as a bladed weapon rather than traditional dress, thus giving "the racists and the bigots justifications for their ignorant hatred".[^cite_note-96] An investigation on [BBC Asian Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Asian_Network) found that these disruptions over [interfaith marriage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interfaith_marriage) had been going on for years.[^cite_note-97]

### Alleged grooming of Sikh girls by Muslim men

See also: [Child sexual abuse in the United Kingdom § Group-based child sexual exploitation](41787253.md#Group-based_child_sexual_exploitation)

A BBC [*Inside Out (London)*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inside_Out_\(2002_TV_programme\)) programme televised in September 2013 interviewed several young Sikh women who were allegedly [groomed](1651190.md) and sexually abused by Muslim men, with one alleged ex-groomer even admitting that they specifically targeted Sikh girls. Bhai Mohan Singh, working for the Sikh Awareness Society (SAS), told the BBC he was investigating 19 alleged cases where Sikh girls were allegedly being groomed by older Muslim men,[^cite_note-103] of which one ended with a successful conviction.[^cite_note-104][^cite_note-Insideoutconvict-105] In August 2013 four Muslims and two Hindus were convicted at [Leicester Crown Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leicester_Crown_Court) of paying a "vulnerable and damaged" 16-year-old Sikh girl for sex,[^cite_note-BBCnews-106] the investigation having been opened due to evidence Bhai Mohan Singh had presented to the police.[^cite_note-Insideoutconvict-105]

However, a report published the previous year by [Faith Matters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faith_Matters) (which runs the [TELL MAMA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tell_Mama_UK) anti-Muslim violence helpline and works closely with the Jewish [Community Security Trust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community_Security_Trust)[^cite_note-107]) claimed that the Sikh Awareness Society included radical anti-Muslim elements among its members;[^cite_note-faithmatterseld-108][^cite_note-HuffPostJessicaElgot-109] Faith Matters furthermore alleged it was a matter of "common consensus" that the radical Sikhs said to have had secret meetings with the [English Defence League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Defence_League) were members of the SAS.[^cite_note-faithmatterseld-108][^cite_note-HuffPostJessicaElgot-109] The SAS denied allegations and distanced themselves from the organization,[^cite_note-faithmatterseld-108][^cite_note-HuffPostJessicaElgot-109] a spokesperson telling [Hope not Hate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hope_not_Hate): "We would have nothing to do with any racist or fascist group, certainly one that uses religion to divide people…I know nothing about this and no, we are not in any kind of talks and discussion with them".[^cite_note-110] [*The Nihal Show*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nihal_Arthanayake) on the [BBC Asian Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Asian_Network) discussed the issue and debated the merits of the grooming claims in September 2013.[^cite_note-111]

In 2018, a report by a Sikh activist organisation, *Sikh Youth UK*, entitled “The Religiously Aggravated Sexual Exploitation of Young Sikh Women Across the UK" made allegations of similarities between the case of Sikh Women and the Rotherham child sexual exploitation scandal.[^cite_note-112] However in 2019 this report was criticised by researchers and an official UK government report led by two Sikh academics for false and misleading information.[^cite_note-CockbainTufail2020-113][^cite_note-Jagbir_Jhutti-Johal;_Sunny_Hundal_(August_2019)p15-114] It noted: "The RASE report lacks solid data, methodological transparency and rigour. It is filled instead with sweeping generalisations and poorly substantiated claims around the nature and scale of abuse of Sikh girls and causal factors driving it. It appealed heavily to historical tensions between Sikhs and Muslims and narratives of honour in a way that seemed designed to whip up fear and hate".[^cite_note-Jagbir_Jhutti-Johal;_Sunny_Hundal_(August_2019)p15-114]

### Allegations of forced conversions of Sikh girls to Islam

In 2007, a Sikh girl's family claimed that she had been [forcibly converted to Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forced_conversion#United_Kingdom), and after being attacked by an armed gang, they received a police guard.[^cite_note-115] In response to these news stories, an [open letter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_letter) to [Sir Ian Blair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Ian_Blair) signed by ten academics argued that claims that Hindu and Sikh girls were being forcefully converted were "part of an arsenal of myths propagated by right-wing [Hindu supremacist organisations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindutva) in India".[^cite_note-116] The [Muslim Council of Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_Council_of_Britain) issued a press release pointing out there was a lack of evidence of any forced conversions and suggested it was an underhand attempt to smear the British Muslim population.[^cite_note-117]

An academic paper by Katy Sian published in the journal *South Asian Popular Culture* in 2011 explored the question of how "forced conversion narratives" arose around the [Sikh diaspora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikh_diaspora) in the United Kingdom.[^cite_note-Forced-118] Sian, who reports that claims of conversion through courtship on campuses are widespread in the UK, says that rather than relying on actual evidence they primarily rest on the word of "a friend of a friend" or on personal [anecdote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anecdote). According to Sian, the narrative is similar to accusations of "[white slavery](19333662.md)" lodged against the Jewish community and foreigners to the UK and the US, with the former having ties to [antisemitism](1078.md) that mirror the [Islamophobia](162173.md) betrayed by the modern narrative. Sian expanded on these views in her 2013 monograph *Unsettling Sikh and Muslim Conflict:* *Mistaken Identities, Forced Conversions, and Postcolonial Formations*.[^cite_note-Sian2013-119]

### Racially and religiously motivated attacks against Sikh women

In 2025, several racially and religiously aggravated attacks against Sikh women in the West Midlands were reported by UK media and investigated by police. In October 2025, West Midlands Police said a woman in her 20s had been raped in a racially aggravated attack in Walsall, and confirmed they were also investigating the rape of a Sikh woman in neighbouring Sandwell.[^cite_note-120]

A further Sikh female was raped in Oldbury, and the attacks led to increased fear within the Sikh community and discussions with police and MPs about anti-Sikh hate crime.[^cite_note-121]
