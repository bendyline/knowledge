The **euro sign** (**€**) is the [currency sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency_sign) used for the [euro](9472.md), the official currency of the [eurozone](184391.md). The design was presented to the public by the [European Commission](9974.md) on 12 December 1996. It consists of a stylized letter [E](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E) (or [epsilon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsilon)), crossed by two lines instead of one. Depending on convention in each nation, the symbol can either precede or follow the value, e.g., *€10* or *10 €*, often with an intervening space.

## Design



Graphic construction of the euro logo



The euro sign in a selection of [typefaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typeface)



The euro sign; [logotype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logotype) and [handwritten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handwriting)

There were originally 30 proposed designs for a symbol for Europe's new common currency. Of these, the Commission made a short-list of ten candidates and put these ten out to a public survey. The [President of the European Commission](24468.md) at the time ([Jacques Santer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Santer)) and the European Commissioner with responsibility for the euro ([Yves-Thibault de Silguy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yves-Thibault_de_Silguy)) then chose the winning design.[^cite_note-1] The other designs that were considered but not shortlisted are not available for the public to view, nor is any information about the designers available for public query. The Commission considers the process of designing to have been internal and keeps these records secret. The eventual winner was a design created by a team of four experts whose identities have not been revealed. *[Gazet van Antwerpen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gazet_van_Antwerpen)* has attributed the symbol to Belgian graphic designer Alain Billiet.[^cite_note-2]

> The symbol € is based on the Greek letter epsilon (ε), with the first letter in the word "Europe" and with 2 parallel lines signifying stability.

— [European Union](9317.md)[^cite_note-3]

The official story of the design history of the euro sign is disputed by [Arthur Eisenmenger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Eisenmenger), a former chief graphic designer for the [European Economic Community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Economic_Community), who says he had the idea 25 years before the Commission's decision.[^cite_note-4]

The Commission specified a euro logo with exact proportions and colours ([PMS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantone_Matching_System) Yellow foreground, PMS Reflex Blue background[^cite_note-ec.europa.eu-5]), for use in public-relations material related to the euro introduction. While the Commission intended the logo to be a prescribed [glyph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glyph) shape, type designers made it clear that they intended instead to adapt the design to be consistent with the [typefaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typeface) to which it was to be added.[^cite_note-6]

## &#x20;Usage

See also: [Language and the euro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_and_the_euro)



Euro sign appears in the top-left corner of a [€50 banknote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/50_euro_note)&#x20;



A [euro light sculpture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro-Skulptur) at the [European Central Bank](9474.md) in [Frankfurt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankfurt_Rhine-Main)

Placement of the sign varies. Countries have generally continued the style used for their former currencies. In those countries where previous convention was to place the currency sign before the figure, the euro sign is placed in the same position (e.g., €3.50).[^cite_note-7] In those countries where the amount preceded the national currency sign, the euro sign is again placed in that relative position (e.g., 3,50 €).

In English, the euro sign – like the [dollar sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dollar_sign) ⟨\$⟩ and the [pound sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound_sign) ⟨£⟩ – is usually placed before the figure, unspaced,[^cite_note-8] the reverse of usage in many other European languages. In When written out, "euro" is placed after the value in lower case; the plural is used for two or more units,[^cite_note-13] and euro cents are [separated with a full-stop, not a comma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_separator) as in many countries (e.g., *€1.50*, *14 euros*). The European Union's *[Interinstitutional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutions_of_the_European_Union) [Style Guide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Style_Guide)* (for EU staff) states that the euro sign should be placed in front of the amount without any space in English, but after the amount in most other languages.[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15][^cite_note-16][^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18]

Prices of items costing less than one euro are often written using a local abbreviation like ct. (particularly in Spain and Lithuania); c. (Ireland); Λ (Greece ([lambda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambda))); snt. or written -, without any symbol (Finland). For example, 10 cents may be written as 10 ct., 10c., 10Λ, 10 snt. or -,10, depending on location. The US style ¢ is rarely seen in formal contexts. In all cases, prices may be written as decimals, e.g. €0.07 or 0,07 €, according to [national conventions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_and_the_euro).

## Use on computers and mobile phones

The euro is represented in [Unicode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode) as U+20AC € EURO SIGN. In modern computer systems and mobile phones, this is the only [codepoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codepoint) used. When first introduced, however, work to retrofit the symbol into crowded pre-existing [character set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Character_set) standards and vendor-specific schemas presented challenges that were not fully resolved until widespread adoption of Unicode.

### History of implementation

Initially, different vendors assigned the euro sign to different code positions in their historic encoding schemes.[^cite_note-19] This led to some initial problems exchanging electronic documents containing the euro sign. This was not an issue when all participants used the same [operating system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operating_system) (OS) but files transferred between users of different operating systems often produced errors. Initially, Apple, Microsoft and vendors of Unix systems each chose a different [code point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_point) to represent a euro symbol. Thus a user of one OS might have written a euro symbol but, when the file was transferred to colleague using another OS, the colleague would see a different symbol, a question mark, or nothing at all.&#x20;

Initially, some mobile phone companies issued an interim software update for their special [SMS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_message_service) character set, replacing the (less-frequently-used in Europe) Japanese [yen sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yen_sign) with the euro sign. Subsequent mobile phones have both currency signs.

In 1998, the [Unicode Consortium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode_Consortium) allocated a unique codepoint (U+20AC € EURO SIGN) to this currency sign, with the release of [Unicode 2.1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode_2.1),[^cite_note-20] but it was still some time before many vendors integrated Unicode into their operating systems and applications.

### Entry methods

Depending on [keyboard layout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keyboard_layout) and the operating system, there is a variety of ways to enter the symbol. The symbol is engraved on most keyboards used in Europe. (For entry methods in other territories, please refer to local sources or the article [Unicode input](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode_input).)&#x20;
