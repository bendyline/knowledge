# Penalty shoot-out (field hockey)

In [field hockey](10886.md), a **penalty shootout** is a method used to decide which team progresses to the next stage of a tournament (or wins the tournament) following a tied game. Two methods have been used: the original **penalty stroke competition** is a best-of-five [penalty strokes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penalty_stroke) with [sudden death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudden_death_\(sport\)) if scores were level after five strokes. An alternate **penalty shoot-out competition** was introduced at major tournaments in 2011. Sometimes known as a **penalty shuffle**,[^cite_note-1] the method is similar to [penalty shots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penalty_shot_\(ice_hockey\)) in [ice hockey](14790.md) and consists of one-on-ones between an attacking player and a goalkeeper. Up to 2013, up to two 7.5-minute [golden goal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_goal) periods were played first; that method ceased after.

## Penalty stroke competition (before 2011)

To determine matches that end in a tie, a penalty stroke competition was used. Similar to a [penalty shoot-out in association football](511302.md), teams alternately take [penalty strokes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penalty_stroke), subject to the normal rules, to determine the winner. Each team is represented by any five players chosen from the team sheet submitted prior to the match[^cite_note-susp-4] and the order in which they participate is also chosen. Players from each team take alternate strokes in a best-of-five competition until a team has won or 10 strokes have been completed. The goal used is selected by the umpires and the team to take the first penalty stroke is determined by a [coin toss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coin_flipping). In the result of a tie after 10 strokes, the same players[^cite_note-rep-5] will continue to take strokes in a [sudden death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudden_death_\(sport\)) format until a winner is established. The order in which players take strokes may change and the team that started first in the shoot-out goes second for the duration of sudden death strokes.[^cite_note-Stroke_rules-2]

## Penalty shoot-out competition (since 2011)

Similar to a [penalty shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penalty_shot_\(ice_hockey\)) in [ice hockey](14790.md), the attacker gets a chance to run with the ball in a one-on-one situation against the goalkeeper.[^cite_note-EHL-6] The attacker starts on the 23-metre line with the ball and the goalkeeper starts on the goal line. When the whistle is blown, both can move and the attacker has 8 seconds to score a goal.[^cite_note-Shootout_Intro-7] Unlike a penalty stroke or penalty corner there are no restrictions on strokes the attacker may use to score and a goal is scored in the usual way. If the attacker commits an offence, the ball travels outside the [field of play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_hockey_pitch#Field_of_play),[^cite_note-PC-8] or 8 seconds elapse before the ball crosses the line a goal is not awarded. If the goalkeeper unintentionally fouls the attacker then the penalty shoot-out is re-taken; in the event the foul was intentional[^cite_note-PC-8] a penalty stroke is awarded.[^cite_note-Shootout_rules-3]

Just like its predecessor, the penalty shoot-out uses five players chosen from each team against a goalkeeper.[^cite_note-susp-4] It is a best-of-five competition and, if scores are tied at the end of this, it progresses to sudden-death with the same players[^cite_note-rep-5] until a winner is established. Which goal to use is chosen by umpires and which team starts is determined by a coin toss. Sudden-death is started by the team that did not start the shoot-out and players may proceed in a different order from the best-of-five.[^cite_note-Shootout_rules-3]

## History

The first Olympic match to be decided by a penalty stroke competition was during the [1972 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1972_Summer_Olympics) in the [11th-place classification match](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_hockey_at_the_1972_Summer_Olympics#Men's_Classification_Matches) when [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland_men's_national_field_hockey_team) defeated [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France_men's_national_field_hockey_team). Following the introduction of women's hockey to the Olympics in 1992, the first Olympic women's penalty stroke competition was at the [1996 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_Summer_Olympics) and decided the [bronze medal match](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_hockey_at_the_1996_Summer_Olympics_%E2%80%93_Women's_tournament#Bronze_medal_match) between [Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Britain_women's_national_field_hockey_team) and the [Netherlands](7325406.md).[^cite_note-9] However, penalty strokes had already long been used in the [Women's Hockey World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women's_Hockey_World_Cup) which started in 1974;[^cite_note-10] notably, the winner ([West Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany_women's_national_field_hockey_team)) of the [1981 final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1981_Women's_Hockey_World_Cup) was determined by this method.

Penalty shoot-outs using a one-on-one had previously been trialled in the [Australian Hockey League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Hockey_League) since 2001. Alternate tie-breaking methods were also tested; these including a trial in domestic Australian leagues where an [extra time](633242.md) situation was used where "players from both teams are reduced gradually" after a fixed amount of time—the idea being that this creates space for a goal to be scored.[^cite_note-11] The [Euro Hockey League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_Hockey_League) used shoot-outs since its [inaugural 2007–08 season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_Hockey_League_2007%E2%80%9308) and that year's match for third-place was determined using a shoot-out.[^cite_note-EHL-6][^cite_note-Shootout_Intro-7] The [International Hockey Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Hockey_Federation)'s view was that the shoot-outs "better replicate real game situations and tend to require more skill" and were a better method of tie-breaking.[^cite_note-Shootout_Intro-7]

In April 2011, the International Hockey Federation announced that a penalty shoot-out competition would replace the penalty stroke competition and the first major tournament to feature this tie-breaking procedure was the [2011 Women's Hockey Champions Challenge II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Women's_Hockey_Champions_Challenge_II);[^cite_note-Shootout_Intro-7] the first penalty shoot-out occurred in the [3rd place match](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Women's_Hockey_Champions_Challenge_II#Third_and_fourth_place) with [Belarus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belarus_women's_national_field_hockey_team) defeating [Chile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chile_women's_national_field_hockey_team) 3–1 in the shoot-out.[^cite_note-12] The second shoot-out was used to determine the [3rd place match](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Women's_Hockey_Champions_Challenge_I#Third_and_fourth_place) in [2011 Women's Hockey Champions Challenge I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Women's_Hockey_Champions_Challenge_I).[^cite_note-13] The [Champions Trophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hockey_Champions_Trophy) is the premier annual hockey tournament and the first time a shoot-out was used in the competition was to determine the winner of the [women's 2011 tournament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Women's_Hockey_Champions_Trophy); [Netherlands](7325406.md) won the shoot-out 3–2 against [Argentina](3642950.md).[^cite_note-14] The first Olympics to feature the penalty shoot-out was [London 2012](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Summer_Olympics);[^cite_note-Shootout_Intro-7] they were not required in the [men's tournament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_hockey_at_the_2012_Summer_Olympics_%E2%80%93_Men's_tournament) but was used once in the [women's tournament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_hockey_at_the_2012_Summer_Olympics_%E2%80%93_Women's_tournament) where the Netherlands progressed from their semi-final against [New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_women's_national_field_hockey_team).[^cite_note-15][^cite_note-16]
