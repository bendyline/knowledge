The **European Organisation for Astronomical Research in the Southern Hemisphere**,[^cite_note-2] commonly referred to as the **European Southern Observatory** (**ESO**), is an [intergovernmental research organisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intergovernmental_organization) made up of 16 member states for ground-based [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy). Created in 1962, ESO has provided astronomers with state-of-the-art research facilities and access to the southern sky. The organisation employs over 750 staff members and receives annual member state contributions of approximately €162 million.[^cite_note-3] Its observatories are located in northern [Chile](5489.md).

ESO has built and operated some of the largest and most technologically advanced [telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telescope). These include the 3.6 m [New Technology Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Technology_Telescope), an early pioneer in the use of [active optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_optics), and the [Very Large Telescope](151648.md) (VLT), which consists of four individual 8.2 m telescopes and four smaller auxiliary telescopes which can all work together or separately. The [Atacama Large Millimeter Array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atacama_Large_Millimeter_Array) observes the [universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe) in the [millimetre and submillimetre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terahertz_radiation) wavelength ranges, and is the world's largest ground-based astronomy project to date. It was completed in March 2013 in an international collaboration by Europe (represented by ESO), North America, East Asia and Chile.[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5]

Currently under construction is the [Extremely Large Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extremely_Large_Telescope). It will use a 39.3-metre-diameter [segmented mirror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Segmented_mirror), and become the [world's largest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_optical_reflecting_telescopes) optical reflecting telescope when operational towards the end of this decade. Its light-gathering power will allow detailed studies of planets around other stars, the first objects in the universe, [supermassive black holes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supermassive_black_hole), and the nature and distribution of the [dark matter](8651.md) and [dark energy](19604228.md) which dominate the universe.

ESO's observing facilities have made astronomical discoveries and produced several [astronomical catalogues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Southern_Observatory_Catalog).[^cite_note-6] Its findings include the discovery of the most distant [gamma-ray burst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma-ray_burst) and evidence for a [black hole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_hole) at the centre of the [Milky Way](2589714.md).[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8] In 2004, the VLT allowed astronomers to obtain the first picture of an [extrasolar planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extrasolar_planet) ([2M1207b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2M1207b)) orbiting a [brown dwarf](44401.md) 173 light-years away.[^cite_note-9] The High Accuracy Radial Velocity Planet Searcher ([HARPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HARPS)) instrument installed on the older ESO 3.6 m telescope led to the discovery of extrasolar planets, including [Gliese 581c](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gliese_581c)—one of the smallest planets seen outside the [Solar System](26903.md).[^cite_note-10]

## Past



The ESO headquarters in [Garching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garching), Germany, in 1997



The same site in 2014, a year after a new extension was built (in the foreground)

The idea that European astronomers should establish a common large observatory was broached by [Walter Baade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Baade) and [Jan Oort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Oort) at the [Leiden Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leiden_Observatory) in the Netherlands in spring 1953.[^cite_note-11] It was pursued by Oort, who gathered a group of astronomers in Leiden to consider it on 21 June that year. Immediately thereafter, the subject was further discussed at the Groningen conference in the Netherlands. On 26 January 1954, an ESO declaration was signed by astronomers from six European countries expressing the wish that a joint European observatory be established in the southern hemisphere.[^cite_note-ESO_Timeline-12]

At the time, all [reflector telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflector_telescope) with an [aperture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aperture) of 2 metres or more were located in the northern hemisphere. The decision to build the observatory in the southern hemisphere resulted from the necessity of observing the southern sky; some research subjects (such as the central parts of the [Milky Way](2589714.md) and the [Magellanic Clouds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magellanic_Clouds)) were accessible only from the southern hemisphere.[^cite_note-13]

It was initially planned to set up telescopes in South Africa where several European observatories were located ([Boyden Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boyden_Observatory)), but tests from 1955 to 1962 demonstrated that a site in the [Andes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andes) was preferable: When [Jürgen Stock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%BCrgen_Stock_\(astronomer\)) enthusiastically reported his observations from [Chile](5489.md), [Otto Heckmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Heckmann) decided to leave the South African project on hold. ESO—at that time about to sign the contracts with South Africa—decided to establish their observatory in Chile.[^cite_note-15] The ESO Convention was signed 5 October 1962 by Belgium, Germany, France, the Netherlands and Sweden. Otto Heckmann was nominated as the organisation's first director general on 1 November 1962. On 15 November 1963 Chile was chosen as the site for ESO's observatory.[^cite_note-16]



Directors general of ESO (from left to right): Lodewijk Woltjer, Harry van der Laan, Catherine Cesarsky, Tim de Zeeuw and Xavier Barcons

A preliminary proposal for a convention of astronomy organisations in these five countries was drafted in 1954. Although some amendments were made in the initial document, the convention proceeded slowly until 1960 when it was discussed during that year's committee meeting. The new draft was examined in detail, and a council member of [CERN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CERN) (the European Organization for Nuclear Research) highlighted the need for a convention between governments (in addition to organisations).[^cite_note-17] The convention and government involvement became pressing due to rapidly rising costs of site-testing expeditions. The final 1962 version was largely adopted from the CERN convention, due to similarities between the organisations and the dual membership of some members.[^cite_note-18]

In 1966, the first ESO telescope at the La Silla site in Chile began operating.[^cite_note-ESO_Timeline-12] Because CERN (like ESO) had sophisticated instrumentation, the astronomy organisation frequently turned to the nuclear-research body for advice and a collaborative agreement between ESO and CERN was signed in 1970. Several months later, ESO's telescope division moved into a CERN building in [Geneva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geneva) and ESO's Sky Atlas Laboratory was established on CERN property.[^cite_note-19] ESO's European departments moved into the new ESO headquarters in [Garching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garching) (near [Munich](19058.md)), Germany, in 1980.

In 2015, [Guillem Anglada-Escudé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guillem_Anglada-Escud%C3%A9) confirmed the existence of [Proxima Centauri b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proxima_Centauri_b) at the Southern Observatory.

Although ESO is headquartered in Germany, its telescopes and observatories are in northern [Chile](5489.md), where the organisation operates advanced [ground-based](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observatory) astronomical facilities:

- [La Silla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Silla_Observatory), which hosts the [New Technology Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Technology_Telescope) (NTT)

- [Paranal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paranal_Observatory), where the [Very Large Telescope](151648.md) (VLT) is located

- [Llano de Chajnantor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llano_de_Chajnantor_Observatory), where ALMA, the [Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atacama_Large_Millimeter/submillimeter_Array), is located

These are among the best locations for astronomical observations in the southern hemisphere.[^cite_note-21] An ESO project is the [Extremely Large Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extremely_Large_Telescope) (ELT), a 40-metre-class telescope based on a five-mirror design and the formerly planned [Overwhelmingly Large Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overwhelmingly_Large_Telescope). The ELT will be the largest visible and near-infrared telescope in the world. ESO began its design in early 2006, and aimed to begin construction in 2012.[^cite_note-The_E-ELT_project-22] Construction work at the ELT site started in June 2014.[^cite_note-beginning-23] As decided by the ESO council on 26 April 2010, a fourth site ([Cerro Armazones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerro_Armazones)) is to be home to ELT.[^cite_note-24][^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26]

Each year about 2,000 requests are made for the use of ESO telescopes, for four to six times more nights than are available. Observations made with these instruments appear in a number of peer-reviewed publications annually; in 2017, more than 1,000 reviewed papers based on ESO data were published.[^cite_note-ESOstats-27]

ESO telescopes generate large amounts of data at a high rate, which are stored in a permanent archive facility at ESO headquarters. The archive contains more than 1.5 million images (or spectra) with a total volume of about 65 terabytes (65,000,000,000,000 bytes) of data.

### La Silla

Main article: [La Silla Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Silla_Observatory)



La Silla cluster of telescopes

La Silla, located in the southern [Atacama Desert](18952975.md) 600 kilometres (370 mi) north of [Santiago de Chile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santiago_de_Chile) at an altitude of 2,400 metres (7,900 ft), is the home of ESO's original observation site. Like other observatories in the area, La Silla is far from sources of [light pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_pollution) and has one of the darkest night skies on Earth.[^cite_note-33] In La Silla, ESO operates three telescopes: a 3.6-metre telescope, the New Technology Telescope (NTT) and the 2.2-metre Max-Planck-ESO Telescope.

The observatory hosts visitor instruments, attached to a telescope for the duration of an observational run and then removed. La Silla also hosts national telescopes, such as the 1.2-metre Swiss and the 1.5-metre Danish telescopes.

About 300 reviewed publications annually are attributable to the work of the observatory. Discoveries made with La Silla telescopes include the [HARPS-spectrograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Accuracy_Radial_Velocity_Planet_Searcher) detection of the planets orbiting within the [Gliese 581 planetary system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gliese_581_planetary_system), which contains the first known rocky planet in a habitable zone outside the solar system.[^cite_note-34][^cite_note-35] Several telescopes at La Silla played a role in linking [gamma-ray bursts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma-ray_burst), the most energetic explosions in the universe since the [Big Bang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Bang), with the explosions of massive stars. The ESO La Silla Observatory also played a role in the study of supernova [SN 1987A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SN_1987A).[^cite_note-36]

#### ESO 3.6-metre telescope

Main article: [ESO 3.6 m Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ESO_3.6_m_Telescope)



The [ESO 3.6 m Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ESO_3.6_m_Telescope)

The ESO 3.6-metre telescope began operations in 1977. It has been upgraded, including the installation of a new [secondary mirror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_mirror).[^cite_note-37] The conventionally designed [horseshoe-mount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telescope#Telescope_mountings) telescope was primarily used for [infrared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared) [spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectroscopy); it now hosts the HARPS spectrograph, used in search of [extra-solar planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extra-solar_planets) and for [asteroseismology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroseismology). The telescope was designed for very high long-term [radial velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radial_velocity) accuracy (on the order of 1 m/s).[^cite_note-38]

#### New Technology Telescope

Main article: [New Technology Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Technology_Telescope)



The [New Technology Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Technology_Telescope)

The New Technology Telescope (NTT) is an [altazimuth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altazimuth_mount), 3.58-metre [Ritchey–Chrétien telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ritchey%E2%80%93Chr%C3%A9tien_telescope), inaugurated in 1989 and the first in the world with a computer-controlled main mirror. The flexible mirror's shape is adjusted during observation to preserve optimal image quality. The secondary mirror position is also adjustable in three directions. This technology (developed by ESO and known as [active optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_optics)) is now applied to all major telescopes, including the VLT and the future ELT.[^cite_note-39]

The design of the octagonal enclosure housing the NTT is innovative. The telescope dome is relatively small and ventilated by a system of flaps directing airflow smoothly across the mirror, reducing turbulence and resulting in sharper images.[^cite_note-40]

#### MPG/ESO 2.2-metre telescope

Main article: [MPG/ESO telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MPG/ESO_telescope)

The 2.2-metre telescope has been in operation at La Silla since early 1984, and is on indefinite loan to ESO from the [Max Planck Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Planck_Society) (*Max-Planck-Gesellschaft zur Förderung der Wissenschaften*, or MPG, in German). Telescope time is shared between MPG and ESO observing programmes, while operation and maintenance of the telescope are ESO's responsibility.

Its instrumentation includes a 67-million-pixel wide-field imager (WFI) with a [field of view](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_of_view#Astronomy) as large as the full moon,[^cite_note-41] which has taken many images of celestial objects. Other instruments used are [GROND](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GROND) (Gamma-Ray Burst Optical Near-Infrared Detector), which seeks the afterglow of gamma-ray bursts—the most powerful explosions in the universe,[^cite_note-42] and the high-resolution spectrograph FEROS (Fiber-fed Extended Range Optical Spectrograph), used to make detailed studies of stars.

### Paranal

Main article: [Paranal Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paranal_Observatory)

The Paranal Observatory is located atop [Cerro Paranal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerro_Paranal) in the Atacama Desert in northern Chile. Cerro Paranal is a 2,635-metre-high (8,645 ft) mountain about 120 kilometres (75 mi) south of [Antofagasta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antofagasta) and 12 kilometres (7.5 mi) from the Pacific coast.[^cite_note-51]

The observatory has seven major telescopes operating in visible and infrared light: the four 8.2-metre (27 ft) telescopes of the Very Large Telescope, the 2.6-metre (8 ft 6 in) VLT Survey Telescope (VST) and the 4.1-metre (13 ft) Visible and Infrared Survey Telescope for Astronomy. In addition, there are four 1.8-metre (5 ft 11 in) auxiliary telescopes forming an array used for [interferometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interferometric) observations.[^cite_note-52] In March 2008, Paranal was the location for several scenes of the 22nd James Bond film, *[Quantum of Solace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_of_Solace)*.[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-54]

#### Very Large Telescope

Main article: [Very Large Telescope](151648.md)



[Very Large Telescope](151648.md) (VLT). Complex of four large telescopes and several smaller ones.



VLT Laser Guide Star. The orange laser beam from the telescope is used for [adaptive optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaptive_optics).

The main facility at Paranal is the VLT, which consists of four nearly identical 8.2-metre (27 ft) unit telescopes (UTs), each hosting two or three instruments. These large telescopes can also work together in groups of two or three as a giant [interferometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interferometry). The ESO Very Large Telescope Interferometer (VLTI) allows astronomers to see details up to 25 times finer than those seen with the individual telescopes. The light beams are combined in the VLTI with a complex system of mirrors in tunnels, where the light paths must diverge less than 1/1000 mm over 100 metres. The VLTI can achieve an [angular resolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_resolution) of milliarcseconds, equivalent to the ability to see the headlights of a car on the Moon.[^cite_note-55]

The first of the UTs had its [first light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_light_\(astronomy\)) in May 1998, and was offered to the astronomical community on 1 April 1999.[^cite_note-A_Great_Moment_for_Astronomy-56] The other telescopes followed suit in 1999 and 2000, making the VLT fully operational. Four 1.8-metre auxiliary telescopes (ATs), installed between 2004 and 2007, have been added to the VLTI for accessibility when the UTs are used for other projects.[^cite_note-57]

Data from the VLT have led to the publication of an average of more than one peer-reviewed scientific paper per day; in 2017, over 600 reviewed scientific papers were published based on VLT data.[^cite_note-ESOstats-27] The VLT's scientific discoveries include imaging an extrasolar planet,[^cite_note-58] tracking individual stars moving around the [supermassive black hole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supermassive_black_hole) at the centre of the Milky Way[^cite_note-eso.org-59] and observing the afterglow of the furthest known gamma-ray burst.[^cite_note-60]

At the Paranal inauguration in March 1999, names of celestial objects in the [Mapuche language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mapuche_language) were chosen to replace the technical designations of the four VLT Unit Telescopes (UT1–UT4). An essay contest was prior arranged for schoolchildren in the region concerning the meaning of these names which attracted many entries dealing with the cultural heritage of ESO's host country. A 17-year-old adolescent from [Chuquicamata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuquicamata), near [Calama](1090788.md), submitted the winning essay and was awarded an amateur telescope during the inauguration.[^cite_note-61] The four unit telescopes, UT1, UT2, UT3 and UT4, are since known as *Antu* (sun), *Kueyen* (moon), *Melipal* (Southern Cross), and *Yepun* (Evening Star),[^cite_note-62] with the latter having been originally mistranslated as "Sirius", instead of "Venus".[^cite_note-63]

#### Survey telescopes



Enclosure of British developed [VISTA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VISTA_\(telescope\))



[VST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VLT_Survey_Telescope) seen in the back between VLT's dome-shaped auxiliary telescopes

[Visible and Infrared Survey Telescope for Astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_and_Infrared_Survey_Telescope_for_Astronomy) (VISTA) is housed on the peak adjacent to the one hosting the VLT, sharing observational conditions. VISTA's main mirror is 4.1 metres (13 ft) across, a highly curved mirror for its size and quality. Its deviations from a perfect surface are less than a few thousandths the thickness of a human hair, and its construction and polishing presented a challenge.[^cite_note-64]

VISTA was conceived and developed by a consortium of 18 universities in the United Kingdom led by [Queen Mary, University of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Mary,_University_of_London), and it became an in-kind contribution to ESO as part of the UK's ratification agreement. The telescope's design and construction were managed by the Science and Technology Facilities Council's [UK Astronomy Technology Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_Astronomy_Technology_Centre) (STFC, UK ATC). Provisional acceptance of VISTA was formally granted by ESO at the December 2009 ceremony at ESO headquarters in Garching, which was attended by representatives of Queen Mary, University of London and STFC. Since then the telescope has been operated by ESO,[^cite_note-65] capturing quality images since it began operation.[^cite_note-66][^cite_note-67]

The [VLT Survey Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VLT_Survey_Telescope) (VST) is a state-of-the-art, 2.6-metre (8 ft 6 in) telescope equipped with OmegaCAM, a 268-megapixel CCD camera with a field of view four times the area of the full moon. It complements VISTA by surveying the sky in visible light. The VST (which became operational in 2011) is the result of a joint venture between ESO and the [Astronomical Observatory of Capodimonte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_Observatory_of_Capodimonte) (Naples), a research centre at the Italian National Institute for Astrophysics [INAF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/INAF).[^cite_note-68][^cite_note-69]

The scientific goals of both surveys range from the nature of dark energy to assessing [near-Earth objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near-Earth_object). Teams of European astronomers will conduct the surveys; some will cover most of the southern sky, while others will focus on smaller areas. VISTA and the VST are expected to produce large amounts of data; a single picture taken by VISTA has 67 megapixels, and images from OmegaCam (on the VST) will have 268 megapixels. The two survey telescopes collect more data every night than all the other instruments on the VLT combined. The VST and VISTA produce more than 100 terabytes of data per year.[^cite_note-70]

### Llano de Chajnantor

Main article: [Llano de Chajnantor Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llano_de_Chajnantor_Observatory)

Three large parabolic-dish telescopes, seen from behind

Three [ALMA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atacama_Large_Millimeter_Array) antennas on Chajnantor

Large white parabolic-dish antenna on yellow, multi-wheeled vehicle

ALMA antenna on route to Chajnantor plateau

The Llano de Chajnantor is a 5,100-metre-high (16,700 ft) plateau in the Atacama Desert, about 50 kilometres (31 mi) east of [San Pedro de Atacama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Pedro_de_Atacama). The site is 750 metres (2,460 ft) higher than the [Mauna Kea Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mauna_Kea_Observatory) and 2,400 metres (7,900 ft) higher than the [Very Large Telescope](151648.md) on [Cerro Paranal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerro_Paranal). It is dry and inhospitable to humans, but a good site for [submillimetre astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submillimetre_astronomy); because [water vapour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_vapour) molecules in [Earth's atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_atmosphere) absorb and attenuate [submillimetre radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submillimetre_radiation), a dry site is required for this type of [radio astronomy](82961.md).[^cite_note-71] The telescopes are:

- [Atacama Cosmology Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atacama_Cosmology_Telescope) (ACT; not operated by ESO)

- [Atacama Pathfinder Experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atacama_Pathfinder_Experiment) (Operated on behalf of the Max Planck Institute for Radio Astronomy (MPIfR))

- [Atacama Large Millimeter Array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atacama_Large_Millimeter_Array)

- [Q/U Imaging Experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QUIET) (QUIET; not operated by ESO)

- [POLARBEAR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/POLARBEAR) (on the Huan Tran Telescope; not operated by ESO)

ALMA is a telescope designed for millimetre and submillimetre astronomy. This type of astronomy is a relatively unexplored frontier, revealing a universe which cannot be seen in more-familiar visible or infrared light and ideal for studying the "cold universe"; light at these wavelengths shines from vast cold clouds in interstellar space at temperatures only a few tens of degrees above [absolute zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_zero). Astronomers use this light to study the chemical and physical conditions in these [molecular clouds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_cloud), the dense regions of gas and cosmic dust where new stars are being born. Seen in visible light, these regions of the universe are often dark and obscure due to dust; however, they shine brightly in the millimetre and submillimetre portions of the [electromagnetic spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_spectrum). This wavelength range is also ideal for studying some of the earliest (and most distant) galaxies in the universe, whose light has been [redshifted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redshift) into longer wavelengths from the expansion of the universe.[^cite_note-72][^cite_note-73]

#### Atacama Pathfinder Experiment

ESO hosts the Atacama Pathfinder Experiment, APEX, and operates it on behalf of the [Max Planck Institute for Radio Astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Planck_Institute_for_Radio_Astronomy) (MPIfR). APEX is a 12-metre (39 ft) diameter telescope, operating at millimetre and submillimetre wavelengths—between infrared light and radio waves.

#### Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array

Main article: [Atacama Large Millimeter Array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atacama_Large_Millimeter_Array)

ALMA is an astronomical interferometer initially composed of 66 high-precision antennas and operating at wavelengths of 0.3 to 3.6 mm. Its main array will have 50 12-metre (39 ft) antennas acting as a single [interferometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interferometer). An additional compact array of four 12-metre and twelve 7-metre (23 ft) antennas, known as the Morita array is also available.[^cite_note-74] The antennas can be arranged across the desert plateau over distances from 150 metres to 16 kilometres (9.9 mi), which will give ALMA a variable "zoom". The array will be able to probe the universe at millimetre and submillimeter wavelengths with unprecedented sensitivity and resolution, with vision up to ten times sharper than the [Hubble Space Telescope](40203.md). These images will complement those made with the [VLT Interferometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VLT_Interferometer).[^cite_note-75] ALMA is a collaboration between East Asia (Japan and [Taiwan](25734.md)), Europe (ESO), North America (US and Canada) and Chile.

The scientific goals of ALMA include studying the origin and formation of stars, galaxies, and planets with observations of molecular gas and dust, studying distant galaxies towards the edge of the observable universe and studying [relic radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_microwave_background_radiation) from the [Big Bang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Bang).[^cite_note-76] A call for ALMA science proposals was issued on 31 March 2011,[^cite_note-77] and early observations began on 3 October.[^cite_note-78][^cite_note-79]
