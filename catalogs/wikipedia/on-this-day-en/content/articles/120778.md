Referendums have been held in Canada at the federal, provincial and city levels.

National referendums are seldom used in Canada. The first two [referendums](51582.md) in [1898](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1898_Canadian_prohibition_plebiscite) and [1942](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1942_Canadian_conscription_plebiscite) saw a large number of voters in [Quebec](7954867.md) and in the remainder of [Canada](5042916.md) take dramatically opposing stands, and the third in 1992 saw most of the voters take a stand opposed to that of the party in power.

### Proposed referendums

During the [2004 federal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Canadian_federal_election), the [NDP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Democratic_Party_\(Canada\)) stated that it would require the federal government to hold a national referendum on [electoral reform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_reform) (specifically [proportional representation](23298.md)) for support from the NDP should the [Liberals](149536.md) win a [minority government](271434.md). The Liberals won a minority, and the NDP announced they would press for electoral reform through a referendum. The possibility of a national referendum on electoral reform was made more likely through the [Throne speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Throne_speech) that opened Parliament in October 2004, in which former Liberal [Prime Minister](24135.md) [Paul Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Martin) included electoral reform in his plan for the next Parliament. However, no action was taken.

Conservative Leader [Stephen Harper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Harper) became Prime Minister as a result of the [2006 federal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_Canadian_federal_election), ending the Liberals' opportunity to move on their promise.

Liberal Leader [Justin Trudeau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justin_Trudeau) campaigned on a platform of electoral reform in the [2015 federal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_Canadian_federal_election), promising to eliminate the current "first-past-the-post" single-member plurality voting system. While Trudeau had said that he liked a system where the distribution of seats was more in line with the popular vote on a Canada-wide basis, he had acquired a preference for [Instant-runoff voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant-runoff_voting), which he called preferential voting as it uses ranked ballots. [His government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/29th_Canadian_Ministry) announced in December 2015 that an all-party parliamentary committee would be formed in early 2016 to consider a variety of alternative election systems, including [proportional representation](23298.md). During a discussion of the plan, [Democratic Institutions Minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_of_Democratic_Institutions) [Maryam Monsef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryam_Monsef) referred to it as "an open and robust process of consultation". However, she refused to commit to the Conservative Party's demand for a public referendum that would allow Canadians to vote on their preferred electoral system, indicating that she did not want to "prejudice the outcome of that consultation process". In the end the parliamentary committee did not settle on one alternative system, and PM Trudeau did not move to reform the election system.

There had been discussion regarding a national referendum over the issue of [same-sex marriage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Same-sex_marriage_in_Canada), which was a divisive issue in Canada. A national plebiscite had been suggested by [Alberta](717.md) [Premier](470556.md) [Ralph Klein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Klein) and some [Conservatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Party_of_Canada) and [Liberal](149536.md) [backbenchers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backbencher). However, Paul Martin's Liberal government, with the support of the NDP and Bloc Québécois, passed the *[Civil Marriage Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Marriage_Act)*, legalizing same-sex marriage through Parliament in [July 2005](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Members_of_the_38th_Canadian_Parliament_and_same-sex_marriage#Votes) without holding a plebiscite. In December 2006, [Stephen Harper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Harper)'s government introduced a [motion to re-open the marriage debate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Members_of_the_39th_Canadian_Parliament_and_same-sex_marriage#Votes), which lost. Notably, members of all parties, including some Conservative cabinet members, voted it down.

As well, in 1920 six provinces held [referendums on federal restrictions on the inter-provincial trade in liquor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1920_Canadian_liquor_plebiscite).

## Provincial and Territorial referendums

For a list of Wikipedia articles on provincial referendums, see [Category\:Referendums in Canada by province or territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category\:Referendums_in_Canada_by_province_or_territory).

### Alberta

[Alberta](717.md) has held several referendums during its history.

Three concerned Prohibition (ban on the sale of liquor). The referendums resulted in the adoption, retention and replacement of Prohibition -- [1915](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1915_Alberta_liquor_plebiscite), [1920](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1920_Canadian_liquor_plebiscite) and [1923](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1923_Alberta_prohibition_plebiscite), respectively.

Another referendum held in [1948](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1948_Alberta_general_election) was on whether or not the provincial government should take control of the province's electrical generation and distribution system, at the time a mixed system of municipal and private corporate ownership, and the preferred method of rural electrification - private corporations or provincial government.

That province also saw referendums on daylight savings in [1967](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1967_Alberta_daylight_saving_time_plebiscite) and [1971](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1971_Alberta_daylight_saving_time_plebiscite).

In 2021, Alberta conducted a [two-part referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Alberta_referendum) on whether equalization payments should be eliminated from Canada's constitution and whether Alberta should observe daylight saving time year-round.

### British Columbia

In [British Columbia](3392.md) has held several referendums. A few of them are listed here.

BC held a [plebiscite on inter-provincial trade in liquor in 1920](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1920_Canadian_liquor_plebiscite).

In BC, a [Treaty Referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Columbia_Treaty_Referendum) was held on [First Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Nations_in_Canada) treaty rights in 2002. The referendum proposed eight questions that voters were asked to either support or oppose. Critics claimed the phrasing was flawed or biased toward a predetermined response. Critics, especially First Nations and religious groups, called for a boycott of the referendum, and only about one third of ballots were returned, significantly less than the usual turnout in provincial general elections. The ballots that were returned showed enthusiastic support, with over 80 per cent of participating voters agreeing to all eight proposed principles.

A [referendum on electoral reform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_British_Columbia_electoral_reform_referendum) on May 17, 2005, was held in conjunction with the [provincial election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_British_Columbia_general_election) that year. British Columbian voters were asked to approve a new electoral system based on the [Single Transferable Vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_Transferable_Vote) called [BC STV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BC_STV). It passed with the support of a majority of voters (57%), but failed to meet the required "supermajority" threshold of 60%. Premier [Gordon Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Campbell_\(Canadian_politician\)) announced due to the large support shown for electoral reform a [second referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_British_Columbia_electoral_reform_referendum) would be held in correspondence with the [2009 British Columbia general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_British_Columbia_general_election). This referendum would also have required approval by 60% of those voting.

The second referendum was held on May 12, 2009, in conjunction with the provincial election. The results were a "supermajority" of 60.92% voting for retaining the current "first past the post" electoral system and 39.8% voting for the proposed Single Transferable Vote.

A [mail-in referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_British_Columbia_sales_tax_referendum) was held from June 13 to August 5, 2011, on the fate of the province's harmonized sales tax. The government pledged to discontinue the tax if more than 50% of the voters opt to have the tax discontinued. It was passed, with 55% in favour.

The province held [another referendum on proportional representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_British_Columbia_electoral_reform_referendum) in late 2018. With 42% turnout, proportional representation was defeated by 61% of the vote.

### Manitoba

Manitoba held a referendum on Prohibition in 1892. A majority voted in favour.[^cite_note-2]

Manitoba held a referendum on Prohibition in 1902. A majority voted against prohibition.[^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4]

Prohibition referendums were also held in 1916. Manitoba held a [plebiscite on inter-provincial trade in liquor in 1920](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1920_Canadian_liquor_plebiscite). In a 1923 referendum, a majority voted to drop prohibition.[^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6]

Around 2019 the Manitoba legislative assembly debated Bill 8, The Referendum Act.[^cite_note-7]

### Newfoundland and Labrador

Main article: [1948 Newfoundland referendums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1948_Newfoundland_referendums)

The island of [Newfoundland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newfoundland_\(island\)), then a British colony, held [two referendums in 1948](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1948_Newfoundland_referendums) to determine its future. An initial referendum was held on June 3, 1948, to decide between continuing with the British appointed [Commission of Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commission_of_Government) that had ruled the island since the 1930s, revert to [dominion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Dominions) status with [responsible government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Responsible_government), or join [Canadian Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Confederation). The result was inconclusive, with 44.6% supporting the restoration of dominion status, 41.1% for confederation with Canada, and 14.3% for continuing the Commission of Government. A second referendum on July 22, 1948, which asked Newfoundlanders to choose between confederation and dominion status, was decided by a vote of 52% to 48% for confederation with Canada. Newfoundland joined Canada on March 31, 1949.

A referendum was held in [Newfoundland and Labrador](21980.md) in 1995 that approved replacing the province's system of [parochial schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parochial_school) with a largely public school system. In 1997, a second referendum to amend the Terms of Union to allow for the Catholic and Pentecostal school systems to be disbanded and brought into the public system.

In the lead up to and following his victory in the [2025 Newfoundland and Labrador general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Newfoundland_and_Labrador_general_election), [Progressive Conservative Party of Newfoundland and Labrador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_Conservative_Party_of_Newfoundland_and_Labrador) leader and Premier-designate [Tony Wakeham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Wakeham) promised that, following a review of a recent deal struck with [Hydro-Québec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydro-Qu%C3%A9bec) by the outgoing [Liberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Party_of_Newfoundland_and_Labrador) government of [John Hogan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hogan_\(Newfoundland_and_Labrador_politician\)), a referendum would be held on the deal.[^cite_note-8]

### Nova Scotia

Nova Scotia held a [plebiscite on inter-provincial trade in liquor in 1920](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1920_Canadian_liquor_plebiscite). In another referendum, Nova Scotia voted against prohibition on October 31, 1929.[^cite_note-9]

In 2004, [Nova Scotia](21184.md) held a [plebiscite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Nova_Scotia_Sunday_shopping_plebiscite) on whether to allow '[Sunday shopping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunday_shopping)'. The result was a slight victory for the No side although the government went ahead and legalized Sunday Shopping the following year after a court decision overturned the law.

### Ontario

On October 10, 2007, Ontario held a [referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_Ontario_electoral_reform_referendum) on whether or not to adopt a [mixed-member proportional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed-member_proportional_representation) election system. The proposed change failed, with 63% voting for the status quo [first-past-the-post voting](261709.md). This was the first referendum in that province since 1924 when a [referendum on prohibition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1924_Ontario_prohibition_referendum) was held.

### Prince Edward Island

The small province of Prince Edward Island (under 150,000 people and therefore in scale more like a [municipal government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Municipal_government)) has had several referendums in its past, although the term used in the province is a plebiscite.

On January 18, 1988, a provincial plebiscite was held to determine if Islanders were in favour of a fixed link to the mainland. It passed 60% to 40%. This allowed the provincial and federal governments to attract contractors to build what is now the [Confederation Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederation_Bridge).

On November 28, 2005, Islanders were asked to vote by plebiscite whether or not they wanted [mixed-member proportional representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed-member_proportional_representation) - partly district contest-based; partly "[party list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Party_list)-based" - [electoral system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_system). [Islanders decided, 64% to 36%](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_Prince_Edward_Island_electoral_reform_referendum), to keep the status quo [first-past-the-post](261709.md) system. A second [plebiscite was held in 2016](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Prince_Edward_Island_electoral_reform_referendum) concerning mixed-member proportional representation, with the result of 52% of Islanders voting to make the change. Citing low voter turnout (below 40%) in the plebiscite, the government refused to implement the reform. A [third plebiscite in 2019](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Prince_Edward_Island_electoral_reform_referendum) saw a reversal: roughly 52% of voters *rejected* the proposed change to the electoral system.

### Quebec

At least three referendums have been held in [Quebec](7954867.md):

- 1919 - April 10: [Referendum on the legalization of the sale of alcohol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1919_Quebec_prohibition_referendum). The Yes side won.

- 1980 - May 20: [Referendum on the Sovereignty-Association proposal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980_Quebec_referendum). The No side won.

- 1995 - October 30: [Referendum on Sovereignty with optional partnership offer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1995_Quebec_referendum). The No side narrowly won.

As well, the 1992 country-wide referendum on the [Charlottetown Accord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlottetown_Accord) was organized by the DGEQ in Quebec, while Elections Canada organized it in the rest of Canada.

#### Proposed referendums

In 2021, the Quebec government cancelled an electoral reform referendum.[^cite_note-EndOfReform-10]

In 2022, the Quebec Premier [François Legault](6899647.md) raised the idea of holding a referendum on getting more immigration powers from federal government.[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12][^cite_note-13] In 2024, Quebec Premier [François Legault](6899647.md) repeated his threat that Quebec might hold a referendum on immigration powers if Prime Minister [Justin Trudeau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justin_Trudeau) does not give the province more control over immigration.[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15][^cite_note-16]

### Saskatchewan

Saskatchewan held a referendum on prohibition in 1915 in which a majority voted in favour. It also held a [plebiscite on inter-provincial trade in liquor in 1920](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1920_Canadian_liquor_plebiscite). It conducted a referendum on prohibition on July 16, 1923. This time a majority voted to replace prohibition with government-run liquor stores.[^cite_note-17]

## Municipal referendums

City referendums are quite common.

They are held on questions of spending money for large-scale projects and on more general social questions.

In 1915 voters in Ottawa voted on switching to [proportional representation](23298.md), and a majority were in favour.

In 1921 Montrealers voted the on adoption of STV for city elections. A majority were opposed.[^cite_note-21]
