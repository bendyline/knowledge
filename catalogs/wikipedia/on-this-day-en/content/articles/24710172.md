A **commoner**, also known as the ***common man***, ***commoners***, the ***common people*** or the ***masses***, was in earlier use an ordinary person in a community or nation who did not have any significant [social status](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_status), especially a member of neither [royalty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_family), [nobility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobility), nor any other part of the [aristocracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristocracy_\(class\)). Depending on culture and period, other elevated persons (such as members of [clergy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clergy)) may have had higher social status in their own right, or were regarded as commoners if lacking an aristocratic background.

This class overlaps with the legal class of people who have a property interest in [common land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_land), a longstanding feature of land law in England and Wales. Commoners who have rights for a particular common are typically neighbors, not the public in general.

In [monarchic marriage terminology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morganatic_marriage), aristocracy and nobility may be included in the term.

## History

Various [sovereign states](23604120.md) throughout history have governed, or claimed to govern, in the name of *the common people*. In Europe, a distinct concept analogous to *common people* arose in the [Classical civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_civilization) of ancient Rome around the 6th century BC, with the social division into [patricians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrician_\(ancient_Rome\)) (nobles) and [plebeians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plebeians) (commoners). The division may have been instituted by [Servius Tullius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Servius_Tullius), as an alternative to the previous clan-based divisions that had been responsible for internecine conflict.[^cite_note-class-1] The ancient Greeks generally had no concept of class and their leading social divisions were simply non-Greeks, free-Greeks and slaves.[^cite_note-2] The early organization of [Ancient Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Athens) was something of an exception with certain official roles like [archons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archon), magistrates and treasurers being reserved for only the wealthiest citizens – these class-like divisions were weakened by the democratic reforms of [Cleisthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleisthenes) who created new *horizontal* social divisions in contrasting fashion to the *vertical* ones thought to have been created by Tullius.[^cite_note-civ-3]

Both the [Roman Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republic) and the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire) used the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) term *[Senatus Populusque Romanus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SPQR)* (the Senate and People of Rome). This term was fixed to Roman legionary standards, and even after the [Roman Emperors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Emperor) achieved a state of total personal [autocracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocracy), they continued to wield their power in the name of the Senate and People of Rome.



A Medieval French [manuscript illustration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illuminated_manuscript) depicting the three estates: clergy (*oratores*), nobles (*bellatores*), and commoners (*laboratores*).

With the growth of Christianity in the 4th century AD, a new world view arose that underpinned European thinking on social division until at least early modern times.[^cite_note-class-1] [Saint Augustine](2030.md) postulated that social division was a result of the [Fall of Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Man).[^cite_note-class-1] The three leading divisions were considered to be the priesthood ([clergy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clergy)), the nobility, and the common people. Sometimes this was expressed as "those who prayed", "those who fought" and "those who worked". The [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) terms for the three classes – *oratores*, *bellatores* and *laboratores* – are often found even in modern textbooks, and have been used in sources since the 9th century.[^cite_note-4] This threefold division was formalized in the estate system of [social stratification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_stratification), where again commoners were the bulk of the population who are neither members of the nobility nor of the [clergy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clergy).[^cite_note-bundle_commoners-5] They were the third of the [Three Estates of the Realm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Estates_of_the_Realm) in [medieval Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Europe), consisting of [peasants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peasant) and [artisans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artisan).

[Social mobility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_mobility) for commoners was limited throughout the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages). Generally, the [serfs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serfs) were unable to enter the group of the *bellatores*. Commoners could sometimes secure entry for their children into the *oratores* class; usually they would serve as rural parish priests. In some cases they received education from the clergy and ascended to senior administrative positions; in some cases nobles welcomed such advancement as former commoners were more likely to be neutral in dynastic feuds. There were cases of serfs becoming clerics in the [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire),[^cite_note-6] though from the [Carolingian era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carolingian_era), clergy were generally recruited from the nobility.[^cite_note-7] Of the two thousand [bishops](4092.md) serving from the 8th to the 15th century, just five came from the [peasantry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peasant).[^cite_note-8]

The social and political order of medieval Europe was relatively stable until the development of the mobile [cannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannon) in the 15th century. Up until that time a noble with a small force could hold their castle or walled town for years even against large armies - and so they were rarely disposed.[^cite_note-shield-9] Once effective cannons were available, walls were of far less defensive value and rulers needed expensive field armies to keep control of a territory. This encouraged the formation of princely and kingly states, which needed to tax the common people much more heavily to pay for the expensive weapons and armies required to provide security in the new age. Up until the late 15th century, surviving medieval treaties on government were concerned with advising rulers on how to serve the common good: [Assize of Bread](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assize_of_Bread_and_Ale) is an example of medieval law specifically drawn up in the interests of the common people.[^cite_note-shield-9] But then works by [Philippe de Commines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippe_de_Commines), [Niccolò Machiavelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niccol%C3%B2_Machiavelli), and later [Cardinal Richelieu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_Richelieu) began advising rulers to consider their own interests and that of the state ahead of what was "good", with Richelieu explicitly saying the state is above morality in doctrines such as [*Raison d'Etat*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_interest).[^cite_note-shield-9] This change of orientation among the nobles left the common people less content with their place in society. A similar trend occurred regarding the clergy, where many priests began to abuse the great power they had due to the sacrament of contrition. The [Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant_Reformation) was a movement that aimed to correct this, but even afterwards the common people's trust in the clergy continued to decline – priests were often seen as greedy and lacking in true faith. An early major social upheaval driven in part by the common people's mistrust of both the nobility and clergy occurred in Great Britain with the [English Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Civil_War) of 1642. After the forces of [Oliver Cromwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Cromwell) triumphed, movements like the [Levellers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levellers) rose to prominence demanding equality for all. When the general council of Cromwell's army met to decide on a new order at the [Putney Debates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Putney_Debates) of 1647, one of the commanders, Colonel [Thomas Rainsborough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Rainsborough), requested that political power be given to the common people. According to historian Roger Osbourne, the Colonel's speech was the first time a prominent person spoke in favor of universal male suffrage, but it was not to be granted until 1918. After much debate it was decided that only those with considerable property would be allowed to vote, and so after the revolution political power in England remained largely controlled by the nobles, with at first only a few of the most wealthy or well-connected common people sitting in Parliament.[^cite_note-civ-3]

The rise of the [bourgeoisie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourgeoisie) during the [Late Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Middle_Ages), had seen an intermediate class of wealthy commoners develop, which ultimately gave rise to the modern [middle classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_class). Middle-class people and [upper bourgeoisie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_bourgeoisie) could still be called commoners until after [World War I](4764461.md). For example, [Pitt the Elder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitt_the_Elder) was often called *The Great Commoner* in England, and this appellation was later used for the 20th-century American anti-elitist campaigner [William Jennings Bryan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Jennings_Bryan). The interests of the middle class were not always aligned with their fellow commoners of the working class.

According to [social historian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_history) [Karl Polanyi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Polanyi), [Britain's middle class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_class_in_the_United_Kingdom#Middle_class) in [19th-century Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_era) turned against their fellow commoners by seizing political power from the [British upper class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_class_in_the_United_Kingdom#Upper_class) via the [Reform Act 1832](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reform_Act_1832). The emergence of the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution) had caused severe economic distress to a large number of [working class commoners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_class_in_the_United_Kingdom#Working_class), leaving many of them with no means to earn a living as the traditional system of tenant farming was replaced with large-scale agriculture run by a small number of individuals. The upper class had responded to their plight by establishing institutions such as [workhouses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Workhouse), where unemployed lower-class Britons could find a source of employment, and [outdoor relief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outdoor_relief), where monetary and other forms of assistance were given to both the unemployed and those on low income without them needing to enter a workhouse to receive it.[^cite_note-Polanyi_2002-10]

Though initial middle class opposition to the [Poor Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poor_Law) reform of [William Pitt the Younger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Pitt_the_Younger) had prevented the emergence of a coherent and generous nationwide provision, the resulting [Speenhamland system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speenhamland_system) did generally manage to prevent working class commoners from starvation. In 1834, [outdoor relief was abolished](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poor_Law_Amendment_Act_1834) and workhouses were deliberately made into places so unappealing that many often preferred to starve rather than enter them. For Polanyi this related to the [economic doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_economics) prevalent at the time which held that only the spur of hunger could make workers flexible enough for the proper functioning of the free market. By the end of the 19th century, at least in [mainland Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Britain), economic progress has been sufficient that even the working class were generally able to earn a good living, and as such working and middle class interests began to converge, lessening the division within the ranks of common people. Polanyi notes that in [Continental Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_Europe), middle and working class interests did not diverge anywhere near as markedly as they had in Britain.[^cite_note-Polanyi_2002-10]

## Trifold division breakdown



US vice president [Henry A. Wallace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_A._Wallace) proclaimed the "arrival of the century of the common man" in a 1942 speech broadcast nationwide in the United States.

After the [French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution) and the [Napoleonic Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars) along with [industrialization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrialization), the division in three estates – nobility, clergy and commoners – had become somewhat outdated. The term "common people" continued to be used, but now in a more general sense to refer to regular people as opposed to the privileged elite.

[Communist theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_theory) divided society into [capitalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalists) on one hand, and the [proletariat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proletariat) or *the masses* on the other. In [Marxism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism), the people are considered to be the creator of history. By using the word "people", Marx did not gloss over the class differences, but united certain elements, capable of completing the revolution. The Intelligentsia's sympathy for the common people gained strength in the 19th century in many countries. For example, in Imperial Russia a big part of the intelligentsia was striving for its emancipation. Several great writers (Nekrasov, Herzen, Tolstoy etc.) wrote about sufferings of the common people. Organizations, parties and movements arose, proclaiming the liberation of the people. These included among others: "[People's Reprisal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergey_Nechayev)", "[People’s Will](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narodnaya_Volya_\(organization\))", "[Party of Popular Freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_Democratic_Party)" and the "People's Socialist Party".

In the United States, a famous 1942 speech by vice president [Henry A. Wallace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_A._Wallace) proclaimed the arrival of the "century of the common man" saying that all over the world the "common people" were on the march, specifically referring to Chinese, Indians, Russians, and as well as Americans.[^cite_note-11] Wallace's speech would later inspire the widely reproduced popular work *[Fanfare for the Common Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fanfare_for_the_Common_Man)* by Aaron Copland.[^cite_note-12] In 1948, US president [Harry S. Truman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_S._Truman) made a speech saying there needs to be a government "that will work in the interests of the common people and not in the interests of the men who have all the money."[^cite_note-13]

## Social divisions in non-Western civilizations

[Comparative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_history) historian [Oswald Spengler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_Spengler) found the social separation into nobility, priests and commoners to occur again and again in the various civilizations that he surveyed (although the division may not exist for pre-civilized society).[^cite_note-spengler-14] As an example, in the Babylonian civilization, the [Code of Hammurabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_of_Hammurabi) made provision for punishments to be harsher for harming a noble than a commoner.[^cite_note-15]
