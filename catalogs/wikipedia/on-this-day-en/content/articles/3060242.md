# Religion in Russia

[Orthodox Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodoxy) is the most widely professed **religion in Russia**, with significant minorities of [non-religious people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreligion) and adherents of other faiths.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECurtisLeighton1998202–220Religion-1][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPuchkov199441-2][^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3] Russia has the world's [largest Orthodox population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodoxy_by_country).[^cite_note-pew_nov2017-4]

The [constitution of Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Russia) recognises the right to freedom of conscience and creed to all the citizenry,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECurtisLeighton1998202Religion-5] the spiritual contribution of Orthodox Christianity to the [history of Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Russia), and respect to "[Christianity](5211.md), [Islam](6037917.md), [Buddhism](3267529.md), [Judaism](15624.md) and other religions and creeds which constitute an inseparable part of the historical heritage of Russia's peoples",[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBourdeaux200347-6] including [ethnic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_religion) or [paganism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paganism), either preserved, or revived.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFagan2013127-7]

According to the Russian law, any religious organisation may be recognised as "traditional", if it was already in existence before 1982, and each newly founded religious group has to provide its credentials and re-register yearly for fifteen years, and, in the meantime until eventual recognition, stay without rights.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBourdeaux200347-6]

The [Russian Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church), though its influence is thin in some parts of [Siberia](29639.md) and southern Russia, where there has been a perceptible revival of pre-Christian religion,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBourdeaux200346-8] acts as the *de facto,* if not *de jure,* privileged religion of the state, claiming the right to decide which other religions or denominations are to be granted the right of registration.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBourdeaux200347-6] Some [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism) churches which were already in existence before the [Russian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution) have been unable to re-register, and the [Catholic Church](606848.md) has been forbidden to develop its own [territorial jurisdictions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisdiction_\(area\)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBourdeaux200347–48-9] According to some Western observers, respect for freedom of religion by Russian authorities has declined since the late 1990s and early 2000s.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEUzzell2000168:_"Religious_freedom_grew_steadily_in_Russia_from_about_the_mid-1980s_to_approximately_1993._..._The_real_religious_freedom_that_existed_in_practice_was_..._a_result_of_the_turmoil_and_chaos_of_the_early_1990s,_which_prevented_the_Russian_elite_from_keeping_a_steady_hand_on_things._That_hand_is_firmer_now,_and_there_has_been_a_steady_deterioration_of_religious_freedom_over_the_past_five_years._In_1994,_the_first_provincial_law_restricting_the_rights_of_religious_minorities_was_passed,_in_\[\[Tula_Oblast|Tula\]\],_about_two_hundred_miles_south_of_Moscow\._About_one-third_of_Russia's_provinces_have_passed_similar_laws_since_then,_and_in_1997_the_national_government_passed_a_law_explicitly_distinguishing_between_first-class_'religious_organizations'_and_second-class_'religious_groups',_which_have_far_fewer_rights"-10][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnox2008282–283-11] For example, the activities of the [Jehovah's Witnesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jehovah's_Witnesses) are currently banned in Russia. According to [International Christian Concern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Christian_Concern), during 2021 "crackdowns on religious freedom have intensified in Russia".[^cite_note-12]

Since the dissolution of the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) in 1991 there has been a revival and spread of [Siberian shamanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siberian_shamanism)[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKharitonova2015passim-13] (often mixed with Orthodox elements[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEZnamenski2003passim-14]), and the emergence of [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism)[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETkatcheva1994passim-15] and [new religious movements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_religious_movements) throughout Russia. There has been an "exponential increase in new religious groups and alternative spiritualities", [Eastern religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_religions) and [Neopaganism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neopaganism), even among self-defined "Christians"—a term which has become a loose descriptor for a variety of eclectic views and practices.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett201127–28Borenstein1999441-16] Russia has been defined by the scholar [Eliot Borenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eliot_Borenstein) as the "Southern California of Europe" because of such a blossoming of new religious movements, and the latter are perceived by the Russian Orthodox Church as competitors in a "war for souls".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett201127–28Borenstein1999441-16] However, the multiplicity of religions in Russia has been a traditional component of Russian identities for hundreds of years, contributing to a long-established ethno-cultural pluralism.[^cite_note-17]

One of the [2020 amendments to the Constitution of Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_amendments_to_the_Constitution_of_Russia) has [Constitutional references to God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_references_to_God).[^cite_note-18]

## History

Further information: [Slavic paganism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavic_paganism), [Christianisation of Kievan Rus'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianisation_of_Kievan_Rus'), and [religion in the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_the_Soviet_Union)



*The Baptism of Rus'* (1885–1896), by [Viktor Vasnetsov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viktor_Vasnetsov).

Before the tenth century, [Russians](59510.md) practised [Slavic religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavic_paganism). As recalled by the *[Primary Chronicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_Chronicle)*, Orthodox Christianity was made the state religion of [Kievan Rus'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kievan_Rus') in 987 by [Vladimir the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_the_Great), who opted for it among other possible choices as it was the religion of the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire). Since then, religion, [mysticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mysticism), and statehood remained intertwined elements in Russia's identity.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFagan20131-19] The [Russian Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church), perceived as the glue consolidating the nation, accompanied the expansion of the [Russian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire) in the eighteenth century. Czar [Nicholas I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_I_of_Russia)'s ideology, under which the empire reached its widest extent, proclaimed "Orthodoxy, autocracy, and nation" (*Pravoslavie*, *samoderzhavie*, *narodnost'*) as its foundations. The dominance of the Russian Orthodox Church was sealed by law, and, as the empire incorporated peoples of alternative creeds, religions were tied to [ethnicities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_group) to skirt any issue of integration. Until 1905, only the Russian Orthodox Church could engage in missionary activity to convert non-Orthodox people, and apostasy was treated as an offense punishable by law. [Catholicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholicism), [Islam](6037917.md) and other religions were tolerated only among outsider (*inoroditsy*) peoples but forbidden from spreading among Russians.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFagan20131–2-20]

Throughout the history of early and imperial Russia there were, however, religious movements which posed a challenge to the monopoly of the Russian Orthodox Church and put forward stances of freedom of conscience, namely the [Old Believers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Believers)—who separated from the Russian Orthodox Church after [Patriarch Nikon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarch_Nikon_of_Moscow)'s reform in 1653 (the *[Raskol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raskol)*)—, and [Spiritual Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiritual_Christianity).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFagan20136–7-21] It is worth noting that the Russian Orthodox Church itself never forbade personal religious experience and speculative mysticism, and [Gnostic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnosticism) elements had become embedded in Orthodox Christianity since the sixth century, and later strengthened by the popularity of [Jakob Böhme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jakob_B%C3%B6hme)'s [thought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theosophy_\(Boehmian\)) in sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Orthodox seminaries.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERosenthal199710-22]

By the end of the eighteenth century, *dvoeverie* ("double faith"), popular religion which preserved Slavic pantheism under a Christianised surface, found appreciation among intellectuals who tried to delineate Russian distinctiveness against the West.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERosenthal199710-22] On 17 April 1905, [Tsar Nicholas II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsar_Nicholas_II) decreed that religious minorities had the right to publicly celebrate their respective liturgies.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEUzzell2000-23][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBudkina200524-24] At the dawn of the twentieth century, [esoteric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_esotericism) and [occult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occult) philosophies and movements, including [Spiritualism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiritualism_\(movement\)), [Theosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theosophy), [Anthroposophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthroposophy), [Hermeticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermeticism), [Russian cosmism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_cosmism) and others, became widespread.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERosenthal1997135_ff,_153_ff,_185_ff-25] At the same time the empire had begun to make steps towards the recognition of the multiplicity of religions that it had come to encompass, but they came to an abrupt end with the [Russian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution) in 1917.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFagan20133-26] After the revolution, the Russian Orthodox Church lost its privileges, as did all minority religions, and the new state verged towards an [atheist official ideology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_atheism).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFagan20133-26] Under the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union), the Russian Orthodox Church lived periods of repression and periods of support and cooptation by the state.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBalzer20156–11-27] Despite the policies of state atheism, censuses reported a high religiosity among the population; in 1929, 80% of the population were believers, and in 1937 two-thirds described themselves as believers, of whom three-fourths as Orthodox Christians.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBalzer20157-28] The Russian Orthodox Church was supported under [Joseph Stalin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Stalin) in the 1940s, after the [Second World War](32927.md), then heavily suppressed under [Nikita Khrushchev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikita_Khrushchev) in the 1960s, and then revived again by the 1980s.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBalzer20156–11-27] While it was legally reconstituted only in 1949,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFagan2013129-29] throughout the Soviet period the church functioned as an arm of the [KGB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KGB); many hierarchs of the post-Soviet church were former KGB agents, as demonstrated by the opening of KGB archives in the 1990s.[^cite_note-30]

Since the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1987–1991, the Russian Orthodox Church has struggled to regain its erstwhile monopoly of religious life, despite it and other Christian churches which existed since before the Revolution have found themselves in a radically transformed context characterised by a religious pluralism unknown before 1917.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFagan20133-26] During the Soviet period, religious barriers were shattered, as religions were no longer tied to ethnicity and family tradition, and an extensive displacement of peoples took place. This, together with the more recent swift ongoing development of communications, has resulted in an unprecedented mingling of different religious cultures.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFagan20133-26] After the collapse of the Soviet Union, there was a renewal of religions in Russia, with the revival of the traditional faiths and the emergence of new forms within the traditional faiths as well as many [new religious movements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_religious_movement).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett201127–28Borenstein1999441-16][^cite_note-31] The [amendments of 2020 to the constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_amendments_to_the_Constitution_of_Russia) added, in the Article 67, the continuity of the Russian state in history based on preserving "the memory of the ancestors" and general "ideals and belief in God" which the ancestors conveyed.[^cite_note-32]

## Study approaches



Syncretic [Temple of All Religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_All_Religions) in [Kazan](202295.md).

In the study of religions in Russia, the "ethnic principle" is based on the assumption that the entire number of people belonging to a given ethnic group are adherents of that group's traditional religion. This principle is often used to estimate the magnitude of very small groups, for instance Finnish [Lutheranism](23371382.md) at 63,000, assuming that all the 34,000 Finns and 28,000 Estonians of Russia are believers in their historical religion; or German Lutheranism at 400,000, assuming that all Germans in Russia believe in their historical religion. However, whether for small or larger groups, this approach may lead to gross mistakes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFilatovLunkin200633–35-33]

The ethnic principle is sometimes misused to deliberately inflate the prevalence of certain religions, especially the larger ones, for political aims. For instance, Islamic and Orthodox leaders routinely claim that their religions have respectively 20 million and 120 million adherents in Russia, by counting all the individuals belonging to the ethnic groups which historically belonged to these religions.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFilatovLunkin200633–35-33] By applying the ethnic principle, people who are indifferent to religion or are outspoken [atheists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheism), those who have converted to a different faith to that assigned by nationality, and people who participate in religions which historically have not been associated to specific ethnic groups in Russia—namely [Old Believers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Believers), new Russian converts to [Protestantism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism), [Catholicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholicism) and [Eastern religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_religions), and others—are automatically excluded from the calculations.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFilatovLunkin200633–35-33]

Another criterion to count religious populations in Russia is that of "religious observance". Based on this principle, very few Russians would be religious. It has been found that between 0.5% and 2% of people in big cities attend [Easter](9325.md) services, and overall just between 2% and 10% of the total population (3 to 15 million people) are actively practising Orthodox Christians. The proportion of practising Muslims among ethnic groups which are historically Islamic is larger, 40% to 90% depending on the group, and yet smaller than any assumption based on the ethnic principle.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFilatovLunkin200640–43-34]

The most accurate criterion to count religious populations in Russia is that of "self-identification", which allows to count also those people who identify themselves with a given religion but do not actually practise it. This principle provides a picture of how much given ideas and outlooks are widespread among the people.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFilatovLunkin200635-35] Nevertheless, it has been noted that different people often give different meanings to the same identity markers; for instance, large percentages of people who self-identify as "Orthodox" have been found to believe that God is a "life force", to believe in [reincarnation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reincarnation), astral connections, and other [New Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Age) ideas.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFilatovLunkin200640-36]

Another method that has sometimes been used to determine the magnitude of religions in Russia is to count the number of their officially registered organisations. Such criterion, however, leads to inaccurate assumptions for various reasons. There is not the same arithmetic relationship between religions' number of local organisations and the number of their believers, as different religions have different organisational structures. Furthermore, different religions have different attitudes towards the registration of their organisations, and secular authorities register some without difficulties while hinder the registration of others. For instance, the Russian Orthodox Church is eager to register its communities when they are still at the embryonal stage, and many of them are actually inactive; the Old Believers traditionally do not consider registration as essential, and some branches reject it in principle; and Protestant churches have the largest number of unregistered congregations, probably around ten thousand, most of them extremely small groups, and while many denominations discourage registration, they often also face a negative disposition from secular authorities.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFilatovLunkin200644–45-37]

In the August of 2012 the first large-scale survey and mapping of religions in Russia based on [self-identification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-identification) was published in the *Sreda Arena Atlas*, an extension of the [2010 Census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Census_\(2010\)), with data on seventy-nine out of eighty-three of the [federal subjects of Russia](701438.md).[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3][^cite_note-Filina-38] On a rounded total population of 142,800,000 the survey found that 66,840,000 persons, or 47.4% of the total population, were [Christians](5211.md).[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3] Among them, 58,800,000 or 41.1% of the population were believers in the [Russian Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church), 5,900,000 or 4.1% were Christians without any denomination, 2,100,000 or 1.5% were believers in [Orthodox Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthodoxy#Christianity) without belonging to any church or (a smaller minority) belonging to non-Russian Orthodox churches (including [Armenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Apostolic_Church) and [Georgian](433340.md)), 400,000 or 0.2% were [Orthodox Old Believers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Believers), 300,000 or 0.2% were [Protestants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism), and 140,000 (less than 0.1%) were [Catholics](606848.md).[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3] Among the non-Christians, 9,400,000 or 6.5% of the population were [Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim) (including [Sunni Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam), [Shia Islam](26961.md), and a majority of [unaffiliated Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unaffiliated_Muslim)), 1,700,000 or 1.2% were [pagans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Paganism) (including [Rodnovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavic_Native_Faith), [Assianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assianism), and other religions) or [Tengrists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tengrism) ([Turco-Mongol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turco-Mongol) [Shamanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shamanism) religions and new religions), 700,000 or 0.5% were [Buddhists](3267529.md) (mostly of the [Tibetan schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibetan_Buddhism)), 140,000 or 0.1% were [Hindus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism) (including [Krishnaites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krishnaism)), and 140,000 were [religious Jews](15624.md).[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3] Among the [not religious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreligion) population, 36,000,000 people or 25% declared to ["believe in God (or in a higher power)" but to "not profess any particular religion"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiritual_but_not_religious), 18,600,000 or 13% were [atheists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheism), and 7,900,000 or 5.5% did not state any religious, spiritual or atheist belief.[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3]

### Religious observance

In addition to the respondents’ nominal self-determination of their religious affiliation, experts are also interested in the degree of real religiosity ("religious observance", “church-going”) of Russian residents. Based on this principle, very few Russians would be religious. It has been found that between 0.5% and 2% of people in big cities attend [Easter](9325.md) services, and overall just between 2% and 10% of the total population (3 to 15 million people) are actively practising Orthodox Christians. The proportion of practising Muslims among ethnic groups which are historically Islamic is larger, 40% to 90% depending on the group, and yet smaller than any assumption based on the ethnic principle.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFilatovLunkin200640–43-34]

In contrast to the above data, in a 2025 survey conducted by the [Russian Public Opinion Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Public_Opinion_Research_Center) among believers, the degree of religiosity was determined by the number of religious practices performed (wearing religious attributes, praying, observing fasts, visiting and helping temples, reading spiritual books, and so on). As a result, the majority of Russians turned out to be practicing believers:[^cite_note-wciom-41]

### Other surveys

- A 2026 survey by the [Saint Tikhon's Orthodox University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Tikhon's_Orthodox_University) (PSTGU) found that 65% of respondents in Russia identified as Orthodox, while 22% identified as non-religious or atheist, 9% as Muslim, and 3% as belonging to other religions; 1% did not provide an answer. The survey indicated a decline in Orthodox identification from 78% in 2011 and 67% in 2020.

## Christianity

Main article: [Christianity in Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_Russia)



Russian Orthodox [Annunciation Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annunciation_Cathedral,_Voronezh) in [Voronezh](467218.md).

Russia's largest religion is Christianity.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECurtisLeighton1998203–210The_Russian_Orthodox_Church-67][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPuchkov199441-2][^cite_note-pew_may2017-68] Christianity was the religious self-identification of 47.1% of the Russian population in 2012.[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3] Other polls give different results: regarding the total number of Christians in Russia by 2020 the [Pew Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pew_Research_Center) counted 102,4 million people or 69,9% of the population[^cite_note-69] (in 2011 it estimated 71%);[^cite_note-70] in the same year 2020 the [Levada Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levada_Center) estimated that 63% of Russians were Christians;[^cite_note-ВЕРА_В_СВЕРХЪЕСТЕСТВЕННОЕ-54] in 2020 the Public Opinion Foundation[^cite_note-Об_оскорблении_религиозных_чувств-71] estimated that 63% of the population was Christian; in 2011 [Ipsos MORI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ipsos_MORI) estimated that 69% of Russians were Christians;[^cite_note-72] and in 2021 the [Russian Public Opinion Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Public_Opinion_Research_Center) (VCIOM) estimated that \~67% of Russians were Christians.[^cite_note-73]

[Orthodox Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodoxy), including [Old Believers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Believers), is the dominant religion of the country, and, besides it, [Lutheranism](23371382.md) also has had a considerable role in the multiethnic history of Russia. [Evangelicalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelicalism) and [Catholicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholicism) (among Russians) are relatively recent additions to Christianity in Russia.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECurtisLeighton1998210–220Other_religions-74][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBalzer20151–2-75]

### Eastern Orthodoxy



[Trinity Sunday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_Sunday) in Russia

Russia has the world's [largest Orthodox population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodoxy_by_country).[^cite_note-pew_nov2017-4]

In accordance with some reference books, Christian Orthodoxy is professed by about 75% of Russia's believers.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECurtisLeighton1998202–220Religion-1] According to the Arena Atlas Survey, Orthodox Christian believers constituted 42.6% of the total population of Russia in 2012, while according to survey by the [Pew Forum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pew_Research_Center) in 2017, Orthodox Christian believers constituted 71% of the total population of Russia.[^cite_note-Pew2017-76]

In terms of their ethnicity, Orthodox are not only the majority of believers of the [Russians](59510.md) and many indigenous peoples of Russia ([Karelians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karelians), [Mari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mari_people), [Mordvins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mordvins), [Komi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Komi_peoples), [Chuvash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuvash_people), [Ossetians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ossetians), [Khakassians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khakassians), [Yakuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakuts) and others), but also large diasporas of [Belarusians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belarusians_in_Russia), [Georgians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgians_in_Russia), [Moldovans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moldovans) and [Ukrainians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainians_in_Russia) in Russia. Among some non-Orthodox Russian peoples there are [ethnoreligious groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnoreligious_group) professing Orthodoxy, such as: [Kryashens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kryashens) among the Tatars, Mozdok [Kabardins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabardins) and Western [Buryats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buryats).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPuchkov199441-2]

Most of them were members of the [Russian Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECurtisLeighton1998203–210The_Russian_Orthodox_Church-67] while small minorities were Old Believers and Orthodox Christian believers who either did not belong to any church or belonged to non-Russian Orthodox churches (including the [Georgian Orthodox Church](433340.md)). Unaffiliated Orthodox Christians or non-Russian Orthodox Christians were 1.5% (2,100,000) of the total population. Minor Orthodox Christian churches are represented among ethnic minorities of Ukrainians and Georgians. Unaffiliated Orthodox Christians and minorities of non-Russian Orthodox Christians comprised over 4% of the population in [Tyumen Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyumen_Oblast) (9%), [Irkutsk Oblast](79477.md) (6%), the [Jewish Autonomous Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_Autonomous_Oblast) (6%), [Chelyabinsk Oblast](475230.md) (5%), [Astrakhan Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrakhan_Oblast) (4%) and [Chuvashia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuvashia) (4%).[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3] [Cossacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cossacks), historically and some of them also in modern Russia, are among the fiercer supporters of Orthodox theocratic monarchism.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDevlin199979-77]

#### Russian Orthodoxy

Further information: [Russian Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church)



[Church of the Savior on Blood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_the_Savior_on_Blood) in Saint Petersburg

In 2012, 58,800,000 people or 41% of the total population of Russia declared to believe in the Russian Orthodox Church. It was the religion of 21% to 40% of the population in most of the [federal subjects of the country](701438.md), with peaks of 41% to over 60% in [Western Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Russia), including 41% to 60% in [Yamalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamalia) and [Perm Krai](1765550.md) and over 60% in [Kursk Oblast](480916.md) (69%), [Voronezh Oblast](473401.md) (62%), [Lipetsk Oblast](480918.md) (71%), [Tambov Oblast](480933.md) (78%), [Penza Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penza_Oblast) (63%), [Ulyanovsk Oblast](481965.md) (61%), [Mordovia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mordovia) (69%) and [Nizhny Novgorod Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nizhny_Novgorod_Oblast) (69%).[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3]

The contemporary Russian Orthodox Church (the Moscow Patriarchate; *Russkaia Pravoslavnaia Tserkov'*), despite legally dating back only to 1949, claims to be the direct successor of the pre-revolutionary Orthodox Russian Church (*Pravoslavnaia Rossiskaia Tserkov'*).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECurtisLeighton1998203–210The_Russian_Orthodox_Church-67] There are also a variety of small Orthodox Christian churches which claim as well to be the direct successors of the pre-revolutionary religious body, including the Russian Orthodox Catholic Church and the Russian Orthodox Autonomous Church. There have often been disputes between these churches and the Russian Orthodox Church over the reappropriation of disused churches, with the Russian Orthodox Church winning most cases thanks to the complicity of secular authorities.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFagan2013129-29]

#### Old Believers

Main article: [Old Believers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Believers)



The communal Old Believers' service for the [Bright Easter Week](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Week), [Moscow Oblast](372315.md)



The Church of the Intercession cathedral of the [Russian Old-Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Old-Orthodox_Church) in Moscow

The Old Believers constituted 0.2% (400,000) of the total population of the country in 2012, with proportions higher than 1% only in [Smolensk Oblast](480938.md) (1.6%), the [Altai Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altai_Republic) (1.2%), [Magadan Oblast](480997.md) (1%) and [Mari El](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mari_El) (1%).[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3] The Old Believers are the religious group which experienced the most dramatic decline since the end of the Russian Empire and throughout the Soviet Union. In the final years of the empire they constituted 10% of the population of Russia, while today their number has shrunk to far less than 1% and there are few descendants of Old Believers' families who feel a cultural link with the faith of their ancestors.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFilatovLunkin200636-78]

### Assyrian Church of the East



St. Mary Assyrian Church in Moscow.

[Assyrians in Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrians_in_Russia) have long lived in the south of the country, as well as in Moscow. ### Catholicism

Main article: [Catholic Church in Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_Russia)

Catholicism was the religion of 140,000 Russian citizens, about 0.1% of the total population, in 2012. They are concentrated in Western Russia with numbers ranging between 0.1% and 0.7% in most of the federal subjects of that region.[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3] The number of "ethnic Catholics" in Russia, that is to say Poles and Germans, and smaller minorities, is continually declining due to deaths, emigration, and secularisation. At the same time there has been a discrete rise of ethnic Russian converts to the Catholic Church.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFilatovLunkin200635-35]

The [Archdiocese of Moscow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Moscow) administers the [Latin Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Church) of the Catholic Church in Russia. Further suffragan bishoprics exist in [Irkutsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Diocese_of_Saint_Joseph_at_Irkutsk), [Novosibirsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Diocese_of_Transfiguration_at_Novosibirsk) and [Saratov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Diocese_of_Saint_Clement_at_Saratov). The Diocese of Irkutsk is in fact the largest Catholic bishopric on earth, covering an area of 9,960,000 squared kilometres. Almost all Russian Catholics are members of the Latin Church. However, the Catholic Church recognises the extremely small [Russian Greek Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Greek_Catholic_Church) as a [Byzantine Rite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Rite) church *sui juris* ("of its own jurisdiction") in [full communion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_communion) with the Catholic Church. A small number of parishes and communities belonging to [Armenian Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Catholic_Church), [Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_Greek_Catholic_Church), and [Belarusian Greek Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belarusian_Greek_Catholic_Church) operate in Russia too.

### Protestantism and other Christians

Main article: [Protestantism in Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism_in_Russia)

Various denominations of Protestantism, both historical and [Evangelical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelicalism), as well as [Pentecostalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentecostalism), were the religion of 0.2% (300,000) of the population of Russia in 2012. Their number was slightly more than 1% only in [Tuva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuva) (1.8%), [Udmurtia](447145.md) (1.4%) and the [Altai Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altai_Republic) (1%).[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3] At the same time, according to American encyclopedic data from 2008, the number of Protestants was 2 million, with 21% of religious organizations registered in Russia.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEShterin20102455,_2469-80]

[Lutheranism](23371382.md) has been on a continuous decline among Finnish and German ethnic minorities, while it has seen some Russian converts, so that some traditionally Finnish churches, like the [Evangelical Lutheran Church of Ingria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelical_Lutheran_Church_of_Ingria), today have more Russian than Finnish believers.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFilatovLunkin200635-35] [Adventists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adventism), [Baptists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptists) ([Russian Union of Evangelical Christians-Baptists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Union_of_Evangelical_Christians-Baptists) and "Unregistered Baptists"), [Evangelical Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelicalism), [Russian Mennonites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Mennonites), the [New Apostolic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Apostolic_Church), [Presbyterians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyterian) (mainly of Korean origin), [Anglicans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_in_Europe), [Quakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quakers), [Disciples of Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Church_\(Disciples_of_Christ\)), [Charismatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charismatic_movement) and [Neo-charismatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-charismatic_movement). [Methodists](20119.md) and [Holiness movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holiness_movement) (including [The Salvation Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Salvation_Army) and the [Church of the Nazarene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_the_Nazarene)) and Pentecostals are of relatively recent introduction, having at most 120 years of history in Russia.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECurtisLeighton1998210–220Other_Religions-81][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFilatovLunkin200636-78] Such Evangelical movement as [Messianic Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messianic_Judaism) has congregations in Moscow and Saint Petersburg.[^cite_note-82]

[Spiritual Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiritual_Christianity) is the group of Russian movements, so-called folk Protestants. Their origins are varied: some were influenced by western Protestants, others from disgust of the behavior of official Orthodox priests.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBerdyaev1999-83] The Spiritual Christian movements are [Khlysts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khlysts) (virtually extinct), [Skoptsy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skoptsy) (virtually extinct), [Doukhobors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doukhobors), [Molokans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molokans), and Spiritual Christian Teetotalers (Churikovites). The [Tolstoyan movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tolstoyan_movement) was close to the Spiritual Christians.

[Yehowists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yehowists), a.k.a. the Message of Zion, is a Russian [millenarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millenarian) movement, that closed both to Spiritual Christianity and Judaism, founded in the 1840s.[^cite_note-84]

People who considered themselves Christians without affiliation to any church or denomination formed 4.1% (5,900,000) of the population, with numbers ranging between 1% and 8% in most of Russia's federal subjects, and over 8% only in [Nenetia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nenets_Autonomous_Okrug) (14%), [North Ossetia–Alania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Ossetia%E2%80%93Alania) (10%), [Tver Oblast](463952.md) (9%) and the [Jewish Autonomous Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_Autonomous_Oblast) (9%).[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3] [Jehovah's Witnesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jehovah's_Witnesses) were estimated to have 255,000 believers in Russia in the mid-2000s.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFilatovLunkin200638-85]

[Islam](6037917.md) is the second largest religion in Russia after Orthodox Christianity.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECurtisLeighton1998202–220Religion-1][^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3] It is the historically dominant religion among some [Caucasian ethnic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peoples_of_the_Caucasus) (notably the [Chechens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chechens), the [Ingush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ingush_people) and the Adyghe), and some [Turkic peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkic_peoples) (notably the [Tatars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tatars) and the [Bashkirs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bashkirs)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECurtisLeighton1998202–220Religion-1][^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3]

In accordance with some reference books, Islam is professed by about 19% of Russia's believers in the mid-1990s.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECurtisLeighton1998202–220Religion-1] In the 2012 Arena Atlas survey, Muslims in Russia were 9,400,000 or 6.5% of the total population. However, the *Arena Atlas* did not survey the populations of two federal subjects with Islamic majorities which together had a population of nearly 2 million, namely [Chechnya](6095.md) and [Ingushetia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ingushetia),[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3] thus the total number of Muslims may be slightly larger. Among these Muslims, 6,700,000 or 4.6% of the total population of Russia were not affiliated with any [Islamic schools and branches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_schools_and_branches). This is mainly because it is not essential for Muslims to be affiliated with any specific sect or organisation. Those who are unaffiliated are mostly Sunni Muslims. These unaffiliated Muslims constitute significant percentages of over 10% in [Kabardino-Balkaria](817725.md) (50%), [Bashkortostan](735530.md) (54%), [Karachay-Cherkessia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karachay-Cherkessia) (50%), [Tatarstan](67135.md) (51%), [Dagestan](751099.md) (36%), [Yamalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamalia) (13%), [Orenburg Oblast](481957.md) (11%), [Adygea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adygea) (23%) and [Astrakhan Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrakhan_Oblast) (11%). Most of the regions of [Siberia](29639.md) have an unaffiliated Muslim population of 1% to 2%.[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3]

[Sunni Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam) was the religion of 2,400,000 of the Muslims, or 1.6% of the total population of Russia. It had significant following of more than 10% of the population only in [Dagestan](751099.md) (60%) and [Karachay-Cherkessia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karachay-Cherkessia) (13%). Percentages higher than 2% are found in [Kabardino-Balkaria](817725.md) (10%), [Yugra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khanty-Mansi_Autonomous_Okrug) (Khantia-Mansia) (5%), [Yamalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamalia) (4%), [Astrakhan Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrakhan_Oblast) (3%), [Chelyabinsk Oblast](475230.md) (3%) and [Tyumen Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyumen_Oblast) (2%). [Yakutia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakutia) had a population of Sunnis ranging between 1% and 2%. Many other federal subjects had a Muslim population of 0.1% to 0.9%.[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3] [Shia Islam](26961.md), otherwise, was a branch of 300,000 people, or 0.2% of the total population of Russia. It was primarily represented in [Dagestan](751099.md) (2%), [Adygea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adygea) (1%), [Karachay-Cherkessia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karachay-Cherkessia) (1%), [Kabardino-Balkaria](817725.md) (1%), [Novgorod Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novgorod_Oblast) (1%), [Penza Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penza_Oblast) (1%), [Tatarstan](67135.md) (1%) and [Yugra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khanty-Mansi_Autonomous_Okrug) (1%).[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3]

The federal subjects of Russia with an Islamic absolute majority—more than 50%—were Chechnya, Ingushetia, [Dagestan](751099.md) (98%), [Kabardino-Balkaria](817725.md) (60%), [Tatarstan](67135.md) (51%), [Karachay-Cherkessia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karachay-Cherkessia) (64%) and [Bashkortostan](735530.md) (54%). Significant percentages (over 5%) were found in (38.6%), [Yamalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamalia) (17.4%), [Astrakhan Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrakhan_Oblast) (14.6%), [Adygea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adygea) (24%), [Orenburg Oblast](481957.md) (13.9%) and [Yugra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khanty-Mansi_Autonomous_Okrug) (10.9%).[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3]

### Judaism

Main article: [History of the Jews in Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_Russia)



Bolshaya Bronnaya Synagogue in Moscow.

In 2012 there were 140,000 religious Jews in Russia,[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3] while the number of ethnic [Jews](25955086.md) was significantly larger. Indeed, most ethnic Jews in Russia are not Jewish by religion, [Judaism](15624.md) being the religion of just a minority of ethnic Jews; most of them are atheists and not religious, many are Christians, and a significant proportion of them are Buddhists.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFilatovLunkin200637–38-86] According to the Arena Atlas survey In 2012, only 13% of ethnic Jews believed in Judaism, 13% were Orthodox Christians, 4% simply Christians, 27% atheists, 25% believers but not affiliated with an organized religion, 4% Buddhists and 3% Pagans.[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3] Religious Jews were mostly concentrated in [Kamchatka Krai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamchatka_Krai) (0.4%), [Saint Petersburg](24320051.md) (0.4%), [Kursk Oblast](480916.md) (0.4%), [Khabarovsk Krai](87626.md) (0.3%), [Stavropol Krai](474074.md) (0.3%), [Buryatia](741441.md) (0.2%), the [Jewish Autonomous Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_Autonomous_Oblast) (0.2%), [Kalmykia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalmykia) (0.2%) and [Kabardino-Balkaria](817725.md) (0.2%).[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3]

### Buddhism

Main article: [Buddhism in Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism_in_Russia)



Russian *[dharmachakra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharmachakra)* illustrated in the essay entitled *Apology of Russian Buddhism* published by B. S. Grechin, the leader of an ethnic Russian Buddhist monastery in [Yaroslavl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaroslavl), in 2016.

[^cite_note-87]

In 2012, Buddhism was practised by 700,000 people in Russia, or 0.5% of the total population.[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3] It is the traditional religion of some Mongolic and Turkic ethnic groups in Russia such as the [Buryats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buryat_people), the [Kalmyks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalmyk_people), and the [Tuvans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuvans), as well as a part of the Tungusic [Evenks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evenki_people).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPuchkov199453-88] In 2012 it was the religion of 62% of the total population of [Tuva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuva), 38% of [Kalmykia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalmykia) and 20% of [Buryatia](741441.md).[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3] Buddhism also has believers accounting for 6% in [Zabaykalsky Krai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zabaykalsky_Krai), primarily consisting in ethnic Buryats, having a special [Agin-Buryat Okrug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agin-Buryat_Okrug), and of 0.5% to 0.9% in [Tomsk Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomsk_Oblast) and [Yakutia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakutia). Buddhist communities may be found in other federal subjects of Russia, between 0.1% and 0.5% inbetween 0.1% and 0.5% in [Irkutsk Oblast](79477.md) (primarily Buryats, having a special [Ust-Orda Buryat Okrug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ust-Orda_Buryat_Okrug)), [Sakhalin Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sakhalin_Oblast), [Khabarovsk Krai](87626.md), [Amur Oblast](87627.md), [Altai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altai_Republic), [Khakassia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khakassia), [Novosibirsk Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novosibirsk_Oblast), [Tomsk Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomsk_Oblast), [Tyumen Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyumen_Oblast), [Orenburg Oblast](481957.md), [Arkhangelsk Oblast](472779.md), [Murmansk Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murmansk_Oblast), [Moscow](19004.md) and [Moscow Oblast](372315.md), [Saint Petersburg](24320051.md) and [Leningrad Oblast](480845.md), and in [Kaliningrad Oblast](17311.md).[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3] In cities like Moscow, Saint Petersburg and [Samara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samara), often up to 1% of the population identify as Buddhists.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFilatovLunkin200638-85]

Buddhism in Russia is almost exclusively of the [Tibetan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibetan_Buddhism) [Vajrayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vajrayana) schools, especially [Gelug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gelug) but increasingly also [Nyingma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nyingma) and [Kagyu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kagyu) ([Diamond Way Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Way_Buddhism)). There are many Russian converts, and the newer schools have been often criticised by representatives of the Gelug as the result of a Russianised (*Rossiysky*) Buddhism and of Western Buddhist missionaries.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFagan2013130–131-89]

### Hinduism

Main article: [Hinduism in Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism_in_Russia)

Hinduism, especially in the forms of Krishnaism, [Vedism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_Vedic_religion) and [Tantrism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tantra), but also in other forms, has gained a following among Russians since the end of the Soviet period,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETkatcheva1994passim-15] primarily through the missionary work of itinerant [gurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guru) and [swamis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swami), and organisations like the [International Society for Krishna Consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Society_for_Krishna_Consciousness) and the [Brahma Kumaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahma_Kumaris). The Tantra Sangha originated in Russia itself. The excavation of an ancient idol representing [Vishnu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishnu) in the [Volga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volga) region in 2007 fueled the interest for Hinduism in Russia.[^cite_note-90]

However, Russian Hare Krishna face the hostility of the Russian Orthodox Church. In 2011, prosecutors in [Tomsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomsk) unsuccessfully tried to outlaw the *[Bhagavad-Gītā As It Is](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhagavad-G%C4%ABt%C4%81_As_It_Is)*, the central text of the Krishnaite movement, on charge of extremism.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFagan2013168-91] Russian Krishnaites in Moscow have long struggled for the construction of a large Krishna temple in the capital, which would compensate premises which were assigned to them in 1989 and later confiscated for municipal construction plans; the allocation of land for the temple has been repeatedly hindered and delayed, and Archbishop Nikon of [Ufa](321112.md) asked the secular authorities to prevent the construction "in the very heart of Orthodox Russia" of an "idolatrous heathen temple to Krishna".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFagan201399-92] In August 2016, the premises of the Divya Loka monastery, a Vedic monastery founded in 2001 in [Nizhny Novgorod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nizhny_Novgorod), were dismantled by local authorities after having been declared illegal in 2015.[^cite_note-sova-center_ru-93]

Hinduism in Russia was practised by 140,000 people, or 0.1% of the total population, in 2012. It constituted 2% of the population in the [Altai Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altai_Republic), 0.5% in [Samara Oblast](481962.md), 0.4% in [Khakassia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khakassia), [Kalmykia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalmykia), [Bryansk Oblast](476473.md), [Kamchatka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamchatka_Krai), [Kurgan Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurgan_Oblast), [Tyumen Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyumen_Oblast), [Chelyabinsk Oblast](475230.md), 0.3% in [Sverdlovsk Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sverdlovsk_Oblast), 0.2% to 0.3% in [Yamalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamalia), [Krasnodar Krai](474125.md), [Stavropol Krai](474074.md), [Rostov Oblast](478744.md), [Sakhalin Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sakhalin_Oblast), and 0.1% to 0.2% in other federal subjects.[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3]

### Sikhism

Main article: [Sikhism in Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhism_in_Russia)

[Sikhism](27964.md) is practised in Russia by a number of immigrants from the [Indian](14533.md) state of [Punjab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punjab,_India) and from [Afghanistan](737.md), though there is a small number of Russian converts. There are two Sikh organisations in Russia: the "Gurudwara Komiti" of Moscow, catering to Afghan-Indian Sikhs, founded in the 1990s and officially registered in 2000 and "Amrit Nam Sarovar", an international orthodox Sikh missionary organisation which teaches Kundalini Yoga.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopov2016Сикхизм_/_Sikhism-94]

### Taoism

[Taoism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoism) in Russia is practised by [Chinese immigrants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_immigrants) and some Russian converts.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopov2016Даосизм_/_Taoism-95] It started to be disseminated in Russia after the end of the Soviet Union, particularly through the work of Master [Alex Anatole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alex_Anatole), a Russian himself and [Taoist priest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoist_priest), founder of the Center of Traditional Taoist Studies, which has been active in Moscow since 2002.[^cite_note-96] The "Victor Xiao's Studio of Taiji" in Moscow represents [Longmen Taoism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longmen_Taoism).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopov2016Даосизм_/_Taoism-95] Another branch present in Russia is [Wuliu Taoism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wuliupai), headquartered in Saint Petersburg as the "Dao De Taoist Centre" since 1993, with branches in Moscow, [Rostov-on-Don](317562.md) and [Nizhny Novgorod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nizhny_Novgorod).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopov2016Даосизм_/_Taoism-95] The "Shen Taoist Centre", headquartered in Moscow, with branches in [Novosibirsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novosibirsk) and [Krasnodar](474128.md), is a branch of the international organisation "Universal Healing Tao" of [Mantak Chia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantak_Chia).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopov2016Даосизм_/_Taoism-95]

### Yazidism

[Yazidism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yazidism) is practised by a minority that established itself in Russia already during the [Russo-Turkish wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Russo-Turkish_wars) and especially during the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War), though their number has grown in the 2010s with new immigrants from [Iraq](7515928.md) fleeing anti-Yazidi persecution by Muslims. Yazidi communities are registered in [Yekaterinburg](66524.md), [Irkutsk](56872.md), [Nizhny Novgorod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nizhny_Novgorod), [Samara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samara), [Tula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tula,_Russia), [Ulyanovsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulyanovsk), [Yaroslavl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaroslavl) and [Krasnodar Krai](474125.md). In 2016, the Research Institute of Yazidi History and Religion was established as a branch of the Institute of Oriental Studies of the [Russian Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Academy_of_Sciences).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopov2016Езидизм_/_Yezidism-97]

### Zoroastrianism

[Zoroastrianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism) is practised in Russia by a number of recent Russian converts, though the religion was historically influent in the region of the [Northern Caucasus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Caucasian_Federal_District), among the [Scythians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scythians) and later in [Alania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alania) and [Caucasian Albania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caucasian_Albania).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopov2016Зороастризм_/_Zoroastrianism-98] There are two Russian Zoroastrian organisations: the "Zoroastrian Community of Saint Petersburg" registered in 1994 and originally founded at the beginning of the 1990s by Pavel Globa as the "[Avestan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avesta) School of Astrology"; and the "Russian Anjoman", headquartered in Moscow with branches in several other cities, that collaborates with the "Anjoman Bozorg Bazgasht", an organisation of Iranian Zoroastrian immigrants in Europe.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopov2016Зороастризм_/_Zoroastrianism-98] The Russian Anjoman calls its faith "Blagovery", and in general Zoroastrianism in Russia has close links with [Rodnovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodnovery).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStausbergTessmann2013455-99]

## Ethnic religions, paganism and Tengrism

Further information: [Shamanism in Siberia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shamanism_in_Siberia), [Tengrism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tengrism), [Slavic Native Faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavic_Native_Faith), [Assianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assianism), [Caucasian Neopaganism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caucasian_Neopaganism?action=edit\&redlink=1), and [Uralic Neopaganism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uralic_Neopaganism)



Rodnovers gathered at the Temple of Svarozhich's Fire of the [Union of Slavic Native Belief Communities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_of_Slavic_Native_Belief_Communities), in Krasotinka, [Kaluga Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaluga_Oblast), to celebrate Perun Day.



People in circle around the big candle for Rasjken Ozks, the major festival of [Mordvin Native Faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mordvin_Native_Faith).

Certain peoples of Russia, for example, the [Altaians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altai_people), [Nganasans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nganasan_people)[^cite_note-100] and [Nenets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nenets),[^cite_note-101] for the most part, continue to practice [ethnic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_religion) ([indigenous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_religion)) religions. Thus, according to a survey conducted in 2008 by the Research State Institute of Altaic Studies, among Altai believers, 81% were adherents of [Burkhanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burkhanism) (Altai ethnic religion), and 5.3% of shamanism.[^cite_note-102]

[Paganism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Paganism) and Tengrism, counted together as "traditional religions of the forefathers"[^cite_note-Filina-38] were the third-largest religious group after Christianity and Islam, with 1,700,000 believers or 1.2% of the total population of Russia in 2012.[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3] These religions are protected under the 1997 law, whose commentary specifies that "other religions and creeds which constitute an inseparable part of the historical heritage of Russia's peoples" also applies to "ancient Pagan cults, which have been preserved or are being revived".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFagan2013127-7] Tengrism is a term which encompasses the traditional ethnic and [shamanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shamanism) religions of the [Turkic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkic_peoples) and [Mongolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol) peoples, and modern movements reviving them in Russia. Paganism in Russia is primarily represented by the revival of the [ethnic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_religion) of the Russian Slavic people and communities, the [Ossetians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ossetians) (Scythian), but also by those of [Caucasian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peoples_of_the_Caucasus) and [Finno-Ugric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finno-Ugric_peoples) ethnic minorities.

In 2012, Slavic Native Faith ([Rodnovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodnovery)), Caucasian Neopaganism, and Ossetian Assianism were represented by significant numbers of believers in [North Ossetia–Alania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Ossetia%E2%80%93Alania) (29%), [Karachay-Cherkessia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karachay-Cherkessia) (12%), [Kabardino-Balkaria](817725.md) (3%), [Orenburg Oblast](481957.md) (over 3%), [Kemerovo Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kemerovo_Oblast) (over 3%), 2% to 3% in [Dagestan](751099.md), [Astrakhan Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrakhan_Oblast), [Kaluga Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaluga_Oblast), [Tyumen Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyumen_Oblast), [Irkutsk Oblast](79477.md) and [Magadan Oblast](480997.md). The Slavic Native Faith was also present in many of the federal subjects of Western Russia in percentages ranging between 1% and 2%.[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3]

Rodnovery alone represented 44% of the followers of the "traditional religions of the forefathers", thus approximately 750,000 people.[^cite_note-Filina-38] Rodnover organisations include the Union of Slavic Rodnover (Native Faith) Communities headquartered in [Kaluga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaluga). The Moscow Community was the first to be registered by the state in 1994. Russian Rodnovers believe in [Rod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rod_\(Slavic_religion\)), the supreme God, and in lesser deities who include [Perun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perun) and [Dazhbog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dazhbog). Russian centers of Rodnovery are situated also in [Dolgoprudny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolgoprudny), [Pskov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pskov) and other cities, and [Moscow](19004.md) has several shrines.[^cite_note-103]

Uralic Neopaganism is practised by the Finnic ethnic minorities (primarily the [Mari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mari_people), the [Mordvins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mordvins), the [Udmurts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Udmurt_people) and the [Komi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Komi_people)). Among these peoples, paganism survived as an unbroken tradition throughout the Soviet period.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFagan2013127-7] The [Mari Native Faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mari_Native_Faith) was practised by 6% of the population of [Mari El](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mari_El) in 2012.[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3] Other studies reported a higher proportion of 15%.[^cite_note-104] Paganism was practised by between 2% and 3% of the population of [Udmurtia](447145.md) ([Udmurt Vos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Udmurt_Vos)) and [Perm Krai](1765550.md), and by between 1% and 2% of the population of the [Komi Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Komi_Republic).[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3]

There are Rodnover groups that mix Finnic and Slavic elements, while other Rodnover organisations also cater to people who follow Scandinavian ([Heathen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heathenry_\(new_religious_movement\))) and Greek ([Hellenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenism_\(religion\))) traditions.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAitamurto201648,_78-105]

Paganism is supported by the governments of some federal subjects, for instance Mari El. Although paganism often faces the hostility of the Orthodox clergy, [Patriarch Alexy II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarch_Alexy_II_of_Moscow) stressed that Protestant missionaries pose a greater danger than ethnic religions, and the latter should be respected.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFagan2013128-106] Pagans have faced violence in some Islamic regions of the Caucasus. For instance, Aslan Tsipinov was murdered by Islamists in 2010, in [Kabardino-Balkaria](817725.md). Months before his death, Tsipinov was intimated by the extremists to stop his work of popularisation of [Circassian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circassians) ([Kabardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabarday)) Pagan rituals.[^cite_note-107]

Tengrism and Turco-Mongol shamanic religions are found primarily in Siberia and the [Russian Far East](173135.md). In 2012, 13% of the inhabitants of the [Altai Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altai_Republic) believed in [indigenous religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_religions)—which include [Burkhanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burkhanism) or "White Faith"[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBalzer2015245–246-108]—, like 13% in [Yakutia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakutia), 8% in [Tuva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuva), 3% in [Kalmykia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalmykia), between 2% and 3% in [Khakassia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khakassia), [Buryatia](741441.md) and [Kamchatka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamchatka_Krai).[^cite_note-ArenaAtlas2012-3] The *Arena Atlas* did not count the population of [Chukotka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chukotka_Autonomous_Okrug), where much of the [Chukchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chukchi_people) practise their [indigenous religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_religion).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFagan2013148-109]

## New religious and spiritual movements

Further information: [New religious movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_religious_movement), [New Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Age), and [Scientology in Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientology_in_Russia)

In modern Russia, "all kinds of occult, Pagan and pseudo-Christian faiths are widespread". Some of them are "disciplined organisations with a well-defined membership".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFilatovLunkin200638-85] The scholars of religion Sergei Filatov and Roman Lunkin, estimated in the mid-2000s that well-organised new religious movements had about 300,000 members. Nevertheless, well-organised movements constitute only "a drop in the 'new religious' ocean". Most of them are indeed "amorphous, eclectic and fluid",[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFilatovLunkin200638-85] difficult to measure, concerned with health, healing, and lifestyle, made up of fragments borrowed from Eastern religions like Buddhism, Hinduism and [yoga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoga). According to Filatov and Lunkin, these movements, albeit mostly unorganised, represent a "self-contained system" rather than a "transitional stage on the way to some other religion".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFilatovLunkin200639-110]

Native new religious movements of Russia are [Bazhovism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bazhovism), [Ivanovism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivanovism), [Roerichism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roerichism), [Ringing Cedars' Anastasianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ringing_Cedars'_Anastasianism), and others.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopov2016Новые_российские_религии_/_New_Russian_religions-111] The [Fourth Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Way), the [Theosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theosophical_Society), the [Anthroposophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthroposophical_Society), and the [Radha Soami Satsang Beas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radha_Soami_Satsang_Beas) are also represented.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPopov2016Новые_западные_религии_/_New_Western_religions-112] Roerichism, which was started before the *[perestroika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perestroika)*, is a paradigmatic example of a movement which adapts Eastern religious beliefs to the conditions of contemporary Russia. It is not a centrally structured movement, but takes the form of a dust of clubs and associations.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFilatovLunkin200639-110] Another movement, Ivanovism, is a system of healing through cold and relationship between humanity and nature founded by the mystic [Porfiry Ivanov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porfiry_Ivanov) (1898–1983), called "messenger of the Cosmos" by his followers. His disciples, the Ivanovites, are recognisable by their lightweight clothing and sandals worn in winter.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERosenthal1997199,_368-113] Ringing Cedars' Anastasianism is a new religious, spiritual, and social movement close to Rodnovery that began in 1997 in Central Russia, based on the series of ten books entitled *The Ringing Cedars of Russia* written by [Vladimir Megre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Megre).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPranskevičiūtė2015442-114] Other movements rely upon [astrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology), which is believed by about 60% of Russians, emphasising the imminent start of the [Age of Aquarius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Aquarius), the end of the world as it is currently known, and the formation of a superior "Aquarian race".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFilatovLunkin200639-110]

## Freedom of religion

Main article: [Freedom of religion in Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_religion_in_Russia)



Russian religious leaders (Armenian, Jewish, Muslim, Buddhist, Orthodox, Old Believer) during the official celebrations of the [National Unity Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unity_Day_\(Russia\)), 4 November 2012

According to some Western commentators, respect for [freedom of religion](46006.md) by secular authorities has declined in Russia since the late 1990s and early 2000s.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEUzzell2000168:_"Religious_freedom_grew_steadily_in_Russia_from_about_the_mid-1980s_to_approximately_1993._..._The_real_religious_freedom_that_existed_in_practice_was_..._a_result_of_the_turmoil_and_chaos_of_the_early_1990s,_which_prevented_the_Russian_elite_from_keeping_a_steady_hand_on_things._That_hand_is_firmer_now,_and_there_has_been_a_steady_deterioration_of_religious_freedom_over_the_past_five_years._In_1994,_the_first_provincial_law_restricting_the_rights_of_religious_minorities_was_passed,_in_\[\[Tula_Oblast|Tula\]\],_about_two_hundred_miles_south_of_Moscow\._About_one-third_of_Russia's_provinces_have_passed_similar_laws_since_then,_and_in_1997_the_national_government_passed_a_law_explicitly_distinguishing_between_first-class_'religious_organizations'_and_second-class_'religious_groups',_which_have_far_fewer_rights"-10][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnox2008282–283-11] In addition, the government continues to grant privileges to the Russia Orthodox Church not accorded to any other church or religious association.[^cite_note-115] In 2006, a Mari Pagan priest, Vitaly Tanakov, was successfully convicted of extremism and sentenced to 120 hours of compulsory labour for having published a politico-religious tract, *A Priest Speaks* (*Onajeng Ojla*), which in 2009 was added to the federal list of material deemed "extremist".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFagan2013166-116]

In 2011 there was an unsuccessful attempt to ban the *[Bhagavad-Gītā As It Is](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhagavad-G%C4%ABt%C4%81_As_It_Is)* on the same charge.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFagan2013168-91] In August 2016, the premises of a Vedic monastery founded in 2001 in [Nizhny Novgorod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nizhny_Novgorod) were demolished by local authorities after having been declared illegal in 2015.[^cite_note-sova-center_ru-93] It has been observed that the categories of "extremist" and "totalitarian sect" have been consistently used to try to outlaw religious groups which the Russian Orthodox Church classifies as "not traditional", including the newest Protestant churches and Jehovah's Witnesses.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFagan2013167–168-117] There is a ban of Jehovah's Witnesses activities in Russia.[^cite_note-118]

In 2017, a report from the [U.S. Commission on International Religious Freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Commission_on_International_Religious_Freedom) stated that: "The Russian government views independent religious activity as a major threat to social and political stability, an approach inherited from the Soviet period".[^cite_note-USCIRFApr2017-119] Thus, for the first time, the USCIRF classified Russia as one of the world's worst violators of freedom of religion, a "country of particular concern" under the [International Religious Freedom Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Religious_Freedom_Act_of_1998). The report also affirmed that Russia "is the sole state to have not only continually intensified its repression of religious freedom ..., but also to have expanded its repressive policies. ...Those policies, ranging from administrative harassment to arbitrary imprisonment to [extrajudicial killing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extrajudicial_killing), are implemented in a fashion that is systematic, ongoing, and egregious".[^cite_note-USCIRFApr2017-119] Many other countries and international organizations have spoken out on Russia's religious abuses.[^cite_note-120][^cite_note-121]

### Bhagavad Gita trial

Main article: [Bhagavad Gita trial in Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhagavad_Gita_trial_in_Russia)



Alexander Shakov, the representative of the defense for the *Bhagavad-Gītā As It Is*, holding a copy of the book at the first court hearing in 2011.

In 2011, a group linked to the Russian Orthodox Church demanded a ban of the *[Bhagavad-Gītā As It Is](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhagavad-G%C4%ABt%C4%81_As_It_Is)*, the book of the [International Society for Krishna Consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Society_for_Krishna_Consciousness), in [Tomsk Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomsk_Oblast), on charge of extremism. The case was eventually dismissed by the federal judge on 28 December 2011.[^cite_note-122]

The Russian ambassador [Alexander Kadakin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Kadakin) condemned the "madmen" who sought the ban, and underlined that Russia is a secular country.[^cite_note-123] To protest the attempted ban, 15,000 Indians in Moscow, and followers of the International Society for Krishna Consciousness throughout Russia, appealed to the government of [India](14533.md) asking an intervention to resolve the issue.[^cite_note-124] The move triggered strong protests by members of the [Parliament of India](410238.md) who wanted the government to take a strong position. The final court hearing in Tomsk was then scheduled on 28 December, after the court agreed to seek the opinion of the Tomsk [ombudsman](26289198.md) on human rights and of [Indologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indology) from Moscow and Saint Petersburg.[^cite_note-125]
