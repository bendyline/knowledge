**Liechtenstein** ([/ˈlɪk.tən.staɪn/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/1/1b/En-us-Liechtenstein.ogg/En-us-Liechtenstein.ogg.mp3](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/1/1b/En-us-Liechtenstein.ogg/En-us-Liechtenstein.ogg.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup>, [*LIK-tən-stine*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key);[^cite_note-10] German pronunciation: [\[ˈlɪçtn̩ʃtaɪn\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Standard_German) [//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/a/ae/De-Liechtenstein.ogg/De-Liechtenstein.ogg.mp3](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/a/ae/De-Liechtenstein.ogg/De-Liechtenstein.ogg.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup>; [Alemannic German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alemannic_German_language): *Liachtaschta*), officially the **Principality of Liechtenstein** (German: *Fürstentum Liechtenstein* [\[ˈfʏʁstn̩tuːm ˈlɪçtn̩ʃtaɪn\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Standard_German) [//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/9/96/De-F%C3%BCrstentum_Liechtenstein.ogg/De-F%C3%BCrstentum_Liechtenstein.ogg.mp3](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/9/96/De-F%C3%BCrstentum_Liechtenstein.ogg/De-F%C3%BCrstentum_Liechtenstein.ogg.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup>),[^cite_note-11] is a [doubly landlocked country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landlocked_country#Doubly_landlocked) in the Central European [Alps](981.md). A [microstate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microstate), it is located between [Austria](26964606.md) to the east and north-east and [Switzerland](26748.md) to the north-west, west and south.[^cite_note-12]

Formed in 1719,[^cite_note-formed-13] Liechtenstein became fully independent upon the dissolution of the [German Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Confederation) in 1866.[^cite_note-independent-14] Liechtenstein is a [monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarchy) headed by the [prince of Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarchy_of_Liechtenstein). [Hans-Adam II, Prince of Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans-Adam_II,_Prince_of_Liechtenstein) has reigned over Liechtenstein since 1989.[^cite_note-15] Liechtenstein is [Europe's fourth-smallest country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_European_countries_by_area), with an area of just over 160 square kilometres (62 square miles) and a population of 41,389.[^cite_note-16] It is the world's smallest country to border two countries.[^cite_note-17] The official language of Liechtenstein is [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language).

Liechtenstein is divided into [11 municipalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Municipalities_of_Liechtenstein). Its capital is [Vaduz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaduz), and its largest municipality is [Schaan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schaan). It is a member of the [United Nations](31769.md), the [European Free Trade Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Free_Trade_Association), and the [Council of Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Europe). It is not a member state of the [European Union](9317.md), but it participates in both the [Schengen Area](16565139.md) and the [European Economic Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Economic_Area). It has a [customs union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Customs_union) and a [monetary union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monetary_union) with Switzerland, with its usage of the [Swiss franc](361362.md). It is one of the few countries with no debt.[^cite_note-18] A constitutional referendum in [2003](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_Liechtenstein_constitutional_referendum) granted the [monarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarchy_of_Liechtenstein) greater powers, including the power to dismiss the government, nominate judges and [veto](74661.md) legislation. This was reaffirmed in [2012](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Liechtenstein_constitutional_referendum). As such, the monarch is not a [figurehead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Figurehead). This creates a semi-constitutional monarchy.[^cite_note-powers-19]

Liechtenstein has a strong [financial sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_services) centred in Vaduz. It was once known as a billionaire [tax haven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_haven), culminating in a [tax affair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Liechtenstein_tax_affair) in 2008, but the [principality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principality) has since made significant efforts to shed this reputation. An [Alpine country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpine_states), Liechtenstein is mountainous, making it a [winter sport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_sport) destination.

## History

### Early history



[Gutenberg Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gutenberg_Castle), Balzers



[Vaduz Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaduz_Castle), overlooking the capital, is home to the [Prince of Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans-Adam_II,_Prince_of_Liechtenstein).

[Liechtenstein Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtenstein_Museum), Vienna

The oldest traces of human existence in the area that later became Liechtenstein date back to the [Middle Paleolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Paleolithic) era.[^cite_note-Early-20] [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic) farming settlements appeared in the valleys around 5300 BC.

The [Hallstatt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallstatt_culture) and [La Tène cultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_T%C3%A8ne_culture) flourished during the late [Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age), from around 450 BC—possibly under some influence of both the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) and [Etruscan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etruscan_civilization) civilisations. During the Iron Age and into the Roman era, the [Vennones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vennones) occupied the [Alpine Rhine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpine_Rhine) valley. In 58 BC, at the [Battle of Bibracte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Bibracte), [Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar) defeated the Alpine tribes, thereby bringing the region under [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome) subjugation. By 15 BC, [Tiberius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiberius)—later the second Roman emperor—with his brother, [Drusus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nero_Claudius_Drusus), conquered the entire Alpine area.[^cite_note-Römerzeit_–_Historisches_Lexikon-21]

The lands that would later become known as Liechtenstein then became integrated into the [Roman province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_province) of [Raetia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raetia). The area was garrisoned by the [Roman army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Roman_army), which maintained large legionary camps at [Brigantium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bregenz) (Bregenz, Austria), near [Lake Constance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Constance), and at [Magia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maienfeld) (Maienfeld, Switzerland). The Romans built and maintained a [road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_road) which ran through the territory. Around 260 AD Brigantium was destroyed by the [Alemanni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alemanni), a [Germanic people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_people) who later settled in the area around 450.[^cite_note-Römerzeit_–_Historisches_Lexikon-21]

In the [Early Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Middle_Ages), the Alemanni settled the eastern [Swiss plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_plateau) by the 5th century and the [valleys of the Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valleys_of_the_Alps) by the end of the 8th century. The territory that would later be known as Liechtenstein was located at the eastern edge of [Alamannia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alamannia). In the 6th century, the area became part of the [Frankish Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankish_Empire) following [Clovis I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clovis_I)'s victory over the Alemanni at [Tolbiac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tolbiac) in 504.[^cite_note-Nationsonline-22]

The area that later became Liechtenstein remained under Frankish hegemony ([Merovingian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merovingians) and [Carolingian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carolingian_Empire) dynasties) until the [Treaty of Verdun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Verdun) divided the Carolingian empire in 843, following the death of [Charlemagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlemagne) in 814.[^cite_note-Early-20] The territory that later became Liechtenstein formed part of [East Francia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Francia) and would later be reunified with [Middle Francia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Francia) under the [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire), around 1000.[^cite_note-Early-20] Until about 1100, the predominant language of the area was [Romansch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romansh_language); thereafter, [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language) began to gain ground in the territory. In 1300, another Alemannic population—the [Walsers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walser), who originated in [Valais](198789.md)—entered the region and settled; the mountain village of [Triesenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triesenberg) today preserves features of the [Walser dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walser_German).[^cite_note-23]

By 1200, dominions across the Alpine plateau were controlled by the Houses of [Savoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Savoy), [Zähringer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z%C3%A4hringer), [Habsburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habsburg), and [Kyburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Kyburg). Other regions were accorded the [Imperial immediacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_immediacy) that granted the empire direct control over the mountain passes. When the Kyburg dynasty fell in 1264, the Habsburgs under [King Rudolph I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolph_I_of_Germany), the Holy Roman Emperor in 1273, extended their territory to the eastern Alpine plateau.[^cite_note-24]

In 1396, [Vaduz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaduz), which would later become the southern region of Liechtenstein, gained [imperial immediacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_immediacy), i.e. it became subject to the Holy Roman Emperor alone.[^cite_note-Eccardt_2005_176-25]

The family from which Liechtenstein takes its name originally came from [Liechtenstein Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtenstein_Castle) south of [Vienna](55866.md) in [Lower Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Austria), which they had possessed since at least 1140 until the 13th century, and again from 1807 onwards. The Liechtensteins acquired land, predominantly in [Moravia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moravia), Lower Austria, [Silesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silesia), and [Styria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Styria). As these territories were all held in [feudal tenure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fee_\(feudal_tenure\)) from more senior feudal lords, particularly various branches of the [Habsburgs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Habsburg), the Liechtenstein dynasty was unable to meet a primary requirement to qualify for a seat in the Imperial Diet (parliament), the *[Reichstag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Diet_\(Holy_Roman_Empire\))*. Even though several Liechtenstein princes served several Habsburg rulers as close advisers, without any territory held directly from the Imperial throne, they held little power in the Holy Roman Empire.

For this reason, the family sought to acquire lands that would be classed as *unmittelbar*, or held without any intermediate feudal tenure, directly from the [Holy Roman Emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Emperor). During the early 17th century, [Karl I of Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_I,_Prince_of_Liechtenstein) was made a *Fürst* (prince) by the Holy Roman Emperor [Matthias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthias,_Holy_Roman_Emperor) after siding with him in a political battle.[^cite_note-\:0-26][^cite_note-27] [Hans-Adam I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans-Adam_I,_Prince_of_Liechtenstein) was allowed to purchase the minuscule [*Herrschaft* ('Lordship') of Schellenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lordship_of_Schellenberg) and the [county of Vaduz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_of_Vaduz) (in 1699 and 1712, respectively) from the Hohenems. Tiny Schellenberg and Vaduz had exactly the political status required: no feudal superior ([suzerain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzerain)) other than the emperor.[^cite_note-\:0-26]

### Principality of Liechtenstein



[Ducal hat of Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ducal_hat_of_Liechtenstein)

On 23 January 1719,[^cite_note-creation-28] after the lands had been purchased, [Charles VI, Holy Roman Emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_VI,_Holy_Roman_Emperor), decreed that Vaduz and Schellenberg were united and elevated the newly formed territory to the dignity of *Reichsfürstentum* ([imperial principality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_of_the_Holy_Roman_Empire)) with the name "Liechtenstein" in honour of "\[his] true servant, [Anton Florian of Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anton_Florian,_Prince_of_Liechtenstein)". On this date, Liechtenstein became a [mostly-sovereign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landeshoheit) [immediate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_immediacy) member state of the [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire).[^cite_note-creation-28]

By the early 19th century, as a result of the [Napoleonic Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars) in Europe, the Holy Roman Empire came under the effective control of France, following the crushing defeat at [Austerlitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Austerlitz) by [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon) in 1805. In 1806, Emperor [Francis II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_II,_Holy_Roman_Emperor) abdicated and [dissolved the Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Holy_Roman_Empire), ending more than 960 years of feudal government. Napoleon reorganized much of the Empire into the [Confederation of the Rhine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederation_of_the_Rhine). This political restructuring had broad consequences for Liechtenstein: the historical imperial, legal, and political institutions had been dissolved. The state ceased to owe an obligation to any feudal lord beyond its borders.[^cite_note-creation-28]

Due to these events, the prince of Liechtenstein ceased to owe an obligation to any [suzerain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzerainty). From 25 July 1806, when the [Confederation of the Rhine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederation_of_the_Rhine) was founded, the Prince of Liechtenstein was a member, in fact a vassal, of its [hegemon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hegemon), styled *protector*, the French Emperor Napoleon I, until the dissolution of the confederation on 19 October 1813. Soon afterward, Liechtenstein joined the [German Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Confederation) (20 June 1815 – 23 August 1866), which was presided over by the [Emperor of Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_of_Austria).

In 1818, [Prince Johann I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_I_Joseph,_Prince_of_Liechtenstein) granted the territory a limited constitution. In that same year [Prince Aloys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aloys_II,_Prince_of_Liechtenstein) became the first member of the House of Liechtenstein to set foot in the principality that bore their name. The next visit would not occur until 1842.

In 1862, a [new constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1862_Constitution_of_Liechtenstein) was adopted.

Upon the dissolution of the German Confederation in 1866, Liechtenstein became fully independent.[^cite_note-independent-14]

The Liechtenstein Army was abolished for financial reasons[^cite_note-\:9-29] in February 1868.[^cite_note-\:10-30]

Other developments during the 19th century included the following:

- 1872: A [railway line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feldkirch%E2%80%93Buchs_railway) between Switzerland and the Austro-Hungarian Empire was constructed through Liechtenstein.

- 1884: [Johann II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_II,_Prince_of_Liechtenstein) appointed [Carl von In der Maur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_von_In_der_Maur), an Austrian aristocrat, to serve as the [Governor of Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_Liechtenstein).

- 1886: Two bridges over the Rhine to Switzerland were built.

### 20th and 21st centuries

Until the end of [World War I](4764461.md), Liechtenstein was closely tied first to the [Austrian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_Empire) and later to [Austria-Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria-Hungary); the ruling princes continued to derive much of their wealth from estates in the Habsburg territories, and spent much of their time at their two palaces in Vienna. During World War I it did officially stay neutral, but was dependent on its neighbours and some, like [Prince Heinrich of Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Heinrich_of_Liechtenstein) got involved working for Austria-Hungary. The economic devastation caused by World War I forced the country to conclude a [customs and monetary union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtenstein%E2%80%93Switzerland_relations#Cooperation) with its other neighbour, [Switzerland](26748.md).[^cite_note-31] In addition, popular unrest caused from economic devastation in the war directly led to the [November 1918 Liechtenstein putsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/November_1918_Liechtenstein_putsch), which created the process of a [new constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Liechtenstein) based on [constitutional monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_monarchy) being introduced in 1921.[^cite_note-\:02-32][^cite_note-\:1-33]

In 1929, 75-year-old [Prince Franz I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_I,_Prince_of_Liechtenstein) succeeded to the throne. He had just married [Elisabeth von Gutmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elisabeth_von_Gutmann), a wealthy woman from Vienna whose father was a Jewish businessman from Moravia. Although Liechtenstein had no official [Nazi party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_party), a Nazi sympathy movement arose within its National Union party. [Local Liechtenstein Nazis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_National_Movement_in_Liechtenstein) identified Elisabeth as their Jewish "problem".[^cite_note-34][^cite_note-35] Pro-Nazi agitation remained in Liechtenstein throughout the 1930s.

In March 1938, just after the [annexation of Austria by Nazi Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anschluss), Franz named as regent his 31-year-old grandnephew and heir-presumptive, [Prince Franz Joseph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Joseph_II,_Prince_of_Liechtenstein). After making his grandnephew regent he moved to [Feldsberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valtice), [Czechoslovakia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czechoslovakia) and on 25 July, he died while at one of his family's castles, Castle Feldberg, and Franz Joseph formally succeeded him as the Prince of Liechtenstein.[^cite_note-franz_life-36][^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38]

In March 1939 a [coup was attempted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1939_Liechtenstein_putsch) while [Franz Joseph II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Joseph_II,_Prince_of_Liechtenstein) was on a state visit to [Berlin](3354.md).[^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40]



[Franz I, Prince of Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_I,_Prince_of_Liechtenstein) from 1929 to 1938



[Franz Joseph II, Prince of Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Joseph_II,_Prince_of_Liechtenstein) from 1938 to 1989

During [World War II](32927.md), Liechtenstein remained officially neutral, looking to neighbouring Switzerland for assistance and guidance, while family treasures from dynastic lands and possessions in [Bohemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohemia), [Moravia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moravia), and [Silesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silesia) were taken to Liechtenstein for safekeeping. [Operation Tannenbaum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Tannenbaum), the Nazi plan for conquest of Switzerland, also included Liechtenstein, and the Nazi "Pan German" dream of uniting all German-speakers in the Reich would have also included the population of Liechtenstein. However in 1944, the Nazis abandoned implementing this plan after the [Allied invasion of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normandy_landings), and Liechtenstein was spared from enduring a Nazi occupation.

At the close of the conflict, [Czechoslovakia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Czechoslovak_Republic) and [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provisional_Government_of_National_Unity), acting to seize what they considered German possessions, expropriated all of the Liechtenstein dynasty's properties in [Bohemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohemia), [Moravia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moravia), and [Silesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silesia).[^cite_note-41] The expropriations (subject to [modern legal dispute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_Liechtenstein#International_dispute_with_Czechoslovakia,_Czech_Republic_and_Slovakia) at the [International Court of Justice](14918.md)) included over 1,600 km<sup>2</sup> (618 sq mi) of agricultural and forest land (most notably the UNESCO listed [Lednice–Valtice Cultural Landscape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lednice%E2%80%93Valtice_Cultural_Landscape)), and several family castles and palaces.

Liechtenstein was in dire financial straits following the end of World War II. The Liechtenstein dynasty often resorted to selling family artistic treasures, including the portrait *[Ginevra de' Benci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ginevra_de'_Benci)* by [Leonardo da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci), which was purchased by the [National Gallery of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Gallery_of_Art) of the United States in 1967 for [US\$](18717338.md)5 million (\$48 million in 2025 dollars),[^cite_note-42] then a record price for a painting.[^cite_note-43]

Citizens of Liechtenstein were forbidden to enter Czechoslovakia during the [Cold War](325329.md). The diplomatic conflict revolving around the controversial postwar [Beneš decrees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bene%C5%A1_decrees) resulted in Liechtenstein not having international relations with the [Czech Republic](5321.md) or [Slovakia](26830.md). Diplomatic relations were established between Liechtenstein and the Czech Republic on 13 July 2009,[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46] and with Slovakia on 9 December 2009.[^cite_note-47]

The [Constitution of Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Liechtenstein) was [amended in March 2003](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtenstein_constitutional_referendum,_2003) to give additional powers to the monarch.[^cite_note-absolute-48]

In 2004, Prince Hans-Adam II transferred day-to-day governmental duties to his eldest son [Hereditary Prince Alois](30873557.md) as [regent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regent), as his father had done to him in 1984 to prepare him for the role of Prince.[^cite_note-49]

In 2005, a government-commissioned investigation revealed that Jewish slave labourers from the [Strasshof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strasshof_an_der_Nordbahn) [concentration camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_concentration_camps), provided by the [SS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schutzstaffel), had worked on estates in Austria owned by Liechtenstein's Princely House.[^cite_note-50] The report indicated that though no evidence was found of the House's knowledge of the slave labour, the House bore responsibility.[^cite_note-51]

## Geography

Main article: [Geography of Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Liechtenstein)



Topographic map of Liechtenstein in German



The [Alpine Rhine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpine_Rhine) border between Liechtenstein and Switzerland (view towards the [Swiss Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_Alps))

Liechtenstein is situated in the Upper [Rhine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhine) valley of the European [Alps](981.md) and is bordered to the east by the Austrian state of [Vorarlberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vorarlberg), to the south by the [canton of Grisons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canton_of_Grisons) (Switzerland) and to the west by the [canton of St. Gallen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canton_of_St._Gallen) (Switzerland). The Rhine forms the entire western border of Liechtenstein. Measured south to north the country is about 24 km (15 mi) long. New [surveys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying) using more accurate measurements of the country's borders in 2006 have set its area at 160 km<sup>2</sup> (62 sq mi), with borders of 77.9 km (48.4 mi).[^cite_note-52] As a result, Liechtenstein's area is slightly larger by 0.5 km<sup>2</sup> (0.2 sq mi) and its borders are 1.9 km (1.2 mi) longer than previously thought.[^cite_note-53]

Liechtenstein is one of the world's two [doubly landlocked countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doubly_landlocked_country)[^cite_note-LiechCIA-54] – countries wholly surrounded by other landlocked countries (the other is [Uzbekistan](31853.md)). Liechtenstein is the [sixth-smallest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_and_dependencies_by_area) sovereign state in the world by area.

The principality of Liechtenstein is [divided into 11 communes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Municipalities_of_Liechtenstein) called *Gemeinden* (singular *Gemeinde*). The *Gemeinden* mostly consist of only a single town or village. Five of them ([Eschen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eschen), [Gamprin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamprin), [Mauren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mauren), [Ruggell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruggell), and [Schellenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schellenberg)) fall within the electoral district *[Unterland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unterland_\(electoral_district\))* (the lower county), and the remainder ([Balzers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balzers), [Planken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planken), [Schaan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schaan), [Triesen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triesen), [Triesenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triesenberg), and [Vaduz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaduz)) within *[Oberland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oberland_\(electoral_district\))* (the upper county).

The World Bank did not include Liechtenstein on its list of 50 "[small states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_states)" by its inclusion criteria.[^cite_note-55]

### Climate



Panorama of [Vaduz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaduz), capital of Liechtenstein

Despite its [alpine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpine_climate) location, the prevailing southerly winds temper Liechtenstein's climate. Its climate is continental, with cloudy and cold winters, with frequent rain and snowfall. Summers are cool to slightly warm, cloudy, and humid.[^cite_note-56]

The country's climate is relatively mild despite its mountainous location. It is strongly influenced by the action of [foehn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foehn) (warm and dry autumn wind), so the vegetation period is prolonged in spring and autumn and temperatures around 15 °C (59 °F) due to the strong foehn are not uncommon even in winter. The mountain ranges of [Switzerland](26748.md) and the Vorarlberg upstream protect from the cold polar and Atlantic air, creating a typical alpine inland protective layer. The principality has orchards with leafy meadows and a long tradition of [viticulture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viticulture). Liechtenstein's small land area hardly plays a role in climatic differences, but the vertical division into different altitudes is of great importance, so that significant climatic differences arise.

In [winter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter) the temperature rarely drops below −15 °C (5 °F), while in summer the average temperatures range between 20 and 28 °C (68 and 82 °F). Annual precipitation measurements amount to an average of about 900 to 1,200 millimetres (35 to 47 in), in the direct alpine region, however, [precipitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precipitation) is often up to 1,900 mm (75 in). The average duration of sunshine is about 1600 hours per year.

### Rivers and lakes

Main article: [List of rivers of Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_rivers_of_Liechtenstein)

The [Rhine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhine) is the longest and largest [body of water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_of_water) in Liechtenstein. With a length of approximately 27 kilometres (17 mi), it represents the [natural border](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_border) with [Switzerland](26748.md) and is of great importance for Liechtenstein's water supply. Furthermore, the Rhine is an important recreational area for the population.

At 10 kilometres (6 mi), the [Samina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samina_\(river\)) is the second-longest river in the Principality. This [whitewater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitewater) river begins at [Triesenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triesenberg) and flows into the [Ill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ill_\(Vorarlberg\)) in Austria (near [Feldkirch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feldkirch,_Vorarlberg)).

The only naturally-formed lake in Liechtenstein is the [Gampriner Seelein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gampriner_Seele), which was formed in 1927 by a [flooding](50482.md) of the Rhine with enormous erosion. In addition, there are other artificially-created [lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakes), which are mainly used to generate [electricity](9550.md). One of them is the Steg Reservoir, the largest lake in Liechtenstein.

In 1943, an [inland canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtenstein_inland_canal) was built parallel to the Rhine, starting at the southern end of the country and emptying into the Rhine at the northern end.[^cite_note-59]

### Mountains

About half of Liechtenstein's territory is mountainous.[^cite_note-Geologie_Gebirge-60] Liechtenstein lies entirely in the [Rhaetikon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%A4tikon) and is thus – depending on the classification of the Alps – assigned either to the [Eastern Alps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Alps) (two-part division of the Alps) or to the Central Alps (three-part division of the Alps).

The highest point of Liechtenstein is the [Grauspitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grauspitz) (Vordergrauspitz) with an altitude of 2,599 m (8,527 ft) above sea level, while the lowest point is the [Ruggeller Riet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruggeller_Riet) with an altitude of 430 metres (1,410 ft) above sea level.

In total, there are 32 mountains in Liechtenstein with an altitude of at least 2,000 metres (6,562 ft). The Falknishorn, at 2,452 metres (8,045 ft) above sea level, is the fifth highest mountain in Liechtenstein and represents the southernmost point of the country. The Liechtenstein-Graubünden-Vorarlberg border triangle is the [Naafkopf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naafkopf) (2,570 m (8,430 ft) above sea level).[^cite_note-Geologie_Gebirge-60]

In addition to the peaks of the Alpine chain,[^cite_note-61] which belong to the Limestone Alps, two [inselbergs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inselberg), Fläscherberg (1,135 m or 3,724 ft [above sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Above_sea_level)) in the south and [Eschnerberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eschnerberg) (698 m or 2,290 ft) in the north, rise from the Rhine Valley and belong to the Helvetic cover or [flysch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flysch) zone of the Alps.[^cite_note-62] Eschnerberg represents an important settlement area in the Liechtenstein [Unterland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unterland_\(electoral_district\)).

### Politics

Main article: [Politics of Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics_of_Liechtenstein)

Liechtenstein has a distinctive political system within Europe, combining elements of [absolute monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_monarchy), [representative democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representative_democracy), and [direct democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_democracy). The [reigning prince](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarchy_of_Liechtenstein) retains extensive executive and legislative powers, and exercises considerable influence over the daily conduct of politics and across all three [branches of government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Branches_of_government). Representative and direct democratic mechanisms operate concurrently: an elected parliament enacts [legislation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislation), while citizens can propose and adopt laws and constitutional changes independently of the legislature.[^cite_note-Marxer_2007-63] The Prince serves as the head of state and represents Liechtenstein in its international relations, although Switzerland assumes responsibility for much of the country's diplomatic relations.[^cite_note-64]



[Hans-Adam II, Prince of Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans-Adam_II,_Prince_of_Liechtenstein) in 1974

In March 2003, a [constitutional referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_Liechtenstein_constitutional_referendum) approved amendments that expanded the prince's prerogatives, granting him broad veto authority, the power to dismiss the government, and the capacity to rule by emergency decree.[^cite_note-powers-19] The [BBC](19344654.md) characterised the resulting institutional framework as making Liechtenstein "Europe's only [absolute monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_monarchy)".[^cite_note-65] Both the 2003 expansion of princely powers and their reaffirmation in 2012 were approved by substantial majorities in high-turnout referendums.[^cite_note-rejects-66][^cite_note-67] The [Venice commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venice_commission) of the [Council of Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Europe) issued an extensive report concluding that several provisions were incompatible with prevailing European democratic standards.[^cite_note-68] A further [constitutional referendum in 2012](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Liechtenstein_constitutional_referendum) reaffirmed the prince's powers, with 76% of voters rejecting a proposal to limit the princely veto over legislation approved by referendum.[^cite_note-rejects-66] In 2025, [Freedom House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_House)'s *[Freedom in the World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_in_the_World)* survey ranked Liechtenstein 64th out of 194 countries for political rights and 20th for civil liberties, noting that despite the prince's significant political role, human rights and civil liberties are generally well protected.[^cite_note-69]

Legislative authority is vested in the unicameral [Landtag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landtag_of_Liechtenstein), made up of 25 members elected for maximum four-year terms according to a proportional representation formula. Fifteen members are elected from the [Oberland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oberland_\(electoral_district\)) (Upper Country or region) and ten from the [Unterland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unterland_\(electoral_district\)) (Lower Country or region).[^cite_note-70] Parties must receive at least 8% of the national vote to win seats in parliament, i.e., enough for two seats in the 25-seat legislature. Parliament proposes and approves a government, which the Prince formally appoints. Parliament may also pass votes of no confidence in the entire government or individual members.

The [government](12229.md) comprises the head of government ([prime minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_minister)) and four government councillors (ministers), who are appointed by the Prince upon the proposal of parliament and with its concurrence, and reflect the balance of parties in parliament. The constitution stipulates that at least two government members be chosen from each of the two regions.[^cite_note-71] The members of the government are collectively and individually responsible to parliament; parliament may ask the Prince to remove an individual minister or the entire government, or the Prince may do so unilaterally.

Parliament elects from among its members a "Landesausschuss" (National Committee) made up of the president of the parliament and four additional members. The National Committee is charged with performing functions of parliamentary supervision. Parliament shares the authority to propose new legislation with the Prince, and with the citizenry, as both parliament and the citizenry may initiate referendums.[^cite_note-72]

[Judicial authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_courts_of_Liechtenstein) is vested in the ordinary courts (the Regional Court at Vaduz in the first instance, the Princely High Court of Appeal at Vaduz in the second instance, and the Princely Supreme Court as a court of last resort),[^cite_note-73] the Administrative Court, and the Constitutional Court.[^cite_note-74] The Constitutional Court rules on the conformity of laws with the constitution and has five members (and five alternates) appointed by the Reigning Prince.[^cite_note-75]

In the absence of political or military power, Liechtenstein has sought to preserve its [sovereignty](28791.md) over the past 300 years through membership in legal communities. [International cooperation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multilateralism) and European integration are therefore constants of Liechtenstein's foreign policy, aimed at continuing to safeguard the country's sovereignty as recognized under international law. Decisive for the domestic legitimacy and sustainability of this foreign policy were and are strong direct-democratic and citizen-oriented decision-making mechanisms, which are anchored in Liechtenstein in the Constitution of 1921.

Important historical stages in Liechtenstein's integration and cooperation policy were its accession to the [Confederation of the Rhine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederation_of_the_Rhine) in 1806,[^cite_note-76] to the German Confederation in 1815,[^cite_note-77] the conclusion of bilateral customs and currency agreements with the [Habsburg monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habsburg_monarchy) in 1852, and finally the Customs Treaty with Switzerland in 1923, which was followed by a range of other important bilateral treaties.

Post-war economic reconstruction was followed by accession to the Statute of the [International Court of Justice](14918.md) in 1950, Liechtenstein signed the CSCE Helsinki Final Act (today's OSCE) together with 34 other states in 1975, Liechtenstein joined the [Council of Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Europe) in 1978,[^cite_note-78] and Liechtenstein was admitted to the United Nations (UN) on 18 September 1990.[^cite_note-79] In 1991, Liechtenstein joined the European Free Trade Association (EFTA) as a full member, and since 1995 Liechtenstein has been a member of the [European Economic Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Economic_Area) (EEA) and the [World Trade Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Trade_Organization) (WTO).[^cite_note-80]

In 2008, Liechtenstein joined the Schengen/Dublin Agreement together with Switzerland. From an economic and integration policy perspective, relations within the framework of the EEA and the EU occupy a special position in Liechtenstein's foreign policy. The Hereditary Prince of Liechtenstein also participates in the annual [meetings of the heads of state of the German-speaking countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meeting_of_the_heads_of_state_of_German-speaking_countries) (consisting of EU and non-EU members).[^cite_note-81]

Relations with Switzerland are particularly extensive because of the close cooperation in many areas; Switzerland performs tasks in some places that would be difficult for the [Principality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principality) to handle on its own because of its small size. Since 2000, Switzerland has appointed an ambassador to Liechtenstein, but he resides in Bern. Liechtenstein's consular representation has been mostly handled by Switzerland since the [Customs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Customs) Treaty with Switzerland of 1923.

Liechtenstein maintains direct diplomatic missions in [Vienna](55866.md), Bern, Berlin, Brussels, Strasbourg, and [Washington, D.C.](108956.md), as well as Permanent Missions in New York and Geneva to the [United Nations](31769.md).[^cite_note-82] Currently, diplomatic missions from 78 countries are accredited to Liechtenstein, but mostly reside in Bern. The [Embassy](8970.md) in Brussels coordinates contacts with the European Union, Belgium, and also the [Holy See](13393.md).

For a long time, [diplomatic relations with Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany%E2%80%93Liechtenstein_relations) were maintained through a non-resident ambassador; that is, a contact person who was not permanently resident in Germany. Since 2002, however, Liechtenstein has had a permanent [ambassador](68651.md) in Berlin, while the German embassy in Switzerland is also responsible for the Principality. Liechtenstein's Ministry of Foreign Affairs considers the contacts to be extremely fruitful and important for the country's development, especially on the economic level.

Conflicts over the handling of [banking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank) and tax data have repeatedly strained relations with Germany.[^cite_note-83] On 2 September 2009, Liechtenstein and Germany signed an agreement on cooperation and the exchange of information in tax matters. The text of the agreement followed the OECD model agreement and provides for an exchange of information on tax matters upon request as of the 2010 tax year. In addition, Liechtenstein regards Germany as an important partner in safeguarding its interests in European integration. At the cultural level, project sponsorship plays a particularly important role. For example, the Hilti Foundation financed the exhibition "Egypt's Sunken Treasures" in Berlin, and the state donated 20,000 euros following the fire at the Duchess Anna Amalia Library in [Weimar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weimar).

Liechtenstein is a member of the [Forum of Small States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forum_of_Small_States), a group founded in 1992 by Singapore currently containing 108 nations that have fewer than ten million inhabitants at the time of joining.[^cite_note-Trinidad-84]

Liechtenstein is a member of the [International Monetary Fund](15251.md) since the annual meeting of [World Bank Group](36752.md) in Washington, D.C. on 21 October 2024.[^cite_note-85][^cite_note-86]

### Security and defence

Main article: [Military history of Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_history_of_Liechtenstein)



Liechtenstein Police

The [Liechtenstein National Police force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_enforcement_in_Liechtenstein) is responsible for keeping order within the country. It consists of 87 field officers and 38 civilian staff, totaling 125 employees. All officers are equipped with [small arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_arms). The country has one of the world's lowest [crime rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime_statistics). Liechtenstein's prison holds few, if any, inmates, and those with sentences over two years are transferred to [Austrian jurisdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judiciary_of_Austria).[^cite_note-87] The Liechtenstein National Police maintains a trilateral treaty with Austria and Switzerland that enables close cross-border cooperation among the police forces of the three countries.[^cite_note-88]

Liechtenstein follows a policy of [neutrality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrality_\(international_relations\)) and is one of the few [countries in the world that maintain no military](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_without_armed_forces) although its police force maintains a paramilitary force, the Princely Liechtenstein Security Corps, within the organisation that might act as its *de facto* army if an invasion of Liechtenstein ever occurred. The corps provides heavy backup for the National Police as well as Honour Guards at the Royal Palace and official functions. However, Liechtenstein can reinstate its military if deemed necessary.[^cite_note-89]

The army was abolished for financial reasons soon after the [Austro-Prussian War of 1866](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Prussian_War_of_1866), in which Liechtenstein fielded an army of 80 men, although they were not involved in any fighting.[^cite_note-\:9-29][^cite_note-\:10-30] No casualties were incurred. The unit numbered 81 upon return due to an Italian military liaison who accompanied the army back home.[^cite_note-90] The demise of the [German Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Confederation) in that war freed Liechtenstein from its international obligation to maintain an army, and parliament seized this opportunity and refused to provide funding for one. The Prince objected, as such a move would leave the country defenceless, but relented on 12 February 1868, and disbanded the force.[^cite_note-\:10-30] The last soldier to serve under the colours of Liechtenstein, [Andreas Kieber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andreas_Kieber), died in 1939 at age 94.[^cite_note-91]

In 1985, the Swiss Army fired off shells during an exercise and mistakenly burned a patch of forest inside Liechtenstein. The incident was said to have been resolved "over a case of white wine".[^cite_note-Letzing-92] In March 2007, a 170-man Swiss infantry unit got lost during a training exercise and inadvertently crossed 1.5 kilometers (0.9 miles) into Liechtenstein. The accidental invasion ended when the unit realized their mistake and turned back.[^cite_note-93] The Swiss Army later informed Liechtenstein of the incursion and offered official apologies,[^cite_note-94] to which an interior ministry spokesperson responded, "No problem, these things happen."[^cite_note-95]

On 20 September 2017, Liechtenstein signed the United Nations [Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_on_the_Prohibition_of_Nuclear_Weapons).[^cite_note-96]

### Human rights

Main article: [Human rights in Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights_in_Liechtenstein)

On 1 July 1984, Liechtenstein became the last country in [Europe](9239.md) to grant women the right to vote, following three previous referendums which rejected it in [1968](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1968_Liechtenstein_referendums), [1971](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1971_Liechtenstein_women's_suffrage_referendum) and [1973](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_Liechtenstein_referendums). The [referendum on women's suffrage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtenstein_women's_suffrage_referendum,_1984) that year, in which only men were allowed to participate, narrowly passed with 51.3% in favour.[^cite_note-97]

In 2024, Liechtenstein passed [legislation legalizing same-sex marriage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Same-sex_marriage_in_Liechtenstein), which entered into force in 2025.[^cite_note-98][^cite_note-99][^cite_note-100] [Abortion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abortion_in_Liechtenstein) remains criminalised within Liechtenstein.[^cite_note-101]

## Economy

Main article: [Economy of Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Liechtenstein)



Looking southward at Vaduz city centre



[Silver coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_coin): 5 kronen of Liechtenstein, 1904, the front of the coin is a portrait of Johann II

As of September 2019 the [Prince of Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_of_Liechtenstein) is the world's seventh [wealthiest monarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_the_richest_royals), with an estimated wealth of US\$3.5 billion.[^cite_note-102]

Despite its limited natural resources, Liechtenstein is one of the few countries in the world with more registered companies than citizens; it has developed a prosperous, highly industrialized free-enterprise economy and a financial service sector as well as a living standard that compares favourably with those of the urban areas of Liechtenstein's much larger European neighbours.

Liechtenstein participates in a [customs union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Customs_union) with Switzerland and employs the [Swiss franc](361362.md) as the national currency. The country imports about 85% of its energy. Liechtenstein has been a member of the [European Economic Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Economic_Area) (an organization serving as a bridge between the [European Free Trade Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Free_Trade_Association) (EFTA) and the [European Union](9317.md)) since May 1995.

The government is working to harmonize its economic policies with those of an integrated Europe. In 2008, the unemployment rate was 1.5%. Liechtenstein has only one hospital, the Liechtensteinisches Landesspital in Vaduz. As of 2014 the [CIA World Factbook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CIA_World_Factbook) estimated the gross domestic product (GDP) on a [purchasing power parity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purchasing_power_parity) basis to be \$4.978 billion. As of 2021 the estimate per capita was \$184,083.[^cite_note-LiechCIA-54]

Industries include electronics, textiles, precision instruments, metal manufacturing, power tools, anchor bolts, calculators, pharmaceuticals and food products. Its most recognizable international company and largest employer is [Hilti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilti), a manufacturer of [direct fastening systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nail_gun) and other high-end power tools. Many cultivated fields and small farms are found both in the [Oberland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oberland_\(electoral_district\)) and [Unterland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unterland_\(electoral_district\)). Liechtenstein produces wheat, barley, corn, potatoes, dairy products, livestock and [wine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtenstein_wine).

Liechtenstein is one of the few countries in Europe (along with [Monaco](19261.md) and [San Marino](27248.md)) [not to have a tax treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_haven) with the [United States](3434750.md), and efforts towards one seem to have stalled.[^cite_note-103][^cite_note-104]

### Taxation



Since 1923, there has been no [border control](247927.md) between Liechtenstein and [Switzerland](26748.md).

The government of Liechtenstein taxes personal income, business income and principal (wealth). The basic rate of [personal income tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Income_tax#Personal) is between 1% and 8%..[^cite_note-\:3-105] There is no federal income tax on a single person earning less than 15,855 CHF.[^cite_note-\:3-105] When combined with the additional income tax imposed by the communes, the combined income tax rate is 17.82%. An additional income tax of 4.3% is levied on all employees under the country's [social security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_security) programme. This rate is higher for the self-employed, up to a maximum of 11%, making the maximum income tax rate about 29% in total. The basic [tax rate on wealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wealth_tax) is 0.06% per annum, and the combined total rate is 0.89%. The tax rate on corporate profits is 12.5%.[^cite_note-LiechCIA-54]

Liechtenstein mirrors the VAT tax laws of Switzerland.[^cite_note-\:4-106] However, it does have its own VAT tax administration.[^cite_note-\:4-106]

Liechtenstein's [gift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gift_tax) and [estate taxes](5824627.md) vary depending on the relationship the recipient has to the giver and the amount of the inheritance. The tax ranges between 0.5% and 0.75% for spouses and children and 18% to 27% for non-related recipients. The estate tax is progressive.

Liechtenstein has previously received significant revenues from *Stiftungen* ("foundations"), financial entities created to hide the true owner of nonresident foreigners' financial holdings. The foundation is registered in the name of a Liechtensteiner, often a lawyer. This set of laws used to make Liechtenstein a popular [tax haven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_haven) for extremely wealthy individuals and businesses attempting to avoid or evade taxes in their home countries.[^cite_note-107]

In recent years, Liechtenstein has displayed stronger determination to prosecute international money launderers and worked to promote an image as a legitimate finance centre. In February 2008, the country's [LGT Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGT_Group) was implicated in a [tax-fraud scandal in Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Liechtenstein_tax_affair), which strained the ruling family's relationship with the German government. Crown Prince Alois has accused the German government of trafficking in stolen goods, referring to its \$7.3 million purchase of private banking information offered by a former employee of LGT Group.[^cite_note-108] The [United States Senate](24909346.md)'s subcommittee on tax haven banks said that the LGT bank, owned by the princely family, and on whose board they serve, "is a willing partner, and an aider and abettor to clients trying to evade taxes, dodge creditors or defy court orders".[^cite_note-109]



Headquarters of [Hilti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilti) Corporation in [Schaan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schaan), Liechtenstein

The [2008 Liechtenstein tax affair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Liechtenstein_tax_affair) is a series of tax investigations in numerous countries whose governments suspect that some of their citizens have evaded tax obligations by using banks and trusts in Liechtenstein; the affair broke open with the biggest complex of investigations ever initiated for tax evasion in Germany.[^cite_note-110] It was also seen as an attempt to put pressure on Liechtenstein, then one of the remaining [uncooperative tax havens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FATF_blacklist)—along with [Andorra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andorra) and [Monaco](19261.md)—as identified by the Paris-based [Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organisation_for_Economic_Co-operation_and_Development) in 2007.[^cite_note-wsj0219-111] On 27 May 2009, the OECD removed Liechtenstein from the blacklist of uncooperative countries.[^cite_note-112]

In August 2009, the British government department [HM Revenue & Customs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HM_Revenue_&_Customs) agreed with Liechtenstein to start exchanging information. It is believed that up to 5,000 British investors have roughly £3 billion deposited in accounts and trusts in the country.[^cite_note-113]

In October 2015, the European Union and Liechtenstein signed a tax agreement to ensure the automatic exchange of financial information in case of tax disputes. The collection of data started in 2016. It is another step to bring the principality in line with other European countries regarding its taxation of private individuals and corporate assets.[^cite_note-114]

### Transport

#### Road

There are about 250 kilometres (155 mi) of paved roadway within Liechtenstein, with 90 km (56 mi) of marked bicycle paths.

[Liechtenstein Bus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtenstein_Bus) is a subsidiary of the [Swiss Postbus system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PostBus_Switzerland), but separately run, and connects to the Swiss bus and [train](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_transport_in_Switzerland) network at [Buchs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buchs,_St._Gallen) and [Sargans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sargans), respectively.[^cite_note-118] Holders of a Swiss Travel Pass (non-residents of Switzerland or Liechtenstein only) can travel for free on Liechtenstein's buses.[^cite_note-119]

#### Rail

Main article: [Rail transport in Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_transport_in_Liechtenstein)

The single railway line in Liechtenstein is the [Feldkirch–Buchs railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feldkirch%E2%80%93Buchs_railway), of which 9.5 km (6 mi) are located within the principality. This line connects [Feldkirch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feldkirch,_Vorarlberg) in [Vorarlberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vorarlberg) (Austria) with [Buchs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buchs,_St._Gallen) in the [canton of St. Gallen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canton_of_St._Gallen) (Switzerland). There are four railway stations in Liechtenstein, namely [Schaan-Vaduz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schaan-Vaduz_railway_station), [Forst Hilti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forst_Hilti_railway_station), [Nendeln](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nendeln_railway_station) and [Schaanwald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schaanwald_railway_station) (from west to east). With the exception of Schaanwald, which was closed in 2013, these stations are served by a [regional train](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_train), the [S2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vorarlberg_S-Bahn#Lines) of [Vorarlberg S-Bahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vorarlberg_S-Bahn), which runs between [Feldkirch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feldkirch_railway_station) and [Buchs SG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buchs_SG_railway_station) on working days only. The service is operated by [Austrian Federal Railways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_Federal_Railways) (ÖBB).[^cite_note-120] Plans to upgrade the line and increase rail traffic were halted by a referendum in 2020.[^cite_note-121]

Liechtenstein is nominally within the [Ostwind Tariff Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ostwind_Tariff_Network?action=edit\&redlink=1), which also includes northeastern Switzerland.[^cite_note-122] [Railjet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railjet) and [EuroCity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transalpin) long-distance trains do not call at stations in Liechtenstein.

#### Air

Liechtenstein is one of only a few [countries without an airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_without_an_airport). The nearest large airport is [Zurich Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zurich_Airport) near [Zürich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z%C3%BCrich), Switzerland (130 km or 80 miles by road). The nearest small airport is [St. Gallen Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Gallen%E2%80%93Altenrhein_Airport) (50 km or 30 miles). [Friedrichshafen Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrichshafen_Airport) also provides access to Liechtenstein, as it is 85 km (53 miles) away. [Balzers Heliport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balzers_Heliport) is available for chartered helicopter flights.[^cite_note-123][^cite_note-124]

## Demographics

Main article: [Demographics of Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographics_of_Liechtenstein)



Vaduz, Liechtenstein

With a population of 40,542 as of August 2026,[^cite_note-\:2-125] Liechtenstein is [Europe's fourth-smallest country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_European_countries_by_population) after [Vatican City](32408.md), [San Marino](27248.md) and [Monaco](19261.md). It is ranked #214 in world population.[^cite_note-\:2-125]Its population is primarily [Alemannic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alemannic_German)-speaking, although one third is foreign-born, primarily German speakers from [Germany](11867.md), [Austria](26964606.md), and [Switzerland](26748.md), along with other Swiss, Italians, and [Turks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_people). Foreign-born people make up two-thirds of the country's workforce.[^cite_note-126]

Liechtensteiners have an average life expectancy at birth of 82.0 years, subdividing as male: 79.8 years, female: 84.8 years (2018 est.). The infant mortality rate is 4.2 per 1,000 live births, according to 2018 estimates.The median age of citizens is 44.7 years.[^cite_note-\:2-125]

### Languages

Main article: [Languages of Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Liechtenstein)

The official language is German, spoken by 92% of the population as their main language in 2020. 73% of Liechtenstein's population speak an [Alemannic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alemannic_German) [dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialect) of German at home that is highly divergent from [Standard German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_German) but closely related to dialects spoken in neighbouring regions such as Switzerland and [Vorarlberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vorarlberg), Austria.[^cite_note-127] In [Triesenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triesenberg), a [Walser German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walser_German) dialect promoted by the municipality is spoken. [Swiss Standard German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_Standard_German) is also understood and spoken by most Liechtensteiners.

### Religion

Main article: [Religion in Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_Liechtenstein)



St. Florin Catholic Cathedral in Vaduz

According to the [Constitution of Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Liechtenstein), [Catholicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholicism) is its official [state religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_religion):

> The Catholic Church is the State Church and as such shall enjoy the full protection of the State

— [Constitution of Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Liechtenstein)[^cite_note-Temperman2010-128]

Liechtenstein offers protection to adherents of all religions, and considers the "religious interests of the people" a priority of the government.[^cite_note-Temperman2010-128] In Liechtenstein's schools, although exceptions are allowed, religious education in Catholicism or [Protestantism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism) (either [Lutheran](23371382.md) or [Calvinist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvinist), or both) is legally required.[^cite_note-Piano2009-129] [Tax exemption](960403.md) is granted by the government to religious organizations.[^cite_note-Piano2009-129] According to the [Pew Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pew_Research_Center), social conflict caused by religious hostilities is low in Liechtenstein, and so is government restriction on the practice of religion.[^cite_note-130]

According to the 2010 census, 85.8% of the total population were Christian, of whom 75.9% adhered to the Catholic faith, constituted in the [Catholic Archdiocese of Vaduz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Vaduz), while 9.6% were either [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant), mainly organized in the [Evangelical Church in Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelical_Church_in_Liechtenstein) (a [United church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_church), Lutheran & Reformed), the [Evangelical Lutheran Church in Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelical_Lutheran_Church_in_Liechtenstein) and the Free Evangelical Church Schaan ([non-denominational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-denominational_Christianity)), or [Eastern Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodoxy), mainly organized in the Christian-Orthodox Church.[^cite_note-131] There is a small community of 30 Jewish people who attend a synagogue in Switzerland.[^cite_note-132] The largest minority religion is [Islam](6037917.md) (5.4% of the total population).[^cite_note-r2-133]

## Culture

See also: [Music of Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Liechtenstein)



[Liechtenstein National Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtenstein_National_Museum)



Kunstmuseum, [Liechtenstein Art Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kunstmuseum_Liechtenstein)

As a result of its small size, Liechtenstein has been strongly affected by external cultural influences, most notably those originating in the southern regions of German-speaking Europe, including Austria, Baden-Württemberg, Bavaria, Switzerland, and specifically [Tirol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Tyrol) and Vorarlberg.

A notable museum is the [Kunstmuseum Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kunstmuseum_Liechtenstein), an international museum of modern and contemporary art with an international art collection. The building by the Swiss architects Morger, Degelo, and Kerez is a landmark in Vaduz. It was completed in November 2000 and forms a "black box" of tinted concrete and black basalt stone. The museum collection is also the national art collection of Liechtenstein.[^cite_note-137]

The Historical Society of the Principality of Liechtenstein and the [Liechtenstein National Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtenstein_National_Museum) (*Liechtensteinisches Landesmuseum*) also play a role in preserving the culture and history of the country.[^cite_note-138][^cite_note-139] The National Museum shows permanent exhibitions on the cultural and natural history of Liechtenstein as well as special exhibitions. There is also a stamp museum, ski museum, and a 500-year-old Rural Lifestyle Museum.[^cite_note-World_Travel_Guide-140]

The [Liechtenstein State Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtenstein_State_Library) is the library that has [legal deposit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_deposit) for all books published in the country.[^cite_note-141] Poet [Ida Ospelt-Amann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ida_Ospelt-Amann) published her works exclusively in the Alemannic dialect of Vaduz.[^cite_note-142][^cite_note-143]

Historical sites include [Vaduz Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaduz_Castle), [Gutenberg Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gutenberg_Castle) and [Vaduz Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaduz_Cathedral).[^cite_note-World_Travel_Guide-140]

The Private Art Collection of the Prince of Liechtenstein, one of the world's leading private art collections, is shown at the [Liechtenstein Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtenstein_Museum) in Vienna.[^cite_note-144]

On the country's national holiday, all subjects are invited to the castle of the head of state. A significant portion of the population attends the national celebration at the castle where speeches are made and complimentary beer is served.[^cite_note-Letzing-92]

Music and theatre are important parts of the culture. There are numerous music organizations such as the Liechtenstein Musical Company, the annual Guitar Days, and the International [Josef Gabriel Rheinberger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josef_Gabriel_Rheinberger) Society, which play in two main theatres.

### Media

See also: [List of newspapers in Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_newspapers_in_Liechtenstein)

The primary [internet service provider](100245.md) and [mobile network operator](481601.md) of Liechtenstein is Telecom Liechtenstein, located in Vaduz.[^cite_note-145]

There are two conventional television channels in the country. The private channel [1FLTV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1FLTV) was created in 2008 with a goal of joining the [European Broadcasting Union](10380.md), which it did not accomplish and has since abandoned. The *Landeskanal* ([de](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landeskanal)) ("National Channel") is operated by the government's Unit for Information and Communication and carries government proceedings, [public affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_affairs_\(broadcasting\)) programming, and cultural events. Both are seen on local cable providers, along with all major channels from the other German-speaking countries. Since Switzerland shut off its [digital terrestrial television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_terrestrial_television) network in 2019, the only free television signals available are German and Austrian channels from the Sender Pfänder ([de](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sender%20Pf%C3%A4nder)) in [Bregenz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bregenz).

[Radio Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_Liechtenstein), which was established in 2004 along with the public-service broadcaster *[Liechtensteinischer Rundfunk](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtensteinischer%20Rundfunk)* (LRF) that operated it, was the country's only domestic radio station based in Schaan. Radio Liechtenstein ceased operations in 2025 after a [referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Liechtenstein_referendums#Privatization_of_Radio_Liechtenstein) passed requiring the government to privatize it, but no agreement could be reached before the deadline. The three FM programs of the Swiss [SRF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schweizer_Radio_und_Fernsehen) are still broadcast from the Sender Erbi ([de](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sender%20Erbi)) overlooking Vaduz. Liechtenstein also has one major newspaper: *[Liechtensteiner Vaterland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtensteiner_Vaterland)*.

[Amateur radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amateur_radio) is a hobby of some nationals and visitors. However, unlike virtually every other sovereign nation, Liechtenstein does not have its own [ITU prefix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITU_prefix). Conventionally, amateurs are issued call signs with the Swiss prefix "HB", followed by "0" or "L".

### Sports



[Marco Büchel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marco_B%C3%BCchel), the first Liechtensteiner alpine skier to compete at six [Winter Olympics](34069.md)



[Liechtenstein Olympic Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtenstein_Olympic_Committee) in [Schaan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schaan)

Liechtenstein [football](10568.md) teams play in the Swiss football leagues. The [Liechtenstein Football Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtenstein_Football_Cup) allows access for one Liechtenstein team each year to the [UEFA Europa Conference League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UEFA_Europa_Conference_League); [FC Vaduz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FC_Vaduz), a team playing in the [Swiss Challenge League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_Challenge_League), the second division in Swiss football, is the most successful team in the Cup, and scored their greatest success in the [European Cup Winners' Cup in 1996](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996%E2%80%9397_UEFA_Cup_Winners'_Cup) when they drew with and defeated the Latvian team [FC Universitate Riga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FK_Jelgava) by 1–1 and 4–2, to go on to a lucrative (where match revenues are split equally) fixture against [Paris Saint-Germain F.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Saint-Germain_F.C.), which they lost 0–3 and 0–4.

The [Liechtenstein men's national football team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtenstein_national_football_team) and [Liechtenstein women's national football team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtenstein_women's_national_football_team) are consistently ranked low amongst [FIFA's official rankings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FIFA_rankings), often meaning the team struggles against tough European competitors[^cite_note-146][^cite_note-147]; this was the basis for a book about Liechtenstein's unsuccessful qualifying campaign for the [2002 World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2002_FIFA_World_Cup) by British author [Charlie Connelly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlie_Connelly). In one week during autumn 2004, however, the team managed a 2–2 draw with [Portugal](576328.md), who only a few months earlier had been the losing finalists in the [European Championships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UEFA_Euro_2004). Four days later, the Liechtenstein team travelled to Luxembourg, where they defeated [the home team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luxembourg_national_football_team) 4–0 in a [2006 World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_FIFA_World_Cup) qualifying match.

In the qualification stage of the European Championship 2008, Liechtenstein defeated Latvia 1–0, which prompted the Latvian coach's resignation. They went on to defeat Iceland 3–0 on 17 October 2007, which is considered one of the most dramatic losses of the Icelandic national football team. On 7 September 2010, Liechtenstein came within seconds of a 1–1 draw against [Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotland_national_football_team) in Glasgow, having led 1–0 earlier in the second half, but Liechtenstein lost 2–1 after a goal by [Stephen McManus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_McManus) in the 97th minute. On 3 June 2011, Liechtenstein defeated [Lithuania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuania_national_football_team) 2–0.

[Liechtenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liechtenstein_national_football_team) competes in the Switzerland U16 Cup Tournament, which offers young players an opportunity to play against top football clubs.

As an [alpine](981.md) country, the main sporting opportunity for Liechtensteiners to excel is in winter sports such as [downhill skiing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downhill_\(ski_competition\)): the country's single ski area is [Malbun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malbun). [Hanni Wenzel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanni_Wenzel) won two gold medals and one silver medal in the 1980 [Winter Olympics](34069.md) (she won bronze in 1976), her brother [Andreas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andreas_Wenzel) won one silver medal in 1980 and one bronze medal in 1984 in the [giant slalom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giant_slalom_skiing) event, and her daughter [Tina Weirather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tina_Weirather) won a bronze medal in 2018 in the [Super-G](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super-G). With ten medals overall (all in alpine skiing), Liechtenstein has won more Olympic medals per capita than any other nation.[^cite_note-148] It is the smallest country to win a medal in any Olympics, Winter or Summer, and currently the only country to win a medal in the Winter Games but not in the Summer Games. Other notable skiers from Liechtenstein are [Marco Büchel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marco_B%C3%BCchel), [Willi Frommelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willi_Frommelt), [Paul Frommelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Frommelt) and [Ursula Konzett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ursula_Konzett).

Another discipline unusually popular with Liechtensteiners is [motorsport](1022.md); American-born German-Colombian [Rikky von Opel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rikky_von_Opel) raced under the flag of Liechtenstein in [Formula One](10854.md) in [1973](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_Formula_One_season) and [1974](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1974_Formula_One_season), and [Manfred Schurti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manfred_Schurti) competed in 9 editions of the [24 Hours of Le Mans](1401596.md) as a [Porsche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porsche) factory driver with a best finish of 4th overall in [1976](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1976_24_Hours_of_Le_Mans).[^cite_note-149][^cite_note-150] The country is currently represented internationally by [Fabienne Wohlwend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fabienne_Wohlwend) and [Matthias Kaiser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthias_Kaiser) in [endurance racing](699018.md).[^cite_note-151][^cite_note-152]

Other sports Liechtenstein athletes have had success in include [tennis](29773.md), with [Stephanie Vogt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephanie_Vogt) and [Kathinka von Deichmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kathinka_von_Deichmann) both having varying degrees of success on the women's tour, as well as [swimming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swimming_\(sport\)); both [Julia Hassler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_Hassler) and [Christoph Meier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christoph_Meier) represented the country at the [2016 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Summer_Olympics) with the former the nations' flag bearer.[^cite_note-153][^cite_note-154]

In March 2020, the distance world record for electric motorcycles was set in Liechtenstein. Artist [Michel von Tell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_von_Tell) drove over 1,000 miles within 24 hours on the first electric [Harley-Davidson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harley-Davidson). The record is still current in 2023 and ended in [Ruggell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruggell).[^cite_note-155][^cite_note-156][^cite_note-157]
