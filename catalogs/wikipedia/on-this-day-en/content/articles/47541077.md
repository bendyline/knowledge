The **King Charles III England Coast Path** (**KCIIIECP**), originally and still commonly known as the **England Coast Path**, is a long-distance [National Trail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Trail) that follows the coastline of England. Opened on 19 March 2026 by [King Charles III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Charles_III), the trail extends for 2,689 miles (4,328 km).[^cite_note-EnglandCoastPath-2][^cite_note-Rowlatt-3]

Sections of the English coast already had established walking routes, most notably the [South West Coast Path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_West_Coast_Path). However, the [Marine and Coastal Access Act 2009](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_and_Coastal_Access_Act_2009) required [Natural England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_England), under section 298, to create a continuous coastal path.[^cite_note-4] The first section, along [Weymouth Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weymouth_Bay), opened in 2012.[^cite_note-5] The walking route is the longest coastal trail in the world, and its total length increases further when considered alongside the [Wales Coast Path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales_Coast_Path).[^cite_note-Rowlatt-3]

## History



[Gristhorpe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gristhorpe) cliff tops on the [Cleveland Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleveland_Way), [North York Moors National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_York_Moors_National_Park)

### Background

Following the establishment of the [Pembrokeshire Coast National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pembrokeshire_Coast_National_Park) in 1952, the Welsh naturalist and author [Ronald Lockley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Lockley) surveyed a potential route around the coast. This was subsequently constructed and opened in 1970 as the [Pembrokeshire Coast Path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pembrokeshire_Coast_Path).[^cite_note-PembrokeshgireCoastal-6] Lockley's 1953 report for the [Countryside Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countryside_Commission) was well received and largely adopted. Some sections of the route followed existing rights of way, but most of the land was privately owned, requiring negotiation. The majority of landowners supported the project, and many benefitted from the installation of new fencing.[^cite_note-PembrokeshireCoast-7]

In 2000, the government legislated to introduce a limited "[right to roam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_to_roam)". The [Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countryside_and_Rights_of_Way_Act_2000) (CROW) was implemented gradually from 2000 onwards, granting the public a conditional right of access to certain areas of the English and Welsh countryside, including some coastal land. Developed land, gardens and various other areas are specifically excluded. Agricultural land is accessible only where it falls within the types defined in the Act. People exercising the right of access must respect land management needs and the protection of nature. The new rights were introduced region by region across England, with implementation completed in 2005.[^cite_note-8]

In 2004, the [Ramblers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramblers) started their campaign, advocating for a coastal pathway.[^cite_note-2b-1]

### Path history



[Cliffs of Dover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cliffs_of_Dover) on the [Saxon Shore Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saxon_Shore_Way)



King Charles III England Coast Path sign

The King Charles III England Coast Path has been made possible by the introduction of the Right of Coastal Access under Part 9 of the [Marine and Coastal Access Act 2009](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_and_Coastal_Access_Act_2009).[^cite_note-auto-9][^cite_note-10] The Act provides for the creation of both the England Coast Path and, in most cases, a right of access over the associated 'coastal margin'.[^cite_note-11] This includes – where appropriate – any land, other than the trail itself, that forms part of the coastal margin and carries public access rights. Such areas are known as 'spreading room'. The right does not extend to private houses and gardens, or to [Ministry of Defence](203793.md) land. The legislation also introduces 'roll back', meaning that if a section of coastline erodes, the route of the path can be adjusted inland accordingly.[^cite_note-12]

Natural England's Coastal Access Scheme was approved by the [Secretary of State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_State_\(United_Kingdom\)) on 9 July 2013 under section 298(2) of the Marine and Coastal Access Act 2009, and was presented to Parliament pursuant to section 298(6) of the Act.[^cite_note-auto-9]

The first instance of this new law was implemented on a stretch of the English coast at [Weymouth Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weymouth_Bay) on 29 June 2012.[^cite_note-13]

In December 2014, encouraged by the success of the [Wales Coast Path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales_Coast_Path), the government announced that more than £5 million of additional funding would be provided over the following five years to enable completion the England Coast Path by 2020, around a decade earlier than previously anticipated.[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-Progress-15]

In the UK, [public access](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_of_way_\(public_throughway\)#Foreshore) to the [foreshore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreshore) below the [high tide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_tide) line has long existed. The intertidal zone is generally regarded as being owned by [the Crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Crown), although there are exceptions. In England, ownership of land typically extends only to the [high water mark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_water_mark), with the Crown deemed to own the land below it.[^cite_note-16]

Progress slowed due to COVID-19 and a [European Court](87187.md) judgement in April 2018 regarding environmentally protected sites.[^cite_note-17] Natural England hoped to have all stretches approved and work underway by the end of 2024.[^cite_note-18]

In May 2023, to mark the coronation of [King Charles III](125248.md), Secretary of State for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs [Thérèse Coffey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Th%C3%A9r%C3%A8se_Coffey) announced that the England Coast Path would be renamed the King Charles III England Coast Path.[^cite_note-Improving-19] New branding was introduced to reflect the colours of the [English flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_England), with signage being gradually introduced across new stretches of the path.

On 19 March 2026, King Charles III attended a reception hosted by the [South Downs National Park Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Downs_National_Park_Authority) to mark the opening of the coastal path; at that date it was about 80% complete.[^cite_note-Rowlatt-3]

## Linking trails

### Scottish border

The coastal path has four trailheads. At [Berwick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berwick-upon-Tweed), the England Coast Path connects to the [Berwickshire Coastal Path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berwickshire_Coastal_Path). There are plans to create a [Scottish Coastal Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Coastal_Way), which would likely link at Berwick. At [Gretna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gretna,_Dumfries_and_Galloway), the path may connect to the Scottish Coastal Way, if it is built. Here, the path can also connect to [Hadrian's Wall Path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadrian's_Wall_Path), allowing for users to connect back to the England Coast Path, at [Newcastle upon Tyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newcastle_upon_Tyne), south of Berwick.[^cite_note-1a-20]

### Welsh border

At [Queensferry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queensferry,_Flintshire), the England Coastal path connects to the [Wales Coastal Path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales_Coastal_Path). Likewise, at [Aust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aust), the Wales Coast Path can be accessed by crossing the [Severn Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Severn_Bridge). The [England–Wales border](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/England%E2%80%93Wales_border) can be followed from both trailheads, by using the [Offa's Dyke Path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offa's_Dyke_Path).[^cite_note-1a-20]

## The route

The route itself is divided into five separate areas, roughly based on the region. These areas are North-East (Berwick to [Skegness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skegness)), East (Skegness to London), South-East (London to [Southampton](7920751.md)), South-West (Southampton to Aust) and North-West (Queensferry to Gretna).[^cite_note-21]



England Coast Path route sign in [Chatham, Kent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chatham,_Kent)

### Gaps

The path includes two gaps, both around the [Furness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Furness_Peninsula) and [Cartmel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartmel_Peninsula) peninsulas in Cumbria. Walkers are expected to take the train between [Arnside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnside_railway_station) and [Grange-over-Sands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grange-over-Sands_railway_station), to the east of the Cartmel Peninsula. They can then cross by footbridge to the Furness Peninsula, but must take a train west of the peninsula, between [Foxfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foxfield_railway_station) and [Green Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Road_railway_station).[^cite_note-22]
