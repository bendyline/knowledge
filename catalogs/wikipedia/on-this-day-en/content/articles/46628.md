An old [Nixdorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nixdorf_Computer) ATM in Germany (German: *Bankautomat*)



Smaller indoor ATMs dispense money inside [convenience stores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convenience_store) and other busy areas, such as this off-premises [Wincor Nixdorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wincor_Nixdorf) mono-function ICA ATM in Sweden.

An **automated teller machine** (**ATM**) is an electronic [telecommunications](33094374.md) device that enables customers of [financial institutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_institution) to perform [financial transactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_transaction), such as cash withdrawals, deposits, funds transfers, balance or account information inquiries, at any time and without the need for direct interaction with bank staff.

ATMs are known by a variety of other names, including automatic teller machines (ATMs) in the United States[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3] (sometimes [redundantly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAS_syndrome) as "ATM machine"). In Canada, the term **automated banking machine** (**ABM**) is also used,[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5] although ATM is also very commonly used in Canada, with many Canadian organizations using ATM rather than ABM.[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8] In British English, the terms **cashpoint**, **cash machine** and **hole in the wall** are also used.[^cite_note-9][^cite_note-\:1-10][^cite_note-11] ATMs that are [not operated by a financial institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_ATM_deployer) are known as "[white-label](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White-label_ABMs)" ATMs.

Using an ATM, customers can access their [bank deposit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_deposit) or credit accounts in order to make a variety of financial transactions, most notably [cash](55336.md) withdrawals and balance checking, as well as transferring credit to and from mobile phones. ATMs can also be used to withdraw cash in a foreign country. If the currency being withdrawn from the ATM is different from that in which the bank account is denominated, the money will be converted at the financial institution's [exchange rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange_rate).[^cite_note-12] Customers are typically identified by inserting a plastic [ATM card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ATM_card) (or some other acceptable payment card) into the ATM, with authentication being by the customer entering a [personal identification number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_identification_number) (PIN), which must match the PIN stored in the chip on the card (if the card is so equipped), or in the issuing financial institution's database.

According to the [ATM Industry Association (ATMIA)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ATMIA), as of 2015, there were close to 3.5 million ATMs installed worldwide.[^cite_note-13][^cite_note-14] However, the use of ATMs is gradually declining with the increase in cashless payment systems.[^cite_note-decline1-15]

## History

The idea of out-of-hours cash distribution was first put into practice in Japan, the United Kingdom, and Sweden.[^cite_note-Atlantic-16][^cite_note-17]

In 1960, Armenian-American inventor [Luther Simjian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luther_Simjian) invented an automated deposit machine (accepting coins, cash and cheques) although it did not have cash dispensing features.[^cite_note-18] His US patent was first filed on 30 June 1960 and granted on 26 February 1963.[^cite_note-19] The roll-out of this machine, called Bankograph, was delayed by a couple of years, due in part to Simjian's Reflectone Electronics Inc. being acquired by Universal Match Corporation.[^cite_note-20] An experimental Bankograph was installed in New York City in 1961 by the [City Bank of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citibank), but removed after six months due to the lack of customer acceptance.[^cite_note-21]

In 1962, Adrian Ashfield invented the idea of a card system to securely identify a user and control and monitor the dispensing of goods or services. This was granted UK Patent 959,713 in June 1964 and assigned to Kins Developments Limited.[^cite_note-22]

### Invention

In 1966, a Japanese device called the "Computer Loan Machine" dispensed cash as a three-month loan at an annual interest rate of 5% upon inserting a credit card.[^cite_note-23][^cite_note-24] However, little is known about the device.[^cite_note-Atlantic-16]



Actor [Reg Varney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reg_Varney) using the world's first cash machine at [Barclays Bank, Enfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barclays_Bank,_Enfield), north London on 27 June 1967

&#x20;A cash machine was installed at [Barclays Bank, Enfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barclays_Bank,_Enfield), North London in the United Kingdom, on 27 June 1967. This is generally considered the world's first ATM.[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-\:0-26] This machine was inaugurated by English actor [Reg Varney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reg_Varney) as part of the launch publicity.[^cite_note-27] This invention is credited to the engineering team led by [John Shepherd-Barron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Shepherd-Barron) of printing firm [De La Rue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_La_Rue),[^cite_note-Milligan_2007-28] who was awarded an [OBE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_the_British_Empire) in the [2005 New Year Honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_New_Year_Honours).[^cite_note-29][^cite_note-Networld_Media_Group-30] Transactions were initiated by inserting paper cheques issued by a teller or cashier, marked with [carbon-14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon-14) for machine readability and security, which in a later model were matched with a four-digit [personal identification number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_identification_number) (PIN).[^cite_note-Milligan_2007-28][^cite_note-31] Shepherd-Barron stated:&#x20;

> It struck me there must be a way I could get my own money, anywhere in the world or the UK. I hit upon the idea of a chocolate bar dispenser, but replacing chocolate with cash.[^cite_note-Milligan_2007-28]



[Blue plaque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_plaque) on the Enfield Barclays commemorating the world's first cash machine

The Barclays–De La Rue machine (called De La Rue Automatic Cash System or DACS)[^cite_note-32] beat the [Swedish saving banks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_banks_in_Sweden)' and a company called Metior's machine (a device called Bankomat) by a mere nine days and British [Westminster Bank's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Westminster_Bank) [Smith Industries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smiths_Group) [Chubb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chubb_Locks) system (called Chubb MD2) by a month.[^cite_note-33] The online version of the Swedish machine is listed to have been operational on 6 May 1968, while claiming to be the first online ATM in the world, ahead of similar claims by [IBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM) and [Lloyds Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lloyds_Banking_Group) in 1971,[^cite_note-34] and [Oki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OKI_\(company\)) in 1970.[^cite_note-35] The collaboration of a small start-up called Speytec and [Midland Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midland_Bank) developed a fourth machine which was marketed after 1969 in Europe and the US by the [Burroughs Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burroughs_Corporation). The patent for this device (GB1329964) was filed in September 1969 (and granted in 1973) by John David Edwards, Leonard Perkins, John Henry Donald, Peter Lee Chappell, Sean Benjamin Newcombe, and Malcom David Roe. Both the DACS and MD2 accepted only a single-use token or voucher which was retained by the machine, while the Speytec worked with a card with a magnetic stripe at the back. They used principles including [Carbon-14](25856.md) and low-coercivity [magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetism) in order to make fraud more difficult.

The idea of a [PIN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_identification_number) stored on the card was developed by a group of engineers working at [Smiths Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smiths_Group) on the Chubb MD2 in 1965 and which has been credited to [James Goodfellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Goodfellow)[^cite_note-36] (patent GB1197183 filed on 2 May 1966 with Anthony Davies). The essence of this system was that it enabled the verification of the customer with the debited account without human intervention. This patent is also the earliest instance of a complete "currency dispenser system" in the patent record. This patent was filed on 5 March 1968 in the US (US 3543904) and granted on 1 December 1970. It had a profound influence on the industry as a whole. Not only did future entrants into the cash dispenser market such as [NCR Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCR_Corporation) and [IBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM) license Goodfellow's PIN system, but a number of later patents reference this patent as "Prior Art Device".[^cite_note-\:0-26]

### Propagation

Devices designed by British (i.e. Chubb, De La Rue) and Swedish (i.e. Asea Meteor) manufacturers quickly spread out. For example, given its link with [Barclays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barclays), [Bank of Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_Scotland) deployed a DACS in 1968 under the 'Scotcash' brand.[^cite_note-37] Customers were given personal code numbers to activate the machines, similar to the modern PIN. They were also supplied with £10 vouchers. These were fed into the machine, and the corresponding amount was debited from the customer's account.

A Chubb-made ATM appeared in [Sydney](27862.md) in 1969. This was the first ATM installed in Australia. The machine only dispensed \$25 at a time and the bank card itself would be mailed to the user after the bank had processed the withdrawal.

1969 [ABC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Broadcasting_Corporation) news report on the introduction of ATMs in [Sydney, Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney,_Australia). People could only receive [AUS \$](81215.md)25 at a time and the bank card was sent back to the user at a later date. This was a Chubb machine.

Asea Metior's Bancomat was the first ATM installed in Spain on 9 January 1969, in central [Madrid](41188263.md) by [Banesto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banesto). This device dispensed 1,000 [peseta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_peseta) bills (1 to 5 max). Each user had to introduce a security personal key using a combination of the ten numeric buttons.[^cite_note-38] In March of the same year an ad with the instructions to use the Bancomat was published in the same newspaper.[^cite_note-39]

In [West Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Germany), the first ATM was installed in the 50,000-people university city of [Tübingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C3%BCbingen) on 27 May 1968, by Kreissparkasse Tübingen. It was built by [Aalen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aalen)-based safe builder Ostertag AG in cooperation with [AEG-Telefunken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AEG-Telefunken). Each of the 1,000 selected users were given a double-bit key to open the safe with "Geldausgabe" written on it, a plastic identification card, and ten [punched cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punched_card). One punch card functioned as a withdrawal slip for a 100 [DM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsche_Mark) bill, the maximum limit for daily use was 400 DM.[^cite_note-40][^cite_note-41]

### Docutel in the United States



An [NCR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCR_Corporation) Personas 75-Series interior, multi-function ATM in the United States

After looking firsthand at the experiences in Europe, in 1968, the ATM was pioneered in the US by [Donald Wetzel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Wetzel), who was a department head at a company called Docutel.[^cite_note-Networld_Media_Group-30] Docutel was a subsidiary of Recognition Equipment Inc of [Dallas](53838.md), [Texas](29810.md), which was producing optical scanning equipment and had instructed Docutel to explore automated baggage handling and automated gasoline pumps.[^cite_note-42]

On 2 September 1969, [Chemical Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_Bank) installed a prototype ATM in the US at its branch in [Rockville Centre, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockville_Centre,_New_York). The first ATMs were designed to dispense a fixed amount of cash when a user inserted a specially coded card.[^cite_note-43] A Chemical Bank advertisement boasted "On Sept. 2 our bank will open at 9\:00 and never close again."[^cite_note-44] Chemical's ATM, initially known as a Docuteller was designed by [Donald Wetzel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Wetzel) and his company Docutel. Chemical executives were initially hesitant about the electronic banking transition given the high cost of the early machines. Additionally, executives were concerned that customers would resist having machines handling their money.[^cite_note-45] In 1995, the [Smithsonian National Museum of American History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smithsonian_National_Museum_of_American_History) recognized Docutel and Wetzel as the inventors of the networked ATM.[^cite_note-46] To show confidence in Docutel, Chemical installed the first four production machines in a marketing test that proved they worked reliably, customers would use them and even pay a fee for usage. Based on this, banks around the country began to experiment with ATM installations.

By 1974, Docutel had acquired 70 percent of the US market; but as a result of the early 1970s worldwide recession and its reliance on a single product line, Docutel lost its independence and was forced to merge with the US subsidiary of [Olivetti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olivetti).[^cite_note-47]

In 1973, Wetzel was granted [U.S. Patent # 3,761,682](http://patft.uspto.gov/netacgi/nph-Parser?Sect1=PTO1\&Sect2=HITOFF\&d=PALL\&p=1\&u=%2Fnetahtml%2FPTO%2Fsrchnum.htm\&r=1\&f=G\&l=50\&s1=3761682.PN.\&OS=PN/3761682\&RS=PN/3761682) [Archived](https://web.archive.org/web/20170905225829/http://patft.uspto.gov/netacgi/nph-Parser?Sect1=PTO1\&Sect2=HITOFF\&d=PALL\&p=1\&u=%2Fnetahtml%2FPTO%2Fsrchnum.htm\&r=1\&f=G\&l=50\&s1=3761682.PN.\&OS=PN/3761682\&RS=PN/3761682) 5 September 2017 at the [Wayback Machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayback_Machine); the application had been filed in October 1971. However, the US patent record cites at least three previous applications from Docutel, all relevant to the development of the ATM and where Wetzel does not figure, namely [US Patent # 3,662,343](http://patft.uspto.gov/netacgi/nph-Parser?Sect1=PTO1\&Sect2=HITOFF\&d=PALL\&p=1\&u=%2Fnetahtml%2FPTO%2Fsrchnum.htm\&r=1\&f=G\&l=50\&s1=3662343.PN.\&OS=PN/3662343\&RS=PN/3662343) [Archived](https://web.archive.org/web/20170905230255/http://patft.uspto.gov/netacgi/nph-Parser?Sect1=PTO1\&Sect2=HITOFF\&d=PALL\&p=1\&u=%2Fnetahtml%2FPTO%2Fsrchnum.htm\&r=1\&f=G\&l=50\&s1=3662343.PN.\&OS=PN/3662343\&RS=PN/3662343) 5 September 2017 at the [Wayback Machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayback_Machine), [U.S. Patent # 3651976](http://patft.uspto.gov/netacgi/nph-Parser?Sect1=PTO1\&Sect2=HITOFF\&d=PALL\&p=1\&u=%2Fnetahtml%2FPTO%2Fsrchnum.htm\&r=1\&f=G\&l=50\&s1=3651976.PN.\&OS=PN/3651976\&RS=PN/3651976) [Archived](https://web.archive.org/web/20170905184032/http://patft.uspto.gov/netacgi/nph-Parser?Sect1=PTO1\&Sect2=HITOFF\&d=PALL\&p=1\&u=%2Fnetahtml%2FPTO%2Fsrchnum.htm\&r=1\&f=G\&l=50\&s1=3651976.PN.\&OS=PN/3651976\&RS=PN/3651976) 5 September 2017 at the [Wayback Machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayback_Machine) and [U.S. Patent # 3,68,569](http://patft.uspto.gov/netacgi/nph-Parser?Sect1=PTO1\&Sect2=HITOFF\&d=PALL\&p=1\&u=%2Fnetahtml%2FPTO%2Fsrchnum.htm\&r=1\&f=G\&l=50\&s1=368569.PN.\&OS=PN/368569\&RS=PN/368569) [Archived](https://web.archive.org/web/20170905183830/http://patft.uspto.gov/netacgi/nph-Parser?Sect1=PTO1\&Sect2=HITOFF\&d=PALL\&p=1\&u=%2Fnetahtml%2FPTO%2Fsrchnum.htm\&r=1\&f=G\&l=50\&s1=368569.PN.\&OS=PN/368569\&RS=PN/368569) 5 September 2017 at the [Wayback Machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayback_Machine). ### Further advances

In April 1971, [Busicom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Busicom) began to manufacture ATMs based on the first commercial [microprocessor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microprocessor), the [Intel 4004](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intel_4004). Busicom manufactured these microprocessor-based automated teller machines for several buyers, with [NCR Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCR_Corporation) as the main customer.[^cite_note-48]

[Mohamed Atalla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohamed_Atalla) invented the first [hardware security module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardware_security_module) (HSM),[^cite_note-Stiennon-49] dubbed the "Atalla Box", a security system which encrypted [PIN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_identification_number) and ATM messages, and protected offline devices with an un-guessable PIN-generating key.[^cite_note-Lazo-50] In March 1972, Atalla filed [U.S. patent 3,938,091](https://patents.google.com/patent/US3938091) for his PIN verification system, which included an encoded [card reader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Card_reader) and described a system that utilized [encryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encryption) techniques to assure telephone link security while entering personal ID information that was transmitted to a remote location for verification.[^cite_note-nist-51]

He founded [Atalla Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atalla_Corporation) (now [Utimaco Atalla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utimaco_Atalla)) in 1972,[^cite_note-Langford-52] and commercially launched the "Atalla Box" in 1973.[^cite_note-Lazo-50] The product was released as the Identikey. It was a card reader and [customer identification system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_verification_service), providing a terminal with [plastic card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_card) and PIN capabilities. The Identikey system consisted of a card reader console, two customer [PIN pads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PIN_pad), an intelligent controller and a built-in electronic interface package.[^cite_note-Computerworld1978-53] The device consisted of two [keypads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keypads), one for the customer and one for the teller. It allowed the customer to type in a secret code, which is transformed by the device, using a microprocessor, into another code for the teller.[^cite_note-54] During a [transaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_transaction), the customer's [account number was read by the card reader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_card_number). This process replaced manual entry and avoided possible keystroke errors. It allowed users to replace traditional customer verification methods such as signature verification and test questions with a secure PIN system.[^cite_note-Computerworld1978-53] The success of the "Atalla Box" led to the wide adoption of hardware security modules in ATMs.[^cite_note-55] Its PIN verification process was similar to the later [IBM 3624](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_3624).[^cite_note-56] Atalla's HSM products protect 250 million [card transactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Card_Transaction_Data) every day as of 2013,[^cite_note-Langford-52] and secure the majority of the world's ATM transactions as of 2014.[^cite_note-Stiennon-49]

The IBM 2984 was a modern ATM and came into use at Lloyds Bank, High Street, Brentwood, Essex, United Kingdom in December 1972. The IBM 2984 was designed at the request of [Lloyds Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lloyds_Bank_\(historic\)). The 2984 Cash Issuing Terminal was a true ATM, similar in function to today's machines and named Cashpoint by Lloyds Bank. Cashpoint is still a [registered trademark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Registered_trademark) of Lloyds Bank plc in the UK[^cite_note-57] but is often used as a [generic trademark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_generic_and_genericized_trademarks) to refer to ATMs of all UK banks.[^cite_note-\:1-10] All were online and issued a variable amount which was immediately deducted from the account. A small number of 2984s were supplied to a US bank. A couple of well-known historical models of ATMs include the [Atalla Box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atalla_Corporation),[^cite_note-Lazo-50] IBM 3614, [IBM 3624](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_3624) and 473x series, [Diebold 10xx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diebold_10xx) and TABS 9000 series, NCR 1780 and earlier NCR 770 series.

The first switching system to enable shared automated teller machines between banks went into production operation on 3 February 1979, in Denver, Colorado, in an effort by Colorado National Bank of Denver and Kranzley and Company of Cherry Hill, New Jersey.[^cite_note-58]

In 2012, a new ATM at [Royal Bank of Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Bank_of_Scotland) allowed customers to withdraw cash up to £130 without a card by inputting a six-digit code requested through their smartphones.[^cite_note-59]

## Location



The world's highest ATM at the [Khunjerab Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khunjerab_Pass) in [Gilgit Baltistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilgit_Baltistan), [Pakistan](23235.md) by [NBP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bank_of_Pakistan), which is located at the height of 4,693 metres (15,397 ft) above sea level

[^cite_note-60]



Mobile ATM after [Hurricane Sandy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Sandy) in [New Jersey](21648.md), US

ATMs can be placed at any location but are most often placed near or inside [banks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank), [shopping centers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shopping_center), [airports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airport), [railway stations](26161590.md), [metro stations](274273.md), [grocery stores](239196.md), [gas stations](61260.md), [restaurants](26437.md), and other locations. ATMs are also found on [cruise ships](314855.md) and on some [US Navy](20518076.md) ships, where sailors can draw out their pay.[^cite_note-61]

ATMs may be on- and off-premises. On-premises ATMs are typically more advanced, multi-function machines that complement a bank branch's capabilities, and are thus more expensive. Off-premises machines are deployed by financial institutions where there is a simple need for cash, so they are generally cheaper single-function devices. [Independent ATM deployers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_ATM_deployer) unaffiliated with banks install and maintain [white-label ATMs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White-label_ATM).

In the US, Canada and some [Gulf countries](3088555.md),[^cite_note-62] banks may have [drive-thru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drive-thru) lanes providing access to ATMs using an automobile.

In recent times, countries like India and some countries in Africa are installing solar-powered ATMs in rural areas.[^cite_note-nanoATM-63]

The world's highest ATM is located at the [Khunjerab Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khunjerab_Pass) in [Pakistan](23235.md). Installed at an elevation of 4,693 metres (15,397 ft) by the [National Bank of Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bank_of_Pakistan), it is designed to work in temperatures as low as -40-degree Celsius.[^cite_note-64]

An ATM in the [Netherlands](21148.md). The [logos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logo) of a number of [interbank networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interbank_network) to which it is connected are shown. The [PIN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PIN_\(debit_card\)) card logo is not displayed, although this system was in use here at the time.

Most ATMs are connected to [interbank networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interbank_network), enabling people to withdraw and deposit money from machines not belonging to the bank where they have their accounts or in the countries where their accounts are held (enabling cash withdrawals in local currency). Some examples of interbank networks include [NYCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NYCE), [PULSE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulse_\(interbank_network\)), [PLUS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plus_\(interbank_network\)), [Cirrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cirrus_\(interbank_network\)), [AFFN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AFFN), [Interac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interac),[^cite_note-65] Interswitch, [STAR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STAR_\(interbank_network\)), [LINK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LINK_\(UK\)), [MegaLink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MegaLink), and [BancNet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BancNet).

ATMs rely on the authorization of a [financial transaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_transaction) by the card issuer or other authorizing institution on a communications network. This is often performed through an [ISO 8583](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_8583) messaging system.

Many banks charge [ATM usage fees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ATM_usage_fees). In some cases, these fees are charged solely to users who are not customers of the bank that operates the ATM; in other cases, they apply to all users.

To allow a more diverse range of devices to attach to their networks, some interbank networks have passed rules expanding the definition of an ATM to be a terminal that either has the vault within its footprint or utilizes the vault or cash drawer within the merchant establishment, which allows for the use of a [scrip cash dispenser](#Scrip_Cash_Dispenser).

In addition to methods employed for transaction security and secrecy, all communications traffic between the ATM and the Transaction Processor may also be encrypted using methods such as [SSL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secure_Sockets_Layer).[^cite_note-66]

There are no hard international or government-compiled numbers totaling the complete number of ATMs in use worldwide. Estimates as of 2015 developed by [ATMIA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ATMIA) placed the number of ATMs in use at 3 million units, or approximately 1 ATM per 3,000 people in the world.[^cite_note-67][^cite_note-68]

To simplify the analysis of ATM usage around the world, financial institutions generally divide the world into seven regions, based on the penetration rates, usage statistics, and features deployed. Four regions (USA, Canada, Europe, and Japan) have high numbers of ATMs per million people.[^cite_note-69][^cite_note-70] Despite the large number of ATMs, there is additional demand for machines in the Asia/Pacific area as well as in Latin America.[^cite_note-71][^cite_note-72] [Macau](19068.md) may have the highest density of ATMs at 254 ATMs per 100,000 adults.[^cite_note-73]

With the uptake of [cashless payment solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cashless_society) in the late 2010s, ATM numbers and usage started to decline. This happened first in developed countries at a time when ATM numbers were still increasing in Asia and Africa. As of 2021, there had been a global decline in the number of ATMs in use, with the average dropping to 39 per 100,000 adults from a peak of 41 per 100,000 adults in 2020.[^cite_note-decline1-15][^cite_note-74]

## Hardware



A block diagram of an ATM

An ATM is typically made up of the following devices:

- [CPU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CPU) (to control the user interface and transaction devices)

- [Magnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_stripe_card) or [chip card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chip_card) reader (to identify the customer)

- a [PIN pad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PIN_pad) for accepting and encrypting [personal identification number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_identification_number) EPP4 (similar in layout to a [touch tone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Touch_tone) or [calculator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculator) keypad), manufactured as part of a secure enclosure

- [Secure cryptoprocessor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secure_cryptoprocessor), generally within a secure enclosure

- Display (used by the customer for performing the transaction)

- [Function key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_key) buttons (usually close to the display) or a [touchscreen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Touchscreen) (used to select the various aspects of the transaction)

- Record printer (to provide the customer with a record of the transaction)

- [Vault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_vault) (to store the parts of the machinery requiring restricted access)

- Housing (for [aesthetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetics) and to attach signage to)

- Sensors and indicators

Due to heavier computing demands and the falling price of [personal computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_computer)–like architectures, ATMs have moved away from custom hardware architectures using [microcontrollers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microcontroller) or [application-specific integrated circuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Application-specific_integrated_circuit) and have adopted the hardware architecture of a personal computer, such as USB connections for peripherals, Ethernet and IP communications, and use personal computer operating systems.

Business owners often lease ATMs from service providers. However, based on the economies of scale, the price of equipment has dropped to the point where many business owners are simply paying for ATMs using a credit card.

New ADA voice and text-to-speech guidelines imposed in 2010, but required by March 2012[^cite_note-75] have forced many ATM owners to either upgrade non-compliant machines or dispose them if they are not upgradable, and purchase new compliant equipment. This has created an avenue for hackers and thieves to obtain ATM hardware at junkyards from improperly disposed decommissioned machines.[^cite_note-76]



Two [Loomis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loomis_\(company\)) employees refilling an ATM at the Downtown [Seattle](11388236.md) [REI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recreational_Equipment,_Inc.)

The vault of an ATM is within the footprint of the device itself and is where items of value are kept. [Scrip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scrip) cash dispensers, which print a receipt or scrip instead of cash, do not incorporate a vault.

Mechanisms found inside the vault may include:

- Dispensing mechanism (to provide [cash](55336.md) or other items of value)

- Deposit mechanism including a cheque processing module and bulk note acceptor (to allow the customer to make deposits)

- Security sensors (magnetic, thermal, seismic, gas)

- Locks (to control access to the contents of the vault)

- Journaling systems; many are electronic (a sealed flash memory device based on in-house standards) or a solid-state device (an actual printer) which accrues all records of activity including access timestamps, number of notes dispensed, etc. This is considered sensitive data and is secured in a similar fashion to the cash as it is a similar liability.

ATM vaults are supplied by manufacturers in several grades. Factors influencing vault grade selection include cost, weight, regulatory requirements, ATM type, operator risk avoidance practices and internal volume requirements.[^cite_note-77] Industry standard vault configurations include [Underwriters Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underwriters_Laboratories) UL-291 "Business Hours" and Level 1 Safes,[^cite_note-78] RAL TL-30 derivatives,[^cite_note-79] and CEN EN 1143-1 - CEN III and CEN IV.[^cite_note-80][^cite_note-81]

ATM manufacturers recommend that a vault be attached to the floor to prevent theft,[^cite_note-82] though there is a record of a theft conducted by tunneling into an ATM floor.[^cite_note-tunnel-83]

## Software



Although Microsoft discontinued support for the operating system in 2014, a significant number of ATMs as of 2020 still use versions of [Windows XP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Windows_XP), as seen with this machine at a branch of [Tesco Express](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesco_Express) in [Slough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slough), Berkshire.

With the migration to commodity Personal Computer hardware, standard commercial "off-the-shelf" operating systems and programming environments can be used inside of ATMs. Typical platforms previously used in ATM development include [RMX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RMX_\(operating_system\)) or [OS/2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OS/2).

Today, the vast majority of ATMs worldwide use [Microsoft Windows](18890.md). In early 2014, 95% of ATMs were running Windows XP.[^cite_note-84] A small number of deployments may still be running older versions of the Windows OS, such as [Windows NT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows_NT), [Windows CE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows_CE), or [Windows 2000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows_2000), even though Microsoft only supports [Windows 10](43989914.md) and [Windows 11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows_11).

There is a computer industry security view that general public desktop operating systems have greater risks as operating systems for cash dispensing machines than other types of operating systems like (secure) [real-time operating systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-time_operating_system) (RTOS). [RISKS Digest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RISKS_Digest) has many articles about ATM operating system vulnerabilities.[^cite_note-85]

[Linux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linux) is also finding some reception in the ATM marketplace. An example of this is [Banrisul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banrisul), the largest bank in the south of [Brazil](3383.md), which has replaced the [MS-DOS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MS-DOS) operating system in its ATMs with Linux. [Banco do Brasil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banco_do_Brasil) is also migrating ATMs to Linux. Indian-based [Vortex Engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vortex_Engineering) is manufacturing ATMs that operate only with Linux. Common application layer transaction protocols, such as [Diebold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diebold) 91x (911 or 912) and [NCR NDC or NDC+](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCR_Corporation) provide [emulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardware_emulation) of older generations of hardware on newer platforms with incremental extensions made over time to address new capabilities, although companies like NCR continuously improve these protocols issuing newer versions (e.g. NCR's AANDC v3.x.y, where x.y are subversions). Most major ATM manufacturers provide software packages that implement these protocols. Newer protocols such as IFX have yet to find wide acceptance by transaction processors.[^cite_note-86]

With the move to a more standardized software base, financial institutions have been increasingly interested in the ability to pick and choose the application programs that drive their equipment. [WOSA/XFS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WOSA/XFS), now known as [CEN XFS (or simply XFS)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CEN/XFS), provides a common [API](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/API) for accessing and manipulating the various devices of an ATM. [J/XFS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J/XFS) is a Java implementation of the CEN XFS API.

While the perceived benefit of XFS is similar to Java's "[write once, run anywhere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Write_once,_run_anywhere)" mantra, often different ATM hardware vendors have different interpretations of the XFS standard. The result of these differences in interpretation means that ATM applications typically use a [middleware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middleware) to even out the differences among various platforms.

With the onset of Windows operating systems and XFS on ATMs, the software applications have the ability to become more intelligent. This has created a new breed of ATM applications commonly referred to as programmable applications. These types of applications allow for an entirely new host of applications in which the ATM terminal can do more than only communicate with the ATM switch. It is now empowered to connect to other content servers and [video banking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_banking) systems.

Notable ATM software that operates on XFS platforms include Triton PRISM, [Diebold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diebold) Agilis EmPower, [NCR APTRA Edge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCR_Corporation), Absolute Systems AbsoluteINTERACT, KAL Kalignite Software Platform, Phoenix Interactive VISTAatm, [Wincor Nixdorf ProTopas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wincor_Nixdorf), [Euronet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euronet_Worldwide) EFTS and Intertech inter-ATM.

With the move of ATMs to industry-standard computing environments, concern has risen about the integrity of the ATM's software stack.[^cite_note-87]

## Impact on labor

The number of tellers in the United States increased from approximately 300,000 in 1970 to approximately 600,000 in 2010. A contributing factor may have been the introduction of automated teller machines. ATMs allow a branch to operate with fewer tellers, making it more economical for banks to open more branches, necessitating more tellers to staff those additional branches. Further automation and online banking, however, may reverse this increase resulting in a trend toward fewer bank teller positions.[^cite_note-88]

## Security

Main article: [Security of automated teller machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Security_of_automated_teller_machines)

ATM [security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Security) has several dimensions. ATMs also provide a practical demonstration of a number of security systems and concepts operating together and how various security concerns are addressed.

### Physical



A [Wincor Nixdorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wincor_Nixdorf) Procash 2100xe Frontload that was opened with an [angle grinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_grinder)

Early ATM security focused on making the terminals invulnerable to physical attack; they were effectively safes with dispenser mechanisms. A number of attacks resulted, with thieves attempting to steal entire machines by [ram-raiding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ram-raiding).[^cite_note-89] Since the late 1990s, criminal groups operating in Japan improved ram-raiding by stealing and using a truck loaded with heavy construction machinery to effectively demolish or uproot an entire ATM and any housing to steal its cash.

Another attack method, *[plofkraak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ATM_blow_up)* (a [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_language) term), is to seal all openings of the ATM with [silicone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicone) and fill the vault with a combustible gas or to place an explosive inside, attached, or near the machine. This gas or explosive is ignited and the vault is opened or distorted by the force of the resulting explosion and the criminals can break in.[^cite_note-90]

ATM bombings began in the Netherlands, but as the nation reduced the number of machines in the country from 20000 to 5000 and discouraged cash use, the mostly [Moroccan-Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moroccan-Dutch) gangs expert in the attacks moved elsewhere.[^cite_note-matussek20240809-91] Such theft has also occurred in Belgium, France, Denmark, Germany, Australia,[^cite_note-92][^cite_note-93] and the United Kingdom.[^cite_note-94] When anti-gas explosion prevention devices and reinforced ATMs were installed, criminals began using [leaf blowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf_blowers) to remove smoke, and more powerful solid explosives. Despite German banks spending more than €300 million on additional security, the [Federal Criminal Police Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Criminal_Police_Office_\(Germany\)) estimated that as of 2024 60% of attacks on ATMs in the country succeeded.[^cite_note-matussek20240809-91]

Several attacks in the UK (at least one of which was successful) have involved digging a concealed tunnel under the ATM and cutting through the reinforced base to remove the money.[^cite_note-tunnel-83]

Modern ATM physical security, per other modern money-handling security, concentrates on denying the use of the money inside the machine to a thief by using different types of [intelligent banknote neutralization systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligent_banknote_neutralisation_system) (IBNS).

A common method is to simply rob the staff filling the machine with money. To avoid this, the schedule for filling them is kept secret, varying and random. The money is often kept in cassettes, which will dye the money if incorrectly opened.

### Transactional secrecy and integrity

The security of ATM transactions relies mostly on the integrity of the secure [cryptoprocessor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptoprocessor): the ATM often uses general commodity components that sometimes are not considered to be "[trusted systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trusted_system)".

Encryption of personal information, required by law in many jurisdictions, is used to prevent fraud. Sensitive data in ATM transactions are usually [encrypted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encryption) with [DES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_Encryption_Standard), but transaction processors now usually require the use of [Triple DES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triple_DES).[^cite_note-95] Remote Key Loading techniques may be used to ensure the secrecy of the initialization of the encryption keys in the ATM. [Message Authentication Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Message_Authentication_Code) (MAC) or [Partial MAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_MAC) may also be used to ensure messages have not been tampered with while in transit between the ATM and the financial network.

### Customer identity integrity



A [BTMU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bank_of_Tokyo-Mitsubishi_UFJ) ATM with a [palm scanner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palm_scanner?action=edit\&redlink=1) (to the right of the screen)

There have also been a number of incidents of fraud by [man-in-the-middle attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Man-in-the-middle_attack), where criminals have attached fake keypads or card readers to existing machines. These have then been used to record customers' PINs and bank card information in order to gain unauthorized access to their accounts. Various ATM manufacturers have put in place countermeasures to protect the equipment they manufacture from these threats.[^cite_note-96][^cite_note-97]

Alternative methods to verify cardholder identities have been tested and deployed in some countries, such as finger and palm vein patterns,[^cite_note-98] [iris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iris_recognition), and [facial recognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facial_recognition_system) technologies. Cheaper mass-produced equipment has been developed and is being installed in machines globally that detect the presence of foreign objects on the front of ATMs. Current tests have shown 99% detection success for all types of [skimming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skimming_\(credit_card_fraud\)) devices.[^cite_note-99]

### Device operation integrity



ATMs that are exposed to the outside must be vandal- and weather-resistant.

Openings on the customer side of ATMs are often covered by mechanical shutters to prevent tampering with the mechanisms when they are not in use. Alarm sensors are placed inside ATMs and their servicing areas to alert their operators when doors have been opened by unauthorized personnel.

To protect against hackers, ATMs have a built-in firewall. Once the firewall has detected malicious attempts to break into the machine remotely, the firewall locks down the machine.

Rules are usually set by the government or ATM operating body that dictate what happens when integrity systems fail. Depending on the jurisdiction, a bank may or may not be liable when an attempt is made to dispense a customer's money from an ATM and the money either gets outside of the ATM's vault, or is exposed in a non-secure fashion, or they are unable to determine the state of the money after a failed transaction.[^cite_note-100] Customers often commented that it is difficult to recover money lost in this way, but this is often complicated by the policies regarding suspicious activities typical of the criminal element.[^cite_note-101]

### Customer security

In some countries, multiple [security cameras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Security_camera) and [security guards](19283898.md) are a common feature.[^cite_note-102] The [New York State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_State) Comptroller's Office has advised the New York State Department of Banking to have more thorough safety inspections of ATMs in high crime areas.[^cite_note-103]

Consultants of ATM operators assert that the issue of customer security should have more focus by the banking industry;[^cite_note-104] it has been suggested that efforts are now more concentrated on the preventive measure of deterrent legislation than on the problem of ongoing forced withdrawals.[^cite_note-105]



Dunbar armored personnel watching over ATMs that have been installed in a [van](63679.md)

At least as far back as 30 July 1986, consultants of the industry have advised for the adoption of an emergency PIN system for ATMs, where the user can send a [silent alarm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panic_alarm) in response to a threat.[^cite_note-106] Legislative efforts to require an emergency PIN system have appeared in [Illinois](14849.md),[^cite_note-107] [Kansas](16716.md)[^cite_note-108][^cite_note-109] and [Georgia](48830.md),[^cite_note-110] but none has succeeded yet. In January 2009, Senate Bill 1355 was proposed in the Illinois Senate that revisits the issue of the reverse emergency PIN system.[^cite_note-111] The bill is again supported by the police and opposed by the banking lobby.[^cite_note-112]

In 1998, three towns outside Cleveland, Ohio, in response to an ATM crime wave, adopted legislation requiring that an [emergency telephone number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergency_telephone_number) switch be installed at all outdoor ATMs within their jurisdiction. In the wake of a homicide in Sharon Hill, Pennsylvania, the city council passed an ATM security bill as well.

In China and elsewhere, many efforts to promote security have been made. On-premises ATMs are often located inside the bank's lobby, which may be accessible 24 hours a day. These lobbies have extensive security camera coverage, a courtesy telephone for consulting with the bank staff, and a security guard on the premises. Bank lobbies that are not guarded 24 hours a day may also have secure doors that can only be opened from outside by swiping the bank card against a wall-mounted scanner, allowing the bank to identify which card enters the building. Most ATMs will also display on-screen safety warnings and may also be fitted with convex mirrors above the display allowing the user to see what is happening behind them.

As of 2013, the only claim available about the extent of ATM-connected homicides is that they range from 500 to 1,000 per year in the US, covering only cases where the victim had an ATM card and the card was used by the killer after the known time of death.[^cite_note-113]

### Jackpotting

The term *jackpotting* is used to describe one method criminals utilize to steal money from an ATM. The thieves gain physical access through a small hole drilled in the machine. They disconnect the existing hard drive and connect an external drive using an industrial endoscope. They then depress an internal button that reboots the device so that it is now under the control of the external drive. They can then have the ATM dispense all of its cash.[^cite_note-114]

### Encryption

In recent years, many ATMs also encrypt the hard disk. This means that actually creating the software for [jackpotting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackpotting) is more difficult, and provides more security for the ATM.

## Reliability



An ATM running [Microsoft Windows](18890.md) that has [crashed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_screen_of_death) due to a peripheral component failure

Before an ATM is placed in a public place, it typically has undergone extensive testing with both [test money](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Test_Money) and the [backend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Front_and_back_ends) computer systems that allow it to perform transactions. Banking customers also have come to expect high reliability in their ATMs,[^cite_note-132] which provides incentives to ATM providers to minimize machine and network failures. Financial consequences of incorrect machine operation also provide high degrees of incentive to minimize malfunctions.[^cite_note-133]

ATMs and the supporting electronic financial networks are generally very reliable, with industry benchmarks typically producing 98.25% customer availability for ATMs[^cite_note-134] and up to 99.999% availability for host systems that manage the networks of ATMs. If ATM networks do go out of service, customers could be left without the ability to make transactions until the beginning of their bank's next time of opening hours.



A NCR Interactive Teller Machine running uGenius software

This said, not all errors are to the detriment of customers; there have been cases of machines giving out money without debiting the account, or giving out higher-value notes as a result of incorrect [denomination](524783.md) of [banknote](208286.md) being loaded in the money cassettes.[^cite_note-135] The result of receiving too much money may be influenced by the cardholder agreement in place between the customer and the bank.[^cite_note-136][^cite_note-137]

Errors that can occur may be [mechanical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine) (such as card transport mechanisms; keypads; [hard disk failures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_disk_failure); envelope deposit mechanisms); [software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software) (such as [operating system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operating_system); [device driver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Device_driver); [application](454746.md)); [communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_transfer); or purely down to operator error.

To aid in reliability, some ATMs print each transaction to a roll-paper journal that is stored inside the ATM, which allows its users and the related financial institutions to settle things based on the records in the journal in case there is a dispute. In some cases, transactions are posted to an electronic journal to remove the cost of supplying journal paper to the ATM and for more convenient searching of data.

Improper money checking can cause the possibility of a customer receiving [counterfeit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterfeit) banknotes from an ATM. While bank personnel are generally trained better at spotting and removing counterfeit cash,[^cite_note-138][^cite_note-139] the resulting ATM money supplies used by banks provide no guarantee for proper banknotes, as the [Federal Criminal Police Office of Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Criminal_Police_Office_\(Germany\)) has confirmed that there are regularly incidents of false banknotes having been dispensed through ATMs.[^cite_note-140] Some ATMs may be stocked and wholly owned by outside companies, which can further complicate this problem. [Bill validation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_validator) technology can be used by ATM providers to help ensure the authenticity of the cash before it is stocked in the machine; those with cash recycling capabilities include this capability.[^cite_note-141]

In India, whenever a transaction fails with an ATM due to network or technical issues and if the amount does not get dispensed in spite of the account being debited then the banks are supposed to return the debited amount to the customer within seven working days from the day of receipt of a complaint. Banks are also liable to pay the late fees in case of delay in repayment of funds post seven days.[^cite_note-142]

## Fraud



ATM lineup



Some ATMs may display warning messages to customers to be vigilant of possible [tampering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tampering_\(crime\)).



[10 euro notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/10_euro_note) from an ATM robbery made unusable with [red dye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligent_banknote_neutralisation_system)

As with any device containing objects of value, ATMs and the systems they depend on to function are the targets of fraud. Fraud against ATMs and people's attempts to use them takes several forms.

The first known instance of a fake ATM was installed at a shopping mall in [Manchester, Connecticut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester,_Connecticut), in 1993. By modifying the inner workings of a [Fujitsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fujitsu) model 7020 ATM, a criminal gang known as the Bucklands Boys stole information from cards inserted into the machine by customers.[^cite_note-143]

[WAVY-TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WAVY-TV) reported an incident in Virginia Beach in September 2006 where a hacker, who had probably obtained a factory-default administrator password for a filling station's white-label ATM, caused the unit to assume it was loaded with US\$5 bills instead of \$20s, enabling himself—and many subsequent customers—to walk away with four times the money withdrawn from their accounts.[^cite_note-144] This type of scam was featured on the TV series *[The Real Hustle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Real_Hustle)*.

ATM behavior can change during what is called "stand-in" time, where the bank's cash dispensing network is unable to access databases that contain account information (possibly for database maintenance). To give customers access to cash, customers may be allowed to withdraw cash up to a certain amount that may be less than their usual daily withdrawal limit, but may still exceed the amount of available money in their accounts, which could result in fraud if the customers intentionally withdraw more money than they had in their accounts.[^cite_note-145]

### Card fraud

In an attempt to prevent criminals from [shoulder surfing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoulder_surfing_\(computer_security\)) the customer's [personal identification number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_identification_number) (PIN), some banks draw privacy areas on the floor.

For a low-tech form of fraud, the easiest is to simply steal a customer's card along with its PIN. A later variant of this approach is to trap the card inside of the ATM's card reader with a device often referred to as a [Lebanese loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_loop). When the customer gets frustrated by not getting the card back and walks away from the machine, the criminal is able to remove the card and withdraw cash from the customer's account, using the card and its PIN.

This type of fraud has spread globally. Although somewhat replaced in terms of volume by skimming incidents, a re-emergence of card trapping has been noticed in regions such as Europe, where EMV chip and PIN cards have increased in circulation.[^cite_note-146]

Another simple form of fraud involves attempting to get the customer's bank to issue a new card and its PIN and stealing them from their mail.[^cite_note-147]

By contrast, a newer high-tech method of operating, sometimes called **card skimming** or **card cloning**, involves the installation of a magnetic card reader over the real ATM's card slot and the use of a wireless surveillance camera or a modified digital camera or a false PIN keypad to observe the user's PIN. Card data is then cloned into a duplicate card and the criminal attempts a standard cash withdrawal. The availability of low-cost commodity wireless cameras, keypads, card readers, and card writers has made it a relatively simple form of fraud, with comparatively low risk to the fraudsters.[^cite_note-148]

In an attempt to stop these practices, countermeasures against card cloning have been developed by the banking industry, in particular by the use of [smart cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smart_card) which cannot easily be copied or spoofed by unauthenticated devices, and by attempting to make the outside of their ATMs [tamper evident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamper-evident). Older chip-card security systems include the French [Carte Bleue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carte_Bleue), [Visa Cash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visa_Cash), [Mondex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mondex), [Blue from American Express](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_from_American_Express)[^cite_note-149] and [EMV '96 or EMV 3.11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EMV_96). The most actively developed form of smart card security in the industry today is known as [EMV 2000 or EMV 4.x](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EMV_2000).

[EMV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EMV) is widely used in the UK ([Chip and PIN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chip_and_PIN)) and other parts of Europe, but when it is not available in a specific area, ATMs must fall back to using the easy–to–copy magnetic stripe to perform transactions. This fallback behavior can be exploited.[^cite_note-150] However, the fallback option has been removed on the ATMs of some UK banks, meaning if the chip is not read, the transaction will be declined.

Card cloning and [skimming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skimming_\(credit_card_fraud\)) can be detected by the implementation of magnetic card reader heads and firmware that can read a signature embedded in all magnetic stripes during the card production process. This signature, known as a "MagnePrint" or "BluPrint", can be used in conjunction with common two-factor authentication schemes used in ATM, debit/retail point-of-sale and prepaid card applications.

The concept and various methods of copying the contents of an ATM card's magnetic stripe onto a duplicate card to access other people's financial information were well known in the hacking communities by late 1990.[^cite_note-151]

In 1996, Andrew Stone, a computer security consultant from Hampshire in the UK, was convicted of stealing more than £1 million by pointing high-definition video cameras at ATMs from a considerable distance and recording the card numbers, expiry dates, etc. from the embossed detail on the ATM cards along with video footage of the PINs being entered. After getting all the information from the videotapes, he was able to produce clone cards which not only allowed him to withdraw the full daily limit for each account, but also allowed him to sidestep withdrawal limits by using multiple copied cards. In court, it was shown that he could withdraw as much as £10,000 per hour by using this method. Stone was sentenced to five years and six months in prison.[^cite_note-152]

### Talking ATM

A [talking ATM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talking_ATM) is a type of ATM that provides audible instructions so that people who cannot read a screen can independently use the machine, therefore effectively eliminating the need for assistance from an external, potentially malevolent source. All audible information is delivered privately through a standard [headphone jack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Headphone_jack) on the face of the machine. Alternatively, some banks such as the [Nordea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nordea) and [Swedbank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedbank) use a built-in external speaker which may be invoked by pressing the talk button on the keypad.[^cite_note-153] Information is delivered to the customer either through pre-recorded [sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound) files or via text-to-speech [speech synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speech_synthesis).

### Postal Interactive Kiosk

A postal [interactive kiosk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interactive_kiosk) may share many components of an ATM (including a vault), but it only dispenses items related to postage.[^cite_note-154][^cite_note-155]

### Scrip Cash Dispenser

A **scrip cash dispenser** or **cashless ATM** may have many components in common with an ATM, but it cannot dispense physical cash and consequently requires no vault. Instead, the customer requests a withdrawal transaction from the machine, which prints a receipt or [scrip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scrip). The customer then takes this receipt to a nearby sales clerk, who then exchanges it for cash from the till.[^cite_note-156]

### Teller Assist Unit

A [teller assist unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teller_assist_unit) (TAU) is distinct in that it is designed to be operated solely by trained personnel and not by the general public, integrates directly into interbank networks, and usually is controlled by a computer that is not directly integrated into the overall construction of the unit.

A Web ATM is an online interface for ATM card banking that uses a [smart card reader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Card_reader#Smart_card_readers). All the usual ATM functions are available, except for withdrawing cash. Most banks in [Taiwan](25734.md) provide these online services.[^cite_note-157][^cite_note-158]
