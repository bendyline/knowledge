The **North Sea** lies between [Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Britain), [Denmark](76972.md), [Norway](21241.md), [Germany](11867.md), the [Netherlands](21148.md), [Belgium](3343.md), and [France](5843419.md). A sea on the [European](9239.md) [continental shelf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_shelf), it connects to the [Atlantic Ocean](698.md) through the [English Channel](9230.md) in the south and the [Norwegian Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_Sea) in the north. It is more than 970 kilometres (600 mi) long and 580 kilometres (360 mi) wide, covering 570,000 square kilometres (220,000 sq mi).

It hosts key north European [shipping lanes](2018420.md) and is a major [fishery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fishery). The coast is a popular destination for recreation and tourism in bordering countries, and a rich source of energy resources, including [wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_energy) and [wave power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_power).

The North Sea has featured prominently in geopolitical and military affairs, particularly in [Northern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Europe), from the Middle Ages to the modern era. It was also important globally through the power northern Europeans projected worldwide during much of the Middle Ages and into the modern era. The North Sea was the centre of the [Vikings' rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viking_Age). The [Hanseatic League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanseatic_League), the [Dutch Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Golden_Age), and [Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Great_Britain) all sought to gain command of the North Sea and access to the world's markets and resources. As Germany's only outlet to the ocean, the North Sea was strategically important through both world wars.

The coast has diverse geology and geography. In the north, deep [fjords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fjord) and sheer cliffs mark much of its [Norwegian](21241.md) and [Scottish](26994.md) coastlines respectively, whereas in the south, the coast consists mainly of sandy beaches, estuaries of long rivers and wide [mudflats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mudflat). Due to the dense population, heavy [industrialisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrialisation), and intense use of the sea and the area surrounding it, various environmental issues affect the sea's ecosystems. Adverse environmental issues – commonly including [overfishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overfishing), industrial and agricultural [runoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_runoff), [dredging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dredging), and dumping, among others –  have led to several efforts to prevent degradation and to safeguard long-term economic benefits.

The North Sea is bounded by the [Orkney Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orkney_Islands) and east coast of [Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Britain) to the west[^cite_note-Britannica-1] and the northern and [central European](5188.md) mainland to the east and south, including [Norway](21241.md), [Denmark](76972.md), [Germany](11867.md), the [Netherlands](21148.md), [Belgium](3343.md), and [France](5843419.md).[^cite_note-American-2] In the southwest, beyond the [Straits of Dover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strait_of_Dover), the North Sea becomes the [English Channel](9230.md) connecting to the Atlantic Ocean.[^cite_note-Britannica-1][^cite_note-American-2] In the east, it connects to the [Baltic Sea](3335.md) via the [Skagerrak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skagerrak) and [Kattegat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kattegat),[^cite_note-American-2] narrow straits that separate Denmark from Norway and [Sweden](5058739.md) respectively.[^cite_note-Britannica-1] In the north it is bordered by the [Shetland Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shetland_Islands), and connects with the [Norwegian Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_Sea), which is a marginal sea in the [Arctic Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_Ocean).[^cite_note-Britannica-1][^cite_note-3]

The North Sea is more than 970 kilometres (600 mi) long and 580 kilometres (360 mi) wide, with an area of 750,000 square kilometres (290,000 sq mi) and a volume of 54,000 cubic kilometres (13,000 cu mi).[^cite_note-Safety-4] Around the edges of the North Sea are [sizeable islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_the_largest_islands_in_the_North_Sea) and [archipelagos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archipelago), including [Shetland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shetland), [Orkney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orkney), and the [Frisian Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frisian_Islands).[^cite_note-American-2] The North Sea receives freshwater from a number of European continental watersheds, as well as the [British Isles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Isles). A large part of the European [drainage basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage_basin) empties into the North Sea, including water from the [Baltic Sea](3335.md). The largest and most important rivers flowing into the North Sea are the [Elbe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elbe) and the [Rhine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhine) – [Meuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meuse_\(river\)).[^cite_note-Ray-5] Around 185 million people live in the [catchment area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage_basin) of [the rivers discharging into the North Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_rivers_discharging_into_the_North_Sea) encompassing some highly industrialized areas.[^cite_note-NorthSeaPDF-6]

### Major features

For the most part, the sea lies on the European [continental shelf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_shelf) with a mean depth of 90 metres (300 ft).[^cite_note-Britannica-1][^cite_note-Calow-7] The only exception is the [Norwegian trench](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_trench), which extends parallel to the Norwegian shoreline from [Oslo](22309.md) to an area north of [Bergen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bergen).[^cite_note-Britannica-1] It is between 20 and 30 kilometres (12 and 19 mi) wide and has a maximum depth of 725 metres (2,379 ft).[^cite_note-Lis-8]

The [Dogger Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dogger_Bank), a vast [moraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moraine), or accumulation of unconsolidated [glacial debris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glacial_debris), rises to a mere 15 to 30 m (50 to 100 ft) below the surface.[^cite_note-Ostergren-9][^cite_note-10] This feature has produced the finest fishing location of the North Sea.[^cite_note-Britannica-1] The [Long Forties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Forties) and the [Broad Fourteens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broad_Fourteens) are large areas with roughly uniform depth in [fathoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fathom) (forty fathoms and fourteen fathoms or 73 and 26 m or 240 and 85 ft deep, respectively). These great banks and others make the North Sea particularly hazardous to navigate,[^cite_note-Tuckey-11] which has been alleviated by the implementation of [satellite navigation systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_navigation_satellite_system).[^cite_note-Bradford-12] The [Devil's Hole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devil's_Hole_\(North_Sea\)) lies 320 kilometres (200 mi) east of [Dundee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dundee), Scotland. The feature is a series of asymmetrical trenches between 20 and 30 kilometres (12 and 19 mi) long, one and two kilometres (0.6 and 1.2 mi) wide and up to 230 metres (750 ft) deep.[^cite_note-13]

Other areas which are less deep are [Cleaver Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleaver_Bank), [Fisher Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_Bank) and [Noordhinder Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noordhinder_Bank).

The average temperature is 17 °C (63 °F) in the summer and 6 °C (43 °F) in the winter.[^cite_note-Safety-4] The average temperatures have been trending higher since 1988, which has been attributed to [climate change](5042951.md).[^cite_note-16][^cite_note-17] Air temperatures in January range on average between 0 and 4 °C (32 and 39 °F) and in July between 13 and 18 °C (55 and 64 °F). The winter months see frequent gales and storms.[^cite_note-Britannica-1]

The [salinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salinity) averages between 34 and 35 grams per litre (129 and 132 g/US gal) of water.[^cite_note-Safety-4] The salinity has the highest variability where there is [fresh water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresh_water) inflow, such as at the Rhine and Elbe estuaries, the Baltic Sea exit and along the coast of Norway.[^cite_note-Reddy-18]

#### Water circulation and tides

The main pattern to the flow of water in the North Sea is an [anti-clockwise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-clockwise) rotation along the edges.[^cite_note-19]

The North Sea is an arm of the Atlantic Ocean receiving the majority of [ocean current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_current) from the northwest opening, and a lesser portion of warm current from the smaller opening at the English Channel. These tidal currents leave along the Norwegian coast.[^cite_note-SaS-20] Surface and deep water currents may move in different directions. Low salinity surface coastal waters move offshore, and deeper, denser high salinity waters move inshore.[^cite_note-21]

The North Sea located on the continental shelf has different waves from those in deep ocean water. The wave speeds are diminished and the wave amplitudes are increased. In the North Sea there are two [amphidromic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphidromic_point) systems and a third incomplete amphidromic system.[^cite_note-Dyke-22][^cite_note-Carter-23] In the North Sea the average tide difference in wave amplitude is between zero and eight metres (26 ft).[^cite_note-Safety-4]

The Kelvin tide of the Atlantic Ocean is a [semidiurnal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semidiurnal_tide) wave that travels northward. Some of the energy from this wave travels through the English Channel into the North Sea. The wave continues to travel northward in the Atlantic Ocean, and once past the northern tip of Great Britain, the [Kelvin wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelvin_wave) turns east and south and once again enters the North Sea.[^cite_note-Changing-24]

### Coasts

Main article: [Coastline of the North Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coastline_of_the_North_Sea)



The German North Sea coast

The eastern and western coasts of the North Sea are jagged, formed by [glaciers](12463.md) during the [ice ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternary_glaciation). The coastlines along the southernmost part are covered with the remains of deposited glacial sediment.[^cite_note-Britannica-1] The Norwegian mountains plunge into the sea, creating deep [fjords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fjord) and [archipelagos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archipelago). South of Stavanger, the coast softens, the islands become fewer.[^cite_note-Britannica-1] The eastern Scottish coast is similar, though less severe than Norway. Moving south from [the north-east of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_East_England), the cliffs become lower and are composed of less resistant [moraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moraine), which erodes more easily, so that the coasts have more rounded contours.[^cite_note-EastRiding-59][^cite_note-Eurosion-60] In the Netherlands, Belgium and in [East Anglia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Anglia) the [littoral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Littoral) is low and marshy.[^cite_note-Britannica-1] The east coast and south-east of the North Sea ([Wadden Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wadden_Sea)) have coastlines that are mainly sandy and straight owing to [longshore drift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longshore_drift), particularly along Belgium and Denmark.[^cite_note-GHC-61]

### Coastal management

Further information: [Afsluitdijk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afsluitdijk), [Delta Works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_Works), [Flood control in the Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flood_control_in_the_Netherlands), [Thames Barrier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thames_Barrier), and [Zuiderzee Works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zuiderzee_Works)



The [Afsluitdijk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afsluitdijk) (Closure-dike) is a major dam in the Netherlands.

The southern coastal areas were originally [flood plains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flood_plain) and swampy land. In areas especially vulnerable to storm surges, people settled behind elevated levees and on natural areas of high ground such as [spits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spit_\(landform\)) and [geestland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geest).[^cite_note-Wefer-62]<sup>: \[302, 303] </sup> As early as 500 BC, people were constructing [artificial dwelling hills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_dwelling_hill) higher than the prevailing flood levels.[^cite_note-Wefer-62]<sup>: \[306, 308] </sup> It was only around the beginning of the [High Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Middle_Ages), in 1200 AD, that inhabitants began to connect single ring dikes into a dike line along the entire coast, thereby turning amphibious regions between the land and the sea into permanent solid ground.[^cite_note-Wefer-62]

The modern form of the dikes supplemented by overflow and lateral diversion channels, began to appear in the 17th and 18th centuries, built in the Netherlands.[^cite_note-63] The [North Sea Floods of 1953](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Sea_flood_of_1953) and [1962](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Sea_flood_of_1962) were the impetus for further raising of the dikes as well as the shortening of the coast line so as to present as little surface area as possible to the punishment of the sea and the storms.[^cite_note-64] Currently, 27% of the Netherlands is below sea level protected by dikes, dunes, and beach flats.[^cite_note-65]

[Coastal management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coastal_management) today consists of several levels.[^cite_note-BASF-66] The dike slope reduces the energy of the incoming sea, so that the dike itself does not receive the full impact.[^cite_note-BASF-66] Dikes that lie directly on the sea are especially reinforced.[^cite_note-BASF-66] The dikes have, over the years, been repeatedly raised, sometimes up to 9 metres (30 ft) and have been made flatter to better reduce wave erosion.[^cite_note-67] Where the dunes are sufficient to protect the land behind them from the sea, these dunes are planted with beach grass (*[Ammophila arenaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammophila_arenaria)*) to protect them from erosion by wind, water, and foot traffic.[^cite_note-68]

### Storm tides

Main article: [Storm tides of the North Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storm_tides_of_the_North_Sea)



[Zuid-Beveland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zuid-Beveland) during the [North Sea flood of 1953](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Sea_flood_of_1953)

[Storm surges](449744.md) threaten, in particular, the coasts of the Netherlands, Belgium, Germany, and Denmark and low-lying areas of eastern England particularly around [The Wash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wash) and [Fens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Fens).[^cite_note-GHC-61] Storm surges are caused by changes in [barometric pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_pressure) combined with strong wind created [wave action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_surface_wave).[^cite_note-69]

The first recorded storm tide flood was the *Julianenflut*, on 17 February 1164. In its wake, the [Jadebusen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jadebusen), (a bay on the coast of Germany), began to form. A storm tide in 1228 is recorded to have killed more than 100,000 people.[^cite_note-Morin-70] In 1362, the [Second Marcellus Flood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Marcellus'_flood), also known as the *Grote Manndrenke*, hit the entire southern coast of the North Sea. Chronicles of the time again record more than 100,000 deaths, large parts of the coast were lost permanently to the sea, including the now legendary [lost city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_city) of [Rungholt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rungholt).[^cite_note-Untergang-71] In the 20th century, the [North Sea flood of 1953](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Sea_flood_of_1953) flooded several nations' coasts and cost more than 2,000 lives.[^cite_note-72] 315 citizens of Hamburg died in the [North Sea flood of 1962](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Sea_flood_of_1962).[^cite_note-Lamb-73]<sup>: \[79, 86] </sup>

### Tsunamis

Though rare, the North Sea has been the site of a number of historically documented [tsunamis](31161.md). The [Storegga Slides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storegga_Slide) were a series of underwater landslides, in which a piece of the [Norwegian continental shelf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_continental_shelf) slid into the Norwegian Sea. The immense landslips occurred between 8150 BCE and 6000 BCE, and caused a tsunami up to 20 metres (66 ft) high that swept through the North Sea, having the greatest effect on Scotland and the [Faeroe Islands](17238607.md).[^cite_note-spiegel-74][^cite_note-75] The [Dover Straits earthquake of 1580](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1580_Dover_Straits_earthquake) is among the first recorded earthquakes in the North Sea measuring between 5.6 and 5.9 on the [Richter scale](19467352.md). This event caused extensive damage in [Calais](85468.md) both through its tremors and possibly triggered a tsunami, though this has never been confirmed. The theory is a vast underwater landslide in the [English Channel](9230.md) was triggered by the earthquake, which in turn caused a tsunami.[^cite_note-Belgium-76] The tsunami triggered by the [1755 Lisbon earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1755_Lisbon_earthquake) reached Holland, although the waves had lost their destructive power. The largest earthquake ever recorded in the United Kingdom was the [1931 Dogger Bank earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1931_Dogger_Bank_earthquake), which measured 6.1 on the [Richter magnitude scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richter_magnitude_scale) and caused a small tsunami that flooded parts of the British coast.[^cite_note-Belgium-76]

In 1995, the 25.6 m (84 ft) tall [Draupner wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Draupner_wave) occurred in the North Sea, becoming the first [rogue wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogue_wave) to be observed using scientific instruments.[^cite_note-TheWeek2-77]

## Geology

Main articles: [Geology of the North Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology_of_the_North_Sea) and [Geology of southern North Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology_of_southern_North_Sea)

Shallow [epicontinental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicontinental) seas like the current North Sea have since long existed on the European [continental shelf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_shelf). The [rifting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rift) that formed the northern part of the Atlantic Ocean during the [Jurassic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurassic) and [Cretaceous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cretaceous) periods, from about [150](https://geoltime.github.io/?Ma=150) million years ago, caused [tectonic uplift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tectonic_uplift) in the British Isles.[^cite_note-Ziegler-78] Since then, a shallow sea has almost continuously existed between the uplands of the [Fennoscandian Shield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fennoscandian_Shield) and the British Isles.[^cite_note-79] This precursor of the current North Sea has grown and shrunk with the rise and fall of the [eustatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eustacy) sea level during geologic time. Sometimes it was connected with other shallow seas, such as the sea above the [Paris Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seine) to the south-west, the [Paratethys Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paratethys) to the south-east, or the [Tethys Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tethys_Ocean) to the south.[^cite_note-Torsvik-80]

During the Late [Cretaceous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cretaceous) period, about [85](https://geoltime.github.io/?Ma=85) million years ago, all of modern mainland Europe except for Scandinavia was a scattering of islands.[^cite_note-Glennie-81] By the Early [Oligocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oligocene), [34 to 28](https://geoltime.github.io/?Ma=34%E2%80%9328) million years ago, the emergence of Western and Central Europe had almost completely separated the North Sea from the [Tethys Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tethys_Ocean), which gradually shrank to become the Mediterranean as Southern Europe and South West Asia became dry land.[^cite_note-82] The North Sea was cut off from the English Channel by a narrow [land bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_bridge) until that was breached by at least two catastrophic floods between 450,000 and 180,000 years ago.[^cite_note-83][^cite_note-84] Since the start of the [Quaternary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternary) period about [2.6](https://geoltime.github.io/?Ma=2.6) million years ago, the [eustatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eustatic) sea level has fallen during each glacial period and then risen again. Every time the [ice sheet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_sheet) reached its greatest extent, the North Sea became almost completely dry, the dry landmass being known as [Doggerland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doggerland), whose northern regions were themselves known to have been glaciated.[^cite_note-85] The present-day coastline formed after the [Last Glacial Maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_Glacial_Maximum) when the sea began to flood the European continental shelf.[^cite_note-Plant-86]

In 2006 a bone fragment was found while drilling for oil in the North Sea. Analysis indicated that it was a [Plateosaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plateosaurus) from 199 to 216 million years ago. This was the deepest dinosaur fossil ever found and the first find for Norway.[^cite_note-87]

## Nature

### Fish and shellfish

See also: [List of fish of the North Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fish_of_the_North_Sea)



[Pacific oysters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_oyster), [blue mussels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_mussel) and [cockles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cockle_\(bivalve\)) in the [Wadden Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wadden_Sea) in the Netherlands

[Copepods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copepod) and other [zooplankton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zooplankton) are plentiful in the North Sea. These tiny organisms are crucial elements of the [food chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_chain) supporting many species of fish.[^cite_note-Ecoserve-88] Over 230 species of [fish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish) live in the North Sea. [Cod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cod), [haddock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haddock), [whiting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whiting_\(fish\)), [saithe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saithe), [plaice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plaice), [sole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sole_\(fish\)), [mackerel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mackerel), [herring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herring), [pouting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pouting_\(fish\)), [sprat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sprat), and [sandeel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandeel) are all very common and are fished commercially.[^cite_note-Ecoserve-88][^cite_note-OSPAR-89] Due to the various depths of the North Sea trenches and differences in salinity, temperature, and water movement, some fish such as blue-mouth redfish and [rabbitfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabbitfish) reside only in small areas of the North Sea.[^cite_note-90]

[Crustaceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crustacean) are also commonly found throughout the sea. [Norway lobster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norway_lobster), [deep-water prawns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pandalus_borealis), and [brown shrimp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crangon_crangon) are all commercially fished, but other species of [lobster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lobster), [shrimp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caridea), [oyster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oyster), [mussels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mussel) and [clams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clam) all live in the North Sea.[^cite_note-Ecoserve-88] Recently non-indigenous species have become established including the [Pacific oyster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_oyster) and [Atlantic jackknife clam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_jackknife_clam).[^cite_note-OSPAR-89]

### Birds

The coasts of the North Sea are home to [nature reserves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_reserve) including the [Ythan Estuary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ythan_Estuary), [Fowlsheugh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fowlsheugh) Nature Preserve, and [Farne Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farne_Islands) in the UK and the [Wadden Sea National Parks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wadden_Sea_National_Parks) in Denmark, Germany and the Netherlands.[^cite_note-Ecoserve-88] These locations provide breeding [habitat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habitat_\(ecology\)) for dozens of bird species. Tens of millions of birds make use of the North Sea for breeding, feeding, or migratory stopovers every year. Populations of [black-legged kittiwakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black-legged_kittiwake), [Atlantic puffins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_puffin), [northern gannets](251885.md), [northern fulmars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_fulmar), and species of [petrels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrel), [seaducks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seaduck), [loons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loon) (divers), [cormorants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cormorant), [gulls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gull), [auks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auk), and [terns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tern), and many other seabirds make these coasts popular for [birdwatching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birdwatching).[^cite_note-Ecoserve-88][^cite_note-OSPAR-89]

### Marine mammals



A female [bottlenose dolphin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bottlenose_dolphin) with her young in [Moray Firth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moray_Firth), Scotland

The North Sea is also home to marine mammals. [Common seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_seal), [grey seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grey_seal), and [harbour porpoises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harbour_porpoise) can be found along the coasts, at marine installations, and on islands. The very northern North Sea islands such as the Shetland Islands are occasionally home to a larger variety of [pinnipeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinnipeds) including [bearded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bearded_seal), [harp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harp_seal), [hooded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hooded_seal) and [ringed seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ringed_seal), and even [walrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walrus).[^cite_note-91] North Sea [cetaceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cetaceans) include various [porpoise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porpoise), [dolphin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolphin) and [whale](33777.md) species.[^cite_note-OSPAR-89][^cite_note-92]

### Flora



[Phytoplankton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phytoplankton) bloom in the North Sea

Plant species in the North Sea include species of [wrack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrack_\(science\)), among them [bladder wrack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bladder_wrack), [knotted wrack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knotted_wrack), and serrated wrack. [Algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algae), macroalgal, and [kelp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelp), such as oarweed and laminaria hyperboria, and species of [maerl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maerl) are found as well.[^cite_note-OSPAR-89] [Eelgrass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zostera), formerly common in the entirety of the Wadden Sea, was nearly wiped out in the 20th century by a disease.[^cite_note-93] Similarly, [sea grass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seagrass) used to coat huge tracts of ocean floor, but have been damaged by trawling and dredging have diminished its habitat and prevented its return.[^cite_note-94] Invasive [Japanese seaweed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sargassum_muticum) has spread along the shores of the sea, clogging harbours and inlets and has become a nuisance.[^cite_note-95]

### Biodiversity and conservation

Due to the heavy human populations and high level of industrialization along its shores, the wildlife of the North Sea has suffered from pollution, overhunting, and overfishing. [Flamingos](165927.md) and [pelicans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelican) were once found along the southern shores of the North Sea, but became extinct over the second millennium.[^cite_note-96] Walruses frequented the Orkney Islands through the mid-16th century, as both Sable Island and Orkney Islands lay within their normal range.[^cite_note-97] [Grey whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gray_whale) also resided in the North Sea but were driven to extinction in the Atlantic in the 17th century[^cite_note-98] Other species have dramatically declined in population, though they are still found. [North Atlantic right whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Atlantic_right_whale), [sturgeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sturgeon), [shad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shad), [rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batoidea), [skates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skate_\(fish\)), [salmon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmon), and other species were common in the North Sea until the 20th century, when numbers declined due to [overfishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overfishing).[^cite_note-99][^cite_note-100]

Other factors like the introduction of [non-indigenous species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-indigenous_species), [industrial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_pollution) and [agricultural pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_pollution), [trawling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trawling) and [dredging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dredging), human-induced [eutrophication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eutrophication), construction on coastal breeding and feeding grounds, sand and gravel extraction, [offshore construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offshore_construction), and heavy shipping traffic have also contributed to the decline.[^cite_note-OSPAR-89] For example, a resident [orca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orca) pod was lost in the 1960s, presumably due to the peak in [PCB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polychlorinated_biphenyl) pollution in this time period.[^cite_note-101]

The OSPAR commission manages the [OSPAR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OSPAR) convention to counteract the harmful effects of human activity on wildlife in the North Sea, preserve [endangered species](20110668.md), and provide environmental protection.[^cite_note-102] All North Sea border states are signatories of the [MARPOL 73/78](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MARPOL_73/78) Accords, which preserve the marine environment by preventing pollution from ships.[^cite_note-103] Germany, Denmark, and the Netherlands also have a trilateral agreement for the protection of the [Wadden Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wadden_Sea), or [mudflats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mudflat), which run along the coasts of the three countries on the southern edge of the North Sea.[^cite_note-104]

## Names

The North Sea has had [various names](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_North_Sea#Naming) throughout history. One of the earliest recorded names was *Septentrionalis Oceanus*, or "Northern Ocean", which was cited by [Pliny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliny_the_Elder).[^cite_note-105] He also noted that the [Cimbri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cimbri) called it *Morimarusa* – "Dead Sea".[^cite_note-106] The name "North Sea" probably came into English, however, via the Dutch *Noordzee*, who named it thus either in contrast with the *[Zuiderzee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zuiderzee)* ("South Sea"), located south of [Frisia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frisia), or because the sea is generally to the north of the Netherlands. Before the adoption of "North Sea", the names used in English were "German Sea" or "German Ocean", referred to as the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) names *Mare Germanicum* and *Oceanus Germanicus*,[^cite_note-107] and these persisted in use until the First World War.[^cite_note-108] Other common names in use for long periods were the Latin terms *Mare Frisicum*, as well as the English equivalent, "Frisian Sea".[^cite_note-109][^cite_note-Looijenga-110] The modern names of the sea in the other local languages are: [Danish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_language): *Vesterhavet*, [lit.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literal_translation) 'West Sea' [\[ˈvestɐˌhɛˀvð̩\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Danish) or *Nordsøen* [\[ˈnoɐ̯ˌsøˀn̩\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Danish), [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_language): *Noordzee*, [Dutch Low Saxon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Low_Saxon_language): *Noordzee*, [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language): *Mer du Nord*, [West Frisian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Frisian_language): *Noardsee*, [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language): *Nordsee*, [Low German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_German_language): *Noordsee*, [North Frisian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Frisian_language): *Weestsiie*, [lit.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literal_translation) 'West Sea', [Swedish](26689.md): *Nordsjön*, [Norwegian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_language): *Nordsjøen*, [Scots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scots_language): *North Sea* and [Scottish Gaelic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Gaelic_language): *An Cuan a Tuath*.

## History

Main article: [History of the North Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_North_Sea)

### Early history

The North Sea has provided waterway access for commerce and conquest. Many areas have access to the North Sea because of its long coastline and the European rivers that empty it.[^cite_note-Britannica-1] There is little documentary evidence concerning the North Sea before the [Roman conquest of Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_conquest_of_Britain) in 43 CE, however, archaeological evidence reveals the diffusion of cultures and technologies from across or along the North Sea to Great Britain and Scandinavia and reliance by some prehistoric cultures on fishing, whaling, and seaborne trade on the North Sea. The Romans established organised ports in Britain, which increased shipping and began sustained trade[^cite_note-Cuyvers-111] and many Scandinavian tribes participated in raids and wars against the Romans and Roman coinage and manufacturing were important trade goods. When the Romans [abandoned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End_of_Roman_rule_in_Britain) Britain in 410, the Germanic [Angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angles_\(tribe\)), [Frisians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frisians), [Saxons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saxons), and [Jutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jutes) began the next great migration across the North Sea during the [Migration Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Migration_Period). They made successive invasions of the island from what is now the Netherlands, Denmark, and Germany.[^cite_note-112]

The [Viking Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viking_Age) began in 793 with the attack on [Lindisfarne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindisfarne); for the next 250 years, the Vikings ruled the North Sea. In their superior [longships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longship), they raided, traded, and established colonies and outposts along the coasts of the sea. From the Middle Ages through the 15th century, the [northern European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Europe) coastal ports exported domestic goods, dyes, linen, salt, metal goods and wine. The Scandinavian and Baltic areas shipped grain, fish, naval necessities, and timber. In turn, the North Sea countries imported high-grade cloths, spices, and fruits from the Mediterranean region.[^cite_note-BrtishIsles-113] Commerce during this era was mainly conducted by maritime trade due to underdeveloped roadways.[^cite_note-BrtishIsles-113]

In the 13th century the [Hanseatic League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanseatic_League), though centred on the [Baltic Sea](3335.md), started to control most of the trade through important members and outposts on the North Sea.[^cite_note-EuropeanNaval-114] The League lost its dominance in the 16th century, as neighbouring states took control of former [Hanseatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_route#Hanseatic_trade) cities and outposts. Their internal conflict prevented effective cooperation and defence.[^cite_note-Hansen-115] As the League lost control of its maritime cities, new [trade routes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_route) emerged that provided Europe with Asian, American, and African goods.[^cite_note-116][^cite_note-Ripley-117]

### Age of sail



Painting of the [Four Days' Battle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Days'_Battle) of 1666 by [Willem van de Velde the Younger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willem_van_de_Velde_the_Younger)

The 17th century [Dutch Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Golden_Age) saw Dutch maritime power at its zenith.[^cite_note-118][^cite_note-PowerPlenty-119] Important overseas colonies, a vast merchant marine, a large fishing fleet,[^cite_note-BrtishIsles-113] powerful navy, and sophisticated financial markets made the Dutch the ascendant power in the North Sea, to be challenged by an ambitious England. This rivalry led to the first three [Anglo-Dutch Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Dutch_Wars) between 1652 and 1673, which ended with Dutch victories.[^cite_note-PowerPlenty-119] After the [Glorious Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glorious_Revolution) in 1688, the Dutch prince [William](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_III_of_England) ascended to the English throne. With unified leadership, commercial, military, and political power began to shift from Amsterdam to London.[^cite_note-MacDonald-120] The British did not face a challenge to their dominance of the North Sea until the 20th century.[^cite_note-121]

### Modern era



German cruiser [SMS *Blücher*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS_Bl%C3%BCcher) sinks in the [Battle of Dogger Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dogger_Bank_\(1915\)) on 25 January 1915.

Tensions in the North Sea were again heightened in 1904 by the [Dogger Bank incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dogger_Bank_incident). During the [Russo-Japanese War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Japanese_War), several ships of the Russian Baltic Fleet, which was on its way to the Far East, mistook British fishing boats for Japanese ships and fired on them, and then upon each other, near the Dogger Bank, nearly causing Britain to enter the war on the side of Japan.

During the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War), Great Britain's [Grand Fleet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Fleet) and Germany's [Kaiserliche Marine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaiserliche_Marine) faced each other in the North Sea,[^cite_note-Halpern-122] which became the main [theatre of the war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_warfare_of_World_War_I#Theaters) for surface action.[^cite_note-Halpern-122] Britain's larger fleet and [North Sea Mine Barrage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Sea_Mine_Barrage) were able to establish an effective blockade for most of the war, which restricted the [Central Powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Powers)' access to many crucial resources.[^cite_note-Tucker-123] Major battles included the [Battle of Heligoland Bight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Heligoland_Bight_\(1914\)),[^cite_note-Osborne-124] the [Battle of the Dogger Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dogger_Bank_\(1915\)),[^cite_note-navies-125] and the [Battle of Jutland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Jutland).[^cite_note-navies-125] World War I also brought the first extensive use of [submarine warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_warfare), and a number of submarine actions occurred in the North Sea.[^cite_note-126]

The [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War) also saw action in the North Sea, though it was restricted more to aircraft reconnaissance and action by fighter/bomber aircraft, submarines and smaller vessels such as [minesweepers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minesweeper_\(ship\)) and [torpedo boats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torpedo_boat).[^cite_note-Frank-127][^cite_note-128]

After the war, hundreds of thousands of tons of chemical weapons were disposed of by being dumped in the North Sea.[^cite_note-Kaffka-129]

After the war, the North Sea lost much of its military significance because it is bordered only by [NATO](21133.md) member-states. However, it gained significant economic importance in the 1960s as the states around the North Sea began full-scale exploitation of its [oil and gas resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Sea_oil).[^cite_note-130] The North Sea continues to be an active trade route.[^cite_note-131]

## Economy



The [exclusive economic zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exclusive_economic_zone) in the North Sea

### Political status

Countries that border the North Sea all claim the 12 nautical miles (22 km; 14 mi) of [territorial waters](496983.md), within which they have exclusive fishing rights.[^cite_note-Barry-132] The [Common Fisheries Policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Fisheries_Policy) of the [European Union](9317.md) (EU) exists to coordinate fishing rights and assist with disputes between EU states and the EU border state of Norway.[^cite_note-133]

After the discovery of mineral resources in the North Sea during the early 1960s, the [Convention on the Continental Shelf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convention_on_the_Continental_Shelf) established country rights largely divided along the median line. The median line is defined as the line "every point of which is equidistant from the nearest points of the baselines from which the breadth of the territorial sea of each State is measured".[^cite_note-134] The ocean floor border between Germany, the Netherlands, and Denmark was only reapportioned in 1969 after protracted negotiations and a judgment of the [International Court of Justice](14918.md).[^cite_note-Barry-132][^cite_note-135]

### Oil and gas

Further information: [North Sea oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Sea_oil) and [List of oil and gas fields of the North Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_oil_and_gas_fields_of_the_North_Sea)

As early as 1859, oil was discovered in onshore areas around the North Sea and [natural gas](22131.md) as early as 1910.[^cite_note-Glennie-81] Onshore resources, for example the [K12-B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K12-B) field in the Netherlands continue to be exploited today.



Oil platform [Statfjord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statfjord_oil_field) A with the [flotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flotel) Polymarine

Offshore test drilling began in 1966 and then, in 1969, [Phillips Petroleum Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phillips_Petroleum_Company) discovered the [Ekofisk oil field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ekofisk_oil_field)[^cite_note-Ekofisk-136] distinguished by valuable, low-sulphur oil.[^cite_note-Lohne-137] Commercial exploitation began in 1971 with [tankers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_tanker) and, after 1975, by a [pipeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipeline_transport), first to [Teesside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teesside), England and then, after 1977, also to [Emden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emden), Germany.[^cite_note-138]

The exploitation of the North Sea [oil reserves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_reserves) began just before the [1973 oil crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_oil_crisis), and the climb of international oil prices made the large investments needed for extraction much more attractive.[^cite_note-139] The start in 1973 of the oil reserves by the UK allowed them to stop the declining position in international trade in 1974, and a huge increase after the discovery and exploitation of the huge oil field by Phillips group in 1977 as the [Brae field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brae_field).

Although the production costs are relatively high, the quality of the oil, the political stability of the region, and the proximity of important markets in [western Europe](33800.md) have made the North Sea an important oil-producing region.[^cite_note-Lohne-137] The largest single humanitarian catastrophe in the North Sea [oil industry](994887.md) was the destruction of the offshore [oil platform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_platform) [Piper Alpha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piper_Alpha) in 1988 in which 167 people lost their lives.[^cite_note-140]

Besides the Ekofisk oil field, the [Statfjord oil field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statfjord_oil_field) is also notable as it was the cause of the first pipeline to span the [Norwegian trench](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_trench).[^cite_note-141] The largest [natural gas field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas_field) in the North Sea, [Troll gas field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troll_gas_field), lies in the Norwegian trench, dropping over 300 metres (980 ft), requiring the construction of the enormous [Troll A platform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troll_A_platform) to access it.

The price of [Brent Crude](1150532.md), one of the first types of oil extracted from the North Sea is used today as a standard price for comparison for [crude oil](23195.md) from the rest of the world.[^cite_note-142] The North Sea contains western Europe's largest oil and natural gas reserves and is one of the world's key non-OPEC producing regions.[^cite_note-143]

In the UK sector of the North Sea, the oil industry invested £14.4 billion in 2013 and was on track to spend £13 billion in 2014. Industry body [Oil & Gas UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_&_Gas_UK) put the decline down to rising costs, lower production, high tax rates, and less exploration.[^cite_note-ShellUK-144]

In January 2018, The North Sea region contained 184 offshore rigs, which made it the region with the highest number of offshore rigs in the world at the time.[^cite_note-145]\


The British North Sea's oil and gas production, peaking in 2000, had declined by 2024, while offshore wind projects like Dogger Bank grew to support renewable energy goals, with windfall taxes funding the transition.[^cite_note-146]

### Fishing

Main article: [Fishing in the North Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fishing_in_the_North_Sea)



A trawler in [Nordstrand, Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nordstrand,_Germany)

The North Sea is Europe's main fishery accounting for over 5% of international commercial fish caught.[^cite_note-Britannica-1] Fishing in the North Sea is concentrated in the southern part of the coastal waters. The main method of fishing is [trawling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trawling).[^cite_note-147] In 1995, the total volume of fish and shellfish caught in the North Sea was approximately 3.5 million tonnes.[^cite_note-148] Besides saleable fish, it is estimated that one million tonnes of unmarketable [by-catch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/By-catch) is caught and discarded to die each year.[^cite_note-149]

In recent decades, [overfishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overfishing) has left many fisheries unproductive, disturbing marine [food chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_chain) dynamics and costing jobs in the [fishing industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fishing_industry).[^cite_note-150] Herring, cod and plaice fisheries may soon face the same plight as mackerel fishing, which ceased in the 1970s due to overfishing.[^cite_note-151] The objective of the European Union [Common Fisheries Policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Fisheries_Policy) is to minimize the environmental impact associated with resource use by reducing fish discards, increasing the productivity of fisheries, stabilising markets of fisheries and fish processing, and supplying fish at reasonable prices for the consumer.[^cite_note-152]

### Whaling

Whaling was an important economic activity from the 9th until the 13th century for Flemish whalers.[^cite_note-auto-153] The medieval Flemish, Basque and Norwegian whalers who were replaced in the 16th century by Dutch, English, Danes, and Germans, took massive numbers of whales and dolphins and nearly depleted the right whales. This activity likely led to the extinction of the Atlantic population of the once common [grey whale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grey_whale).[^cite_note-154] By 1902 the whaling had ended.[^cite_note-auto-153] After being absent for 300 years a single grey whale returned in 2010,[^cite_note-155] it probably was the first of many more to find its way through the now ice-free [Northwest Passage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Passage).

### Mineral resources



Unpolished [amber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amber) stones, in varying hues

In addition to oil, gas, and fish, the states along the North Sea also take millions of cubic metres per year of [sand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sand) and [gravel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravel) from the ocean floor. These are used for [beach nourishment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beach_nourishment), [land reclamation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_reclamation) and construction.[^cite_note-Phua-156] Rolled pieces of [amber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amber) may be picked up on the east coast of England.[^cite_note-Rice-157]

### Renewable energy

Further information: [Renewable energy in the European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renewable_energy_in_the_European_Union) and [List of offshore wind farms in the North Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_offshore_wind_farms_in_the_North_Sea)

Due to the strong [prevailing winds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prevailing_winds), and shallow water, countries on the North Sea, particularly Germany and Denmark, have used the shore for [wind power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_power) since the 1990s.[^cite_note-158] The North Sea is the home of one of the first large-scale [offshore wind farms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offshore_wind_farm) in the world, [Horns Rev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horns_Rev) 1, completed in 2002. Since then many other [wind farms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_farm) have been commissioned in the North Sea (and elsewhere). As of 2013, the 630 [megawatt](21347693.md) (MW) [London Array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Array) is the largest offshore wind farm in the world, with the 504 (MW) [Greater Gabbard wind farm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Gabbard_wind_farm) the second largest, followed by the 367 MW [Walney Wind Farm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walney_Wind_Farm). All are off the coast of the UK. These projects will be dwarfed by subsequent wind farms that are in the pipeline, including [Dogger Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dogger_Bank_Wind_Farm) at 4,800 MW, Norfolk Bank (7,200 MW), and [Irish Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Sea) (4,200 MW). At the end of June 2013 total European combined offshore wind energy capacity was 6,040 MW. The UK installed 513.5 MW of offshore wind power in the first half-year of 2013.[^cite_note-EWEA2013-159] The development of the offshore wind industry in UK-controlled areas of the North Sea is traced to three phases: coastal, off-coastal and deep offshore in the period 2004 – 2021.[^cite_note-160]

The expansion of offshore wind farms has met with some resistance. Concerns have included shipping collisions[^cite_note-161] and [environmental effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_effects_of_wind_power#Offshore) on ocean ecology and wildlife such as fish and migratory birds,[^cite_note-162] however, these concerns were found to be negligible in a long-term study in Denmark released in 2006 and again in a UK government study in 2009.[^cite_note-163][^cite_note-164] There are also concerns about reliability,[^cite_note-165] and the rising costs of constructing and maintaining offshore wind farms.[^cite_note-166] Despite these, development of North Sea wind power is continuing, with plans for additional wind farms off the coasts of Germany, the Netherlands, and the UK.[^cite_note-167] There have also been proposals for a [transnational power grid in the North Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Sea_Offshore_Grid)[^cite_note-168][^cite_note-169] to connect [new offshore wind farms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Sea_Wind_Power_Hub).[^cite_note-hub-170]

Energy production from [tidal power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_power) is still in a pre-commercial stage. The [European Marine Energy Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Marine_Energy_Centre) has installed a wave testing system at Billia Croo on the Orkney mainland[^cite_note-171] and a tidal power testing station on the nearby island of [Eday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eday).[^cite_note-172] Since 2003, a prototype [Wave Dragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_Dragon) energy converter has been in operation at Nissum Bredning fjord of northern Denmark.[^cite_note-173]

### Tourism



The beach in [Scheveningen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheveningen), Netherlands c. 1900

The beaches and coastal waters of the North Sea are destinations for tourists. The English, Belgian, Dutch, German and Danish coasts[^cite_note-174][^cite_note-Hall-175] are developed for tourism. The North Sea coast of the United Kingdom has tourist destinations with beach resorts and [links](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Links_\(golf\)) golf courses; the coastal town of [St. Andrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Andrews) in Scotland is renowned as the home of [golf](19568112.md), and is a popular location among golfing pilgrims.[^cite_note-176]

The [North Sea Trail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Sea_Trail) is a [long-distance trail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long-distance_trail) linking seven countries around the North Sea.[^cite_note-177] Windsurfing and sailing[^cite_note-178] are popular sports because of the strong winds. [Mudflat hiking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mudflat_hiking),[^cite_note-179] [recreational fishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fishing) and birdwatching[^cite_note-Hall-175] are among other activities.

The climatic conditions on the North Sea coast have been claimed to be healthy. As early as the 19th century, travellers visited the North Sea coast for curative and restorative vacations. The sea air, temperature, wind, water, and sunshine are counted among the beneficial conditions that are said to activate the body's defences, improve circulation, strengthen the immune system, and have healing effects on the skin and the respiratory system.[^cite_note-180]

The [Wadden Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wadden_Sea) in Denmark, Germany and the Netherlands is an [UNESCO World Heritage Site](44940.md).

### Marine traffic

See also: [List of North Sea ports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_North_Sea_ports)



[Rotterdam](26049.md), Netherlands

The North Sea is important for marine transport and its shipping lanes are among the busiest in the world.[^cite_note-Barry-132] Major ports are located along its coasts: [Rotterdam](26049.md), the busiest port in Europe and the [fourth busiest port in the world by tonnage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_busiest_ports_by_cargo_tonnage) as of 2013, [Antwerp](32149462.md) (was 16th) and [Hamburg](13467.md) (was 27th), [Bremen](9420388.md)/[Bremerhaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bremerhaven) and [Felixstowe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Felixstowe), both in the top 30 busiest [container seaports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_busiest_container_ports),[^cite_note-181] as well as the [Port of Bruges-Zeebrugge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Zeebrugge), Europe's leading [ro-ro](1457484.md) port.[^cite_note-182]

Fishing boats, service boats for offshore industries, sport and pleasure craft, and merchant ships to and from [North Sea ports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_North_Sea_ports) and [Baltic ports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ports_of_the_Baltic_Sea) must share routes on the North Sea. The Dover Strait alone sees more than 400 commercial vessels a day.[^cite_note-183] Because of this volume, navigation in the North Sea can be difficult in high traffic zones, so ports have established elaborate [vessel traffic services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vessel_traffic_service) to monitor and direct ships into and out of port.[^cite_note-Freestone-184]

The North Sea coasts are home to numerous canals and canal systems to facilitate traffic between and among rivers, artificial harbours, and the sea. The [Kiel Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiel_Canal), connecting the North Sea with the Baltic Sea, is the most heavily used artificial seaway in the world reporting an average of 89 ships per day not including sporting boats and other small watercraft in 2009.[^cite_note-Kiel-185] It saves an average of 250 nautical miles (460 km; 290 mi), instead of the voyage around the [Jutland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jutland) peninsula.[^cite_note-Kiel2-186] The [North Sea Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Sea_Canal) connects [Amsterdam](844.md) with the North Sea.

## Critical Maritime Infrastructure

The North Sea contains a dense concentration of **critical maritime infrastructure** (CMI). Including offshore and subsea assets that support energy supply, digital communications, and maritime transportation.[^cite_note-BuegerLiebetrau2023-187] In terms of research and policy, "critical" generally refers to infrastructure whose disruption could have significant societal and economic effects and therefor attracts heightened attention in risk management and protection planning[^cite_note-BuegerLiebetrau2023-187] Which maritime assets are treated as "critical" can vary across sectors and jurisdictions, as designation is partly are policy and administrative choice.[^cite_note-BuegerLiebetrau2023-187]

The discovery of the North Sea's physical characteristics has facilitated extensive offshore development. Compared with deep water oceans, the North Sea is relatively shallow, which supports large-scale offshore oil and gas activity.[^cite_note-Neal2025-188] Recently there has also been the expansion of offshore wind and associated cable networks adding to the vastness of critical maritime infrastructure in the North Sea.[^cite_note-Neal2025-188] Alongside fixed offshore installations, the North Sea seabed hosts networks of pipelines, electricity cables, and fibre-optic communications cables in a maritime area with high levels of port activity and shipping traffic.[^cite_note-Neal2025-188] One regional assessment estimates that the Nort Sea hosts around 71,000 km of active subsea cables (about 5% of the global network), in addition to extensive pipelines and other offshore systems.[^cite_note-Neal2025-188]

### Infrastructure

Critical maritime infrastructure in the North Sea includes offshore and subsea assets linked with energy production, transmission, and communications.[^cite_note-BuegerLiebetrau2023-187][^cite_note-Neal2025-188] These infrastructures are often interdependent as offshore production relies on export connections to shore, while maintenance and emergency response depend on ports, specialized vessels, and onshore support basses.[^cite_note-Neal2025-188]

The infrastructure landscape has been described as changing alongside decarbonization and investment policies. Oil and gas activity continues in parts of the North Sea, while late-life operations and decommissioning increase the need for monitoring and seabed management over a longer period of time.[^cite_note-Neal2025-188] At the same time, offshore windfarms have expanded, increasing the number of installations at sea and the complexity of subsea power connections.[^cite_note-BuegerLiebetrau2023-187][^cite_note-Neal2025-188] Additional decarbonization-related developments include producing hydrogen offshore and using depleted oil and gas fields to store CO2 underground.[^cite_note-Neal2025-188] These activities can require new or upgraded infrastructure such as offshore facilities, pipelines, and monitoring systems, which could increase reliance on these assets.

#### Offshore oil and gas installations

Offshore oil and gas infrastructure are complex installation that is essential for economic production in the North Sea. These installations include fixed and floating platforms as well as processing facilities, drilling units and associated subsea systems.[^cite_note-Neal2025-188] Some installations function as hubs that gather hydrocarbons from multiple fields via subsea connection before exports to shore, supported by logistic chains.[^cite_note-Neal2025-188]

#### Subsea Pipelines

Subsea pipelines transport oil and gas from offshore fields to onshore terminals and processing plants, and can also connect offshore hubs and nearby fields.[^cite_note-Neal2025-188] Pipeline systems typically include trunk lines and infield flowlines as well as supporting equipment such as valves and monitoring and control systems.[^cite_note-Neal2025-188] Existing pipeline corridors may also be discussed in connection with infrastructure, including potential reuse or adaptation for new purposes.[^cite_note-Neal2025-188]

#### Offshore wind farms

Offshore wind developments typically include turbine arrays, offshore substation, and export cables that transmit electricity to shore.[^cite_note-Neal2025-188] As projects scale up, associated electrical infrastructure can become more complex, including multiple export connections, onshore reinforcements, and links that support cross-border electricity integration.[^cite_note-Neal2025-188] Construction and maintenance depend on specialised vessels and ports. Operating wind farms requires continuous monitoring, scheduled servicing and repair capacity to manage downtime.[^cite_note-Neal2025-188]

### Vulnerabilities and protection

CMI protection is complicated by the distribution of assets across large sea areas and the location of many components on or beneath the seabed.[^cite_note-BuegerLiebetrau2023-187] Reviews of CMI protection discuss a wide spectrum of risks such as accidents and technical failures, criminal activity and deliberate interference.[^cite_note-BuegerLiebetrau2023-187] Planning often spans multiple policy fields such as marine safety, maritime security, and cybersecurity.[^cite_note-BuegerLiebetrau2023-187] Persistent monitoring is difficult due to limited visibility below the surface and the scale and technical challenges of surveillance in the maritime domain.[^cite_note-BuegerLiebetrau2023-187][^cite_note-Neal2025-188]

Accidental damage is commonly discussed in relation to dense maritime use and seabed activity. Mapping of cables and pipelines can support safe navigation and reduce risk from anchoring and fishing, but subsea infrastructure mapping may be incomplete or imprecise and can be distributed across multiple databases with limited standardization.[^cite_note-Neal2025-188] Environmental and operational conditions can affect integrity and repair capacity; climate-related factors such as increasing storm intensity are discussed as part of the wider risk environment for offshore assets.[^cite_note-Neal2025-188]

Deliberate disruption is frequently discussed with references to subsea cables and pipelines. It is noted that underwater supply lines are difficult to protect effectively on the sea floor and identifies submarines data and power cables as particularly vulnerable.[^cite_note-Schaller2024-189] The same analysis shows that cable damage can be carried out "hidden in common marine traffic" and describes scenarios where relatively common maritime equipment can damage cables if their location is known.[^cite_note-Schaller2024-189]

Cybersecurity is also treated as part of CMI protection since offshore installations and electricity networks increasingly rely on digital control and monitoring systems.[^cite_note-Neal2025-188] It is noted that vulnerabilities associated with the digitalisation of maritime energy infrastructure have been reported hacking incidents affecting European wind farms and industrial control environments.[^cite_note-Neal2025-188] More general critical infrastructure protection is framed as risk management in terms of identifying threats and vulnerabilities while evaluating likelihood and consequences, particularly for networked infrastructures where disruption can have cascading effects.[^cite_note-Yusta2011-190]

Protection and resilience measures include surveillance and maritime domain awareness as well as redundancy and resilience panning, and improved repair and recovery capacity.[^cite_note-BuegerLiebetrau2023-187][^cite_note-Neal2025-188] Subsea monitoring is technically channelling and resources-intensive needing vast monitoring capabilities as well as coordination between public authorities and private operators.[^cite_note-Neal2025-188] It is also important to integrate diverse policy fields and strengthening public-private coordination, alongside investment in repair capacity and protective measures for maritime space.[^cite_note-BuegerLiebetrau2023-187]

International law and jurisdiction are also relevant to CMI protection, as many assets are located beyond territorial waters. It is noted that unauthorised mapping of infrastructure in another states territorial waters is not compatible with innocent passage.[^cite_note-Schaller2024-189] While in EEZ, the law of the sea does not provide coastal states with clear authority to prevent foreign vessels from collecting intelligence on maritime infrastructure, creating legal constraints for some preventative measures.[^cite_note-Schaller2024-189]
