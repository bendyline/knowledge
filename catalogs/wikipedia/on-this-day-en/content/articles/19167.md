A **manuscript** was, traditionally, any document written by hand or [typewritten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typewritten), as opposed to mechanically [printed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing) or reproduced in an indirect or automated way.[^cite_note-1] More recently, the term has come to be understood to further include *any* written, typed, or word-processed copy of an author's work, as opposed to the printed version of the same thing.[^cite_note-2]

Before the arrival of prints, all documents and books were manuscripts. Manuscripts are not defined by their contents, which may combine writing with mathematical calculations, maps, [music notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_notation), explanatory figures, or [illustrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illustration).

## Terminology



Manuscript, [Codex Manesse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codex_Manesse). Most manuscripts were ruled with horizontal lines that served as the baselines on which the text was entered.

First page of [Satie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erik_Satie)'s *[Sports et divertissements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sports_et_divertissements)* (published as a facsimile in 1923)

The word "manuscript" derives from the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language): *manūscriptum* (from *manus*, [hand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hand) and *scriptum* from *scribere*, to [write](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Write)), and is first recorded in English in 1597.[^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4] An earlier term in English that shares the meaning of a handwritten document is "hand-writ" (or "handwrit"), which is first attested around 1175 and is now rarely used.[^cite_note-5] The study of the writing (the "hand") in surviving manuscripts is termed [palaeography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palaeography) (or paleography). The traditional abbreviations are **MS** for manuscript and **MSS** for manuscripts,[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7] while the forms **MS.**, **ms** or **ms.** for singular, and **MSS.**, **mss** or **mss.** for plural (with or without the full stop, all uppercase or all lowercase) are also accepted.[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11] The second *s* is not simply the plural; by an old convention, a doubling of the last letter of the abbreviation expresses the plural, just as *pp.* means "pages".

A manuscript may be a [codex](5691.md) (i.e. [bound as a book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bookbinding)), a [scroll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scroll_\(parchment\)), or bound differently or consist of loose pages. [Illuminated manuscripts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illuminated_manuscript) are enriched with pictures, border decorations, elaborately embossed initial letters or full-page illustrations.

## History



The Isha [Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upanishad) manuscript



Gharib al-Hadith, by [Abu 'Ubaid al-Qasim ibn Sallam al-Harawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Ubaid_al-Qasim_bin_Salam) (d. 837 AD). The oldest known dated Arabic manuscript on paper in [Leiden University Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leiden_University_Library), dated 319 [AH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hijri_year) (931 AD)



A 14th-century [Armenian manuscript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_manuscript), with painting by [Sargis Pitsak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sargis_Pitsak). The first page of the [Gospel of Mark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_Mark). Cod. 2627, fol. 436 r. ([Matenadaran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matenadaran))

Before the inventions of printing, in China by [woodblock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodblock_printing) and in Europe by [movable type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Movable_type) in a [printing press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing_press), all written documents had to be both produced and reproduced by hand. In the west, manuscripts were produced in form of scrolls (*volumen* in Latin) or books (*codex*, plural *codices*). Manuscripts were produced on [vellum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vellum) and other parchment, on [papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papyrus), and on paper.

In [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent) and [Southeast Asia](28741.md), [palm leaf manuscripts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palm_leaf_manuscript), with a distinctive long rectangular shape, were used dating back to the 5th century BCE[^cite_note-12] or earlier, and in some cases continued to be used until the 19th century. In China, [bamboo and wooden slips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bamboo_and_wooden_slips) were used prior to the [introduction of paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_paper). In Russia, [birch bark documents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birch_bark_document) as old as from the 11th century have survived.

[Paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper) spread from China via the Islamic world to [Europe](9239.md) by the 14th century, and by the late 15th century had largely replaced parchment for many purposes there. When Greek or Latin works were published, numerous professional copies were sometimes made simultaneously by scribes in a [scriptorium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scriptorium), each making a single copy from an original that was declaimed aloud.

The oldest written manuscripts have been preserved by the perfect dryness of their Middle Eastern resting places, whether placed within [sarcophagi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarcophagus) in Egyptian tombs, or reused as [mummy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mummy)-wrappings, discarded in the [middens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midden) of [Oxyrhynchus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxyrhynchus) or secreted for safe-keeping in jars and buried ([Nag Hammadi library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nag_Hammadi_library)) or stored in dry caves ([Dead Sea Scrolls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_Sea_Scrolls)). Volcanic ash preserved some of the Roman library of the [Villa of the Papyri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villa_of_the_Papyri) in [Herculaneum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herculaneum). Manuscripts in [Tocharian languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tocharian_languages), written on palm leaves, survived in desert burials in the [Tarim Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarim_Basin) of Central Asia.

Ironically, the manuscripts that were being most carefully preserved in the libraries of [antiquity](252905.md) are virtually all lost. Papyrus has a life of at most a century or two in relatively humid Italian or Greek conditions; only those works copied onto parchment, usually after the general conversion to Christianity, have survived, and by no means all of those.

Originally, all books were in manuscript form. In China, and later other parts of East Asia, [woodblock printing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodblock_printing) was used for books from about the 7th century. The earliest dated example is the [Diamond Sutra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Sutra) of 868. In the Islamic world and the West, all books were in manuscript until the introduction of movable type printing in about 1450. Manuscript copying of books continued for a least a century, as printing remained expensive. Private or government documents remained hand-written until the invention of the typewriter in the late 19th century. Because of the likelihood of errors being introduced each time a manuscript was copied, the [filiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stemmatics) of different versions of the same text is a fundamental part of the study and criticism of all texts that have been transmitted in manuscript.

In [Southeast Asia](28741.md), in the first millennium, documents of sufficiently great importance were inscribed on soft metallic sheets such as [copperplate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intaglio_printing), softened by refiner's fire and inscribed with a metal stylus. In the [Philippines](23440.md), for example, as early as 900 AD, specimen documents were not inscribed by stylus, but were punched much like the style of today's [dot-matrix printers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dot-matrix_printer). This type of document was rare compared to the usual leaves and bamboo staves that were inscribed. However, neither the leaves nor paper were as durable as the metal document in the hot, humid climate. In [Burma](19457.md), the kammavaca, Buddhist manuscripts, were inscribed on brass, copper or ivory sheets, and even on discarded monk robes folded and lacquered. In [Italy](14532.md) some important [Etruscan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etruria) texts were similarly inscribed on thin gold plates: similar sheets have been discovered in [Bulgaria](3415.md). Technically, these are all inscriptions rather than manuscripts.

In the Western world, from the [classical period](252905.md) through the early centuries of the [Christian era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_era), manuscripts were written without spaces between the words ([scriptio continua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scriptio_continua)), which makes them especially hard for the untrained to read. Extant copies of these early manuscripts written in [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) or [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) and usually dating from the 4th century to the 8th century, are classified according to their use of either all [upper case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majuscule) or all [lower case letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_case). [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language) manuscripts, such as the [Dead Sea scrolls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_Sea_scrolls) make no such differentiation. Manuscripts using all upper case letters are called [majuscule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majuscule), those using all lower case are called [minuscule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_case). Usually, the majuscule scripts such as [uncial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncial) are written with much more care. The scribe lifted his pen between each stroke, producing an unmistakable effect of regularity and formality. On the other hand, while minuscule scripts can be written with pen-lift, they may also be [cursive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cursive), that is, use little or no pen-lift.

## Islamic world

Main article: [Islamic Manuscripts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Manuscripts)

[Islamic manuscripts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_manuscripts) were produced in different ways depending on their use and time period. [Parchment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parchment) (vellum) was a common way to produce manuscripts.[^cite_note-13] Manuscripts eventually transitioned to using paper in later centuries with the diffusion of paper making in the Islamic empire. When Muslims encountered paper in Central Asia, its use and production spread to Iran, Iraq, Syria, Egypt, and North Africa during the 8th century.[^cite_note-14]

## Africa

Main article: [Timbuktu Manuscripts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timbuktu_Manuscripts)

See also: [West African manuscripts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_African_manuscripts)

4,203 of [Timbuktu's manuscripts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timbuktu_Manuscripts) were burned or stolen during the armed [conflict in Mali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conflict_in_Mali) between 2012 and 2013. 90% of these manuscripts were saved by the population organized around the NGO "Sauvegarde et valorisation des manuscrits pour la défense de la culture islamique" (SAVAMA-DCI).[^cite_note-\:0-15] Some 350,000 manuscripts were transported to safety, and 300,000 of them were still in [Bamako](56606.md) in 2022.[^cite_note-\:1-16][^cite_note-17] An international consultation on the safeguarding, accessibility and promotion of ancient manuscripts in the [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel) was held at the [UNESCO](21786641.md) office in Bamako in 2020.[^cite_note-\:0-15][^cite_note-\:1-16]

## Western world



After plummeting in the [Early Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Middle_Ages), the [high](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Middle_Ages) and [late medieval period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Middle_Ages) witnessed a sharp increase of manuscript production.

[^cite_note-18]



[Lectionary 183](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lectionary_183)

Most surviving pre-modern manuscripts use the codex format (as in a modern book), which had replaced the scroll by [Late Antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Antiquity). [Parchment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parchment) or [vellum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vellum), as the best type of parchment is known, had also replaced [papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papyrus), which was not nearly so long lived and has survived to the present almost exclusively in the very dry climate of [Egypt](8087628.md),[^cite_note-19] although it was widely used across the Roman world. Parchment is made of animal skin, normally calf, sheep, or goat, but also other animals. With all skins, the quality of the finished product is based on how much preparation and skill was put into turning the skin into parchment. Parchment made from calf or sheep was the most common in Northern Europe, while civilizations in Southern Europe preferred goatskin.[^cite_note-Intro-20] Often, if the parchment is white or cream in color and veins from the animal can still be seen, it is calfskin. If it is yellow, greasy or in some cases shiny, then it was made from sheepskin.[^cite_note-Intro-20]

Vellum comes from the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) word vitulinum which means "of calf"/ "made from calf". For modern parchment makers and calligraphers, and apparently often in the past, the terms parchment and vellum are used based on the different degrees of quality, preparation and thickness, and not according to which animal the skin came from, and because of this, the more neutral term "membrane" is often used by modern academics, especially where the animal has not been established by testing.[^cite_note-Intro-20]

### Scripts

[Merovingian script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merovingian_script), or "Luxeuil minuscule", is named after an abbey in Western France, the [Luxeuil Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbey_of_Luxeuil), founded by the Irish missionary St [Columba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columba) c. 590.[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-22] [Caroline minuscule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caroline_minuscule) is a [calligraphic script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calligraphy) developed as a writing standard in [Europe](9239.md) so that the [Latin alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_alphabet) could be easily recognized by the literate class from different regions. It was used in the [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire) between approximately 800 and 1200. Codices, classical and Christian texts, and educational material were written in Carolingian minuscule throughout the [Carolingian Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carolingian_Renaissance). The script developed into blackletter and became obsolete, though its revival in the Italian renaissance forms the basis of more recent scripts.[^cite_note-Intro-20] In *Introduction to Manuscript Studies*, Clemens and Graham associate the beginning of this text coming from the Abby of Saint-Martin at [Tours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tours).[^cite_note-Intro-20]

Caroline Minuscule arrived in England in the second half of the 10th century. Its adoption there, replacing [Insular script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insular_script), was encouraged by the importation of continental European manuscripts by Saints [Dunstan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunstan), [Aethelwold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%86thelwold_of_Winchester), and [Oswald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_of_Worcester). This script spread quite rapidly, being employed in many English centres for copying Latin texts. English scribes adapted the Carolingian script, giving it proportion and legibility. This new revision of the Caroline minuscule was called English Protogothic Bookhand. Another script that is derived from the Caroline Minuscule was the German Protogothic Bookhand. It originated in southern Germany during the second half of the 12th century.[^cite_note-script-23] All the individual letters are Caroline; but just as with English Protogothic Bookhand it evolved. This can be seen most notably in the arm of the letter h. It has a hairline that tapers out by curving to the left. When first read the German Protogothic h looks like the German Protogothic b.[^cite_note-manu-24] Many more scripts sprang out of the German Protogothic Bookhand. After those came Bastard Anglicana, which is best described as:[^cite_note-Intro-20]

> &#x20;The coexistence in the Gothic period of formal hands employed for the copying of books and cursive scripts used for documentary purposes eventually resulted in cross-fertilization between these two fundamentally different writing styles. Notably, scribes began to upgrade some of the cursive scripts. A script that has been thus formalized is known as a *bastard* script (whereas a bookhand that has had cursive elements fused onto it is known as a hybrid script). The advantage of such a script was that it could be written more quickly than a pure bookhand; it thus recommended itself to scribes in a period when demand for books was increasing and authors were tending to write longer texts. In England during the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, many books were written in the script known as Bastard Anglicana.

### Genres

From ancient texts to medieval maps, anything written down for study would have been done with manuscripts. Some of the most common genres were bibles, religious commentaries, philosophy, law and government texts.

#### Biblical

Main article: [Biblical manuscript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblical_manuscript)

"[The Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bible) was the most studied book of the Middle Ages".[^cite_note-beryl-25] The Bible was the center of medieval religious life. Along with the Bible came scores of commentaries. Commentaries were written in volumes, with some focusing on just single pages of scripture. Across Europe, there were universities that prided themselves on their biblical knowledge. Along with universities, certain cities also had their own celebrities of biblical knowledge during the medieval period.

#### Book of hours



Illuminated letter S (for *Spiritus domini*) from the [Sherbrooke Missal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherbrooke_Missal): [Aberystwyth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aberystwyth), [National Library of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Library_of_Wales), MS 15536E, fol. 169v, parchment, c. 1310–1320, depicting the Holy Spirit descending on the apostles at Pentecost.

A [book of hours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_hours) is a type of devotional text which was widely popular during the Middle Ages. They are the most common type of surviving medieval [illuminated manuscripts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illuminated_manuscripts). Each book of hours contain a similar collection of texts, [prayers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_prayer), and [psalms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psalms_\(Christian\)) but decoration can vary between each and each example. Many have minimal illumination, often restricted to ornamented [initials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initials), but books of hours made for wealthier patrons can be extremely extravagant with full-page [miniatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miniature_\(illuminated_manuscript\)). These books were used for owners to recite prayers privately eight different times, or hours, of the day.[^cite_note-26]

#### Liturgical books and calendars

Main articles: [Liturgical book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liturgical_book) and [Liturgical calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liturgical_calendar)

Along with Bibles, large numbers of manuscripts made in the Middle Ages were received in Church. Due to the complex church system of rituals and worship these books were the most elegantly written and finely decorated of all medieval manuscripts. Liturgical books usually came in two varieties. Those used during mass and those for divine office.[^cite_note-Intro-20]

Most liturgical books came with a calendar in the front. This served as a quick reference point for important dates in Jesus' life and to tell church officials which saints were to be honored and on what day.

## Modern variations&#x20;

In the context of [library science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_science), a manuscript is defined as any hand-written item in the collections of a library or an archive. For example, a library's collection of hand-written letters or diaries is considered a manuscript collection. Such manuscript collections are described in finding aids, similar to an index or table of contents to the collection, in accordance with national and international content standards such as [DACS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Describing_Archives:_A_Content_Standard) and [ISAD(G)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISAD\(G\)).

In other contexts, however, the use of the term "manuscript" no longer necessarily means something that is hand-written. By analogy a ***typescript*** has been produced on a typewriter.[^cite_note-merriam-webster.com-27]

### Publishing

Main article: [Manuscript (publishing)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuscript_\(publishing\))

In book, magazine, and music publishing, a manuscript is an [autograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autograph_\(manuscript\)) or copy of a work, written by an author, composer or copyist. Such manuscripts generally follow standardized typographic and formatting rules, in which case they can be called [fair copy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fair_copy) (whether original or copy). The staff paper commonly used for handwritten music is, for this reason, often called "manuscript paper".

### Film and theatre

See also: [Screenplay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screenplay) and [Play (theatre)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Play_\(theatre\))

In film and theatre, a manuscript, or *script* for short, is an author's or dramatist's text, used by a theatre company or film crew during the production of the work's performance or filming. More specifically, a motion picture manuscript is called a screenplay; a television manuscript, a teleplay; a manuscript for the theatre, a stage play; and a manuscript for audio-only performance is often called a radio play, even when the recorded performance is disseminated via non-radio means.

### Insurance

See also: [Insurance policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insurance_policy)

In insurance, a manuscript policy is one that is negotiated between the insurer and the policyholder, as opposed to an off-the-shelf form supplied by the insurer.
