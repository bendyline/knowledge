The **provinces of Afghanistan** ([Pashto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pashto) and [Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari_language): ولايت *[wilāyat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilayah)*) are the primary [administrative divisions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Administrative_division) of the [Islamic Emirate of Afghanistan](737.md). There are 34 provinces in Afghanistan. Each province encompasses a number of [districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Districts_of_Afghanistan) or usually over 1,000 villages.

Provincial governors played a critical role in the reconstruction of the Afghan state following the creation of the new government under [Hamid Karzai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamid_Karzai).[^cite_note-auto1-1] According to international security scholar Dipali Mukhopadhyay, many of the provincial governors of the western-backed government were former [warlords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warlord) who were incorporated into the political system.[^cite_note-auto1-1]

## History of administrative divisions of Afghanistan

The administrative divisions of Afghanistan have evolved through a gradual process of centralisation, shaped by geography, military power, and the state's efforts to extend authority from [Kabul](16826.md) to peripheral regions. Until the 20th century, governance relied largely on personal rule, tribal alliances, and tribute rather than clearly defined territorial units.

### Early modern period

Before the establishment of modern state institutions, the territory of present-day Afghanistan was not divided into fixed provinces. Under the [Durrani Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durrani_Empire) (1747–1823), administration functioned through a loose confederation of tribal leaders and appointed relatives. Core centres of authority included [Kandahar](17260.md), [Kabul](16826.md), [Herat](14128.md), and western regions such as [Sistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sistan). Control was exercised through military levies, revenue extraction, and personal loyalty, while boundaries remained fluid and weakly institutionalised.[^cite_note-2]



Map of Afghanistan 1839–1863, showing the First Anglo-Afghan war, and unification of Afghanistan by Dost Mohammad Khan

Following the death of [Ahmad Shah Durrani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmad_Shah_Durrani) in 1772, internal dynastic conflicts led to fragmentation. By the early 19th century, power was divided among principalities centred on Kabul, Kandahar, and Herat, alongside semi-autonomous northern khanates in [Balkh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balkh), [Kunduz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kunduz), and [Badakhshan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Badakhshan). Local chieftains exercised *de facto* authority in rural areas, often limiting the reach of central rule.[^cite_note-administration-3] A renewed process of unification occurred under [Dost Mohammad Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dost_Mohammad_Khan) from 1826, who gradually brought [Ghazni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghazni), [Hazarajat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazarajat), northern [Turkestan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkestan), Kandahar, and Herat under central control by 1863. Administration remained personal and dynastic, with key regions governed by family members and local rulers integrated through tribute rather than direct bureaucracy.[^cite_note-4]

### Centralization efforts during the 19th and early 20th centuries

During the 19th century, Afghan rulers increasingly sought to formalise territorial administration. Provinces were defined broadly along major river systems and watersheds, such as the [Kabul River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabul_River), [Helmand River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmand_River), [Hari Rud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hari_Rud), and the [Oxus basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amu_Darya).[^cite_note-manteqa-5] By around 1880 and the ascent of Emir [Abdur Rahman Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdur_Rahman_Khan), British historian W. P. Andrew identified the main provinces as *[Cabul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabul_Province)*, *[Jellalabad](457263.md)*, *[Ghuzni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghazni_Province)*, *[Candahar](457241.md)*, *[Herat](312559.md)*, and *[Balkh](457218.md)* (also called *[Afghan Turkestan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghan_Turkestan)*), although these units were not systematically surveyed and are known mainly from written sources.[^cite_note-andrew-6][^cite_note-administration-3] In addition, he identified the rugged regions inhabited by the [Ghilzai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghilji) and [Hazara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazaras), roughly corresponding with the lands between [Kandahar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loy_Kandahar) and [Paktia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loya_Paktia) and [Hazarajat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazarajat) respectively.[^cite_note-andrew-6]

In 1890, Afghanistan was first mentioned in [the Statesman's Yearbook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Statesman's_Yearbook) as a separate entity, stating that the dominions of the [Emirate of Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emirate_of_Afghanistan) are divided into the four provinces of Kabul, Turkistan, Herat, and Kandahar, as well as the districts of [Badakhshan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Badakhshan) and [Wakhan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wakhan), at the time being seen as separate provinces.[^cite_note-7] Since 1892, Wakhan was included into the district of Badakhshan as part of its dependencies.[^cite_note-8] Between 1891 and 1893, a thorough description of the administrative divisions of Afghanistan was compiled by British military officers, drawing on reports, surveys, and [Afghan Boundary Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghan_Boundary_Commission) records. The works cover the six provinces of Badakhshan, Afghan Turkistan, Herat, Kabul, Kandahar, and [Farah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farah_Province), with the latter being formed out of parts of Herat and Kandahar as well as the *de facto* independent [Sistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sistan) and [Garmsir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garmsir_District) regions during the time of [Sher Ali Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sher_Ali_Khan).[^cite_note-farahkandahar-9] An overview over all provinces and their districts and subdistricts is provided below:

### Provincial reform under Amanullah Khan in the 1920s



Historical provinces of Afghanistan since 1921

After Afghanistan became [fully independent from the British in 1919](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Anglo-Afghan_War), a major reform of sub-national administration was introduced under King [Amanullah Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amanullah_Khan) through the *regulation on the territorial divisions of Afghanistan* ([Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari_language): نظامنامه تقسیمات ملکیه افغانستان, romanized: *niẓāmnāmah-i taqsīmāt-i mulkīyah-i Afghānistān*) in 1300 [Hijri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_Hijri_calendar) (1921/22 [Gregorian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorian_calendar)).[^cite_note-nizamnama-19] Drawing on naturally occurring geographic boundaries, existing local identities, and accessibility from the centre, the regulation formalised a five-tier hierarchy of sub-national units:

1. *provinces* ([Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari_language): ولایت, romanized: *wilāyat*) or *high governorates* ([Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari_language): حکومت اعلی, romanized: *ḥukūmat-i ʿālā*)

2. *large governorates* ([Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari_language): حکومت کلان, romanized: *ḥukūmat-i kalān*)

3. *governorates* ([Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari_language): حکومت, romanized: *ḥukūmat*)

4. *sub-districts* ([Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari_language): علاقه, romanized: *ʿalāqa*)

5. *villages* ([Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari_language): قریه, romanized: *qarya*)

The system was administratively complex.[^cite_note-manteqa-5] At the apex of the sub-national structure stood two functionally equivalent unit types. The provinces, headed by a *[deputy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deputy)* or *[viceroy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viceroy)[^cite_note-20] of the government* ([Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari_language): نائب الحکومه, romanized: *nāʾib ul-ḥukūma*), and the high governorates, headed by a *high governor* ([Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari_language): حاکم اعلی, romanized: *ḥākim-i ʿālā*), both reported directly to the central government. The regulation established five provinces and four high governorates:

- [Province of Kabul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabul_Province_\(1921%E2%80%931964\)?action=edit\&redlink=1) ([Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari_language): ولایت کابل, romanized: *Wilāyat-i Kābul*)

- [Province of Kandahar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kandahar_Province_\(1921%E2%80%931964\)?action=edit\&redlink=1) ([Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari_language): ولایت قندهار, romanized: *Wilāyat-i Qandahār*)

- [Province of Herat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herat_Province_\(1921%E2%80%931964\)?action=edit\&redlink=1) ([Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari_language): ولایت هرات, romanized: *Wilāyat-i Herāt*)

- [Province of Turkistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkestan_Province_\(1921%E2%80%931964\)?action=edit\&redlink=1) ([Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari_language): ولایت ترکستان, romanized: *Wilāyat-i Turkistān*)

- [Province of Qataghan and Badakhshan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qataghan_and_Badakhshan_Province) ([Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari_language): ولایت قطغن و بدخشان, romanized: *Wilāyat-i Qaṭaghan wa Badakhshān*)

- [High Governorate of the East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Province,_Afghanistan) ([Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari_language): حکومت اعلی سمت مشرقی, romanized: *Ḥukūmat-i ʿĀlā-yi Samt-i Mashriqi*)

- [High Governorate of the South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Province,_Afghanistan) ([Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari_language): حکومت اعلی سمت جنوبی, romanized: *Ḥukūmat-i ʿĀlā-yi Samt-i Junūbi*)

- [High Governorate of Farah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farah-Chakansur_Province) ([Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari_language): حکومت اعلی فراه, romanized: *Ḥukūmat-i ʿĀlā-yi Farāh*)

- [High Governorate of Maimana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meymaneh_Province) ([Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari_language): حکومت اعلی میمنه, romanized: *Ḥukūmat-i ʿĀlā-yi Maimana*)

Below these, the large governorates, headed by a *large governor* ([Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari_language): حاکم کلان, romanized: *ḥākim-i kalān*), formed the second tier and could encompass governorates, sub-districts and villages beneath it. The governorates, headed by a *governor* ([Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari_language): حاکم, romanized: *ḥākim*), existed in three grades, whereas the sub-districts, headed by a *sub-district chief* ([Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari_language): علاقه دار, romanized: *ʿalāqadār*), existed in two grades, reflecting differences in administrative importance. The villages, headed by a *village chief* ([Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari_language): قریه دار, romanized: *qaryādār*), constituted the lowest unit. Units at any tier could be subordinated directly to any superior level without necessarily passing through all intermediate layers.

The regulation contained several internal ambiguities and inconsistencies, as it was sometimes unevenly applied.[^cite_note-manteqa-5] Within the province of Qataghan and Badakhshan, the two administrative units of *Qataghan* and *Badakhshan* are sometimes referred to as "large governorates" whereas sometimes they are simply called "governorates". A similar inconsistency appears in the provinces of Kabul and Kandahar, each of which contained a governorate of the same name that governed further governorates beneath it, placing it structurally on the level of a large governorate, though the regulation neither gives them this title nor (in the case of Kandahar) specifies who was responsible for leading them. A further exception concerned the province of Kabul as a whole since unlike all other units, the province and all its subordinate divisions at every level fell under the direct authority of the [Ministry of the Interior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_the_Interior_\(Afghanistan\)) rather than the standard provincial chain of command.[^cite_note-nizamnama-19]

### Mid-20th century reforms

Between the 1920s and 1960s, successive governments sought to simplify the administrative structure. Large historical provinces such as [Turkestan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkestan), [Qataghan-Badakhshan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qataghan-Badakhshan_Province), [Eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Province,_Afghanistan), [Southern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Province,_Afghanistan), and [Farah-Chakansur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farah-Chakansur_Province) were gradually dissolved or subdivided. High governorates and governorates were transformed into provinces or districts, while sub-districts remained in use until the late 20th century.[^cite_note-manteqa-5][^cite_note-21]

The [1964 Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_Constitution_of_Afghanistan) marked a turning point by introducing the principle of balanced regional development and consultative provincial councils. An administrative law enacted in 1965 established a clear hierarchy of provinces, districts, and villages and formally listed all recognised units.[^cite_note-manteqa-5] In 1963–1964, the country was reorganised into 28 provinces, replacing fewer, larger units with smaller provinces to improve governance and development planning.[^cite_note-administration-3] This framework remained largely in place until the end of the monarchy in 1973.[^cite_note-22]

### During war times in the 20th and 21st century



Administrative divisions of Afghanistan, c. 1986

From the late 1960s onward, local demands and administrative needs led to a gradual increase in the number of districts and provinces. Between the [Soviet–Afghan War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet%E2%80%93Afghan_War) and the [Afghan civil wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghan_Civil_War_\(1989-2001\)), 4 new provinces were created: [Paktika](457294.md), [Khost](457249.md), [Sar-i Pul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sar-e-Pol_Province), and [Nuristan](457267.md). A law on local administration in 2000 abolished large districts and sub-districts, upgrading most sub-districts to full districts.[^cite_note-manteqa-5] After the [fall of the first Taliban regime in 2001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Kabul_\(2001\)), the interim and subsequent governments retained the existing provincial structure, with governors appointed by the central authority.[^cite_note-manteqa-5]



Provinces of Afghanistan, c. 2003

Two new provinces were created in 2004 following the adoption of the [Constitution of Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Constitution_of_Afghanistan). [Daykundi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daikundi_Province) was formed from districts of [Uruzgan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uruzgan_Province), and [Panjshir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panjshir_Province) was separated from [Parwan](457277.md). These changes increased the total number of provinces to 34. No further provincial boundary changes occurred during the remainder of the [Islamic Republic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Republic_of_Afghanistan), although the number of districts continued to expand. By 2021, Afghanistan consisted of 34 provinces and 378 districts, reflecting long-term administrative expansion rather than major territorial reorganisation.[^cite_note-manteqa-5]

### Since 2021

Following the [Taliban's return to power in August 2021](68481047.md), the 34-province framework was retained. Provincial governors and senior officials have been appointed directly by the central leadership, reinforcing a highly centralised system of administration. While formal provincial boundaries have remained unchanged, governance practices have shifted toward tighter political and ideological control, with provinces functioning primarily as instruments of security and enforcement under the [Islamic Emirate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Emirate_of_Afghanistan).[^cite_note-23][^cite_note-24]
