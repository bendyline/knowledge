**Gliwice** (Polish: [\[ɡliˈvit͡sɛ\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Polish) [//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/8/80/Pl-Gliwice.ogg/Pl-Gliwice.ogg.mp3](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/8/80/Pl-Gliwice.ogg/Pl-Gliwice.ogg.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup>; [Silesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silesian_language): *Glywice/Glajwic*, [Czech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czech_language): *Hlivice*/Glivice;[^cite_note-2] [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language): *Gleiwitz*) is a city in [Upper Silesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Silesia), in southern Poland.[^cite_note-TERYT-3] The city is located in the [Silesian Highlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silesian_Highlands), on the [Kłodnica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C5%82odnica) river (a tributary of the [Oder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oder_River)).

Gliwice is the westernmost city of the [Metropolis GZM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolis_GZM), a conurbation of 2.0 million people, and is the third-largest city of this area, with 175,102 permanent residents as of 2021.[^cite_note-population-1] It also lies within the larger [Katowice-Ostrava metropolitan area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katowice-Ostrava_metropolitan_area) which has a population of about 5.3 million people and spans across most of eastern Upper Silesia, western [Lesser Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesser_Poland) and the [Moravian-Silesian Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moravian-Silesian_Region) in the Czech Republic. Gliwice is bordered by three other cities and towns of the metropolitan area: [Zabrze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zabrze), [Knurów](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knur%C3%B3w) and [Pyskowice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyskowice). It is one of the major [college towns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_town) in Poland, thanks to the [Silesian University of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silesian_University_of_Technology), which was founded in 1945 by academics of [Lwów University of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lviv_Polytechnic). Over 20,000 people study in Gliwice.[^cite_note-4] Gliwice is an important industrial center of Poland. Following an [economic transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_transformation) in the 1990s, Gliwice shifted from [steelworks](1143008.md) and [coal mining](208413.md) to [automotive](163778.md) and [machine industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_industry).

Founded in the 13th century, Gliwice is one of the oldest cities in [Upper Silesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Silesia), with a preserved Old Town core. Gliwice's most historical structures include [St Bartholomew's Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Saint_Bartholomew_church) (15th century), [Gliwice Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gliwice_Castle) and city walls (14th century), [Armenian Church](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko%C5%9Bci%C3%B3%C5%82%20Tr%C3%B3jcy%20%C5%9Awi%C4%99tej%20w%20Gliwicach) (originally a hospital, 15th century) and [All Saints Old Town Church](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ko%C5%9Bci%C3%B3%C5%82%20Wszystkich%20%C5%9Awi%C4%99tych%20w%20Gliwicach) (15th century). Gliwice is also known for its [Radio Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gliwice_Radio_Tower), where the [Gleiwitz incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gleiwitz_incident) took place shortly before the outbreak of [World War II](32927.md) and which is thought to be the world's tallest wooden construction,[^cite_note-5] as well as Weichmann Textile House, one of the first buildings designed by world-renowned architect [Erich Mendelsohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_Mendelsohn). Gliwice hosted the [Junior Eurovision Song Contest 2019](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junior_Eurovision_Song_Contest_2019) which took place on 24 November 2019.[^cite_note-Gliwice-6]

## Etymology

The name of the city is derived from the [Slavic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavic_languages) root *gliw* or *gliv*, suggesting terrain characterized by [loam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loam) or [wetland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wetland).

## History

### Early history



Medieval [fortified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortified_church) [Old Saint Bartholomew church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Saint_Bartholomew_church), one of the oldest structures of Gliwice

Gliwice was first mentioned as a town in 1276, however, it was granted [town rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Town_rights) earlier by Duke [Władysław Opolski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W%C5%82adys%C5%82aw_Opolski) of the [Piast dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piast_dynasty).[^cite_note-dzieje-7] It was located on a [trade route](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_route) connecting [Kraków](16815.md) and [Wrocław](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wroc%C5%82aw) and was part of various [Piast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piast_dynasty)-ruled duchies of [fragmented Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Poland_during_the_Piast_dynasty#Fragmentation_of_the_realm_\(1138%E2%80%931320\)): [Opole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Opole) until 1281, [Bytom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Bytom) until 1322, from 1322 to 1342 Gliwice was a capital of the [Duchy of Gliwice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Gliwice?action=edit\&redlink=1), afterwards again part of the Duchy of Bytom until 1354, later it was also ruled by other regional Polish Piast dukes until 1532,[^cite_note-dzieje-7] although in 1335 it fell under the suzerainty of the [Bohemian Crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lands_of_the_Bohemian_Crown), which itself was part of the [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire), and passed with that crown under suzerainty of the Austrian [Habsburgs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habsburg) in 1526.

According to 14th-century writers, the town seemed defensive in character, when under rule of [Siemowit of Bytom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siemowit_of_Bytom).[^cite_note-history-8] In the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) the city prospered mainly due to trade and crafts, especially brewing.[^cite_note-dzieje-7]

On 17 April 1433, Gliwice was captured by the [Duke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_G%C5%82og%C3%B3wek_and_Prudnik) [Bolko V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolko_V_the_Hussite), who joined the [Hussites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hussites) after they captured [Prudnik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prudnik).[^cite_note-9]

### Early Modern Age



An 1863 Polish plaque at the Town Hall commemorating Polish King [John III Sobieski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_III_Sobieski) to 200th anniversary of the [Battle of Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Vienna)

After the dissolution of the [Duchy of Opole and Racibórz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Opole_and_Racib%C3%B3rz) in 1532, it was incorporated as Gleiwitz into the [Habsburg monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habsburg_monarchy). Because of the vast expenses incurred by the Habsburg monarchy during their [16th century wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_wars_in_Europe) against the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md), Gleiwitz was [leased](635681.md) to Friedrich Zettritz for the amount of 14,000 [thalers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thaler). Although the original lease was for a duration of 18 years, it was renewed in 1580 for 10 years and in 1589 for an additional 18 years. Around 1612, the Reformed Franciscans came from [Kraków](16815.md), and then their [monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redemptorist_Monastery_in_Gliwice?action=edit\&redlink=1) and [Church of the Exaltation of the Holy Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_the_Exaltation_of_the_Holy_Cross_in_Gliwice?action=edit\&redlink=1) were built.[^cite_note-db-10] The city was besieged or captured by various armies during the [Thirty Years' War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirty_Years'_War).[^cite_note-dzieje-7] In 1645 along with the Duchy of Opole and Racibórz it returned to Poland under the [House of Vasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Vasa), and in 1666 it fell to Austria again. In 1683, Polish King [John III Sobieski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_III_Sobieski) stopped in the city before the [Battle of Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Vienna).[^cite_note-dzieje-7] In the 17th and 18th century, the city's economy switched from trading and brewing beer to clothmaking, which collapsed after the 18th-century [Silesian Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silesian_Wars).[^cite_note-dzieje-7]

During the mid 18th century [Silesian Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silesian_Wars), Gleiwitz was taken from the Habsburg monarchy by the [Kingdom of Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Prussia) along with the majority of Silesia. After the end of the [Napoleonic Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars), Gleiwitz was administered in the [Prussian district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Districts_of_Prussia) of Tost-Gleiwitz within the [Province of Silesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Silesia) in 1816. In the 19th century, the city was visited by writers [Julian Ursyn Niemcewicz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_Ursyn_Niemcewicz), [Wincenty Pol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wincenty_Pol), [Honoré de Balzac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honor%C3%A9_de_Balzac) and [Józef Ignacy Kraszewski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%B3zef_Ignacy_Kraszewski).[^cite_note-11] The city was incorporated with Prussia into the [German Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Empire) in 1871 during the [unification of Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unification_of_Germany). In 1897, Gleiwitz became its own Stadtkreis, or urban district.

### Industrialization



Iron foundry in c. 1900

The first coke-fired [blast furnace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blast_furnace) on the European continent was constructed in Gleiwitz in 1796 under the direction of [John Baildon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Baildon). Gleiwitz began to develop into a major city through industrialization during the 19th century. The town's [ironworks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ironworks) fostered the growth of other industrial fields in the area. The city's population in 1875 was 14,156. However, during the late 19th century Gleiwitz had: 14 [distilleries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distillation), 2 [breweries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brewery), 5 [mills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mill_\(grinding\)), 7 brick factories, 3 [sawmills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sawmill), a [shingle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roof_shingle) factory, 8 [chalk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalk) factories and 2 [glassworks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glassblowing).

Other features of the 19th-century era industrialized Gleiwitz were a [gasworks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gasworks), a [furnace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metallurgical_furnace) factory, a beer [bottling company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bottling_company), and a plant for asphalt and paste. Economically, Gleiwitz opened several banks, [savings and loan associations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savings_and_loan_association), and [bond](60737.md) centers. Its tram system was completed in 1892, while its theater was opened in 1899; until [World War II](32927.md), Gleiwitz's theatre featured actors from throughout Europe and was one of the most famous theatres in the whole of Germany. Despite [Germanisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanisation_of_Poles_during_the_Partitions) policies, the [Poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_people) established various Polish organizations, including the ["Sokół" Polish Gymnastic Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_Sok%C3%B3%C5%82_movement), and published local Polish newspapers.[^cite_note-dzieje-7]

### 20th century



Main railway station in c. 1908

According to the [1911 Encyclopædia Britannica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica), Gleiwitz's population in 1905 was 61,324. By 1911, it had two [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant) and four [Roman Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church) churches, a synagogue, a mining school, a [convent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convent), a hospital, two [orphanages](387703.md), and a [barracks](1033489.md). Gleiwitz was the center of the mining industry of [Upper Silesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Silesia). It possessed a royal [foundry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundry), with which were connected machine factories and boiler works.

After the end of [World War I](4764461.md), clashes between [Poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_people) and [Germans](152735.md) occurred during the [Polish insurrections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silesian_Uprisings) in Silesia. Some ethnically Polish inhabitants of Upper Silesia wanted to incorporate the city into the [Second Polish Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Polish_Republic), which had just regained independence. On 1 May 1919, a Polish rally was held in Gliwice.[^cite_note-ips-12] Seeking a peaceful solution to the conflict, the [League of Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Nations) held a [plebiscite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1921_Upper_Silesia_plebiscite) on 20 March 1921 to determine which country the city should belong to. In Gleiwitz, 32,029 votes (78.7% of given votes) were for remaining in Germany, Poland received 8,558 (21.0%) votes, and 113 (0.3%) votes were declared invalid. The total voter turnout was listed as 97.0%. The majority in the present-day districts (then surrounding villages) of Brzezinka, Czechowice, Łabędy, Ostropa, [Sośnica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/So%C5%9Bnica,_Gliwice), Stare Gliwice, Wójtowa Wieś and Żerniki opted to reintegrate with Poland, with the result ranging from 55.7% voting for Poland in Łabędy to 80.2% in Czechowice, while in the present-day districts of Ligota Zabrska and Bojków 56.5% and 98.3%, respectively, voted to remain in Germany.[^cite_note-13][^cite_note-14] The results prompted another insurrection by Poles. The League of Nations determined that three Silesian cities: Gleiwitz (Gliwice), [Hindenburg (Zabrze)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zabrze) and [Beuthen (Bytom)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bytom) would remain in Germany, and the eastern part of [Upper Silesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Silesia) with its main city of [Katowice (Kattowitz)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katowice) would join restored Poland. After delimiting the border in Upper Silesia in 1921, Gliwice found itself in Germany, but near the border with [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Polish_Republic) – nearby [Knurów](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knur%C3%B3w) was already in Poland.



The present Administrative Court building in the 1920s

During the [interbellum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interbellum) the city witnessed not only [anti-Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Polish_sentiment), but also [anti-French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francophobia) incidents and violence by the Germans. In 1920, local Polish doctor and city [councillor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Councillor) [Wincenty Styczyński](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wincenty_Styczy%C5%84ski?action=edit\&redlink=1), protested against the German refusal to treat French soldiers stationed in the city.[^cite_note-ips-12] In January 1922, he himself treated French soldiers shot in the city.[^cite_note-ips-12] On 9 April 1922, 17 Frenchmen died in an explosion during the liquidation of a German militia weapons warehouse in the present-day [Sośnica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/So%C5%9Bnica,_Gliwice) district.[^cite_note-ips-12] Styczyński, who defended the rights of local Poles and protested against German acts of violence against Poles, was himself murdered by a German radical/militant on 18 April 1922.[^cite_note-ips-12] Nevertheless, various Polish organizations and enterprises still operated in the city in the interbellum, including a local branch of the [Union of Poles in Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_of_Poles_in_Germany), Polish banks and a [scout troop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scout_troop).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECygański198424,_31,_33-15]

On 9 June 1933, Gliwice was the site of the first conference of the Nazi anti-Polish organization [Bund Deutscher Osten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bund_Deutscher_Osten) in Upper Silesia.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERosenbaumWęcki201049-16] In a secret *[Sicherheitsdienst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicherheitsdienst)* report from 1934, Gliwice was named one of the main centers of the Polish movement in western Upper Silesia.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERosenbaumWęcki201060-17] Polish activists were increasingly persecuted starting in 1937.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECygański198424-18]



[Gliwice Radio Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gliwice_Radio_Tower)

The [Gleiwitz incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gleiwitz_incident) was a false-flag attack on a radio station in Gleiwitz on 31 August 1939, staged by the German secret police, which served as a pretext, devised by [Reinhard Heydrich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinhard_Heydrich) under orders from Hitler, for [Nazi Germany](21212.md) to [invade Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasion_of_Poland), and which marked the start of the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War). Shortly after the outbreak of the war, on 4 September 1939, the *[Einsatzgruppe I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einsatzgruppen)* entered the city to commit [atrocities against Poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_crimes_against_the_Polish_nation).[^cite_note-19] After the invasion of Poland, the assets of local Polish banks were confiscated by Germany.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECygański198433-20] The Germans formed a *[Kampfgruppe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kampfgruppe)* unit in the city.[^cite_note-21] It was also the [cremation](175142.md) site of many of around 750 Poles murdered in Katowice in September 1939.[^cite_note-22]

In early 1940, the advanced [shaped charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaped_charge) explosive developed for the attack on [Fort Ében-Émael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_%C3%89ben-%C3%89mael) as part of the *[Blitzkrieg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blitzkrieg)* attack on the [Maginot Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maginot_Line) on 10 May 1940 were tested at places in Gleiwitz to ensure secrecy.[^cite_note-23]

During the war, the Germans operated a *Dulag* transit camp for Polish prisoners of war,[^cite_note-24] and a Nazi prison in the city,[^cite_note-25] and established numerous [forced labour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forced_labour_under_German_rule_during_World_War_II) camps,[^cite_note-dzieje-7] including a *[Polenlager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polenlager)* camp solely for Poles,[^cite_note-26] a camp solely for Jews,[^cite_note-27] a penal "education" camp,[^cite_note-28] a subcamp of the prison in [Strzelce Opolskie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strzelce_Opolskie),[^cite_note-29] and six subcamps of the [Stalag VIII-B/344](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stalag_VIII-B) [prisoner of war camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_prisoner-of-war_camps_in_World_War_II).[^cite_note-30] In October 1943, the Germans brought a large transport of [Italian POWs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Military_Internees) to a forced labour camp in today's Łabędy district.[^cite_note-31] From July 1944 to January 1945, Gliwice was the location of four subcamps of the [Auschwitz concentration camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auschwitz_concentration_camp).[^cite_note-32] In the largest subcamp, whose prisoners were mainly Poles, Jews and Russians, nearly 100 either died of hunger, mistreatment and exhaustion or were murdered.[^cite_note-33] During the evacuation of another subcamp, the Germans burned alive or shot 55 prisoners who were unable to walk.[^cite_note-34] There are two mass graves of the victims of the early 1945 [death march](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_marches_during_the_Holocaust) from Auschwitz in the city, both commemorated with monuments.[^cite_note-35]

During the final stages of the war, 124 inhabitants committed suicide fearing the advancing [Red Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Army).[^cite_note-ah-36] On 24 January 1945, Gliwice was occupied by the Red Army. Soviet troops then murdered over 1,000 civilians, mostly women, children and elders.[^cite_note-ah-36] In February 1945, the Soviets carried out deportations of local men to Soviet mines.[^cite_note-ig-37] Under borders changes dictated by the Soviet Union at the [Potsdam Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potsdam_Conference), Gliwice fell inside Poland's new borders after Germany's defeat in the war. It was incorporated into Poland's [Silesian Voivodeship](28969.md) on 18 March 1945, after almost 300 years of being outside of Polish rule.



Parade of the Polish [Border Protection Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Border_Protection_Forces) in 1978

In 1956, Gliwice was the site of a manifestation of solidarity with the [Hungarian Revolution of 1956](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_Revolution_of_1956), and local Poles raised funds and donated blood for the Hungarian insurgents (see also *[Hungary–Poland relations](20026103.md)*).[^cite_note-38]

The earliest population estimate of Gliwice from 1880, gives 1,159 people in 1750.[^cite_note-\:0-40] The same source cites population to have been 2,990 in 1810, 6,415 in 1838, and 10,923 in 1861. A census from 1858 reported the following ethnic makeup: 7,060 - German, 3,566 - [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_people), 11 - [Moravian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moravians), 1 - [Czech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czechs). Since the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution), Gliwice saw rapid economic growth which fuelled fast population increase. In 1890, Gliwice had 19,667 inhabitants, and this number has increased over twofold over the next 10 years to 52,362 in 1900.[^cite_note-41] Gliwice gained its status of a large city (*[Großstadt](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gro%C3%9Fstadt)* in German) in 1927, when population reached 102,452 people.

In 1945, with the approaching Red Army, a significant number of residents were either evacuated or fled the city at their own discretion. Following the [Yalta Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yalta_Conference), Gliwice, along most of [Silesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silesia), was incorporated into [communist Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_People's_Republic), and the remaining German population was [expelled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_and_expulsion_of_Germans_\(1944%E2%80%9350\)). Ethnic Poles, some of them themselves [expelled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_population_transfers_\(1944%E2%80%931946\)) from [the Polish Kresy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kresy) (which were incorporated into [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union)), started to settle down in Gliwice.

As of 31 December 2016, Gliwice's population stood at 182,156 people, a decrease of 1,236 over the previous year.

### Nationality, ethnicity and language

Historically, Gliwice was ethnically diverse, initially inhabited by [Poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_people), later it had a German majority as a result of German [colonization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drang_nach_Osten), with a significant autochthonous Polish minority. In the [Upper Silesian Plebiscite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Silesia_plebiscite) in 1921, 78.9 percent of voters opted for Germany (however 15.1 percent of the vote in Gliwice was cast by non-residents, who are believed to overwhelmingly vote for Germany across the region). After Germany's defeat in World War II, the Germans either fled or were displaced to Allied-occupied Germany in accordance with the [Potsdam Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potsdam_Agreement). Polish inhabitants remained in Gliwice, and were joined by Poles [displaced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_population_transfers_\(1944%E2%80%931946\)) from former [eastern Poland annexed by the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territories_of_Poland_annexed_by_the_Soviet_Union), including the city of [Lwów](49968.md) (Lviv),[^cite_note-ig-37] [Volhynia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volhynia), [Polesie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polesie), the [Wilno](32597.md) region (Vilnius region) and the [Grodno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grodno) region. In addition, Poles from other regions of Poland, including the vicinity of [Kielce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kielce), [Rzeszów](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rzesz%C3%B3w), [Łódź](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%81%C3%B3d%C5%BA) or [Poznań](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pozna%C5%84), as well as Poles from other countries, settled in Gliwice. Many of these new inhabitants were academics from the [Lwów Polytechnic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lviv_Polytechnic) who created the [Silesian University of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silesian_University_of_Technology).

According to the [2011 Polish Census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_census_of_2011), 93.7 percent of people in Gliwice claimed Polish nationality, with the biggest minorities being [Silesians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silesians) (or both Poles and Silesians at the same time) at 9.7 percent (18,169 people) and Germans at 1.3 percent (2,525). 0.3 percent declared another nationality, and the nationality of 2.1 percent of people could not be established.[^cite_note-\:1-42] These numbers do not sum up to 100 percent as responders were allowed to choose up to two nationalities. The most common languages used at home were [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_language) (97.7 percent), [Silesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silesian_ethnolect) (2.3 percent), German (0.7 percent) and English (0.4 percent).[^cite_note-\:1-42]

Except for a short period immediately after [Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reformation), Gliwice has always had a [Catholic](606848.md) majority, with sizeable Protestant and Jewish minorities. According to the population estimate in 1861, 7,476 people (68.4 percent) were [Catholic](606848.md), 1,555 (14.2 percent) [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant), and 1,892 Jewish (17.3 percent, highest share in city history).[^cite_note-\:0-40][^cite_note-\:2-43]

Currently, as of 2011 census, 84.7 percent of inhabitants claim they belong to a religion. The majority – 82.73 percent – belongs to the Catholic Church. This is significantly lower than the Polish average, which is 89.6 and 88.3 percent, respectively. According to the [Catholic Church in Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_Poland), weekly mass attendance in the Diocese of Gliwice is at 36.7 percent of obliged, on par with Polish average.[^cite_note-44] Other larger denominations include [Jehovah's Witnesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jehovah's_Witnesses) (0.56 percent or 1,044 adherents) and [Protestants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelical_Church_of_the_Augsburg_Confession_in_Poland) (0.37 percent or 701 adherents).[^cite_note-\:1-42]

Gliwice is the seat of the [Roman Catholic Diocese of Gliwice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Diocese_of_Gliwice), which has 23 parish churches in the city. Gliwice is also the seat of one of the three [Armenian Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Catholic_Church) parishes in Poland (the other being in [Warsaw](32908.md) and [Gdańsk](12099.md)), which is subject to the [Holy See](13393.md) directly. Other denominations present in the city include a [Greek Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_Greek_Catholic_Church) parish, an [Evangelical Church of Augsburg Confession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelical_Church_of_the_Augsburg_Confession_in_Poland) parish, a [Methodist](20119.md) parish, 9 [Jehovah Witnesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jehovah's_Witnesses) halls (including one offering English-language services), several [evangelical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelicalism) churches, a [Buddhist](3267529.md) temple and a Jewish prayer house.

#### Jews in Gliwice

Gliwice's Jewish population reached its height in 1929 at approximately 2,200 people, but started to decline in the late 1930s, as the [Nazi Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Party) gained power in [Germany](21212.md), of which Gliwice was then a part. In 1933, there were 1,803 Jews living in the city; this number had dropped by half (to 902) by 1939 due to emigration.[^cite_note-Jewish_Community_in_Gliwice-45] Between 1933 and 1937, Jews living in Upper Silesia enjoyed somewhat less [legal persecution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuremberg_Laws) compared to Jews in other parts of Germany, thanks to the Polish-German Treaty of Protection of Minorities' Rights in Upper Silesia. This regional exception was granted thanks to the [Bernheim petition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernheim_petition) that Gliwice citizen Franz Bernheim filed against [Nazi Germany](21212.md) with the [League of Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Nations).[^cite_note-46][^cite_note-47] The [New Synagogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Synagogue,_Gliwice) was destroyed in 1938 during the Nazi November pogroms known as [Kristallnacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kristallnacht). During [the Holocaust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Holocaust), Jews from Gliwice were transported to [Auschwitz-Birkenau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auschwitz-Birkenau) in 1942 and 1943.[^cite_note-Jewish_Community_in_Gliwice-45]



Former synagogue in c. 1920

Only 25 Jews from Gliwice's pre-war Jewish population survived World War II in the city, all of them being in mixed marriages with gentiles.[^cite_note-48] Immediately after the war, Gliwice became a congregation point for Jews who had survived [the Holocaust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Holocaust), with the Jewish population standing at around a 1,000 people in 1945. Since then, the number of Jews in Gliwice has declined as survivors moved to larger cities or emigrated to Israel, the United States, and other western countries.[^cite_note-\:2-43] Currently, Gliwice's Jewish community is estimated at around 25 people and is part of the Katowice Jewish Religious Community.

Gliwice has one [Jewish prayer house](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synagoga%20w%20Gliwicach), where religious services are held every [Sabbath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabbath) and on holidays. It is located in the house that the Jewish Religious Community elected in 1905.

Notable members of the Jewish community in Gliwice include:

- [Wilhelm Freund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Freund) (1806–1894), philologist and director of the Jewish school.

- [Oscar Troplowitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Troplowitz) (1863–1918), German pharmacist, owner of [Beiersdorf AG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beiersdorf) and inventor of [Nivea skin cream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nivea).

- [Eugen Goldstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugen_Goldstein) (1850–1930), German physicist, discoverer of anode rays, sometimes credited for the discovery of the proton.

- [Julian Kornhauser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_Kornhauser) (b. 1946), Polish poet and father of current [first lady](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Lady_of_Poland) [Agata Kornhauser-Duda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agata_Kornhauser-Duda), born in Gliwice to a Jewish father and Polish Silesian mother from [Chorzów](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chorz%C3%B3w).

### Water transport

There is also an older [Kłodnica Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C5%82odnica_Canal) (*Kanał Kłodnicki*) which is no longer operational.
