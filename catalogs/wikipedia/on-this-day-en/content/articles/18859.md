**Michigan** ([/ˈmɪʃɪɡən/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/c/c3/En-us-Michigan.ogg/En-us-Michigan.ogg.mp3](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/c/c3/En-us-Michigan.ogg/En-us-Michigan.ogg.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup> [*MISH-ig-ən*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key)) is a peninsular [state](18618239.md) in the [Great Lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Lakes_region) region of the [Upper Midwestern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Midwest) [United States](3434750.md). It shares water and land boundaries with [Minnesota](19590.md) to the northwest, [Wisconsin](33127.md) to the west, [Indiana](21883857.md) and [Illinois](14849.md) to the southwest, [Ohio](22199.md) to the southeast, and the Canadian [province](75763.md) of [Ontario](22218.md) to the east, northeast and north. With a population of 10.13 million[^cite_note-CensusQuick-3] and an area of 96,716 sq mi (250,490 km<sup>2</sup>), Michigan is the [tenth-largest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_population) state by population, the [11th-largest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_area) by area, and the largest by total area east of the [Mississippi River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippi_River).[^cite_note-5] The state capital is [Lansing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lansing,_Michigan), while its most populous city is [Detroit](8687.md). The [Metro Detroit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metro_Detroit) region in [Southeast Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Michigan) is among the nation's most populous and largest metropolitan economies. Other important [metropolitan areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Michigan_metropolitan_areas) include [Grand Rapids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Rapids_metropolitan_area), [Flint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flint,_Michigan), [Ann Arbor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ann_Arbor,_Michigan), [Kalamazoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalamazoo%E2%80%93Portage_metropolitan_area), the [Tri-Cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saginaw%E2%80%93Midland%E2%80%93Bay_City_combined_statistical_area), and [Muskegon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muskegon,_Michigan).

Michigan consists of two peninsulas: the heavily forested [Upper Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Peninsula) (commonly called "the U.P."), which juts eastward from northern Wisconsin, and the more populated [Lower Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Peninsula_of_Michigan), stretching north from Ohio and Indiana. The peninsulas are separated by the [Straits of Mackinac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straits_of_Mackinac), which connects Lake Michigan and Lake Huron, and are linked by the 5-mile-long [Mackinac Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mackinac_Bridge) along [Interstate 75](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_75). Bordering four of the five [Great Lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Lakes) and [Lake St. Clair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_St._Clair), Michigan has the longest [freshwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freshwater) coastline of any U.S. political subdivision, measuring 3,288 miles.[^cite_note-NOAA-CRM-6] The state ranks second behind [Alaska](624.md) in water coverage by square miles and first in percentage, with approximately 42%, and it also contains 64,980 [inland lakes and ponds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_lakes_of_Michigan).[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8]

The Great Lakes region has largely been inhabited for thousands of years by Indigenous peoples such as the [Ojibwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ojibwe), [Odawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odawa), [Potawatomi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potawatomi), and [Wyandot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wyandot_people). Some people contend that the region's name is derived from the Ojibwe word ᒥᓯᑲᒥ (*mishigami*),[^cite_note-9] meaning "large water" or "large lake".[^cite_note-MiB-pdf-10][^cite_note-11] Others say that it comes from the Mishiiken Tribe of Mackinac Island, also called Michinemackinawgo by Ottawa historian Andrew Blackbird,[^cite_note-12] whose surrounding lands were referred to as Mishiiken-imakinakom, later shortened to Michilimackinac.

In the 17th century, French explorers claimed the area for [New France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_France). French settlers and [Métis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%A9tis) established forts and settlements. After France's defeat in the [French and Indian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_and_Indian_War) in 1762, the area came under British control and later the U.S. following the [Treaty of Paris (1783)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Paris_\(1783\)), though control remained disputed with Indigenous tribes until treaties between 1795 and 1842. The area was part of the larger [Northwest Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Territory); the [Michigan Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_Territory) was organized in 1805.

Michigan was [admitted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_by_date_of_admission_to_the_Union) as the 26th state on January 26, 1837, entering as a [free state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slave_states_and_free_states) and quickly developing into an industrial and trade hub that attracted European immigrants, particularly from [Finland](10577.md), [Macedonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonia_\(region\)), and the [Netherlands](21148.md).[^cite_note-13] In the 1930s, migration from [Appalachia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appalachia) and the [Great Migration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Migration_\(African_American\)) of [Black Southerners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Southerners) further shaped the state, especially in Metro Detroit.[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15]

Michigan has a diversified economy with a gross state product of \$771.1 billion as of Q2 2026, ranking [14th](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_GDP) among the 50 states.[^cite_note-BureauEco-16] Although the state has developed a diverse economy, in the early 20th century it became widely known as the center of the [U.S. automotive industry](23576520.md), which developed as a major national economic force. It is home to the country's [three major automobile companies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Three_\(automobile_manufacturers\)) (whose headquarters are all in Metro Detroit). Once exploited for logging and mining, today the sparsely populated Upper Peninsula is important for tourism because of its abundance of natural resources.[^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18] The Lower Peninsula is a center of [manufacturing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing), [forestry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forestry), [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture), [services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Service_\(economics\)), and [high-tech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-tech) industry.

## History

Main article: [History of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Michigan)

For a chronological guide, see [Timeline of Michigan history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_Michigan_history).

When the first European explorers arrived, the most populous tribes in what is now Michigan were the [Algonquian peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algonquian_peoples), which include the [Anishinaabe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anishinaabe) groups of [Ojibwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ojibwe), [Odaawaa/Odawa (Ottawa)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottawa_\(tribe\)), and the [Boodewaadamii/Bodéwadmi (Potawatomi)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potawatomi). The three nations coexisted peacefully as part of a loose confederation called the [Council of Three Fires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Three_Fires). The Ojibwe, whose numbers are estimated to have been at least 35,000, were the largest.[^cite_note-19]

The Ojibwe Indians (also known as Chippewa in the U.S.), an Anishinaabe tribe, were established in Michigan's Upper Peninsula and [northern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Michigan) and [central Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Michigan). Bands also inhabited [Ontario](22218.md) and southern [Manitoba](18926.md), Canada; and northern [Wisconsin](33127.md), and northern and north-central [Minnesota](19590.md). Smaller groups of Algonquian Indians like the [Noquet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noquet) in the Upper Peninsula were present for thousands of years but subsequently absorbed by neighboring tribes before and during European contact.[^cite_note-20] The Ottawa Indians lived primarily south of the [Straits of Mackinac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straits_of_Mackinac) in northern, [western](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Michigan), and [southern Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Michigan), but also in southern Ontario, northern Ohio, and eastern Wisconsin. The Potawatomi were in southern and western Michigan, in addition to northern and central Indiana, northern Illinois, southern Wisconsin, and southern Ontario. Other Algonquian tribes in Michigan, in the south and east, were the [Mascouten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mascouten), the [Menominee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menominee), the [Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_\(tribe\)), the [Sac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sac_people) (or Sauk), and the [Meskwaki (Fox)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meskwaki). The [Wyandot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wyandot_people) were an Iroquoian-speaking people in this area; they were historically known as the Huron by the French, and were the historical adversaries of the [Iroquois Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iroquois_Confederation).[^cite_note-21]

### 17th century

Main articles: [New France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_France) and [Canada (New France)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canada_\(New_France\))



*Père Marquette and the Indians* (1869), by Wilhelm Lamprecht

French *[voyageurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyageurs)* and *[coureurs des bois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coureur_des_bois)* explored and settled in Michigan in the 17th century. The first Europeans to reach what became Michigan were those of [Étienne Brûlé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89tienne_Br%C3%BBl%C3%A9)'s expedition in 1622. The first permanent European settlement was founded in 1668 on the site where Père [Jacques Marquette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Marquette) established [Sault Ste. Marie, Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sault_Ste._Marie,_Michigan), as a base for Catholic missions.[^cite_note-22][^cite_note-23] Missionaries in 1671–75 founded outlying stations at [Saint Ignace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Ignace,_Michigan) and [Marquette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marquette,_Michigan). Jesuit missionaries were well received by the area's Indian populations, with few difficulties or hostilities. In 1679, [Robert Cavelier, Sieur de la Salle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Cavelier,_Sieur_de_la_Salle) built [Fort Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Miami_\(Michigan\)) at present-day [St. Joseph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Joseph,_Michigan). In 1691, the French established a trading post and Fort St. Joseph along the St. Joseph River at the present-day city of [Niles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niles,_Michigan).

### 18th century

Main articles: [French and Indian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_and_Indian_War), [Treaty of Paris (1763)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Paris_\(1763\)), [Province of Quebec (1763–1791)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Quebec_\(1763%E2%80%931791\)), [Indian Reserve (1763)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Reserve_\(1763\)), [American Revolutionary War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War), [Treaty of Paris (1783)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Paris_\(1783\)), [Northwest Ordinance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Ordinance), and [Northwest Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Territory)

In 1701, French explorer and army officer [Antoine de la Mothe Cadillac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_de_la_Mothe_Cadillac) founded [Fort Pontchartrain du Détroit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Pontchartrain_du_D%C3%A9troit) or "Fort Pontchartrain on-the-Strait" on the strait, known as the [Detroit River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detroit_River), between lakes [St. Clair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_St._Clair) and [Erie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Erie).[^cite_note-24] Cadillac had convinced [Louis XIV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_XIV)'s chief minister, [Louis Phélypeaux, Comte de Pontchartrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Ph%C3%A9lypeaux,_Marquis_of_Ph%C3%A9lypeaux), that a permanent community there would strengthen French control over the upper Great Lakes and discourage [British](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Great_Britain) aspirations.

The hundred soldiers and workers who accompanied Cadillac built a fort enclosing one [arpent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arpent) (about 0.85 acres (3,400 m<sup>2</sup>),[^cite_note-tbaytel-25][^cite_note-histdet-26] the equivalent of just under 200 feet (61 m) per side) and named it [Fort Pontchartrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Pontchartrain). Cadillac's wife, Marie Thérèse Guyon, soon moved to Detroit, becoming one of the first European women to settle in what was considered the wilderness of Michigan. The town quickly became a major [fur-trading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fur_trade) and shipping post. The *[Église de Saint-Anne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ste._Anne_de_Detroit_Catholic_Church)* (Catholic Church of Saint Anne) was founded the same year. While the original building does not survive, the congregation remains active. Cadillac later departed to serve as the French governor of Louisiana from 1710 to 1716. French attempts to consolidate the fur trade led to the [Fox Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fox_Wars), in which the Meskwaki (Fox) and their allies fought the French and their Native allies.



[Fort Michilimackinac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Michilimackinac) at the [Straits of Mackinac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straits_of_Mackinac)

At the same time, the French strengthened [Fort Michilimackinac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Michilimackinac) at the Straits of Mackinac to better control their lucrative fur-trading empire. By the mid-18th century, the French also occupied forts at present-day Niles and Sault Ste. Marie, though most of the rest of the region remained unsettled by Europeans. France offered free land to attract families to Detroit, which grew to 800 people in 1765. It was the largest city between Montreal and New Orleans.[^cite_note-27] French settlers also established small farms south of the Detroit River opposite the fort, near a Jesuit mission and Huron village.



Map of [British America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_America) showing the original boundaries of the [Province of Quebec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Quebec) and its [Quebec Act of 1774](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quebec_Act_of_1774) post-annexation boundaries



*[Treaty of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Paris_\(painting\))*, by [Benjamin West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_West) (1783), an unfinished painting of the American diplomatic negotiators of the [Treaty of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Paris_\(1783\)) which brought official conclusion to the [Revolutionary War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War) and gave possession of Michigan and other territory to the new United States

From 1660 until the end of French rule, Michigan was part of the Royal Province of [New France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_France).[^cite_note-28] In 1760, [Montreal](7954681.md) fell to the British forces, ending the [French and Indian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_and_Indian_War) (1754–1763), the North American front of the [Seven Years' War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Years'_War) in Europe. Under the [1763 Treaty of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Paris_\(1763\)), Michigan and the rest of New France east of the Mississippi River were ceded by defeated France to Great Britain.[^cite_note-29] After the [Quebec Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quebec_Act) was passed in 1774, Michigan became part of the British [Province of Quebec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Quebec_\(1763-1791\)). By 1778, Detroit's population reached 2,144 and it was the third-largest city in Quebec province.[^cite_note-30]

During the [American Revolutionary War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War), Detroit was an important British supply center. Most of the inhabitants were French-Canadians or American Indians, many of whom had been allied with the French because of long trading ties. Because of imprecise cartography and unclear language defining the boundaries in the [1783 Treaty of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Paris_\(1783\)), the British retained control of Detroit and Michigan after the [American Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolution). When Quebec split into Lower and Upper Canada in 1791, Michigan was part of [Kent County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kent_County,_Ontario), Upper Canada. It held its first democratic elections in August 1792 to send delegates to the new provincial parliament at Newark (now [Niagara-on-the-Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niagara-on-the-Lake,_Ontario)).[^cite_note-SFarmer-31]

Under terms negotiated in the 1794 [Jay Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jay_Treaty), Britain withdrew from Detroit and Michilimackinac in 1796. It retained control of territory east and south of the Detroit River, which are now included in Ontario, Canada. Questions remained over the boundary for many years, and the United States did not have uncontested control of the Upper Peninsula and [Drummond Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drummond_Island) until 1818 and 1847, respectively.

### 19th century

Main articles: [Indiana Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiana_Territory), [Organic act § List of organic acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_act#List_of_organic_acts), [Michigan Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_Territory), [British occupation of the Michigan Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_occupation_of_the_Michigan_Territory), [Admission to the Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Admission_to_the_Union), [List of U.S. states by date of admission to the Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_by_date_of_admission_to_the_Union), and [Michigan in the American Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_in_the_American_Civil_War)

During the [War of 1812](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_1812), the commander of the [Army of the Northwest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Army_of_the_Northwest_\(United_States\)), Brigadier General [William Hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Hull), surrendered Detroit and the Michigan Territory to the British after a nearly bloodless [siege](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Detroit) in August 1812. An American attempt to retake [British-occupied Michigan Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_occupation_of_the_Michigan_Territory) in January 1813 resulted in a severe American defeat at the [Battle of Frenchtown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Frenchtown). This battle, still ranked as the bloodiest ever fought in the state, had the highest number of American casualties of any battle of the war.[^cite_note-32]

Michigan was retaken by the Americans in September 1813 after the British abandoned Detroit following the [Battle of Lake Erie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Lake_Erie). Forces led by Major General [William Henry Harrison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Henry_Harrison) reoccupied Detroit and launched an invasion of [Upper Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Canada) which culminated in a British defeat at the [Battle of the Thames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Thames). The more northerly areas of Michigan such as [Fort Mackinac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Mackinac) were held by the British until the 1815 [Treaty of Ghent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Ghent) restored the old boundaries. A number of forts, including [Fort Wayne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Wayne_\(Detroit\)), were built by the United States in Michigan during the 19th century out of fears of renewed fighting with Britain.

The population grew slowly until the opening in 1825 of the [Erie Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erie_Canal) through the Mohawk Valley in New York, connecting the Great Lakes to the Hudson River and New York City.[^cite_note-33] The new route attracted a large influx of settlers to the Michigan territory. They worked as farmers, lumbermen, shipbuilders, and merchants and shipped out grain, lumber, and iron ore. By the 1830s, Michigan had 30,000 residents, more than enough to apply and qualify for statehood.[^cite_note-34]

A constitutional convention of assent was held to lead the territory to statehood.[^cite_note-Progress-35] In October 1835 the people approved the constitution of 1835, thereby forming a state government.[^cite_note-centennial-36] [Congressional](31756.md) recognition was delayed pending resolution of a boundary dispute with [Ohio](22199.md) known as the [Toledo War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toledo_War). Congress awarded the "Toledo Strip" to Ohio. Michigan received the western part of the Upper Peninsula as a concession and formally entered the Union as a [free state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slave_and_free_states) on January 26, 1837. The Upper Peninsula proved to be a rich source of lumber, iron, and copper. Michigan led the nation in lumber production from the 1850s to the 1880s. [Railroads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_railroads_in_Michigan) became a major engine of growth from the 1850s onward, with [Detroit the chief hub](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Detroit).

A second wave of French-Canadian immigrants settled in Michigan during the late 19th to early 20th century, working in lumbering areas in counties on the Lake Huron side of the Lower Peninsula, such as the Saginaw Valley, Alpena, and Cheboygan counties, as well as throughout the Upper Peninsula, with large concentrations in Escanaba and the [Keweenaw Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keweenaw_Peninsula).[^cite_note-37]

The first statewide meeting of the [Republican Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_United_States_Republican_Party) took place on July 6, 1854, in [Jackson, Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackson,_Michigan), where the party adopted its platform.[^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39] The state was predominantly Republican until the 1930s, reflecting the political continuity of migrants from across the Northern Tier of New England and New York. Michigan made [a significant contribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_in_the_American_Civil_War) to the [Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_\(American_Civil_War\)) in the [American Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War) and sent more than forty regiments of volunteers to the federal armies.[^cite_note-40]

### 20th century



Sailors of the [Michigan Naval Militia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_Naval_Militia) standing at attention during a parade in the 1920s, following [World War I](4764461.md)

Michigan's economy underwent a transformation at the turn of the 20th century. Many individuals, including [Ransom E. Olds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ransom_E._Olds), [John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Francis_Dodge) and [Horace Dodge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horace_Elgin_Dodge), [Henry Leland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Leland), [David Dunbar Buick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Dunbar_Buick), [Henry Joy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Bourne_Joy), [Charles King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Brady_King), and [Henry Ford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Ford), provided the concentration of engineering know-how and technological enthusiasm to develop the [automotive industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_automobile).[^cite_note-41] Ford's development of the moving [assembly line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assembly_line) in [Highland Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highland_Park,_Michigan) marked a new era in transportation. Like the [steamship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steamship) and railroad, mass production of automobiles was a far-reaching development. More than the forms of public transportation, the affordable automobile transformed private life. Automobile production became the major industry of Detroit and Michigan, and permanently altered the socioeconomic life of the United States and much of the world.

With the growth, the auto industry created jobs in Detroit that attracted immigrants from Europe and migrants from across the United States, including both blacks and whites from the rural [South](179553.md). By 1920, Detroit was the fourth-largest city in the U.S.[^cite_note-42] Residential housing was in short supply, and it took years for the market to catch up with the population boom. By the 1930s, so many immigrants had arrived that more than 30 languages were spoken in the public schools, and [ethnic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic) communities celebrated in annual heritage festivals.[^cite_note-43] Over the years immigrants and migrants contributed greatly to Detroit's diverse urban culture, including popular music trends. The influential [Motown Sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motown) of the 1960s was led by a variety of individual singers and groups.

[Grand Rapids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Rapids,_Michigan), the second-largest city in Michigan, also became an important center of manufacturing. Since 1838, the city has been noted for its furniture industry.[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45] In the 21st century, it is home to five of the world's leading office furniture companies. Grand Rapids is home to a number of major companies including [Steelcase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steelcase), [Amway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amway), and [Meijer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meijer). Grand Rapids is also an important center for [GE Aviation Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GE_Aviation_Systems).

Michigan held its first [United States presidential primary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_presidential_primary) election in 1910. With its rapid growth in industry, it was an important center of industry-wide union organizing, such as the rise of the [United Auto Workers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Auto_Workers).

In 1920 [WWJ (AM)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WWJ_\(AM\)) in Detroit became the first radio station in the United States to regularly broadcast commercial programs. Throughout that decade, some of the country's largest and most ornate [skyscrapers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_in_Detroit) were built in the city. Particularly noteworthy are the [Fisher Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_Building), [Cadillac Place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cadillac_Place), and the [Guardian Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guardian_Building), each of which has been designated as a [National Historic Landmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Historic_Landmark) (NHL).

In 1927 a school bombing took place in [Clinton County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinton_County,_Michigan). The [Bath School disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bath_School_disaster) resulted in the deaths of 38 schoolchildren and constitutes the deadliest mass murder in a school in U.S. history.[^cite_note-46]

Michigan converted much of its manufacturing to satisfy defense needs during [World War II](32927.md); it manufactured 10.9% of the United States military armaments produced during the war, ranking second (behind [New York](8210131.md)) among the 48 states.[^cite_note-47]



[Detroit](8687.md) in the mid-twentieth century. At the time, the city was the fourth-largest U.S. metropolis by population, and held about one-third of the state's population.

Detroit continued to expand through the 1950s, at one point doubling its population in a decade. After World War II, housing was developed in suburban areas outside city cores to meet demand for residences. The federal government subsidized the construction of [interstate highways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_Highway_System), which were intended to strengthen military access, but also allowed commuters and business traffic to travel the region more easily. Since 1960, modern advances in the auto industry have led to increased automation, high-tech industry, and increased suburban growth. Longstanding tensions in Detroit culminated in the [Twelfth Street riot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1967_Detroit_riot) in July 1967.

During the late 1970s and the early 1980s, increasing [fuel costs](8032596.md) and other factors made significantly more global competition and recession among families. Michigan lost a significant amount of [population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_\(human_biology\)) due to global competition and the dramatic unavailability of manufacturing jobs.[^cite_note-48] Meanwhile, Michigan had increased use of technology, specifically when the [IBM Personal Computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_Personal_Computer) started selling in the state, in which became mostly used at work.

### 21st century



The [Detroit–Windsor](7598140.md) area is North America's largest cross-border conurbation, with a combined population of approximately six million people

Michigan became the leading auto-producing state in the U.S., with the industry primarily located throughout the [Midwestern United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midwestern_United_States); Ontario, Canada; and the Southern United States.[^cite_note-NAM-49] With almost ten million residents in 2010, Michigan is a large and influential state, ranking tenth in population among the fifty states. Detroit is the centrally located metropolitan area of the [Great Lakes megalopolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Lakes_megalopolis) and the second-largest metropolitan area in the U.S. (after [Chicago](6886.md)) linking the [Great Lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Lakes) system.

The [Metro Detroit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metro_Detroit) area in [Southeast Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Michigan) is the state's largest metropolitan area (roughly 50% of the population resides there) and the eleventh largest in the United States. The [Grand Rapids metropolitan area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Rapids_metropolitan_area) in Western Michigan is the state's fastest-growing metro area, with more than 1.3 million residents as of 2006.

## Geography

Further information: [Geography of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Michigan), [Protected areas of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protected_areas_of_Michigan), and [List of Michigan state parks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Michigan_state_parks)



Map of the [Saint Lawrence River/Great Lakes Watershed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Lawrence_River) in North America. Its drainage area includes the [Great Lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Lakes), the world's largest system of freshwater lakes. The basin covers nearly all of Michigan.



The [Huron National Wildlife Refuge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huron_National_Wildlife_Refuge), one of the fifteen [federal wildernesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilderness_area) in Michigan

Michigan consists of two peninsulas separated by the [Straits of Mackinac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straits_of_Mackinac). The [45th parallel north](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/45th_parallel_north) runs through the state, marked by highway signs and the Polar-Equator Trail—[^cite_note-50] along a line including [Mission Point Light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mission_Point_Light) near [Traverse City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traverse_City,_Michigan), the towns of [Gaylord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaylord,_Michigan) and [Alpena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpena,_Michigan) in the Lower Peninsula and [Menominee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menominee,_Michigan) in the Upper Peninsula. With the exception of two tiny areas drained by the [Mississippi River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippi_River) by way of the [Wisconsin River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisconsin_River) in the Upper Peninsula and by way of the [Kankakee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kankakee_River)-[Illinois River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illinois_River) in the Lower Peninsula, Michigan is drained by the Great Lakes-[St. Lawrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Lawrence_River) watershed and is the only state with the majority of its land thus drained. No point in the state is more than six miles (9.7 km) from a natural water source or more than 85 miles (137 km) from a Great Lakes shoreline.[^cite_note-51][^cite_note-mifaq-52]

The Great Lakes that border Michigan from east to west are [Lake Erie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Erie), [Lake Huron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Huron), [Lake Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Michigan) and [Lake Superior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Superior). Michigan also borders [Lake St. Clair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_St._Clair), a smaller lake that is part of the Great Lakes system. The state is bounded on the south by the states of [Ohio](22199.md) and [Indiana](21883857.md), sharing land and water boundaries with both. Michigan's western boundaries are almost entirely water boundaries, from south to north, with [Illinois](14849.md) and [Wisconsin](33127.md) in Lake Michigan; then a land boundary with Wisconsin and the Upper Peninsula, that is principally demarcated by the [Menominee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menominee_River) and [Montreal Rivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montreal_River_\(Wisconsin-Michigan\)); then water boundaries again, in Lake Superior, with Wisconsin and Minnesota to the west, capped around by the Canadian province of Ontario to the north and east.

The heavily forested [Upper Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Peninsula_of_Michigan) is relatively mountainous in the west. The [Porcupine Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porcupine_Mountains), part of one of the oldest mountain chains in the world,[^cite_note-53] rise to an altitude of almost 2,000 feet (610 m) above sea level and form the watershed between the streams flowing into Lake Superior and Lake Michigan. The surface on either side of this range is rugged. The state's highest point, in the [Huron Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huron_Mountains) northwest of Marquette, is [Mount Arvon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Arvon) at 1,979 feet (603 m). The peninsula is as large as Connecticut, Delaware, Massachusetts, and Rhode Island combined but has fewer than 330,000 inhabitants. The people are sometimes called "Yoopers" (from "U.P.'ers"), and their speech (the "[Yooper dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yooper_dialect)") has been heavily influenced by the numerous [Scandinavian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scandinavia) and Canadian immigrants who settled the area during the lumbering and [mining boom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_mining_in_Michigan) of the late 19th century.



[Mackinac Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mackinac_Island), an island and resort area at the eastern end of the [Straits of Mackinac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straits_of_Mackinac). More than 80% of the island is preserved as [Mackinac Island State Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mackinac_Island_State_Park).



[Sleeping Bear Dunes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sleeping_Bear_Dunes), along the northwest coast of the Lower Peninsula of Michigan



The [Tahquamenon Falls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tahquamenon_Falls) in the [Upper Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Peninsula_of_Michigan)



The [Pointe Mouillee State Game Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointe_Mouillee_State_Game_Area), one of the 221 [state game and wildlife areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Michigan_state_game_and_wildlife_areas) in Michigan. It encompasses 7,483 acres of hunting, recreational, and protected wildlife and wetland areas at the mouth of the [Huron River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huron_River_\(Michigan\)) at [Lake Erie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Erie), as well as smaller outlying areas within the [Detroit River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detroit_River).

The [Lower Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Peninsula_of_Michigan) is shaped like a mitten and many residents hold up a hand to depict where they are from.[^cite_note-keilman20111209-54] It is 277 miles (446 km) long from north to south and 195 miles (314 km) from east to west and occupies nearly two-thirds of the state's land area. The surface of the peninsula is generally level, broken by conical hills and glacial [moraines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moraine) usually not more than a few hundred feet tall. It is divided by a low water divide running north and south. The larger portion of the state is on the west of this and gradually slopes toward Lake Michigan. The highest point in the Lower Peninsula is either Briar Hill at 1,705 feet (520 m), or one of several points nearby in the vicinity of [Cadillac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cadillac,_Michigan). The lowest point is the surface of Lake Erie at 571 feet (174 m).

The geographic orientation of Michigan's peninsulas makes for a long distance between the ends of the state. [Ironwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ironwood,_Michigan), in the far western Upper Peninsula, lies 630 miles (1,010 kilometers) by highway from [Lambertville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambertville,_Michigan) in the Lower Peninsula's southeastern corner. The geographic isolation of the Upper Peninsula from Michigan's political and population centers makes the region culturally and economically distinct. Frequent attempts to establish the Upper Peninsula as [its own state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superior_\(proposed_U.S._state\)) have failed to gain traction.[^cite_note-55][^cite_note-56]

A feature of Michigan that gives it the distinct shape of a mitten is [the Thumb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Thumb), which projects into Lake Huron, forming [Saginaw Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saginaw_Bay). Other notable peninsulas of Michigan include the [Keweenaw Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keweenaw_Peninsula), which projects northeasterly into Lake Superior from the Upper Peninsula and largely comprising Michigan's [Copper Country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_Country) region, and the [Leelanau Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leelanau_Peninsula), projecting from the Lower Peninsula into Lake Michigan, forming Michigan's "little finger".

Numerous lakes and [marshes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsh) mark both peninsulas, and the coast is much indented. The primary indentations of the Upper Peninsula include [Keweenaw Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keweenaw_Bay) and [Whitefish Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitefish_Bay), along Lake Superior, [Big](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Bay_de_Noc) and [Little](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Bay_de_Noc) Bays de Noc along Lake Michigan. In the Lower Peninsula, [Grand Traverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Traverse_Bay) and [Little Traverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Traverse_Bay) Bays are indentations along Lake Michigan, and [Thunder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thunder_Bay_\(Michigan\)) and Saginaw Bays are indentations along Lake Huron. Michigan has the second longest shoreline of any state—3,288 miles (5,292 km),[^cite_note-57] including 1,056 miles (1,699 km) of island shoreline.[^cite_note-58]

The state has [numerous large islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islands_of_Michigan), the principal ones being the [North Manitou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Manitou_Island) and [South Manitou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Manitou_Island), [Beaver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaver_Island_\(Lake_Michigan\)), and [Fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fox_Islands_\(Michigan\)) groups in Lake Michigan; [Isle Royale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isle_Royale) and [Grande Isle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Island_\(Michigan\)) in Lake Superior; Marquette, [Bois Blanc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bois_Blanc_Island_\(Michigan\)), and [Mackinac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mackinac_Island) islands in Lake Huron; and [Neebish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neebish_Island), [Sugar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_Island_\(Michigan\)), and Drummond islands in [St. Mary's River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Marys_River_\(Michigan-Ontario\)). Michigan has about 150 [lighthouses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lighthouse), the most of any U.S. state.[^cite_note-59] The first lighthouses in Michigan were built between 1818 and 1822. They were built to project light at night and to serve as a landmark during the day to safely guide the passenger ships and freighters traveling the Great Lakes (see: [lighthouses in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lighthouses_in_the_United_States)).

The [state's rivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Michigan_rivers) are generally small, short and shallow, and few are navigable. The principal ones include the Detroit River, St. Marys River, and [St. Clair River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Clair_River) which connect the Great Lakes; the [Au Sable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Au_Sable_River_\(Michigan\)), [Cheboygan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheboygan_River), and [Saginaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saginaw_River), which flow into Lake Huron; the [Ontonagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontonagon_River), and [Tahquamenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tahquamenon_River), which flow into Lake Superior; and the [St. Joseph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Joseph_River_\(Lake_Michigan\)), [Kalamazoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalamazoo_River), [Grand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_River_\(Michigan\)), [Muskegon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muskegon_River), [Manistee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manistee_River), and [Escanaba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escanaba_River), which flow into Lake Michigan. The state has 11,037 inland lakes—totaling 1,305 square miles (3,380 km<sup>2</sup>) of inland water—in addition to 38,575 square miles (99,910 km<sup>2</sup>) of Great Lakes waters.[^cite_note-mifaq-52]

The state is home to several areas maintained by the [National Park Service](161535.md) including: [Isle Royale National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isle_Royale_National_Park), in Lake Superior, about 30 miles (48 km) southeast of Thunder Bay, Ontario. Other national [protected areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protected_areas) in the state include: [Keweenaw National Historical Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keweenaw_National_Historical_Park), [Pictured Rocks National Lakeshore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pictured_Rocks_National_Lakeshore), [Sleeping Bear Dunes National Lakeshore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sleeping_Bear_Dunes_National_Lakeshore), [Huron National Forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huron_National_Forest), [Manistee National Forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manistee_National_Forest), [Hiawatha National Forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiawatha_National_Forest), [Ottawa National Forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottawa_National_Forest) and [Father Marquette National Memorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Father_Marquette_National_Memorial). The largest section of the [North Country National Scenic Trail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Country_National_Scenic_Trail) passes through Michigan.

With 78 [state parks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_park), 19 state recreation areas, and six [state forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_forests), Michigan has the largest state park and [state forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_forest) system of any state.

### Climate

See also: [Climate change in Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_in_Michigan) and [List of Michigan tornadoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Michigan_tornadoes)



[Köppen climate types](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification) of Michigan, using 1991–2020 [climate normals](42778673.md)

Michigan has a [continental climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humid_continental_climate) with two distinct regions. The southern and central parts of the Lower Peninsula (south of Saginaw Bay and from the Grand Rapids area southward) have a warmer climate ([Köppen climate classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification) *Dfa*) with hot summers and cold winters. The northern part of the Lower Peninsula and the entire Upper Peninsula has a more severe climate (Köppen *Dfb*), with warm, but shorter summers and longer, cold to very cold winters. Some parts of the state average high temperatures below freezing from December through February, and into early March in the far northern parts. During the winter through the middle of February, the state is frequently subjected to heavy [lake-effect snow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake-effect_snow). The state averages from 30 to 40 inches (76 to 102 cm) of precipitation annually; however, some areas in the northern lower peninsula and the upper peninsula average almost 160 inches (4,100 mm) of snowfall per year.[^cite_note-60] Michigan's highest recorded temperature is 112 °F (44 °C) at [Mio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mio,_Michigan) on July 13, 1936, and the coldest recorded temperature is −51 °F (−46 °C) at [Vanderbilt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanderbilt,_Michigan) on February 9, 1934.[^cite_note-netstate-61]

The state averages 30 days of thunderstorm activity per year. These can be severe, especially in the southern part of the state. The state averages 17 [tornadoes](37530.md) per year, which are more common in the state's extreme southern section. Portions of the southern border have been almost as vulnerable historically as states further west and in [Tornado Alley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tornado_Alley). For this reason, many communities in the very southern portions of the state have tornado sirens to warn residents of approaching tornadoes. Farther north, in Central Michigan, Northern Michigan, and the Upper Peninsula, tornadoes are rare.[^cite_note-62][^cite_note-Thunderstorm_hazards-63]

### Geology

The geological formation of the state is greatly varied, with the [Michigan Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_Basin) being the most major formation. Primary boulders are found over the entire surface of the Upper Peninsula (being principally of primitive origin), while Secondary deposits cover the entire Lower Peninsula. The Upper Peninsula exhibits Lower [Silurian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silurian) sandstones, limestones, copper and iron bearing rocks, corresponding to the Huronian system of [Canada](5042916.md). The central portion of the Lower Peninsula contains coal measures and rocks of the [Pennsylvanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvanian_\(geology\)) period. [Devonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devonian) and sub-Carboniferous deposits are scattered over the entire state.

Michigan rarely experiences [earthquakes](10106.md), and those that it does experience are generally smaller ones that do not cause significant damage. A 4.6-magnitude earthquake struck in August 1947. More recently, a 4.2-magnitude earthquake occurred on Saturday, May 2, 2015, shortly after noon, about five miles or eight kilometers south of [Galesburg, Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galesburg,_Michigan) (14 kilometers or 9 miles southeast of [Kalamazoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalamazoo)) in central Michigan, about 230 kilometers (140 miles) west of Detroit, according to the Colorado-based [U.S. Geological Survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Geological_Survey)'s [National Earthquake Information Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Earthquake_Information_Center). No major damage or injuries were reported, according to then-Governor [Rick Snyder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rick_Snyder)'s office.[^cite_note-64]

### Administrative divisions

Main article: [Administrative divisions of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Administrative_divisions_of_Michigan)

See also: [List of counties in Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_counties_in_Michigan), [List of municipalities in Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_municipalities_in_Michigan), and [List of Michigan metropolitan areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Michigan_metropolitan_areas)



[Michigan Municipalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_municipalities_in_Michigan)

State government is decentralized among three tiers—statewide, county and [township](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_township). Counties are administrative divisions of the state, and townships are administrative divisions of a county. Both of them exercise state government authority, localized to meet the particular needs of their jurisdictions, as provided by state law. There are 83 [counties in Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_counties_in_Michigan).[^cite_note-65]

Cities, [state universities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_university_system), and villages are vested with [home rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_rule) powers of varying degrees. Home rule cities can generally do anything not prohibited by law. The fifteen state universities have broad power and can do anything within the parameters of their status as educational institutions that is not prohibited by the state constitution. Villages, by contrast, have limited home rule and are not completely autonomous from the county and township in which they are located.

There are two types of township in Michigan: *general law* township and *charter*. [Charter township](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charter_township) status was created by the Legislature in 1947 and grants additional powers and stream-lined administration in order to provide greater protection against annexation by a city. As of April 2001, there were 127 charter townships in Michigan. In general, charter townships have many of the same powers as a city but without the same level of obligations. For example, a charter township can have its own fire department, water and sewer department, police department, and so on—just like a city—but it is not *required* to have those things, whereas cities *must* provide those services. Charter townships can opt to use county-wide services instead, such as deputies from the county sheriff's office instead of a home-based force of ordinance officers.

Since the [1800 U.S. census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1800_United_States_census), Michigan has experienced relatively positive and stable population growth trends; beginning with a population of 3,757, the [2010 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_United_States_census) recorded 9,883,635 residents. At the [2020 United States census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_United_States_census), its population was 10,077,331, an increase of 2.03% since 2010's tabulation. According to the [United States Census Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Census_Bureau), it is the third-most populous state in the [Midwest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midwestern_United_States) and its [East North Central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_North_Central_states) subregion, behind Ohio and Illinois.

The [center of population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_population) of Michigan is in [Shiawassee County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shiawassee_County,_Michigan), in the southeastern corner of the civil township of [Bennington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bennington_Township,_Michigan), which is northwest of the village of [Morrice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morrice,_Michigan).[^cite_note-69]

According to the [American Immigration Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Immigration_Council) in 2019, an estimated 6.8% of Michiganders were immigrants, while 3.8% were native-born U.S. citizens with at least one immigrant parent.[^cite_note-\:0-70] Numbering approximately 678,255 according to the 2019 survey, the majority of Michigander immigrants came from Mexico (11.5%), India (11.3%), Iraq (7.5%), China (5.3%), and Canada (5.3%); the primary occupations of its immigrants were technology, agriculture, and healthcare. Among its immigrant cohort, there were 108,105 undocumented immigrants, making up 15.9% of the total immigrant population.[^cite_note-\:0-70]

According to [HUD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Housing_and_Urban_Development)'s 2022 [Annual Homeless Assessment Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annual_Homeless_Assessment_Report_to_Congress), there were an estimated 8,206 [homeless](19394651.md) people in Michigan.[^cite_note-71][^cite_note-72]

Since colonial European and American settlement, the majority of Michigan's population has been predominantly [non-Hispanic or non-Latino white](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Hispanic_whites); [Americans of European descent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americans_of_European_descent) live throughout every county in the state, and most of Metro Detroit. Large European American groups include those of [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Americans), [British](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Americans), [Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Americans), [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_Americans) and [Belgian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgian_Americans) ancestry.[^cite_note-86] [Scandinavian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nordic_and_Scandinavian_Americans) and [Finnish Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finnish_Americans) have a notable presence in the Upper Peninsula.[^cite_note-87] [Western Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Michigan) is known for its [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Americans) heritage, especially in [Holland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holland,_Michigan) and metropolitan Grand Rapids.[^cite_note-88]

[Black and African Americans](2154.md)—coming to Detroit and other northern cities in the Great Migration of the early 20th century—have formed a majority of the population in Detroit and other cities including [Flint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flint,_Michigan) and [Benton Harbor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benton_Harbor,_Michigan). Since the 2021 census estimates—while Detroit was still the largest city in Michigan with a majority black population—it was no longer the largest black-majority city in the U.S., citing crime and higher-paying jobs given to whites.[^cite_note-89][^cite_note-90]

As of 2007, about 300,000 people in [Southeastern Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeastern_Michigan) trace their descent from the Middle East and Asia.[^cite_note-91] [Dearborn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dearborn,_Michigan) has a sizeable [Arab American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Americans) community, with many [Assyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_Americans), and [Lebanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_Americans) who immigrated for jobs in the auto industry in the 1920s, along with more recent [Yemenis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yemeni_American) and [Iraqis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_Americans).[^cite_note-92] Dearborn also houses an [Armenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Americans) minority population, initially consisting of entirely young men.[^cite_note-93][^cite_note-Woodfordp186187-94] There is a statue of [Komitas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Komitas), an Armenian priest and composer, standing on Jefferson Avenue, in Detroit.[^cite_note-95] As of 2007, almost 8,000 [Hmong people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hmong_people) lived in the state of Michigan, about double their 1999 presence in the state.[^cite_note-Kaiser2-96] Most lived in northeastern Detroit, but they had been increasingly moving to Pontiac and Warren.[^cite_note-Michdaily-97] By 2015, the number of Hmong in the Detroit city limits had significantly declined.[^cite_note-Rosenremaining-98] Lansing hosts a statewide Hmong New Year Festival.[^cite_note-Michdaily-97] The Hmong community also had a prominent portrayal in the 2008 film *[Gran Torino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gran_Torino)*, which was set in Detroit.

As of 2015, 80% of Michigan's Japanese population lived in the counties of Macomb, Oakland, Washtenaw, and Wayne in the Detroit and Ann Arbor areas.[^cite_note-WilkinsonSookp158-99] As of April 2013, the largest Japanese national population is in [Novi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novi,_Michigan), with 2,666 Japanese residents, and the next largest populations are respectively in Ann Arbor, [West Bloomfield Township](259532.md), [Farmington Hills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farmington_Hills,_Michigan), and [Battle Creek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_Creek,_Michigan). The state has 481 Japanese employment facilities providing 35,554 local jobs. 391 of them are in Southeast Michigan, providing 20,816 jobs, and the 90 in other regions in the state provide 14,738 jobs. The Japanese Direct Investment Survey of the [Consulate-General of Japan, Detroit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consulate-General_of_Japan,_Detroit) stated more than 2,208 additional Japanese residents were employed in the State of Michigan as of 1 October 2012, than in 2011.[^cite_note-100] During the 1990s, the Japanese population of Michigan experienced an increase, and many Japanese people with children moved to particular areas for their proximity to Japanese grocery stores and high-performing schools.[^cite_note-WilkinsonSookp158-99]

Following [British](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_colonization_of_the_Americas) and [French colonization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_colonization_of_the_Americas) of the region surrounding Michigan, [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_the_United_States) became the dominant religion, with [Roman Catholicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholicism) historically being the largest single Christian group for the state. Until the 19th century, the Roman Catholic Church was the only organized religious group in Michigan, reflecting the territory's French colonial roots. Detroit's St. Anne's parish, established in 1701 by Antoine de la Mothe Cadillac, is the second-oldest Roman Catholic parish in the United States.[^cite_note-104] On March 8, 1833, the [Holy See](13393.md) formally established a diocese in the Michigan territory, which included all of Michigan, Wisconsin, Minnesota, and the Dakotas east of the Mississippi River. When Michigan became a state in 1837, the boundary of the Roman Catholic Diocese of Detroit was redrawn to coincide with that of the state; the other dioceses were later carved out from the Detroit Diocese but remain part of the [Ecclesiastical Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesiastical_Province) of Detroit.[^cite_note-105] Several [Native American religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_American_religions) have been practiced in Michigan.

In 2020, there were 1,492,732 adherents of Roman Catholicism.[^cite_note-\:1-106] There's also a significant [Independent Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_Catholicism) presence in Metro Detroit, including the [Ecumenical Catholic Church of Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecumenical_Catholic_Church_of_Christ) established by Archbishop Karl Rodig; the see of this church operates in a former Roman Catholic parish church.[^cite_note-107][^cite_note-108][^cite_note-109]

With the introduction of Protestantism to the state, it began to form the largest collective Christian group. In 2010, the Association of Religion Data Archives reported the largest Protestant denomination was the [United Methodist Church](32304.md) with 228,521 adherents;\[^cite_note-[www.thearda.com3-110](http://www.thearda.com3-110/)] followed by the [Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lutheran_Church%E2%80%93Missouri_Synod) with 219,618, and the [Evangelical Lutheran Church in America](248255.md) with 120,598 adherents. The [Christian Reformed Church in North America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Reformed_Church_in_North_America) had almost 100,000 members and more than 230 congregations in Michigan.[^cite_note-111] The [Reformed Church in America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reformed_Church_in_America) had 76,000 members and 154 congregations in the state.[^cite_note-112] By the 2020 study, [non- and inter-denominational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nondenominational_Christianity) Protestant churches formed the largest Protestant group in Michigan, numbering 508,904. The Lutheran Church–Missouri Synod grew to become the second-largest single Christian denomination, and United Methodists declined to being the third-largest. The Lutheran Protestant tradition was introduced by [German](152735.md) and Scandinavian immigrants. Altogether, Baptists numbered 321,581 between the [National Missionary Baptists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Missionary_Baptist_Convention_of_America), [National Baptists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Baptist_Convention,_USA,_Inc.), [American Baptists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Baptist_Churches_USA), [Southern Baptists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Baptist_Convention), [National Baptists of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Baptist_Convention_of_America_International,_Inc.), [Progressive National Baptists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_National_Baptist_Convention), and [Full Gospel Baptists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_Gospel_Baptist_Church_Fellowship); black Baptists formed the largest constituency.\[^cite_note-\:1-106] In West Michigan, Dutch immigrants fled from the specter of religious persecution and famine in the Netherlands around 1850 and settled in and around what is now Holland, Michigan, establishing a "colony" on American soil that fervently held onto Calvinist doctrine that established a significant presence of Reformed churches.[^cite_note-113]

In the same 2010 survey, Jewish adherents in the state of Michigan were estimated at 44,382, and Muslims at 120,351.[^cite_note-114] The first Jewish synagogue in the state was [Temple Beth El](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_Beth_El_\(Detroit,_Michigan\)), founded by twelve German Jewish families in Detroit in 1850.[^cite_note-115] Islam was introduced by immigrants from the Near East during the 20th century.[^cite_note-116] Michigan is home to the largest mosque in North America, the [Islamic Center of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Center_of_America) in Dearborn. Battle Creek, Michigan, is also the birthplace of the [Seventh-day Adventist Church](28632.md), which was founded on May 21, 1863.[^cite_note-Adventist_org-117][^cite_note-New_indian-118]

## Economy

See also: [Michigan locations by per capita income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_locations_by_per_capita_income) and [List of companies based in Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_companies_based_in_Michigan)



The [Detroit-Windsor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detroit-Windsor) border crossing is the busiest international border crossing in North America by trade volume, with approximately one-third of all [Canada–U.S. trade](17206485.md) passing through this link. Pictured in the background is the under-construction [Gordie Howe International Bridge](26342191.md), which will complement the existing [Ambassador Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambassador_Bridge), shown in the foreground.



[Detroit Financial District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detroit_Financial_District) in 2025

Michigan is among the wealthiest regions in the world. As of 2026, its [gross state product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_GDP) (GSP) is \$771.1 billion (\$76,130 per capita), ranking 14th among the 50 U.S. states.[^cite_note-BureauEco-16] Michigan's nominal GDP is larger than that of every nation in [Africa](1055517.md) and all but [Brazil](3634.md) in [South America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_South_America). Its nominal per capita GDP exceeds all but two nations in the [European Union](1598533.md). When adjusted for [regional price parities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purchasing_power_parity), Michigan's per capita GDP is significantly higher than the European average, surpassing the figures of [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_France), [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Italy), and the [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_the_United_Kingdom).

As of December 2025, the state recorded total [non-farm employment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonfarm_payrolls) of 4,543,300 across 315,965 employer establishments. Michigan is home to 15 [Fortune 500 companies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortune_500_companies) as of 2024, and in 2025 alone, 152,800 new businesses were formed.[^cite_note-119] The state's 983,079 [small businesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_businesses) represent 99.6% of all businesses and employ 47.7% of the state's workforce.[^cite_note-120] As of May 2025, Michigan had an unemployment rate of 5.4%.[^cite_note-121]

The state's five largest employment sectors are education and health services; professional and business services; manufacturing; trade, transportation, and utilities; and leisure and hospitality. In output, the five largest sectors are manufacturing, followed by financial services; professional and business services; education and health services; and trade, transportation, and utilities.[^cite_note-122]

As the busiest international border crossing in North America by trade volume, the [Detroit–Windsor crossing](7598140.md) plays a pivotal role in the [global supply chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_supply_chain_management), handling approximately one third of all [Canada–U.S. trade](17206485.md). Additionally, the [Soo Locks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soo_Locks), which connect [Lake Superior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Superior) to the lower Great Lakes, is the world's busiest [lock system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lock_\(water_navigation\)) by tonnage, with 80 million tons passing through annually.[^cite_note-123]

Michigan's economy is heavily reliant on trade, with [international commerce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_business) accounting for roughly 42% of the state's economy; Canada remains its largest trading partner.[^cite_note-124] Manufacturing, particularly the [automotive industry](163778.md), generates one seventh of the state's economy.[^cite_note-125] In 2023, Michigan exported \$81 billion worth of goods.[^cite_note-\:213-126] Transportation equipment, including motor vehicles and parts, is the state's top export, accounting for 45% of all exports in 2022.[^cite_note-\:213-126] Michigan ranks first in the nation for the export of motor vehicles and parts.[^cite_note-127]

### Taxation and regulation

Michigan's personal [income tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Income_tax) is a flat rate of 4.25%. In addition, 24 cities impose income taxes; rates are set at 1% for residents and 0.5% for non-residents in all but four cities.[^cite_note-city-income-tax-128] Michigan's state [sales tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sales_tax) is 6%, though items such as food and medication are exempted. [Property taxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Property_tax) are assessed on the local level, but every property owner's local assessment contributes six [mills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mill_\(currency\)) (a rate of \$6 per \$1000 of property value) to the statutory State Education Tax. Property taxes are appealable to local boards of review and need the approval of the local electorate to exceed millage rates prescribed by state law and local charters. In 2011, the state repealed its business tax and replaced it with a 6% corporate income tax which substantially reduced taxes on business.[^cite_note-129][^cite_note-130] Article IX of the Constitution of the State of Michigan also provides limitations on how much the state can tax.

A 6% [use tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Use_tax) is levied on goods purchased outside the state (that are brought in and used in state), at parity with the sales tax.[^cite_note-131] The use tax applies to internet sales/purchases from outside Michigan and is equivalent to the sales tax.[^cite_note-132]

On December 20, 2019, Governor [Gretchen Whitmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gretchen_Whitmer) signed a package of bills into law effectively legalizing [online gambling](2213921.md) activities in Michigan, which allowed commercial and [tribal casinos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribal_casinos) to apply for internet gaming licenses.[^cite_note-133]

### Agriculture

See also: [Michigan wine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_wine) and [Cherry production in Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherry_production_in_Michigan)



[Chateau Chantal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chateau_Chantal), a winery and designated [American Viticultural Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Viticultural_Area), is located on the [Old Mission Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Mission_Peninsula)



Michigan is the leading U.S. producer of [tart cherries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prunus_cerasus), [blueberries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blueberries), [pickled cucumbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pickled_cucumbers), [navy beans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navy_beans), and [petunias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petunias)

A wide variety of commodity crops, fruits, and vegetables are grown in Michigan, making it second only to California among US states in the diversity of its agriculture.[^cite_note-134] The state has 54,800 farms utilizing 10,000,000 acres (40,000 km<sup>2</sup>) of land which sold \$6.49 billion worth of products in 2010.[^cite_note-135] The most valuable agricultural product is milk. Leading crops include corn, soybeans, flowers, wheat, sugar beets, and potatoes. Livestock in the state included 78,000 sheep, a million cattle, a million hogs, and more than three million chickens. Livestock products accounted for 38% of the value of agricultural products while crops accounted for the majority.

Michigan is a leading grower of fruit in the US, including blueberries, [tart cherries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherry_production_in_Michigan), apples, grapes, and peaches.[^cite_note-MIAG-136][^cite_note-137] Michigan produces 70 percent of the country's cherries. Most of these cherries are [Montmorency cherries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montmorency_cherries).[^cite_note-138] Plums, pears, and strawberries are also grown in Michigan. These fruits are mainly grown in [West Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Michigan) due to the moderating effect of Lake Michigan on the climate. There is also significant fruit production, especially cherries, but also grapes, apples, and other fruits, in northwest Michigan along Lake Michigan. Michigan produces [wines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_wine), beers and a multitude of processed food products. [Kellogg's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kellogg's) cereal is based in Battle Creek, Michigan and processes many locally grown foods. Thornapple Valley, [Ball Park Franks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ball_Park_Franks), [Koegel Meat Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koegel_Meat_Company), and [Hebrew National](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_National) sausage companies are all based in Michigan.

Michigan is home to very fertile land in the [Saginaw Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tri-Cities_\(Michigan\)) and Thumb areas. Products grown there include corn, sugar beets, navy beans, and soybeans. Sugar beet harvesting usually begins the first of October. It takes the sugar factories about five months to process the 3.7 million tons of sugarbeets into 485,000 tons of pure, white sugar.[^cite_note-139] Michigan's largest sugar refiner, [Michigan Sugar Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_Sugar_Company)[^cite_note-140] is the largest east of the Mississippi River and the fourth largest in the nation. Michigan sugar brand names are Pioneer Sugar and the newly incorporated Big Chief Sugar. Potatoes are grown in Northern Michigan, and corn is dominant in Central Michigan. Alfalfa, cucumbers, and asparagus are also grown.

### Tourism

See also: [List of National Historic Landmarks in Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_National_Historic_Landmarks_in_Michigan), [List of Registered Historic Places in Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Registered_Historic_Places_in_Michigan), and [List of museums in Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_museums_in_Michigan)



[Holland, Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holland,_Michigan), is the home of the [Tulip Time Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulip_Time_Festival), the largest [tulip festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulip_festival) in the U.S.

As of 2011, Michigan's tourists spent \$17.2 billion per year in the state, supporting 193,000 tourism jobs.[^cite_note-141] Michigan's tourism website ranks among the busiest in the nation.[^cite_note-142] Destinations draw vacationers, hunters, and nature enthusiasts from across the United States and Canada. Michigan is over 50% forest land,[^cite_note-143] much of it quite remote. The forests, lakes and thousands of miles of beaches are top attractions. Event tourism draws large numbers to occasions like the [Tulip Time Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulip_Time_Festival) and the [National Cherry Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Cherry_Festival).

In 2006, the Michigan State Board of Education mandated all public schools in the state hold their first day of school after Labor Day, in accordance with the new post-Labor Day school law. A survey found 70% of all tourism business comes directly from Michigan residents, and the Michigan Hotel, Motel, & Resort Association claimed the shorter summer between school years cut into the annual tourism season.[^cite_note-144] However, a bill introduced in 2023 would cancel this requirement, allowing individual districts to decide when their school year should begin.[^cite_note-145][^cite_note-146]



[Mackinac Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mackinac_Island) is well known for cultural events and a wide variety of architectural styles, including the [Victorian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_era) [Grand Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Hotel_\(Mackinac_Island\)).

[Tourism in metropolitan Detroit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism_in_metropolitan_Detroit) draws visitors to leading attractions, especially [The Henry Ford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Henry_Ford), the [Detroit Institute of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detroit_Institute_of_Arts), the [Detroit Zoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detroit_Zoo), and to [sports in Detroit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sports_in_Detroit). Other museums include the [Detroit Historical Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detroit_Historical_Museum), the [Charles H. Wright Museum of African American History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_H._Wright_Museum_of_African_American_History), museums in the [Cranbrook Educational Community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cranbrook_Educational_Community), and the [Arab American National Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_American_National_Museum). The metro area offers four major casinos, [MGM Grand Detroit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MGM_Grand_Detroit), [Hollywood Casino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hollywood_Casino_at_Greektown), [Motor City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motor_City_Casino), and [Caesars Windsor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caesars_Windsor) in Windsor, Ontario, Canada; moreover, Detroit is the largest American city and metropolitan region to offer casino resorts.[^cite_note-Mink-147]

Hunting and fishing are significant industries in the state. Charter boats are based in many Great Lakes cities to fish for salmon, trout, walleye, and perch. Michigan ranks first in the nation in licensed hunters (over one million) who contribute \$2 billion annually to its economy. More than three-quarters of a million hunters participate in [white-tailed deer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White-tailed_deer) season alone. Many school districts in rural areas of Michigan cancel school on the opening day of firearm deer season, because of attendance concerns.[^cite_note-148]



[Marquette, Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marquette,_Michigan), is home to a vast [snowmobile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snowmobile) trail system.

Michigan's Department of Natural Resources manages the largest dedicated state forest system in the nation. The forest products industry and recreational users contribute \$12 billion and 200,000 associated jobs annually to the state's economy. Public hiking and hunting access has also been secured in extensive commercial forests. The state has the highest number of golf courses and registered [snowmobiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snowmobile) in the nation.[^cite_note-149]

The state has numerous [historical markers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_marker), which can themselves become the center of a tour.[^cite_note-150] The [Great Lakes Circle Tour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Lakes_Circle_Tour) is a designated scenic road system connecting all of the Great Lakes and the St. Lawrence River.[^cite_note-151]

With its position in relation to the Great Lakes and the countless ships that have foundered over the many years they have been used as a transport route for people and bulk cargo, Michigan is a world-class scuba diving destination. The [Michigan Underwater Preserves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_Underwater_Preserves) are 13 underwater areas where wrecks are protected for the benefit of sport divers.

## Education

See also: [List of colleges and universities in Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_colleges_and_universities_in_Michigan), [List of high schools in Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_high_schools_in_Michigan), and [List of school districts in Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_school_districts_in_Michigan)

[Washtenaw International High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ypsilanti_Community_Schools) ranked 107th.[^cite_note-164]

The [University of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Michigan) is Michigan's oldest higher educational institution and among the oldest [research universities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_universities) in the nation. It was founded in 1817, 20 years before [Michigan Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_Territory) achieved statehood.[^cite_note-165][^cite_note-166][^cite_note-167] [Kalamazoo College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalamazoo_College) is the state's oldest private liberal arts college, founded in 1833 by a group of [Baptist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptist) ministers as the Michigan and Huron Institute. From 1840 to 1850, the college operated as the Kalamazoo Branch of the University of Michigan. [Methodist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodist) settlers in [Spring Arbor Township](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_Arbor_Township) founded [Albion College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albion_College) in 1835. It is the state's second-oldest private liberal arts college.[^cite_note-168]

[Michigan Technological University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_Technological_University) is the first post-secondary institution in the [Upper Peninsula of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Peninsula_of_Michigan), founded in 1885 as the Michigan Mining School. [Eastern Michigan University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Michigan_University) was founded in 1849 as the Michigan State Normal School for the training of teachers.[^cite_note-169] It was the nation's fourth-oldest [normal school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_school#United_States) and the first U.S. normal school outside [New England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_England). In 1899, the Michigan State Normal School became the nation's first normal school to offer a four-year curriculum. [Michigan State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_State_University) was founded in 1855 as the nation's first [agricultural college](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_college).

The [Carnegie Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnegie_Foundation_for_the_Advancement_of_Teaching) classifies eight of the state's institutions (Michigan State University, Michigan Technological University, Eastern Michigan University, [Wayne State University](302260.md), [Central Michigan University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Michigan_University), [Western Michigan University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Michigan_University), [Oakland University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oakland_University), University of Michigan) as [research universities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_universities).[^cite_note-170]

The state of Michigan has six [MD-granting medical schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_medical_schools_in_the_United_States): [Covenant HealthCare College of Medicine at Central Michigan University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Michigan_University), [University of Michigan Medical School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Michigan_Medical_School), [Michigan State University College of Human Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_State_University_College_of_Human_Medicine), [Oakland University William Beaumont School of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oakland_University_William_Beaumont_School_of_Medicine), [Wayne State University School of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayne_State_University_School_of_Medicine), and [Western Michigan University Homer Stryker M.D. School of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Michigan_University_Homer_Stryker_M.D._School_of_Medicine). Additionally, Michigan is home to five [American Bar Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Bar_Association) accredited [law schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_law_schools_in_the_United_States): [Michigan State University College of Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_State_University_College_of_Law), [Cooley Law School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooley_Law_School), [University of Detroit Mercy School of Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Detroit_Mercy_School_of_Law), [University of Michigan Law School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Michigan_Law_School), and [Wayne State University Law School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayne_State_University_Law_School).

In 2006, the [University Research Corridor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_Research_Corridor) in Michigan was formed with the University of Michigan, Michigan State University, and Wayne State University as members. ## Infrastructure

### Energy

See also: [List of power stations in Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_power_stations_in_Michigan)



[Enrico Fermi Nuclear Generating Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrico_Fermi_Nuclear_Generating_Station) on the shore of [Lake Erie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Erie), near [Monroe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monroe,_Michigan)

In 2020, Michigan consumed 113,740- [gigawatt-hours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilowatt_hour#Watt-hour_multiples) (GWh) of electrical energy and produced 116,700 (GWh) of electrical energy.[^cite_note-MI-ESRP-174]

[Coal power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_power) is Michigan's leading source of electricity, producing roughly half its supply or 53,100 GWh of electrical energy (12.6 GW total capacity) in 2020.[^cite_note-MI-ESRP-174] Although Michigan has no active coal mines, coal is easily moved from other states by train and across the [Great Lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Lakes) by [lake freighters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_freighter). The lower price of natural gas is leading to the closure of most coal plants, with Consumer Energy planning to close all of its remaining coal plants by 2025;[^cite_note-175] DTE plans to retire 2100MW of coal power by 2023.[^cite_note-176] The coal-fired [Monroe Power Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monroe_Power_Plant) in [Monroe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monroe,_Michigan), on the western shore of [Lake Erie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Erie), is the nation's 11th-largest electric plant, with a net capacity of 3,400 MW.

[Nuclear power](22153.md) is also a significant source of electrical power in Michigan, producing roughly one-quarter of the state's supply or 28,000-[gigawatt-hours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilowatt_hour#Watt-hour_multiples) (GWh) of electrical energy (4.3 GW total capacity) in 2020.[^cite_note-MI-ESRP-174] The three active nuclear power plants supply Michigan with about 26% of its electricity. [Donald C. Cook Nuclear Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_C._Cook_Nuclear_Plant), just north of [Bridgman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridgman,_Michigan), is the state's largest [nuclear power plant](207347.md), with a net capacity of 2,213 MW. The [Enrico Fermi Nuclear Generating Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrico_Fermi_Nuclear_Generating_Station) is the second-largest, with a net capacity of 1,150 MW. It is also one of the two nuclear power plants in the [Detroit metropolitan area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detroit_metropolitan_area) (within a 50-mile radius of Detroit's city center), about halfway between [Detroit](8687.md) and [Toledo, Ohio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toledo,_Ohio), the other being the [Davis–Besse Nuclear Power Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davis%E2%80%93Besse_Nuclear_Power_Station), in [Ottawa County, Ohio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottawa_County,_Ohio). The [Palisades Nuclear Power Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palisades_Nuclear_Power_Plant), south of [South Haven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Haven,_Michigan), closed in May 2022.[^cite_note-177] The [Big Rock Point Nuclear Power Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Rock_Point_Nuclear_Power_Plant), Michigan's first nuclear power plant and the nation's fifth, was decommissioned in 1997.

Utility companies were required to generate at least 10% of their energy from renewable sources by 2015, under Public Act 295 of 2008. In 2016, the legislature set another mandate to reach at least 12.5% renewable energy by 2019 and 15% by end of year 2021, which all utilities subject to the law successfully met. By the end of 2022, Michigan had at least 6 GW of renewable generating capacity, and was projected to have at least 8 GW by the end of 2026. Wind energy accounted for 59% of all Michigan energy credits in 2021.[^cite_note-178][^cite_note-179]

## Government

See also: [List of governors of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_governors_of_Michigan) and [Michigan's congressional delegations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan's_congressional_delegations)

### State government

Main article: [Government of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Michigan)



The [Michigan State Capitol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_State_Capitol) in [Lansing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lansing) houses the [legislative branch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_Legislature) of the government of the US state of Michigan.

Michigan is governed as a republic, with three [branches of government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separation_of_powers): the [executive branch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_branch) consisting of the [Governor of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governor_of_Michigan) and the other independently elected constitutional officers; the [legislative branch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislative_branch) consisting of the [House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_State_House_of_Representatives) and [Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_Senate); and the [judicial branch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judicial_branch). The [Michigan Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_Constitution) allows for the direct participation of the electorate by statutory [initiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_initiative) and [referendum](51582.md), [recall](129203.md), and constitutional initiative and [referral](429650.md) (Article II, § 9,[^cite_note-190] defined as "the power to propose laws and to enact and reject laws, called the initiative, and the power to approve or reject laws enacted by the legislature, called the referendum. The power of initiative extends only to laws which the legislature may enact under this constitution"). [Lansing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lansing,_Michigan) is the [state capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_capitals_in_the_United_States) and is home to all three branches of state government.



The floor of the [Michigan House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_House_of_Representatives)

The governor and the other state constitutional officers serve four-year terms and may be re-elected only once. The current governor is [Gretchen Whitmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gretchen_Whitmer). Michigan has two official [Governor's Residences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_Governor's_Residence); one is in Lansing, and the other is on [Mackinac Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mackinac_Island). The other constitutionally elected executive officers are the [lieutenant governor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lieutenant_Governor_of_Michigan), who is elected on a joint ticket with the governor; the [secretary of state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_of_State_of_Michigan); and the [attorney general](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attorney_General_of_Michigan). The lieutenant governor presides over the Senate (voting only in case of a tie) and is also a member of the cabinet. The secretary of state is the chief elections officer and is charged with running many licensure programs including motor vehicles, all of which are done through the branch offices of the secretary of state.

The [Michigan Legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_Legislature) consists of a 38-member Senate and 110-member House of Representatives. Members of both houses of the legislature are elected through [first past the post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_past_the_post) elections by single-member electoral districts of near-equal population that often have boundaries which coincide with county and municipal lines. Senators serve four-year terms concurrent to those of the governor, while representatives serve two-year terms. The [Michigan State Capitol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_State_Capitol) was dedicated in 1879 and has hosted the executive and legislative branches of the state ever since.



Governor Gretchen Whitmer speaking at a National Guard ceremony in 2019

The Michigan judiciary consists of two courts with primary jurisdiction (the Circuit Courts and the District Courts), one intermediate level appellate court (the [Michigan Court of Appeals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_Court_of_Appeals)), and the [Michigan Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_Supreme_Court). There are several administrative courts and specialized courts. District courts are trial courts of [limited jurisdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limited_jurisdiction), handling most traffic violations, small claims, [misdemeanors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misdemeanor), and civil suits where the amount contended is below \$25,000. District courts are often responsible for handling the preliminary examination and for setting bail in felony cases. District court judges are elected to terms of six years. In a few locations, municipal courts have been retained to the exclusion of the establishment of district courts. There are 57 circuit courts in the State of Michigan, which have [original jurisdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Original_jurisdiction) over all civil suits where the amount contended in the case exceeds \$25,000 and all criminal cases involving [felonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felony). Circuit courts are also the only trial courts in the State of Michigan which possess the power to issue [equitable remedies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equitable_remedies). Circuit courts have [appellate jurisdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appellate_jurisdiction) from district and municipal courts, as well as from decisions and decrees of state agencies. Most counties have their own circuit court, but sparsely populated counties often share them. Circuit court judges are elected to terms of six years. State appellate court judges are elected to terms of six years, but vacancies are filled by an appointment by the governor. There are four divisions of the Court of Appeals in Detroit, Grand Rapids, Lansing, and Marquette. Cases are heard by the Court of Appeals by panels of three judges, who examine the application of the law and not the facts of the case unless there has been grievous error pertaining to questions of fact. The Michigan Supreme Court consists of seven members who are elected on non-partisan ballots for staggered eight-year terms. The Supreme Court has original jurisdiction only in narrow circumstances but holds appellate jurisdiction over the entire state judicial system.

### Law

Main article: [Law of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_Michigan)



[Michigan Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_Supreme_Court) at the Hall of Justice

Michigan has had four constitutions, the first of which was ratified on October 5 and 6, 1835.[^cite_note-191] There were also constitutions from 1850 and 1908, in addition to the current constitution from 1963. The current document has a preamble, 11 articles, and one section consisting of a schedule and temporary provisions. The current constitution also includes a provision beginning in the general election held in 1978, and every 16 years thereafter, the question of a general revision of the constitution shall be submitted to the electors of the state, next scheduled to be considered November 2026.[^cite_note-192][^cite_note-193] Michigan, like every U.S. state except [Louisiana](18130.md), has a [common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law) legal system.

### Politics

Main article: [Politics of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics_of_Michigan)



[2024 U.S. presidential election results](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_States_presidential_election_in_Michigan) by county in Michigan&#x20;

&#x20; Democratic

&#x20; Republican

Having been a [Democratic](5043544.md)-leaning state at the presidential level since the 1990s, Michigan has evolved into a [swing state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swing_state) after [Donald Trump](4848272.md) won the state in [2016](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_United_States_presidential_election). He then won it again in [2024](59568546.md), after losing it by a slim 2.8% to Democrat [Joe Biden](145422.md) in [2020](48410011.md). Governors since the 1970s have alternated between the Democrats and [Republicans](32070.md), and statewide offices including [attorney general](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_Attorney_General), [secretary of state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_Secretary_of_State), and [senator](24909346.md) have been held by members of both parties in varying proportion. Additionally, from [1994](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1994_Michigan_gubernatorial_election) until [2022](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Michigan_gubernatorial_election), the governor-elect had always come from the party opposite the presidency. Following the [2024 elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Michigan_elections), control of [Michigan Legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_Legislature) is split, with the Democratic Party having a slim majority of two seats in the Senate while the Republican Party holds a 58-seat majority in the House. The state's [congressional delegation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan's_congressional_delegations) is commonly split, with one party or the other typically holding a narrow majority; as of 2025 Republicans have a 7–6 majority.

Michigan was the home of [Gerald Ford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerald_Ford), the 38th president of the United States. Born in Nebraska, he moved as an infant to Grand Rapids.[^cite_note-194][^cite_note-ford-Nebraska-195] The [Gerald R. Ford Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerald_R._Ford_Museum) is in Grand Rapids, and the [Gerald R. Ford Presidential Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerald_R._Ford_Presidential_Library) is on the campus of his alma mater, the University of Michigan in Ann Arbor.

In a 2020 study, Michigan was ranked as the 13th easiest state for citizens to vote in.[^cite_note-196] Amendments to the constitution in 2020 and 2022 also provide for voting by mail, audits of statewide election results, and to vote free of harassment, threats, and intimidation.[^cite_note-197] The [Cato Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cato_Institute) ranks Michigan 7th in its overall ranking for personal and economic freedom in the United States in the 2021 and 2023 editions of its *Freedom in the 50 States* index.[^cite_note-198] In 2022, Michigan voters passed an amendment recognizing abortion and contraceptive rights within the [state's constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_Constitution).[^cite_note-freep_passage-199]

## State symbols and nicknames



[Dwarf lake iris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwarf_lake_iris)

Michigan is traditionally known as "The Wolverine State", and the University of Michigan uses the wolverine as its mascot. The association is well and long established: for example, many Detroiters volunteered to fight during the American Civil War and [George Armstrong Custer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Armstrong_Custer), who led the Michigan Brigade, called them the "Wolverines". The origins of this association are obscure; it may derive from a busy trade in wolverine furs in Sault Ste. Marie in the 18th century or may recall a disparagement intended to compare early settlers in Michigan with the vicious mammal. Wolverines are, however, extremely rare in Michigan. A sighting in February 2004 near [Ubly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ubly,_Michigan) was the first confirmed sighting in Michigan in 200 years.[^cite_note-msnbc-200] Another wolverine was found dead in 2010.[^cite_note-201]
