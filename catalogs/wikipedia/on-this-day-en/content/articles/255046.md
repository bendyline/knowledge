The **March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom** (commonly known as the **March on Washington** or the **Great March on Washington**) was held in [Washington, D.C.](108956.md), on August 28, 1963.[^cite_note-1] The purpose of the march was to advocate for the civil and economic rights of [African Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_American). At the march, several popular singers of the time, including [Mahalia Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahalia_Jackson) and [Marian Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marian_Anderson), performed and many of the movement's leaders gave speeches. The most notable speech came from the final speaker, [Martin Luther King Jr.](20076.md), standing in front of the [Lincoln Memorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln_Memorial), as he delivered his historic "[I Have a Dream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Have_a_Dream)" speech in which he called for an end to legalized [racism](2162835.md) and [racial segregation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_segregation).[^cite_note-suarez-2]

The march was organized by [Bayard Rustin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayard_Rustin) and [A. Philip Randolph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._Philip_Randolph), who built an alliance of civil rights, labor, and religious organizations[^cite_note-Veterans-3] that came together under the banner of "jobs and freedom."[^cite_note-program-4] Estimates of the number of participants varied from 200,000 to 300,000,[^cite_note-WDL-5] but the most widely cited estimate is 250,000 people.[^cite_note-6] Observers estimated that 75–80% of the marchers were black.[^cite_note-7] The march was one of the largest [political rallies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demonstration_\(people\)) for human rights in United States history.[^cite_note-program-4] [Walter Reuther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Reuther), president of the [United Auto Workers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Auto_Workers), was the most integral and highest-ranking white organizer of the march.[^cite_note-Euchner_book-8][^cite_note-9]

The march is credited with helping to pass the [Civil Rights Act of 1964](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Rights_Act_of_1964).[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11] It preceded the [Selma voting rights movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selma_to_Montgomery_marches), when national media coverage contributed to passage of the [Voting Rights Act of 1965](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_Rights_Act_of_1965) that same year.[^cite_note-12]

## Background

Main article: [Civil rights movement](49001.md)

[African Americans](2154.md) were legally freed from slavery under the [Thirteenth Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirteenth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution) and granted citizenship in the [Fourteenth Amendment](31666.md), and African American men were granted full voting rights by the [Fifteenth Amendment](31667.md) in the years soon after the end of the [American Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War), but Democrats regained power after the end of the [Reconstruction era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconstruction_era) (in 1877) and imposed many restrictions on people of color in the South. At the turn of the century, Southern states passed constitutions and laws that [disenfranchised most black people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disfranchisement_after_Reconstruction_era) and many poor whites, excluding them from the political system. The whites imposed social, economic, and political repression against black people into the 1960s, under a system of legal discrimination known as [Jim Crow laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Crow_laws), which were pervasive in the [American South](179553.md). Black people suffered discrimination from private businesses as well, and most were prevented from voting, sometimes through violent means.[^cite_note-13] Twenty-one states prohibited [interracial marriage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interracial_marriage).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner2010128-14]

During the 20th century, civil rights organizers began to develop ideas for a march on Washington, D.C., to seek justice. Earlier efforts to organize such a demonstration included the [March on Washington Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_on_Washington_Movement) of the 1940s. [A. Philip Randolph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._Philip_Randolph)—the president of the [Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brotherhood_of_Sleeping_Car_Porters), president of the Negro American Labor Council,[^cite_note-WDL-5] and vice president of the [AFL–CIO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AFL%E2%80%93CIO)—was a key instigator in 1941. With [Bayard Rustin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayard_Rustin), Randolph called for 100,000 black workers to march on Washington,[^cite_note-Veterans-3] in protest of discriminatory hiring during World War II by [U.S. military contractors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_United_States_defense_contractors) and demanding an [Executive Order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_order_\(United_States\)) to correct that.[^cite_note-15] Faced with a mass march scheduled for July 1, 1941, [President](24113.md) [Franklin D. Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt) issued [Executive Order 8802](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_8802) on June 25.[^cite_note-16] The order established the [Committee on Fair Employment Practice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_on_Fair_Employment_Practice) and banned discriminatory hiring in the defense industry, leading to improvements for many defense workers.[^cite_note-17] Randolph called off the March.[^cite_note-18]

Randolph and Rustin continued to organize around the idea of a mass march on Washington. They envisioned several large marches during the 1940s, but all were called off (despite criticism from Rustin).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner201016–17-19] Their [Prayer Pilgrimage for Freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prayer_Pilgrimage_for_Freedom), held at the Lincoln Memorial on May 17, 1957, featured key leaders including [Adam Clayton Powell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Clayton_Powell_Jr.), [Martin Luther King Jr.](20076.md), and [Roy Wilkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Wilkins). [Mahalia Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahalia_Jackson) performed.[^cite_note-20]

The 1963 march was part of the rapidly expanding [Civil Rights Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Rights_Movement), which involved demonstrations and [nonviolent direct action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonviolent_direct_action) across the United States.[^cite_note-21] 1963 marked the 100th anniversary of the signing of the [Emancipation Proclamation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emancipation_Proclamation) by President [Abraham Lincoln](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Lincoln). Leaders represented major civil rights organizations. Members of the [National Association for the Advancement of Colored People](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Association_for_the_Advancement_of_Colored_People) (NAACP) and the [Southern Christian Leadership Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Christian_Leadership_Conference) put aside their differences and came together for the march. Many whites and black people also came together in the urgency for change in the nation.

That year violent confrontations broke out in the South: in [Pine Bluff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pine_Bluff,_Arkansas), [Arkansas](1930.md); [Goldsboro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldsboro,_North_Carolina), [North Carolina](21650.md); [Somerville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somerville,_Tennessee), [Tennessee](30395.md); [Saint Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Augustine,_Florida), [Florida](18933066.md); and across [Mississippi](16949861.md). A violent incident also occurred in [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge,_Maryland), [Maryland](18858.md). In most cases, white people attacked nonviolent demonstrators seeking civil rights.[^cite_note-22] Many people wanted to march on Washington, but disagreed over how the march should be conducted. Some called for a complete shutdown of the city through [civil disobedience](37073.md). Others argued that the civil rights movement should remain nationwide in scope, rather than focus its energies on the nation's capital and federal government.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner20102-23] There was a widespread perception that the [Kennedy administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidency_of_John_F._Kennedy) had not lived up to its promises in the 1960 election, and King described Kennedy's race policy as "tokenism".[^cite_note-Bartlett-24]

On May 24, 1963, Attorney General [Robert F. Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_F._Kennedy) invited African-American novelist [James Baldwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Baldwin), along with a large group of cultural leaders, to a meeting in New York to discuss race relations. However, the meeting became antagonistic, as black delegates felt that Kennedy did not have an adequate understanding of the race problem in the nation. The public failure of the meeting, which came to be known as the [Baldwin–Kennedy meeting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baldwin%E2%80%93Kennedy_meeting), underscored the divide between the needs of Black America and the understanding of Washington politicians. But the meeting also provoked the [Kennedy administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidency_of_John_F._Kennedy) to take action on the civil rights of African Americans.[^cite_note-25] On June 11, 1963, President Kennedy gave a notable [civil rights address](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Rights_Address) on national television and radio, announcing that he would begin to push for civil rights legislation. That night (early morning of June 12, 1963), Mississippi activist [Medgar Evers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medgar_Evers) was murdered in his own driveway, further escalating national tension around the issue of [racial inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_inequality_in_the_United_States).[^cite_note-26] After Kennedy's [assassination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assassination_of_John_F._Kennedy), his proposal was signed into law by President [Lyndon B. Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson) as the [Civil Rights Act of 1964](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Rights_Act_of_1964).

## Planning and organization

[A. Philip Randolph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._Philip_Randolph) and [Bayard Rustin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayard_Rustin) began planning the march in December 1961. They envisioned two days of protest, including sit-ins and lobbying followed by a mass rally at the Lincoln Memorial. They wanted to focus on joblessness and to call for a public works program that would employ black people. In early 1963 they called publicly for "a massive March on Washington for jobs".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarber2002144-27] They received help from [Stanley Aronowitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Aronowitz) of the [Amalgamated Clothing Workers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amalgamated_Clothing_Workers); he gathered support from radical organizers who could be trusted not to report their plans to the Kennedy administration. The unionists offered tentative support for a march that would be focused on jobs.[^cite_note-28]

On May 15, 1963, without securing the cooperation of the NAACP or the Urban League, Randolph announced an "October Emancipation March on Washington for Jobs".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner201020-29] He reached out to union leaders, winning the support of the UAW's [Walter Reuther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Reuther), but not of [AFL–CIO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AFL%E2%80%93CIO) president [George Meany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Meany).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner201021-30] Randolph and Rustin intended to focus the March on [economic inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_inequality), stating in their original plan that "integration in the fields of education, housing, transportation and public accommodations will be of limited extent and duration so long as fundamental economic inequality along racial lines persists."[^cite_note-31] As they negotiated with other leaders, they expanded their stated objectives to "Jobs and Freedom", to acknowledge the agenda of groups that focused more on civil rights.[^cite_note-JonesDissent-32]



Leaders of the March on Washington meeting with Vice President [Lyndon B. Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson) and Attorney General [Robert F. Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_F._Kennedy) at the White House on June 22, 1963.

In June 1963, leaders from several different organizations formed the [Council for United Civil Rights Leadership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_for_United_Civil_Rights_Leadership), an umbrella group to coordinate funds and messaging.[^cite_note-33] This coalition of leaders, who became known as the "[Big Six](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Six_\(activists\))", included: Randolph, chosen as titular head of the march; [James Farmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Farmer), president of the [Congress of Racial Equality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_Racial_Equality); [John Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lewis), chairman of the [Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Student_Nonviolent_Coordinating_Committee); Dr. [Martin Luther King Jr.](20076.md), president of the [Southern Christian Leadership Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Christian_Leadership_Conference);[^cite_note-WDL-5] [Roy Wilkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Wilkins), president of the [NAACP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Association_for_the_Advancement_of_Colored_People);[^cite_note-WDL-5] and [Whitney Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitney_Young), president of the [National Urban League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Urban_League). King in particular had become well known for his role in the [Birmingham campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_campaign) and for his [Letter from Birmingham Jail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letter_from_Birmingham_Jail).[^cite_note-34] Wilkins and Young initially objected to Rustin as a leader for the march, worried that he would attract the wrong attention because he was a homosexual, a former Communist, and a draft resister.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner201021-30] They eventually accepted Rustin as deputy organizer, on the condition that Randolph act as lead organizer and manage any political fallout.[^cite_note-35]

About two months before the march, the Big Six broadened their organizing coalition by bringing on board four white men who supported their efforts: [Walter Reuther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Reuther), president of the [United Automobile Workers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Automobile_Workers); [Eugene Carson Blake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Carson_Blake), former president of the [National Council of Churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Council_of_Churches); [Mathew Ahmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathew_Ahmann), executive director of the National Catholic Conference for Interracial Justice; and [Joachim Prinz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joachim_Prinz), president of the [American Jewish Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Jewish_Congress). Together, the Big Six plus four became known as the "Big Ten."[^cite_note-Euchner_book-8][^cite_note-Thompson-36] John Lewis later recalled, "Somehow, some way, we worked well together. The six of us, plus the four. We became like brothers."[^cite_note-Thompson-36]

On June 22, the organizers met with President Kennedy, who warned against creating "an atmosphere of intimidation" by bringing a large crowd to Washington. The civil rights activists insisted on holding the march. Wilkins pushed for the organizers to rule out [civil disobedience](37073.md) and described this proposal as the "perfect compromise". King and Young agreed. Leaders from the [Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Student_Nonviolent_Coordinating_Committee) (SNCC) and [Congress of Racial Equality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_Racial_Equality) (CORE), who wanted to conduct direct actions against the Department of Justice, endorsed the protest before they were informed that civil disobedience would not be allowed. Finalized plans for the March were announced in a press conference on July 2.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarber2002147–148-37] President Kennedy spoke favorably of the March on July 17, saying that organizers planned a peaceful assembly and had cooperated with the Washington, D.C., police.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarber2002149-38]



Leaders of the march in front of the statue of Abraham Lincoln: (sitting L-R) [Whitney Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitney_Young), [Cleveland Robinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleveland_Robinson), [A. Philip Randolph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._Philip_Randolph), [Martin Luther King Jr.](20076.md), and [Roy Wilkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Wilkins); (standing L-R) [Mathew Ahmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathew_Ahmann), [Joachim Prinz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joachim_Prinz), [John Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lewis), [Eugene Carson Blake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Carson_Blake), [Floyd McKissick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floyd_McKissick), and [Walter Reuther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Reuther)

Mobilization and logistics were administered by Rustin, a civil rights veteran and organizer of the 1947 [Journey of Reconciliation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journey_of_Reconciliation), the first of the [Freedom Rides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_Rides) to test the [Supreme Court](31737.md) ruling that banned [racial discrimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_discrimination) in interstate travel. Rustin was a long-time associate of both Randolph and King. With Randolph concentrating on building the march's political coalition, Rustin built and led the team of two hundred activists and organizers who publicized the march and recruited the marchers, coordinated the buses and trains, provided the marshals, and set up and administered all of the logistic details of a mass march in the nation's capital.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBranch1988872-39] During the days leading up to the march, these 200 volunteers used the ballroom of Washington, D.C., radio station [WUST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WUST) as their operations headquarters.[^cite_note-Euchner_book-8]

The march was not universally supported among civil rights activists. Some, including Rustin (who assembled 4,000 volunteer marshals from New York), were concerned that it might turn violent, which could undermine pending legislation and damage the international image of the movement.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBranch1988871-40] The march was condemned by [Malcolm X](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malcolm_X), spokesperson for the [Nation of Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nation_of_Islam), who termed it the "farce on Washington".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBranch1988874-41]

March organizers disagreed about the purpose of the march. The NAACP and Urban League saw it as a gesture of support for the civil rights bill that had been introduced by the [Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Kennedy) Administration. Randolph, King, and the [Southern Christian Leadership Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Christian_Leadership_Conference) (SCLC) believed it could raise both civil rights and economic issues to national attention beyond the Kennedy bill. CORE and SNCC believed the march could challenge and condemn the Kennedy administration's inaction and lack of support for civil rights for African Americans.[^cite_note-Veterans-3]

Despite their disagreements, the group came together on a set of goals:

- Passage of meaningful civil rights legislation;

- Immediate elimination of [school segregation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School_segregation_in_the_United_States) (the Supreme Court had ruled that segregation of public schools was unconstitutional in 1954, in *[Brown v. Board of Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_v._Board_of_Education)*);

- A program of public works, including job training, for the unemployed;

- A Federal [law prohibiting discrimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-discrimination_law) in public or private hiring;

- A \$2-an-hour [minimum wage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum_wage) nationwide (equivalent to \$21 in 2025);

- Withholding Federal funds from programs that tolerate discrimination;

- Enforcement of the 14th Amendment to the Constitution by reducing congressional representation from States that disenfranchise citizens;

- A Fair Labor Standards Act broadened to include employment areas then excluded;

- Authority for the attorney general to institute injunctive suits when constitutional rights of citizens are violated.

  [^cite_note-Federal_Highway_Administration-42]

Although in years past, Randolph had supported "Negro only" marches, partly to reduce the impression that the civil rights movement was dominated by white communists, organizers in 1963 agreed that white and black people marching side by side would create a more powerful image.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarber2002156–157-43]

The Kennedy administration cooperated with the organizers in planning the March, and one member of the Justice Department was assigned as a full-time liaison.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarber2002151-44] Chicago and New York City (as well as some corporations) agreed to designate August 28 as "Freedom Day" and give workers the day off.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarber2002156-45]

To avoid being perceived as radical, organizers rejected support from Communist groups. However, some politicians claimed that the March was Communist-inspired, and the [Federal Bureau of Investigation](11127.md) (FBI) produced numerous reports suggesting the same.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarber2002159-46] [^cite_note-47] In the days before August 28, the FBI called celebrity backers to inform them of the organizers' communist connections and advising them to withdraw their support.[^cite_note-48] When [William C. Sullivan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_C._Sullivan) produced a lengthy report on August 23 suggesting that Communists had failed to appreciably infiltrate the civil rights movement, FBI director [J. Edgar Hoover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Edgar_Hoover) rejected its contents.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner201057–58-49] [Strom Thurmond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strom_Thurmond) launched a prominent public attack on the march as Communist, and singled out Rustin in particular as a Communist and a gay man.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner2010116-50]

Organizers worked out of a building at West 130th St. and Lenox in [Harlem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harlem).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner201071-51] They promoted the march by selling buttons, featuring two hands shaking, the words "March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom", a union bug, and the date August 28, 1963. By August 2, they had distributed 42,000 of the buttons. Their goal was a crowd of at least 100,000 people.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarber2002156-45]

As the march was being planned, activists across the country received bomb threats at their homes and in their offices. The *Los Angeles Times* received a message saying its headquarters would be bombed unless it printed a message calling the president a "Nigger Lover". Five airplanes were grounded on the morning of August 28 due to bomb threats. A man in Kansas City telephoned the FBI to say he would put a hole between King's eyes; the FBI did not respond. Roy Wilkins was threatened with assassination if he did not leave the country.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner201063–65-52]

## Convergence

Thousands traveled by road, rail, and air to Washington, D.C., on Wednesday, August 28. Marchers from Boston traveled overnight and arrived in Washington at 7 a.m. after an eight-hour trip, but others took much longer bus rides from cities such as Milwaukee, Little Rock, and St. Louis. Organizers persuaded New York's MTA to run extra subway trains after midnight on August 28, and the New York City bus terminal was busy throughout the night with peak crowds.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner201073-53] A total of 450 buses left New York City from Harlem. Maryland police reported that "by 8\:00 a.m., 100 buses an hour were streaming through the Baltimore Harbor Tunnel."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarber2002161-54] The United Automobile Workers financed bus transportation for 5,000 of its rank-and-file members, providing the largest single contingent from any organization.[^cite_note-55]

One reporter, Fred Powledge, accompanied African Americans who boarded six buses in Birmingham, Alabama, for the 750-mile trip to Washington. *[The New York Times](30680.md)* carried his report:

> The 260 demonstrators, of all ages, carried picnic baskets, water jugs, Bibles and a major weapon—their willingness to march, sing and pray in protest against discrimination. They gathered early this morning \[August 27] in Birmingham's [Kelly Ingram Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelly_Ingram_Park), where state troopers once \[four months previous in May] used fire hoses and dogs to put down their demonstrations. It was peaceful in the Birmingham park as the marchers waited for the buses. The police, now part of a moderate city power structure, directed traffic around the square and did not interfere with the gathering ... An old man commented on the 20-hour ride, which was bound to be less than comfortable: "You forget we Negroes have been riding buses all our lives. We don't have the money to fly in airplanes."

John Marshall Kilimanjaro, a demonstrator traveling from [Greensboro, North Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greensboro,_North_Carolina), said:[^cite_note-56]

> Contrary to the mythology, the early moments of the March—getting there—was no picnic. People were afraid. We didn't know what we would meet. There was no precedent. Sitting across from me was a black preacher with a white collar. He was an [AME](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Methodist_Episcopal) preacher. We talked. Every now and then, people on the bus sang 'Oh Freedom' and 'We Shall Overcome,' but for the most part there wasn't a whole bunch of singing. We were secretly praying that nothing violent happened.

Other bus rides featured racial tension, as black activists criticized liberal white participants as fair-weather friends.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner201025-57]

Hazel Mangle Rivers, who had paid \$8 for her ticket—"one-tenth of her husband's weekly salary"—was quoted in the August 29 *New York Times*. Rivers said that she was impressed by Washington's civility:

> The people are lots better up here than they are down South. They treat you much nicer. Why, when I was out there at the march a white man stepped on my foot, and he said, "Excuse me," and I said "Certainly!" That's the first time that has ever happened to me. I believe that was the first time a white person has ever really been nice to me.[^cite_note-Federal_Highway_Administration-42]

Some participants who arrived early held an all-night vigil outside the [Department of Justice](52563.md), claiming it had unfairly targeted civil rights activists and that it had been too lenient on [white supremacists](33178.md) who attacked them.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner201043–44-58]

### Security preparations



Aerial view of Washington Monument showing marchers

The Washington, D.C., police forces were mobilized to full capacity for the march, including reserve officers and deputized firefighters. A total of 5,900 police officers were on duty.[^cite_note-59] The government mustered 2,000 men from the [National Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_National_Guard), and brought in 3,000 outside soldiers to join the 1,000 already stationed in the area.[^cite_note-60] These additional soldiers were flown in on helicopters from bases in Virginia and North Carolina. The Pentagon readied 19,000 troops in the suburbs.[^cite_note-pillar-61] All of the forces involved were prepared to implement a coordinated conflict strategy named "Operation Steep Hill".[^cite_note-62]

For the first time since [Prohibition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prohibition_in_the_United_States), liquor sales were banned in Washington, D.C.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarber2002160-63] Hospitals stockpiled blood plasma and cancelled elective surgeries.[^cite_note-64] Major League Baseball cancelled two games between the Minnesota Twins and the last place Washington Senators although the venue, D.C. Stadium, was nearly four miles from the Lincoln Memorial rally site.[^cite_note-pillar-61]

Rustin and [Walter Fauntroy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Fauntroy) negotiated some security issues with the government, gaining approval for private marshals with the understanding that these would not be able to act against outside agitators. The FBI and Justice Department refused to provide preventive guards for buses traveling through the South to reach D.C.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner201060–62-65] William Johnson recruited more than 1,000 police officers to serve on this private force.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner2010102-66] [Julius Hobson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Hobson), an FBI informant who served on the March's security force, told the team to be on the lookout for FBI infiltrators who might act as [agents provocateurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agents_provocateurs).[^cite_note-67] Jerry Bruno, President Kennedy's advance man, was positioned to cut the power to the public address system in the event of any incendiary rally speech.[^cite_note-pillar-61]

### Venue and sound system

The organizers originally planned to hold the march outside the [Capitol Building](31979.md).[^cite_note-David._236-68] However, Reuther persuaded them to move the march to the [Lincoln Memorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln_Memorial).[^cite_note-David._236-68] He believed the Lincoln Memorial would be less threatening to Congress and the occasion would be appropriate underneath the gaze of President Abraham Lincoln's statue.[^cite_note-David._236-68] The committee, notably Rustin, agreed to move the site on the condition that Reuther pay for a \$19,000 (equivalent to \$172,500 in 2021) sound system so that everyone on the [National Mall](167796.md) could hear the speakers and musicians.[^cite_note-David._236-68]

Rustin pushed hard for the expensive sound system, stating that "We cannot maintain order where people cannot hear." The system was obtained and set up at the Lincoln Memorial, but was sabotaged on the day before the March. Its operators were unable to repair it. Fauntroy contacted Attorney General [Robert F. Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_F._Kennedy) and his civil rights liaison [Burke Marshall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burke_Marshall), demanding that the government fix the system. Fauntroy reportedly told them: "We have a couple hundred thousand people coming. Do you want a fight here tomorrow after all we've done?" The system was successfully rebuilt overnight by the [U.S. Army Signal Corps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_Corps_\(United_States_Army\)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner20108–9-69]

## March



Close up of some leaders of the March on Washington walking along Constitution Avenue.



Leaders arrived late and linked arms in front of marchers on Constitution Avenue.

The march commanded national attention by preempting regularly scheduled television programs. As the first ceremony of such magnitude ever initiated and dominated by African Americans, the march also was the first to have its nature wholly misperceived in advance. Dominant expectations ran from paternal apprehension to dread. On *[Meet the Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meet_the_Press)*, reporters grilled Roy Wilkins and Martin Luther King Jr. about widespread foreboding that "it would be impossible to bring more than 100,000 militant Negroes into Washington without incidents and possibly rioting." [*Life* magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_magazine) declared that the capital was suffering "its worst case of invasion jitters since the [First Battle of Bull Run](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Battle_of_Bull_Run)." The jails shifted inmates to other prisons to make room for those arrested in [mass arrest](28079790.md). With nearly 1,700 extra correspondents supplementing the Washington press corps, the march drew a media assembly larger than the Kennedy inauguration two years earlier.[^cite_note-Federal_Highway_Administration-42] Students from the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley), came together as black power organizations and emphasized the importance of the African-American freedom struggle. The march included black political parties; and William Worthy was one of many who led college students during the freedom struggle era.[^cite_note-70]



Nearly 250,000 people marched, including 60,000 white participants

On August 28, more than 2,000 [buses](4146.md), 21 chartered [trains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Train), 10 chartered airliners, and uncounted cars converged on Washington.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBranch1988876-71] All regularly scheduled planes, trains, and buses were also filled to capacity.[^cite_note-Federal_Highway_Administration-42]

Although Randolph and Rustin had originally planned to fill the streets of Washington, D.C., the final route of the March covered only half of the National Mall.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarber2002151-44] The march began at the [Washington Monument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_Monument) and was scheduled to progress to the [Lincoln Memorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln_Memorial). Demonstrators were met at the monument by the speakers and musicians. Women leaders were asked to march down Independence Avenue, while the male leaders marched on Pennsylvania Avenue with the media.[^cite_note-wwno.org-72]

The start of the march was delayed because its leaders were meeting with members of [Congress](31756.md). To the leaders' surprise, the assembled group began to march from the [Washington Monument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_Monument) to the [Lincoln Memorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln_Memorial) without them. The leaders met the march at Constitution Avenue, where they linked arms at the head of a crowd in order to be photographed 'leading the march'.[^cite_note-73]

Marchers were not supposed to create their own signs, though this rule was not completely enforced by marshals. Most of the demonstrators did carry pre-made signs, available in piles at the Washington Monument.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarber2002164-74] The UAW provided thousands of signs that, among other things, read: "There Is No Halfway House on the Road to Freedom,"[^cite_note-75] "Equal Rights and Jobs NOW,"[^cite_note-76] "UAW Supports Freedom March,"[^cite_note-77] "In Freedom we are Born, in Freedom we must Live,"[^cite_note-78] and "Before we'll be a Slave, we'll be Buried in our Grave."[^cite_note-79]

About 50 members of the [American Nazi Party](364176.md) staged a counter-protest and were quickly dispersed by police.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarber2002162-80][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner2010136-81] One member, the second in command [Karl Allen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Allen), was arrested after attempting to give a speech.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner2010136–137-82] The rest of Washington was quiet during the March. Most non-participating workers stayed home. Jailers allowed inmates to watch the March on TV.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner2010130–131-83]

Representatives from each of the sponsoring organizations addressed the crowd from the podium at the Lincoln Memorial. Speakers (dubbed "The Big Ten") included The Big Six; three religious leaders (Catholic, Protestant, and Jewish); and labor leader [Walter Reuther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Reuther). None of the official speeches was by a woman. Dancer and actress [Josephine Baker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephine_Baker) gave a speech during the preliminary offerings, but women were limited in the official program to a "tribute" led by [Bayard Rustin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayard_Rustin), at which [Daisy Bates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daisy_Bates_\(civil_rights_activist\)) also spoke briefly (see "excluded speakers" below.)

[Floyd McKissick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floyd_McKissick) read [James Farmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Farmer)'s speech because Farmer had been arrested during a protest in [Louisiana](18130.md); Farmer wrote that the protests would not stop "until the dogs stop biting us in the [South](179553.md) and the rats stop biting us in the [North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_United_States)."[^cite_note-86]

The order of the speakers was as follows:

1. [A. Philip Randolph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._Philip_Randolph) – March Director

2. [Daisy Bates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daisy_Bates_\(activist\)) – Little Rock, Arkansas

3. Dr. [Eugene Carson Blake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Carson_Blake) – United Presbyterian Church and the National Council of Churches

4. [John Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lewis) – Chair, [SNCC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Student_Nonviolent_Coordinating_Committee)

5. [Walter Reuther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Reuther) – [UAW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Automobile_Workers), [AFL–CIO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AFL%E2%80%93CIO)

6. [Floyd McKissick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floyd_McKissick) – CORE

7. Rabbi Uri Miller—President of the Synagogue Council of America

8. [Whitney Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitney_Young) – National Urban League

9. [Mathew Ahmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathew_Ahmann) - NCCIJ

10. [Roy Wilkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Wilkins) – NAACP

12. Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. – SCLC. His "I Have a Dream" speech has become celebrated for its vision and eloquence.

Closing remarks were made by A. Philip Randolph and Bayard Rustin, March Organizers, leading with The Pledge and a list of demands.[^cite_note-87]

### Official program



March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom program

The actual order of events differed slightly from the official printed program.[^cite_note-88] Noted singer [Marian Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marian_Anderson) was scheduled to lead the [national anthem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_anthem) but was unable to arrive on time; [Camilla Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camilla_Williams) performed in her place. Washington's Roman Catholic Archbishop [Patrick O'Boyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_O'Boyle_\(cardinal\)) delivered the invocation.[^cite_note-89]

The opening remarks were given by march director [A. Philip Randolph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._Philip_Randolph), followed by a tribute to "Negro Women Fighters for Freedom", in which [Daisy Bates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daisy_Bates_\(civil_rights_activist\)) spoke briefly in place of [Myrlie Evers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myrlie_Evers), who had missed her flight.[^cite_note-alternet.org-90][^cite_note-motherjones.com-91][^cite_note-cnn.com-92] The tribute introduced [Daisy Bates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daisy_Bates_\(civil_rights_activist\)), [Diane Nash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diane_Nash), [Prince E. Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_E._Lee?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Rosa Parks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosa_Parks), and [Gloria Richardson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gloria_Richardson).

Following that, speakers were Presbyterian Church leader [Eugene Carson Blake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Carson_Blake), SNCC chairman [John Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lewis), labor leader [Walter Reuther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Reuther), and CORE chairman [Floyd McKissick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floyd_McKissick) (substituting for arrested CORE director [James Farmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Farmer)). The Eva Jessye Choir sang, and Rabbi Uri Miller (president of the Synagogue Council of America) offered a prayer. He was followed by National Urban League director [Whitney Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitney_Young), NCCIJ director [Mathew Ahmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathew_Ahmann), and NAACP leader [Roy Wilkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Wilkins). After a performance by singer [Mahalia Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahalia_Jackson), American Jewish Congress president [Joachim Prinz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joachim_Prinz) spoke, followed by SCLC president [Martin Luther King Jr.](20076.md) Rustin read the March's official demands for the crowd's approval, and Randolph led the crowd in a pledge to continue working for the March's goals. The program was closed with a benediction by Morehouse College president [Benjamin Mays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Mays).

Although one of the officially stated purposes of the march was to support the civil rights bill introduced by the Kennedy administration, several of the speakers criticized the proposed law as insufficient. Two government agents stood by in a position to cut power to the microphone if necessary.[^cite_note-93]

### Roy Wilkins

Roy Wilkins announced that sociologist and activist [W. E. B. Du Bois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._E._B._Du_Bois) had died in [Ghana](12067.md) the previous night, where he had been living in exile; the crowd observed a moment of silence in his memory.[^cite_note-94] Wilkins had initially refused to announce the news because he despised Du Bois for becoming a Communist—but insisted on making the announcement when he realized that Randolph would make it if he did not.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner2010180–181-95] Wilkins said: "Regardless of the fact that in his later years Dr. Du Bois chose another path, it is incontrovertible that at the dawn of the twentieth century his was the voice that was calling you to gather here today in this cause. If you want to read something that applies to 1963 go back and get a volume of *[The Souls of Black Folk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Souls_of_Black_Folk)* by Du Bois, published in 1903."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner2010183-96]

### John Lewis

[John Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lewis) of SNCC was the youngest speaker at the event.[^cite_note-Doak2007-97] He planned to criticize the [Kennedy Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kennedy_Administration) for the inadequacies of the Civil Rights Act of 1963. Other leaders insisted that the speech be changed to be less antagonistic to the government. [James Forman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Forman) and other SNCC activists contributed to the revision. It still complained that the Administration had not done enough to protect southern black people and civil rights workers from physical violence by whites in the Deep South.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBranch1988874-41][^cite_note-98] Deleted from his original speech at the insistence of more conservative and pro-Kennedy leaders[^cite_note-Veterans-3][^cite_note-99] were phrases such as:



John Lewis speaking in the [Great Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Jefferson_Building) of the [Library of Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_Congress) on the 50th anniversary, August 28, 2013

> In good conscience, we cannot support wholeheartedly the administration's civil rights bill, for it is too little and too late. ...
>
> I want to know, which side is the federal government on? ... The revolution is a serious one. Mr. Kennedy is trying to take the revolution out of the streets and put it into the courts. Listen, Mr. Kennedy. Listen, Mr. Congressman. Listen, fellow citizens. The black masses are on the march for jobs and freedom, and we must say to the politicians that there won't be a "cooling-off" period. ... We will march through the South, through the heart of Dixie, the way [Sherman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Tecumseh_Sherman) did. We shall pursue our own [scorched earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scorched_earth) policy and burn Jim Crow to the ground—nonviolently ...&#x20;

Lewis' speech was distributed to fellow organizers the evening before the march; Reuther, O'Boyle, and others thought it was too divisive and militant.[^cite_note-100] O'Boyle objected most strenuously to a part of the speech that called for immediate action and disavowed "patience". The government and moderate organizers could not countenance Lewis's explicit opposition to Kennedy's civil rights bill. That night, O'Boyle and other members of the Catholic delegation began preparing a statement announcing their withdrawal from the March. Reuther convinced them to wait and called Rustin; Rustin informed Lewis at 2 a.m. on the day of the march that his speech was unacceptable to key coalition members. (Rustin also reportedly contacted [Tom Kahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Kahn), who had edited the speech and inserted the line about [Sherman's March to the Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherman's_March_to_the_Sea). Rustin asked, "How could you do this? Do you know what Sherman *did*?") But Lewis did not want to change the speech. Other members of SNCC, including [Stokely Carmichael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stokely_Carmichael), were also adamant that the speech not be censored.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner201045–49-101] The dispute continued until minutes before the speeches were scheduled to begin. Under threat of public denouncement by the religious leaders, and under pressure from the rest of his coalition, Lewis agreed to omit the 'inflammatory' passages.[^cite_note-102] Many activists from SNCC, CORE, and SCLC were angry at what they considered censorship of Lewis's speech.[^cite_note-103] In the end, Lewis added a qualified endorsement of Kennedy's civil rights legislation, saying: "It is true that we support the administration's Civil Rights Bill. We support it with great reservation, however."[^cite_note-JonesDissent-32] Even after toning down his speech, Lewis called for activists to "get in and stay in the streets of every city, every village and hamlet of this nation until true freedom comes".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarber2002169–170-104]

### Martin Luther King Jr.

Main article: [I Have a Dream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Have_a_Dream)



[Martin Luther King Jr.](20076.md) waving his hand to the crowd after delivering his "[I Have a Dream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Have_a_Dream)" speech

The speech given by SCLC president King, who spoke last, became known as the "[I Have a Dream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Have_a_Dream)" speech, which was carried live by TV stations and subsequently considered the most impressive moment of the march.[^cite_note-105] In it, King called for an end to legalized [racism in the United States](2162835.md). It invoked the [Declaration of Independence](31874.md), the [Emancipation Proclamation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emancipation_Proclamation), and the [United States Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Constitution). At the end of the speech, [Mahalia Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahalia_Jackson) shouted from the crowd, "Tell them about the dream, Martin!", and King departed from his prepared text for a partly improvised [peroration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peroration) on the theme of "I have a dream".[^cite_note-tolerance.org-106][^cite_note-107] Over time it has been hailed as a masterpiece of rhetoric, added to the [National Recording Registry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Recording_Registry) and memorialized by the [National Park Service](161535.md) with an inscription on the spot where King stood to deliver the speech.

### Randolph and Rustin

A. Philip Randolph spoke first, promising: "we shall return again and again to Washington in ever growing numbers until total freedom is ours."[^cite_note-108] Randolph also closed the event along with Bayard Rustin. Rustin followed King's speech by slowly reading the list of demands.[^cite_note-109] The two concluded by urging attendees to take various actions in support of the struggle.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarber2002171-110]

### Walter Reuther

Walter Reuther urged Americans to pressure their politicians to act to address racial injustices. He said

> American democracy is on trial in the eyes of the world ... We cannot successfully preach democracy in the world unless we first practice democracy at home. American democracy will lack the moral credentials and be both unequal to and unworthy of leading the forces of freedom against the forces of tyranny unless we take bold, affirmative, adequate steps to bridge the moral gap between American democracy's noble promises and its ugly practices in the field of civil rights.[^cite_note-111]

According to [Irving Bluestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irving_Bluestone), who was standing near the platform while Reuther delivered his remarks, he overheard two black women talking. One asked, "Who is that white man?" The other replied, "Don't you know him? That's the white Martin Luther King."[^cite_note-112]

### Excluded speakers

Author [James Baldwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Baldwin) was prevented from speaking at the March because his comments would be too inflammatory.[^cite_note-113] Baldwin later commented on the irony of the "terrifying and profound" requests that he prevent the March from happening:[^cite_note-114]

> In my view, by that time, there was, on the one hand, nothing to prevent—the March had already been co-opted—and, on the other, no way of stopping the people from descending on Washington. What struck me most horribly was that virtually no one in power (including some blacks or Negroes who were somewhere next door to power) was able, even remotely, to accept the depth, the dimension, the passion, and the faith of the people.



Actress/singer [Lena Horne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lena_Horne) was present but excluded from speaking.

Despite the protests of organizer [Anna Arnold Hedgeman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Arnold_Hedgeman), no women gave a speech at the March. Male organizers attributed this omission to the "difficulty of finding a single woman to speak without causing serious problems vis-à-vis other women and women's groups".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarber2002158-115] Hedgeman read a statement at an August 16 meeting, charging:

> In light of the role of Negro women in the struggle for freedom and especially in light of the extra burden they have carried because of the castration of our Negro men in this culture, it is incredible that no woman should appear as a speaker at the historic March on Washington Meeting at the Lincoln Memorial.

The assembled group agreed that [Myrlie Evers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myrlie_Evers), the recent widow of Medgar Evers, could speak during the "Tribute to Negro Women Fighters for Freedom".[^cite_note-116] However, Evers was unavailable,[^cite_note-117][^cite_note-118] having missed her flight, and [Daisy Bates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daisy_Bates_\(civil_rights_activist\)) spoke briefly (less than 200 words) in place of her.[^cite_note-alternet.org-90][^cite_note-motherjones.com-91][^cite_note-cnn.com-92][^cite_note-tolerance.org-106] Earlier, [Josephine Baker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephine_Baker) addressed the crowd before the official program began.[^cite_note-cnn.com-92][^cite_note-tolerance.org-106] Although [Gloria Richardson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gloria_Richardson) was on the program and had been asked to give a two-minute speech, when she arrived at the stage her chair with her name on it had been removed, and the event marshal took her microphone away after she said "hello".[^cite_note-wwno.org-72] Richardson, along with [Rosa Parks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosa_Parks) and [Lena Horne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lena_Horne), was escorted away from the podium before Martin Luther King Jr. spoke.[^cite_note-wwno.org-72]

Early plans for the March would have included an "Unemployed Worker" as one of the speakers. This position was eliminated, furthering criticism of the March's middle-class bias.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner2010159-119]

## Singers



[Joan Baez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_Baez) and [Bob Dylan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Dylan) performing at the March on Washington

Gospel legend [Mahalia Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahalia_Jackson) sang, "I've been 'buked, and I've been scorned", and "[How I Got Over](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_I_Got_Over_\(song\))". [Marian Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marian_Anderson) sang "[He's Got the Whole World in His Hands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/He's_Got_the_Whole_World_in_His_Hands)". This was not Marian Anderson's first appearance at the Lincoln Memorial. In 1939, the [Daughters of the American Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daughters_of_the_American_Revolution) refused permission for Anderson to sing to an integrated audience in [Constitution Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_Hall). With the aid of First Lady [Eleanor Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleanor_Roosevelt) and her husband [Franklin D. Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt), Anderson performed a critically acclaimed open-air concert on [Easter](9325.md) Sunday, 1939, on the steps of the Lincoln Memorial.

[Joan Baez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_Baez) led the crowds in several verses of "[We Shall Overcome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/We_Shall_Overcome)" and "[Oh Freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oh_Freedom)". Musician [Bob Dylan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Dylan) performed "[When the Ship Comes In](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/When_the_Ship_Comes_In)", for which he was joined by Baez. Dylan also performed "[Only a Pawn in Their Game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Only_a_Pawn_in_Their_Game)", a provocative and not completely popular choice because it asserted that [Byron De La Beckwith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byron_De_La_Beckwith), as a poor white man, was not personally or primarily to blame for the murder of Medgar Evers.[^cite_note-120]

[Peter, Paul and Mary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter,_Paul_and_Mary) sang "[If I Had a Hammer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/If_I_Had_a_Hammer)" and Dylan's "[Blowin' in the Wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blowin'_in_the_Wind)". [Odetta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odetta) sang "[I'm On My Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odetta_Sings_Ballads_and_Blues)".[^cite_note-Federal_Highway_Administration-42]

Some participants, including [Dick Gregory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dick_Gregory), criticized the choice of mostly white performers and the lack of group participation in the singing.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner2010109-121] Dylan himself said he felt uncomfortable as a white man serving as a public image for the Civil Rights Movement. After the March on Washington, he performed at few other immediately politicized events.[^cite_note-122]

## Celebrities

The event featured many prominent celebrities in addition to singers on the program. [Josephine Baker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephine_Baker), [Harry Belafonte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Belafonte), [Sidney Poitier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidney_Poitier), [James Baldwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Baldwin), [Jackie Robinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackie_Robinson), [Sammy Davis Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sammy_Davis_Jr.), [Dick Gregory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dick_Gregory), [Eartha Kitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eartha_Kitt), [Ossie Davis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ossie_Davis), [Ruby Dee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruby_Dee), [Diahann Carroll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diahann_Carroll), and [Lena Horne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lena_Horne) were among the black celebrities attending. There were also quite a few white and Latino celebrities who attended or helped fund the March in support of the cause: [Tony Curtis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Curtis), [James Garner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Garner), [Robert Ryan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Ryan), [Charlton Heston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlton_Heston), [Paul Newman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Newman), [Joanne Woodward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joanne_Woodward), [Rita Moreno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rita_Moreno), [Marlon Brando](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marlon_Brando), [Bobby Darin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobby_Darin) and [Burt Lancaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burt_Lancaster), among others. [Judy Garland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judy_Garland) was part of the planning committee and was also scheduled to perform but had to drop out at the last minute due to commitments to her TV variety series.[^cite_note-post1-123][^cite_note-realclear-124]

## Meeting with President Kennedy



Kennedy meets with march leaders. Left to Right – Willard Wirtz, Matthew Ahmann, Martin Luther King Jr., John Lewis, Rabbi Joachin Prinz, Eugene Carson Blake, A. Philip Randolph, President John F. Kennedy, Vice President Lyndon Johnson, Walter Reuther, Whitney Young, Floyd McKissick, Roy Wilkins (not in order)

After the March, the speakers travelled to the White House for a brief discussion of proposed civil rights legislation with President Kennedy.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarber2002172-125] As the leaders approached the White House, the media reported that Reuther said to King, "Everything was perfect, just perfect."[^cite_note-126] Kennedy had watched King's speech on TV and was very impressed. According to biographer [Thomas C. Reeves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_C._Reeves), Kennedy "felt that he would be booed at the March, and also didn't want to meet with organizers before the March because he didn't want a list of demands. He arranged a 5 p.m. meeting at the White House with the 10 leaders on the 28th."[^cite_note-127]

In full, these participants were [Mathew Ahmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathew_Ahmann) of the National Catholic Conference for Interracial Justice; [Whitney Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitney_Young) of the [National Urban League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Urban_League); [Martin Luther King Jr.](20076.md) (SCLC); [John Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lewis) of the [SNCC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Student_Nonviolent_Coordinating_Committee); Rabbi [Joachim Prinz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joachim_Prinz) of the [American Jewish Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Jewish_Congress); Reverend [Eugene Carson Blake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Carson_Blake) of the United Presbyterian Church; [A. Philip Randolph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._Philip_Randolph); labor leader [Walter Reuther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Reuther); [Roy Wilkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Wilkins) of the NAACP and (not visible shown in the image to the right) Secretary of Labor [Willard Wirtz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._Willard_Wirtz) and [Floyd McKissick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floyd_McKissick) of [CORE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_Racial_Equality).[^cite_note-128]

During the meeting, Reuther described to Kennedy how he was framing the civil rights issue to business leaders in Detroit, saying, "Look, you can't escape the problem. And there are two ways of resolving it; either by reason or riots."[^cite_note-Hill_262-129] Reuther continued, "Now the civil war that this is gonna trigger is not gonna be fought at Gettysburg. It's gonna to be fought in your backyard, in your plant, where your kids are growing up."[^cite_note-Hill_262-129] The March was considered a "triumph of managed protest" and Kennedy felt it was a victory for him as well—bolstering the chances for his [civil rights bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Rights_Act_of_1964).[^cite_note-130]

Allowing civil rights leaders to engage in conversation with Kennedy may be considered an example of [speaking truth to power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speaking_truth_to_power).

## Media coverage



Leaders of the March on Washington speak to the news media after meeting with President Kennedy at the White House.

*[The March](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_March_\(1964_film\))*, a documentary film produced by the [United States Information Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Information_Agency). King's speech has been redacted from this video because of the [copyright held by King's estate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Have_a_Dream#Copyright_dispute).

Media attention gave the march national exposure, carrying the organizers' speeches and offering their own commentary. In his section *The March on Washington and Television News*, William Thomas notes: "Over five hundred cameramen, technicians, and correspondents from the major networks were set to cover the event. More cameras would be set up than had filmed the last presidential inauguration. One camera was positioned high in the Washington Monument, to give dramatic vistas of the marchers".[^cite_note-131] The major networks broadcast some of the March live, though they interspersed footage of interviews with politicians. Subsequent broadcasts focused heavily on the "I have a dream" portion of King's speech.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarber2002175-132]

The [Voice of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voice_of_America) translated the speeches and rebroadcast them in 36 languages. The [United States Information Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Information_Agency) organized a press conference for the benefit of foreign journalists, and also created a documentary film of the event for distribution to embassies abroad.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarber2002151-44] Commented Michael Thelwell of SNCC: "So it happened that Negro students from the South, some of whom still had unhealed bruises from the electric cattle prods which Southern police used to break up demonstrations, were recorded for the screens of the world portraying 'American Democracy at Work.'"[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner201070-133]

Some media figures, especially conservative ones, criticized the march. Syndicated columnist [David Lawrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Lawrence_\(publisher\)) called it a "public disgrace" and "the mess in Washington."[^cite_note-134]

## Responses and memories

### Organizers

Although the mass media generally declared the march successful because of its high turnout, organizers were not confident that it would create change. Randolph and Rustin abandoned their belief in the effectiveness of marching on Washington. King maintained faith that action in Washington could work, but determined that future marchers would need to call greater attention to economic injustice. In 1967–1968, he organized a [Poor People's Campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poor_People's_Campaign) to occupy the National Mall with a shantytown.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarber2002176–178-135]

### Critics

[Black nationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_nationalism) [Malcolm X](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malcolm_X), in his [Message to the Grass Roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Message_to_the_Grass_Roots) speech, criticized the march, describing it as "a picnic" and "a circus". He said the civil rights leaders had diluted the original purpose of the march, which had been to show the strength and anger of black people, by allowing white people and organizations to help plan and participate in the march.[^cite_note-136] One SNCC staffer commented during the march, "He's denouncing us as clowns, but he's right there with the clown show."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner201016-137] But the membership of SNCC, increasingly frustrated with the tactics of the NAACP and other moderate groups, gradually embraced Malcolm X's position.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarber2002176–178-135]

Segregationists including [William Jennings Bryan Dorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Jennings_Bryan_Dorn) criticized the government for cooperating with the civil rights activists.[^cite_note-138] Senator [Olin D. Johnston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olin_D._Johnston) rejected an invitation to attend, writing: "You are committing the worst possible mistake in promoting this March. You should know that criminal, fanatical, and communistic elements, as well as crackpots, will move in to take every advantage of this mob. You certainly will have no influence on any member of Congress, including myself."[^cite_note-139]

### Participants



Leaders of the march leading marchers down the street

Many participants said they felt the March was a historic and life-changing experience. Nan Grogan Orrock, a student at Mary Washington College, said: "You couldn't help but get swept up in the feeling of the March. It was an incredible experience of this mass of humanity with one mind moving down the street. It was like being part of a glacier. You could feel the sense of collective will and effort in the air."[^cite_note-140] SNCC organizer [Bob Zellner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Zellner) reported that the event "provided dramatic proof that the sometimes quiet and always dangerous work we did in the Deep South had had a profound national impact. The spectacle of a quarter of a million supporters and activists gave me an assurance that the work I was in the process of dedicating my life to was worth doing."[^cite_note-141]

Richard Brown, then a white graduate student at Harvard University, recalls that the March fostered direct actions for economic progress: "Henry Armstrong and I compared notes. I realized the Congress of Racial Equality might help black employment in Boston by urging businesses to hire contractors like Armstrong. He agreed to help start a list of reliable contractors that CORE could promote. It was a modest effort—but it moved in the right direction."[^cite_note-142]

Other participants, more sympathetic to Malcolm X and the black nationalists, expressed ambivalence. One marcher from New York explained:[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner201024–25-143]

> It's like [St Patrick's Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Patrick's_Day). I came out of respect for what my people are doing, not because I believe it will do any good. I thought it would do some good in the beginning. But when the march started to get all the official approval from *Mastah* Kennedy, *Mastah* Wagner, *Mastah* Spellman, and they started setting limits on how we had to march peacefully, I knew that the march was going to be a mockery, that *they* were giving *us* something again.

Marcher Beverly Alston thought that the day had its greatest impact within the movement: "Culturally, there has been tremendous progress over the past forty years. Black awareness and self-determination has soared. Politically, I just don't think we've made enough progress."[^cite_note-144] Fifteen-year-old Ericka Jenkins from Washington said:[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner201081–82-145]

> I saw people laughing and listening and standing very close to one another, almost in an embrace. Children of every size, pregnant women, elderly people who seemed tired but happy to be there, clothing that made me know that they struggled to make it day to day, made me know they worked in farms or offices or even nearby for the government. I didn't see teenagers alone; I saw groups of teenagers with teachers.
>
> White people \[were] standing in wonder. Their eyes were open, they were *listening*. Openness and nothing on guard—I saw that in everybody. I was so happy to see that in the white people that they could listen and take in and respect and believe in the words of a black person. I had never seen anything like that.

Some people discussed racism becoming less explicit after the March. Reverend Abraham Woods of Birmingham commented: "Everything has changed. And when you look at it, nothing has changed. Racism is under the surface, and an incident that could scratch it, can bring it out."[^cite_note-146]

## Effects and legacy

The symbolism of the march has been contested since before it even took place. In the years following the march, movement radicals increasingly subscribed to Malcolm X's narrative of the march as a co-optation by the white establishment. However, some black nationalist intellectuals did not see that the liberal reforms of the [Johnson administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidency_of_Lyndon_B._Johnson) would assure "full integration" based upon the existing power structures and persisting racist culture of daily life in America. Former [Communist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_Party_of_the_United_States) member [Harold Cruse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Cruse) posited that full integration was "not possible within the present framework of the American system". [Black Panther Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Panther_Party) member and lawyer [Kathleen Cleaver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kathleen_Cleaver) held radical views that only revolution could transform American society to bring about the [redistribution of wealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redistribution_of_wealth) and power that was needed to end the historical facts of exclusion and inequality.[^cite_note-147]

Liberals and conservatives tended to embrace the March, but focused mostly on King's "I Have a Dream" speech and the legislative successes of 1964 and 1965.[^cite_note-JonesDissent-32] The mass media identified King's speech as the highlight of the event and focused on this oration to the exclusion of other aspects. For several decades, King took center stage in narratives about the March. By the 2010s, historians and commentators began to acknowledge the vital role played by Bayard Rustin in organizing the event.[^cite_note-Wickham-148] The story of his role in the organization of the March on Washington was told in the film *[Rustin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rustin_\(film\))* (2023).[^cite_note-149]

The March started a pattern of labeling mass demonstrations in the nation's capital a "March on Washington". Some [examples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_rallies_and_protest_marches_in_Washington,_D.C.) include the [SDS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Students_for_a_Democratic_Society)-sponsored 1965 [March on Washington to End the War in Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1965_March_against_the_Vietnam_War),[^cite_note-150] the 1979 [National March on Washington for Lesbian and Gay Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_March_on_Washington_for_Lesbian_and_Gay_Rights), and the 2017 [Women's March on Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_Women's_March).

For the 50th anniversary of the 1963 March, the [United States Postal Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Postal_Service) released a [forever stamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forever_stamp) that commemorated it.[^cite_note-151]

### Political effects

Soon after the speakers ended their meetings with Congress to go join the March, both houses passed legislation to create a dispute arbitration board for striking railroad workers.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEuchner201089-152]

The March is credited with propelling the U.S. government into action on civil rights, creating political momentum for the [Civil Rights Act of 1964](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Rights_Act_of_1964) and the [Voting Rights Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_Rights_Act) of 1965.[^cite_note-Bartlett-24]

The cooperation of a Democratic administration with the issue of civil rights marked a pivotal moment in voter alignment within the U.S. The [Democratic Party](5043544.md) gave up the [Solid South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_South)—its undivided support since Reconstruction among the segregated Southern states—and went on to capture a high proportion of votes from Black people from the Republicans.[^cite_note-Bartlett-24][^cite_note-153]

### Anniversary marches



50th Anniversary of the Civil Rights March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom

The 1963 March also spurred anniversary marches that occur every five years, with the 20th and 25th being some of the most well known. The 20th anniversary theme was "We Still have a Dream ... Jobs\*Peace\*Freedom."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarber2002173–174-154]

At the 50th anniversary march in 2013, President [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama) conferred a posthumous [Presidential Medal of Freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidential_Medal_of_Freedom) on Bayard Rustin and 15 others.[^cite_note-JonesDissent-32][^cite_note-Wickham-148]

#### 2020 Virtual March on Washington

On July 20, 2020, the [NAACP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAACP), one of the original organizers of the 1963 march, announced that it would commemorate it by organizing another rally on the steps of the [Lincoln Memorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln_Memorial), in which King's oldest son, [Martin Luther King III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Luther_King_III), would join civil rights leaders and the families of black men and women who died as a result of [police brutality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_brutality_in_the_United_States).[^cite_note-WashPost7.20.20-155] An online tie-in event was also planned, called the 2020 Virtual March on Washington. It was held August 27 and 28, the latter being the anniversary of the iconic "[I Have a Dream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Have_a_Dream)" speech, and the day after President Trump was scheduled to [accept his party's nomination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Republican_National_Convention#President_Donald_Trump) for president at the [Republican National Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Republican_National_Convention).[^cite_note-156] Addressing the ongoing [COVID-19 pandemic](63136490.md), the organizers explained that the virtual component of the rally was organized to enable participation by people unable to travel to Washington, D.C., or safely participate in the in-person event.[^cite_note-WashPost7.20.20-155] The [NAACP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAACP)'s Virtual March featured performances from [Macy Gray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macy_Gray), [Burna Boy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burna_Boy), and speeches from [Stacey Abrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stacey_Abrams), [Nancy Pelosi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nancy_Pelosi), [Cory Booker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cory_Booker), and [Mahershala Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahershala_Ali), among many others. It was a two-night event broadcast on [ABC News Live](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABC_News_Live), [Bounce TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounce_TV), [TV One](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TV_One_\(American_TV_channel\)) and on online platforms.[^cite_note-157][^cite_note-158][^cite_note-159][^cite_note-160]

#### 2021 Voting Rights and D.C. Statehood March

On August 28, 2021, a march calling for voting rights and statehood for Washington D.C. was held in Washington D.C on the 58th anniversary of the March on Washington.[^cite_note-march58th-161] Though the numbers in the march permit revealed that 100,000 people were expected to attend, it was estimated that only 50,000 people attended.[^cite_note-march58th-161][^cite_note-162] However, the smaller crowd size did match the [National Action Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Action_Network)'s earlier estimate.[^cite_note-163] Among the speakers were Martin Luther King III, his wife and [Drum Major Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drum_Major_Institute) president Arndrea Waters King, daughter Yolanda, [National Action Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Action_Network) leader Rev. [Al Sharpton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Sharpton) and Washington, D.C., mayor [Muriel Bowser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muriel_Bowser).[^cite_note-march58th-161] Other speakers at the event included Democratic U.S. representatives [Joyce Beatty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joyce_Beatty), of Ohio, [Terri Sewell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terri_Sewell), of Alabama, [Sheila Jackson Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheila_Jackson_Lee) and [Al Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Green_\(politician\)), both of Texas, and [Mondaire Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mondaire_Jones), of New York; NAACP president [Derrick Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derrick_Johnson); and [Philonise Floyd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philonise_Floyd), activist and brother of George Floyd.[^cite_note-march58th-161]

#### 2023 60th Anniversary March

On August 26, 2023, a march was held in Washington D.C on the 60th anniversary of the March on Washington. Organizers include Martin Luther King III, his wife and Drum Major Institute president Arndrea Waters King, daughter Yolanda and National Action Network leader [Al Sharpton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Sharpton).[^cite_note-164] Tens of thousands of people gathered for a five-hour program that featured dozens of speakers.[^cite_note-165]

## Analysis

In 2013, the [Economic Policy Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_Policy_Institute) launched a series of reports around the theme of "The Unfinished March". These reports analyze the goals of the original march and assess how much progress has been made.[^cite_note-166][^cite_note-167] They echo the message of Randolph and Rustin that civil rights cannot transform people's quality of life unless accompanied by economic justice. They contend that many of the March's primary goals—including housing, integrated education, and widespread employment at living wages—have not been accomplished. They further argued that although legal advances were made, black people still live in concentrated areas of poverty ("ghettoes"), where they receive inferior education and suffer from widespread unemployment.[^cite_note-168]

Dedrick Muhammad of the NAACP writes that racial inequality of income and homeownership have increased since 1963 and worsened during the [Great Recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Recession).[^cite_note-169]
