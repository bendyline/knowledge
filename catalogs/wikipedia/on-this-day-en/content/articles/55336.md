# Cash

This article is about tangible money. For money in general, see [Money](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Money).

For other uses, see [Cash (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash_\(disambiguation\)).



Banknotes and coins of various currencies

**Cash** is [money](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Money) in the tangible form of [currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency), such as [banknotes](208286.md) and [coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coin).

In [book-keeping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book-keeping) and [financial accounting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_accounting), cash is [current assets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_asset) comprising currency or currency equivalents that can be accessed immediately or near-immediately (as in the case of [money market accounts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Money_market_account)). Cash is seen either as a [reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reserve_\(accounting\)) for payments, in case of a structural or incidental negative [cash flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash_flow), or as a way to avoid a downturn on [financial markets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_market).

## Etymology

The English word *cash* originally meant 'money box', and later came to have a secondary meaning 'money'. This secondary usage became the sole meaning in the 18th century. The word *cash* comes from the [Middle French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_French) *caisse* 'money box', which comes from the [Old Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Italian) *cassa*, and ultimately from the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) *capsa* 'box'.[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-2]

In [Western Europe](33800.md), after the [fall of the Western Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_Western_Roman_Empire), coins, silver jewelry and [hacksilver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hacksilver) (silver objects hacked into pieces) were for centuries the only form of money, until [Venetian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Venice) merchants started using silver bars for large transactions in the [early Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Middle_Ages). In a separate development, Venetian merchants started using [paper bills](208286.md), instructing their [banker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank) to make payments. Similar marked silver bars were in use in lands where the Venetian merchants had established representative offices. The [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire) and several states in the [Balkan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balkan) area and [Kievan Rus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kievan_Rus) also used marked silver bars for large payments. As the world economy developed and silver supplies increased, in particular after the colonization of South America, coins became larger and a standard coin for international payment developed from the 15th century: the Spanish and Spanish colonial coin of [8 reales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peso). Its counterpart in gold was the Venetian [ducat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ducat).

Coin types would compete for markets. By conquering foreign markets, the issuing rulers would enjoy extra income from [seigniorage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seigniorage) (the difference between the value of the coin and the value of the metal the coin was made of). Successful coin types of high nobility would be copied by lower nobility for seigniorage. Imitations were usually of a lower weight, undermining the popularity of the original. As feudal states coalesced into kingdoms, imitation of silver types abated, but gold coins, in particular, the gold ducat and the gold [florin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florin_\(Italian_coin\)) were still issued as trade coins: coins without a fixed value, going by weight. Colonial powers also sought to take away market share from [Spain](26667.md) by issuing trade coin equivalents of silver Spanish coins, without much success.

In the early part of the 17th century, [English East India Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_East_India_Company) coins were minted in England and shipped to the East. In England, over time the word *cash* was adopted from [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit) कर्ष karsa, a weight of gold or silver but akin to the [Old Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Persian) [𐎣𐎼𐏁](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%F0%90%8E%A3%F0%90%8E%BC%F0%90%8F%81) karsha, unit of weight (83.30 grams). East India Company coinage had both [Urdu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urdu) and English writing on it, to facilitate its use within the trade. In 1671, the directors of the East India Company ordered a mint to be established at [Bombay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombay), known as Bombaim. In 1677 this was sanctioned by the Crown, the coins, having received royal sanction, were struck as [silver rupees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_rupee); the inscription runs "The rupee of Bombaim", by the authority of Charles II.

Around that time, coins were also being produced for the [East India Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_India_Company) at the [Madras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madras) mint. The Tamil the word for money is kaasu,[^cite_note-3] which may have been modified into 'cash'. Both words, 'kaasu' and 'cash', have the same meaning, unlike money box. The currency at the company's Bombay and Bengal administrative regions was the rupee. At Madras, however, the company's accounts were reckoned in *pagodas*, *fractions*, *fanams*, *faluce* and *cash*. This system was maintained until 1818 when the rupee was adopted as the unit of currency for the company's operations.



Traditional holed Chinese coinage is also known as *[cash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash_\(Chinese_coin\))*.

[Paper money](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper_money) was first used in China during the [Tang dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tang_dynasty), 500 years prior to it catching on in Europe.[^cite_note-4] During his visit to China in the 13th century, Marco Polo was amazed to find that people traded paper money for goods rather than valuable coins made of silver or gold. He wrote extensively about how the Great Kaan used a part of the [mulberry tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morus_\(plant\)) to create the paper money as well as the process with which a seal was used to impress on the paper to authenticate it. Marco Polo also talks about the chance of forgery and states that someone caught forging money would be punished with death.[^cite_note-5] In the 17th century, European countries started to use paper money in part due to a shortage of precious metals, leading to fewer coins being produced and put into circulation.[^cite_note-6] At first, it was most popular in the colonies of European powers. In the 18th century, important paper issues were made in colonies such as [Ceylon](26750.md) and the bordering colonies of [Essequibo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essequibo_\(colony\)), [Demerara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demerara) and [Berbice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berbice). [John Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Law_\(economist\)) did pioneering work on banknotes with the *[Banque Royale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Law's_Bank)*. The relation between [money supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Money_supply) and inflation was still imperfectly understood and the bank went under rendering its notes worthless, because they had been over-issued. The lessons learned were applied to the [Bank of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_England), which played a crucial role in financing the [Peninsular War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peninsular_War) against French troops, hamstrung by a metallic Franc de Germinal.

The ability to create paper money made nation-states responsible for the management of [inflation](38286.md), through control of the [money supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Money_supply). It also made a direct relation between the metal of the coin and its denomination superfluous. From 1816, coins generally became [token money](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Token_money), though some large silver and gold coins remained standard coins until 1927. The [World War I](4764461.md) saw standard coins disappear to a very large extent. Afterward, standard gold coins, mainly [British sovereigns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovereign_\(British_coin\)), would still be used in colonies and less developed economies and silver [Maria Theresa thalers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Theresa_thaler) dated 1780 would be struck as trade coins for countries in East Asia until 1946 and possibly later locally.

Cash has now become a very small part of the money supply. Its remaining role is to provide a form of currency storage and payment for those who do not wish to take part in other systems, and make small payments conveniently and promptly, though this latter role is being replaced more and more frequently by electronic [payment systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Payment_system). Research has found that the demand for cash decreases as debit card usage increases because merchants need to make less change for customer purchases.[^cite_note-7]

Cash is increasing in circulation. The amount of the [United States dollar](18717338.md) in circulation increased by 42% from 2007 to 2012.[^cite_note-8] The amount of [pound sterling](270673.md) banknotes in circulation increased by 29% from 2008 to 2013.[^cite_note-9] The amount of [euro](9472.md) in circulation increased by 34% from August 2008 to August 2013 (2% of the increase was due to the adoption of euro in [Slovakia](26830.md) 2009 and in [Estonia](28222445.md) 2011).[^cite_note-10]

## Motives of cash holding

In economic theory (according [Keynesian economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keynesian_economics)), the cash holding of cash (especially [sight deposits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional-reserve_banking#Types_of_money)) is roughly attributed to three motives:[^cite_note-11]

- Transactions motive

- Precautionary motive

- Speculative motive.

The transactions motive covers the business needs of economic subjects, the precautionary motive serves to hold money for liquidity purposes and to provide for crisis situations,[^cite_note-12] and the speculation motive, according to [John Maynard Keynes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maynard_Keynes), results from the uncertainty about future interest rate developments and relates to financial investments.

In addition to this purely economic importance, there are other aspects of cash use:[^cite_note-13][^cite_note-14][^cite_note-\:0-15]

- Anonymous payment without disclosing personal data

- Trust to the central bank (control and publication of [money creation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Money_creation))

- Activation of a reward center in the brain (anticipation of reaching a specific goal)

- Expenditure control (immediate physical payment)

- Tradition (haptic experience, e.g. monetary donation; long-term reliability of value retention)

- [Inclusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclusion_\(disability_rights\)) (equal participation in economic life for all)

- Identification (symbolic character, solidarity and group membership)

- Educational tool for children (objective handling of assets and expenses)

- Paying a tip as immediate recognition of good service.

In practice, there may be a combination of such motives, with the precautionary motive of preserving value and anonymous payment being decisive. Due to its unique characteristics, there is no perfect substitute for cash. [Demonetisation](267793.md) or [capital control](5015944.md) can destabilize the economy if electronic means of payment are not readily available (e.g. [2016 Indian banknote demonetisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Indian_banknote_demonetisation)).[^cite_note-\:0-15]

## Cash in circulation

Cash in circulation is characterized by strong seasonal fluctuations. Wage and salary payment dates, tax payment dates or holidays lead to statistically perceptible increases in cash in circulation, for which the credit institutions are preparing. Since cash holdings at banks do not earn interest and can also lead to security problems ([bank robbery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_robbery)), banks usually only hold very small amounts of cash. They are therefore forced to involve the central bank in times of higher cash requirements. Therefore, the cash in circulation only remains unchanged if the banks hand over cash from their own cash holdings to their bank customers or take cash deposits from their customers into their own holdings.

The ratio of the cash in circulation in relation to the [gross domestic product](12594.md) (cash to GDP ratio) is a good indicator of cash usage and payment behavior in an economy. In countries like the [United States](3434750.md), increased use of debit and credit cards is increasing the amount of cash in circulation at a slower rate than in countries with a high amount of cash payments. In 2018, it ranged from 1.3% (in [Sweden](5058739.md)) to more than 21% (in [Japan](15573.md)), 10.5% in Switzerland and 10.7% in the [eurozone](184391.md).[^cite_note-16]

Since around 2018, exacerbated by the [COVID-19 pandemic](62750956.md), cash in circulation in the [eurozone](184391.md) has increased significantly while the share of cash payments (i.e. transactions) has decreased, known as the *paradox of banknotes*. Analyses show that private households are increasingly keeping cash as a precaution against crises and that [negative interest rates](163115.md) also play a role.[^cite_note-17] This effect is also observed in many other currency areas, e.g. in the [United States](3434750.md) and [Japan](15573.md).[^cite_note-18]

## Competition of cash

### Cashless payments

Main article: [Cashless society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cashless_society)



[Credit cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credit_card) are used for cashless payments. With a credit card, the credit card company grants a [line of credit](4611307.md) to the card holder. The card holder can make purchases from merchants, and borrow the money for these purchases from the credit card company.

[Cashless society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cashless_society) can be defined as one in which all financial transactions are handled through "digital" forms (debit and credit cards) in preference to cash (physical banknotes and coins). Cashless societies have been a part of history from the very beginning of human existence. Barter and other methods of exchange were used to conduct a wide variety of trade transactions during this time period.[^cite_note-25]

Since the 1980s, the use of banknotes has increasingly been displaced by [credit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credit_card) and [debit cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debit_card), electronic money transfers and [mobile payments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_payment), but much slower than expected. The [cashless society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cashless_society) has been predicted for more than forty years,[^cite_note-26] but cash remains the most widely used payment instrument in the world and on all continents.[^cite_note-CashReport-27]<sup>: 14 </sup> In 17 out of 24 studied countries, cash represents more than 50% of all payment transactions, with [Austria](26964606.md) at 85%, [Germany](11867.md) at 80%, [France](5843419.md) at 68%. The [United Kingdom](31717.md) at 42%, [Australia](4689264.md) at 37%, [United States](3434750.md) at 32%, [Sweden](5058739.md) at 20%, and [South Korea](27019.md) at 14% are among the countries with lower cash usage.[^cite_note-CashReport-27]<sup>: 27 </sup>

By the 2010s, cash was no longer the preferred method of payment in the [United States](3434750.md).[^cite_note-28] In 2016, the United States User Consumer Survey Study reported that three out of four of the participants preferred a debit or credit card payment instead of cash.[^cite_note-29] Some nations have contributed to this trend, by regulating what type of transactions can be conducted with cash and setting limits on the amount of cash that can be used in a single transaction.

Cash is still the primary means of payment (and store of value) for [unbanked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unbanked) people with a low income and helps avoiding debt traps due to uncontrolled spending of money. It supports [anonymity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anonymity) and avoids tracking for economic or political reasons.[^cite_note-30] In addition, cash is the only means for [contingency planning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contingency_plan) in order to mitigate risks in case of [natural disasters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_disasters) or failures of the technical infrastructure like a large-scale power blackout or shutdown of the communication network.[^cite_note-31] Therefore, central banks and governments are increasingly driving the sufficient availability of cash. The [US Federal Reserve](10819.md) has provided guidelines for the continuity of cash services,[^cite_note-32] and the Swedish government is concerned about the consequences in abandoning cash and is considering to pass a law requiring all banks to handle cash.[^cite_note-33]

### Digital and virtual currencies

Main articles: [Digital currency](1226927.md) and [Virtual currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_currency)

[Digital currency](1226927.md) is a generic term for various approaches to support secure transactions of the public or using a [distributed ledger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributed_ledger), like [blockchain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blockchain), as a new technology for decentralized asset management. The blockchain 1.0 era has enabled the application of virtual digital currencies in the marketplace, such as money transfer and payment systems.[^cite_note-34] It considers establishing an electronic version of the national currency which is backed by the central bank as the issuer. [Virtual currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_currency) is a digital representation of value that is neither issued by a central bank or a public authority, such as [Bitcoin](28249265.md).[^cite_note-35] Facebook's concept for the [diem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diem_\(digital_currency\)) is based on a token to be backed by financial assets such as a basket of national currencies.&#x20;

In 2012, [Bank of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_Canada) was considering introducing digital currency.[^cite_note-36][^cite_note-37] Meanwhile, it rates digital currency a fairly complicated decision and is analyzing the pros and cons and working to determine under which conditions it may make sense to, one day, issue a digital currency. As a threat, a central bank digital currency could increase the risk of a run on the banking system.[^cite_note-38]

Also in 2012, [Sveriges Riksbank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sveriges_Riksbank), the central bank of Sweden, was reported to analyze technological advances with regard to electronic money and payment methods for digital currency as an alternative to cash.[^cite_note-39] In 2019, it is investigating whether [Swedish krona](16709.md) need to be made available in electronic form, the so-called e-krona, and if so, how it would affect Swedish legislation and the Riksbank's task. It has started procuring a technical supplier to develop and test solutions for a potential future e-krona. No decisions have yet been taken on issuing an e-krona.[^cite_note-40]

### Costs of payment

An analysis by the [Deutsche Bundesbank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsche_Bundesbank) in 2017 found that a cash payment in retail costs an average of 24 [euro](9472.md) cents, while payments with a [girocard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girocard) cost 30 cents (or often 0.3 to 0.4% of sales plus a transaction fee) and with a [credit card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credit_card) charge one euro which is included in the sales price.[^cite_note-41] This is why retailers often refuse to accept card payments below a minimum amount. Depending on the account model, there are also booking costs for the account holder with an average of 35 euro cents charged for each(!) account posting. Because of this convenient source of income, commercial banks and credit card companies favor cashless payments.

In the case of cashless payment transactions, in addition to the documentation of the payment itself, the personal details of the payer are usually linked to the data of the payee according to the [Know Your Customer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Know_Your_Customer) (KYC) principle. This enables the payment process to be precisely traced for the payer and the payee. The constant increase in digitization leads to a more detailed recording of cashless payment transactions and their evaluation for advertising and marketing campaigns. Since this digital documentation is usually more centralized than before, the potential for abuse increases. On the other hand, the cash transactions are anonymous, unless purchasing profiles are recorded with the help of [loyalty programs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loyalty_program) based on customer cards, and keep the payment landscape competitive.[^cite_note-42]

## Cash in constitutions

### Austria

In August 2023, [Chancellor of Austria](60582.md) [Karl Nehammer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Nehammer) came out in support for enshrining cash in the [Austrian constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_constitution). This came after the [Freedom Party of Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_Party_of_Austria) campaigned on the idea.[^cite_note-43]

### Hungary

In February 2025, [Prime minister of Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_minister_of_Hungary) [Viktor Orbán](750582.md) announced plans to enshrine the right to use cash in the [Hungarian Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_Constitution). This was first proposed by the far-right [Mi Hazánk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mi_Haz%C3%A1nk).[^cite_note-44] In April 2025, the Hungarian parliament passed these constitutional amendments.[^cite_note-45]

### Switzerland

In 2023, the Swiss government supported moves to have a constitutional protection for cash. This came after a popular initiative asked for it.[^cite_note-46]

### Slovakia

In June 2023, the Slovak parliament voted with the support of 111 of 150 MPs to put the right to use cash in the [Constitution of Slovakia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Slovakia). The amendment was proposed by the [Sme Rodina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sme_Rodina) party.[^cite_note-47]
