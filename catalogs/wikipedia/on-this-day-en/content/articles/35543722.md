# Macroscopic quantum phenomena

**Macroscopic quantum phenomena** are processes showing [quantum behaviour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics) at the [macroscopic scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macroscopic_scale), rather than at the [atomic scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom) where quantum effects are prevalent. The best-known examples of macroscopic quantum phenomena are [superfluidity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superfluidity) and [superconductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconductivity); other examples include the [quantum Hall effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_Hall_effect), [Josephson effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephson_effect) and [topological order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_order). Since 2000 there has been extensive experimental work on quantum gases, particularly [Bose–Einstein condensates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bose%E2%80%93Einstein_condensate).&#x20;

As of 2025, seven [Nobel Prizes in Physics](52497.md) have been awarded for work related to macroscopic quantum phenomena.[^cite_note-1] Macroscopic quantum phenomena can be observed in [superfluid helium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superfluid_helium) and in [superconductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconductors),[^cite_note-2] but also in dilute quantum gases, [dressed photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dressed_particle) such as [polaritons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bose%E2%80%93Einstein_condensation_of_polaritons) and in [laser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser) light. Although these media are very different, they are all similar in that they show macroscopic quantum behaviour, and in this respect they all can be referred to as [quantum fluids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_fluid).&#x20;

Quantum phenomena are generally classified as macroscopic when the quantum states are occupied by a large number of particles (of the order of the [Avogadro number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avogadro_number)) or the quantum states involved are macroscopic in size (up to kilometre-sized in [superconducting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconducting) wires).[^cite_note-3]&#x20;

## Superconductivity

Main article: [Superconductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconductivity)

In the original paper[^cite_note-8] Ginzburg and Landau observed the existence of two types of superconductors depending on the energy of the interface between the normal and superconducting states. The [Meissner state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meissner_state) breaks down when the applied magnetic field is too large. Superconductors can be divided into two classes according to how this breakdown occurs. In [Type I superconductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_I_superconductor), superconductivity is abruptly destroyed when the strength of the applied field rises above a critical value *H<sub>c</sub>*. Depending on the geometry of the sample, one may obtain an intermediate state[^cite_note-9] consisting of a baroque pattern of regions of normal material carrying a magnetic field mixed with regions of superconducting material containing no field. In [Type II superconductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_II_superconductor), raising the applied field past a critical value *H*<sub>*c*1</sub> leads to a mixed state (also known as the vortex state) in which an increasing amount of [magnetic flux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_flux) penetrates the material, but there remains no resistance to the flow of electric current as long as the current is not too large. At a second critical field strength *H*<sub>*c*2</sub>, superconductivity is destroyed. The mixed state is actually caused by vortices in the electronic superfluid, sometimes called [fluxons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluxon) because the flux carried by these vortices is [quantized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum). Most pure [elemental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_element) superconductors, except [niobium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niobium) and [carbon nanotubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_nanotube), are Type I, while almost all impure and compound superconductors are Type II.&#x20;

The most important finding from [Ginzburg–Landau theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ginzburg%E2%80%93Landau_theory) was made by [Alexei Abrikosov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexei_Alexeyevich_Abrikosov) in 1957. He used Ginzburg–Landau theory to explain experiments on superconducting alloys and thin films. He found that in a type-II superconductor in a high magnetic field, the field penetrates in a triangular lattice of quantized tubes of flux [vortices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrikosov_vortices). For this and related work, he was awarded the Nobel Prize in 2003 with [Ginzburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitaly_Ginzburg) and [Leggett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_James_Leggett).[^cite_note-10]&#x20;

### Type II superconductivity

Main article: [Type-II superconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type-II_superconductor)



&#x20;Fig. 7. Magnetic flux lines penetrating a type-II superconductor. The currents in the superconducting material generate a magnetic field which, together with the applied field, result in bundles of quantized flux.

[Type-II superconductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type-II_superconductor) is characterized by two critical fields called *B*<sub>c1</sub> and *B*<sub>c2</sub>. At a magnetic field *B*<sub>c1</sub> the applied magnetic field starts to penetrate the sample, but the sample is still superconducting. Only at a field of *B*<sub>c2</sub> the sample is completely normal. For fields in between *B*<sub>c1</sub> and *B*<sub>c2</sub> magnetic flux penetrates the superconductor in well-organized patterns, the so-called [Abrikosov vortex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrikosov_vortex) lattice similar to the pattern shown in Fig. 2.[^cite_note-15] A cross section of the superconducting plate is given in Fig. 7. Far away from the plate the field is homogeneous, but in the material superconducting currents flow which squeeze the field in bundles of exactly one flux quantum. The typical field in the core is as big as 1 tesla. The currents around the vortex core flow in a layer of about 50 nm with current densities on the order of 15×10<sup>12</sup> A/m<sup>2</sup>. That corresponds with 15 million ampère in a wire of one mm<sup>2</sup>.&#x20;

## Dilute quantum gases

The classical types of quantum systems, superconductors and superfluid helium, were discovered in the beginning of the 20th century. Near the end of the 20th century, scientists discovered how to create very dilute atomic or molecular gases, cooled first by [laser cooling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_cooling) and then by [evaporative cooling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evaporative_cooling_\(atomic_physics\)).[^cite_note-16] They are trapped using magnetic fields or optical dipole potentials in ultrahigh vacuum chambers. Isotopes which have been used include rubidium (Rb-87 and Rb-85), strontium (Sr-87, Sr-86, and Sr-84) potassium (K-39 and K-40), sodium (Na-23), lithium (Li-7 and Li-6), and hydrogen (H-1). The temperatures to which they can be cooled are as low as a few nanokelvin. The developments have been very fast in the past few years. A team of NIST and the University of Colorado has succeeded in creating and observing vortex quantization in these systems.[^cite_note-17] The concentration of vortices increases with the angular velocity of the rotation, similar to the case of superfluid helium and superconductivity.&#x20;

## Mechanical systems

Macroscopic quantum effects have been observed in the motion of engineered mechanical objects.[^cite_note-18] Research in the field of quantum optomechanics focuses on the manipulation of the quantized vibrational states ([phonons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonon)) of [mechanical resonators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resonator).[^cite_note-19]&#x20;

Control is realized via two primary experimental architectures. Cavity optomechanics uses laser light in an [optical cavity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_cavity) to couple to the motion of objects like mirrors or membranes.[^cite_note-20] Circuit electromechanics uses [superconducting microwave circuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconducting_quantum_computing) to couple to [piezoelectric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piezoelectricity) resonators, typically realized as [surface acoustic wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_acoustic_wave) (SAW) or [bulk acoustic resonator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thin-film_bulk_acoustic_resonator) (BAR) devices.[^cite_note-21] Both architectures have been used to cool massive resonators to their quantum [ground state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_state).[^cite_note-OConnell2010-22][^cite_note-23]&#x20;

A landmark experiment in 2010, using an electromechanical system, was the first to demonstrate the deterministic preparation of non-classical states in a mechanical resonator.[^cite_note-OConnell2010-22] This work achieved cooling to the quantum ground state (    n = 0   {\displaystyle n=0}  {\displaystyle n=0}), preparation of a single-phonon [Fock state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fock_state) (    n = 1   {\displaystyle n=1}  {\displaystyle n=1}), and the generation of a [quantum superposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_superposition) between these two states (    α  |  0 ⟩ + β  |  1 ⟩   {\displaystyle \alpha |0\rangle +\beta |1\rangle }  {\displaystyle \alpha |0\rangle +\beta |1\rangle }). This achievement was recognized by *Science* as its 2010 [Breakthrough of the Year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breakthrough_of_the_Year).[^cite_note-24]&#x20;

Progress in both architectures has also enabled the demonstration of [quantum entanglement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_entanglement) between two distinct macroscopic mechanical resonators.[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26]&#x20;
