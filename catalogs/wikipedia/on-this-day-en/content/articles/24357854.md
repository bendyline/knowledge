A **nuclear reactor coolant** is a [coolant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coolant) in a [nuclear reactor](22151.md) used to remove heat from the [nuclear reactor core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_reactor_core) and transfer it to [electrical generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_generator) and the [environment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environment_\(systems\)). Frequently, a chain of two coolant loops are used because the primary coolant loop takes on short-term [radioactivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactivity) from the reactor.

## Water

Almost all currently operating [nuclear power plants](207347.md) are [light water reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_water_reactor) using ordinary water under high pressure as coolant and [neutron moderator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_moderator). About 1/3 are [boiling water reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boiling_water_reactor) where the primary coolant undergoes [phase transition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_transition) to [steam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam) inside the reactor. About 2/3 are [pressurized water reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressurized_water_reactor) at even higher pressure. Current reactors stay under the [critical point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_point_\(thermodynamics\)) at around 374 °C and 218 [bar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bar_\(unit\)) where the distinction between liquid and gas disappears, which limits [thermal efficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_efficiency), but the proposed [supercritical water reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercritical_water_reactor) would operate above this point.

[Heavy water reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_water_reactor) use [deuterium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deuterium) oxide which has identical properties to ordinary water but much lower [neutron capture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_capture), allowing more thorough moderation.

### Disadvantages

#### Tritium leak

As the hydrogen atoms in water coolants are bombarded with neutrons, some absorb a neutron to become [deuterium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deuterium), and then some become radioactive [tritium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tritium). Water contaminated with tritium sometimes leaks to groundwater by accident or by official approval.[^cite_note-1]

#### Hydrogen explosion

Fuel rods create high temperatures which boil water into steam. During a power outage, diesel power generators which provide emergency power to water pumps may be damaged by a tsunami, earthquake or both; if no fresh water is being pumped to cool the fuel rods then the fuel rods continue to heat up. Once the fuel rods reach more than 1200°C, the zirconium tubes that contain the nuclear fuel will interact with the steam and split hydrogen from water molecules. This hydrogen may leak from breaches in the reactor core and containment vessel. If hydrogen accumulates in sufficient quantities – concentrations of 4% or more in the air – then it can explode, as has apparently occurred at [Fukushima Daiichi reactors No. 1, 3, and 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukushima_Daiichi_nuclear_disaster).&#x20;

Such an explosion was avoided at [Reactor No. 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukushima_Daiichi_nuclear_disaster), which opened its vent to let out hydrogen, decreasing pressure by releasing radioactive hydrogen gas.[^cite_note-2]

## Borated water

Borated water is used as a coolant during normal operation of [pressurized water reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressurized_water_reactor) (PWRs) as well as in [Emergency Core Cooling Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergency_Core_Cooling_System) (ECCS) of both PWRs and [boiling water reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boiling_water_reactor) (BWRs).[^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5]

### Advantages

[Boron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boron), often in the form of [boric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boric_acid) or sodium borate, is combined with water — a cheap and plentiful resource — where it acts as a coolant to remove heat from the reactor core and transfers the heat to a secondary circuit.[^cite_note-6] Part of the secondary circuit is the [steam generator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_generator_\(nuclear_power\)) that is used to turn turbines and generate electricity. Borated water also provides the additional benefits of acting as a [neutron poison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_poison) due to its large neutron absorption cross-section, where it absorbs excess neutrons to help control the fission rate of the reactor. Thus, the reactivity of the [nuclear reactor](22151.md) can be easily adjusted by changing the boron concentration in the coolant. That is, when the boron concentration is increased (boration) by dissolving more boric acid into the coolant, the reactivity of the reactor is decreased. Conversely, when the boron concentration is decreased (dilution) by adding more water, the reactivity of the reactor is increased.[^cite_note-Monterrosa2012-7]

### Disadvantages

Approximately 90% of the [tritium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tritium) in PWR coolants is produced by reactions of boron-10 with neutrons. Since tritium itself is a radioactive isotope of hydrogen, the coolant becomes contaminated with [radioactive isotopes](37245.md) and must be kept from leaking into the environment. Additionally, this effect must be taken into account for longer cycles of nuclear reactor operation and thus requires higher initial concentration of boron in the coolant.[^cite_note-Monterrosa2012-7]

## Molten metal

[Fast reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_reactor) have a high [power density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_density) and do not need, and must avoid, neutron moderation. Most have been [liquid metal cooled reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_metal_cooled_reactor) using molten [sodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium). Lead, [lead-bismuth eutectic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead-bismuth_eutectic), and other metals have also been proposed and [occasionally used](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead-cooled_fast_reactor). [Mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_\(element\)) was used in the [first fast reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clementine_\(nuclear_reactor\)).

## Molten salt

[Molten salts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molten_salt) share with metals the advantage of low [vapor pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vapor_pressure) even at high temperatures, and are less chemically reactive than [sodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium-cooled_fast_reactor#Disadvantages). Salts containing light elements like [FLiBe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FLiBe) can also provide moderation. In the [Molten-Salt Reactor Experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molten-Salt_Reactor_Experiment) it even served as a solvent carrying the nuclear fuel.

## Gas

See also: [Gas-cooled reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas-cooled_reactor)

Gases have also been used as coolant. [Helium](13256.md) is extremely inert both chemically and with respect to nuclear reactions but has a low [heat capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_capacity).

## Hydrocarbons

[Organically moderated and cooled reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organically_moderated_and_cooled_reactor) were an early concept studied, using hydrocarbons as coolant. They were not successful.
