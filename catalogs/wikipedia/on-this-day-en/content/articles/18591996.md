A **boycott** is an act of [nonviolent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonviolent_resistance), voluntary abstention from a product, person, organization, or country as an expression of protest. It is usually for [moral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morality), [social](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society), [political](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics), or [environmental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmentalism) reasons. The purpose of a boycott is to inflict some [economic loss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_loss) on the target, or to indicate a moral outrage, usually to try to compel the target to alter an objectionable behavior.

The word is named after Captain [Charles Boycott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Boycott), agent of an absentee landlord in Ireland, against whom the tactic was successfully employed after a suggestion by Irish nationalist leader [Charles Stewart Parnell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Stewart_Parnell) and his [Irish Land League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Land_League) in 1880.

Sometimes, a boycott can be a form of [consumer activism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumer_activism), sometimes called [moral purchasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_purchasing). When a similar practice is legislated by a national government, it is known as a [sanction](411315.md). Frequently, however, the threat of boycotting a business is an empty threat, with no significant effect on sales.[^cite_note-1]

## Etymology



*[Vanity Fair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanity_Fair_\(British_magazine\))* caricature of Charles C. Boycott

The word *boycott* entered the English language during the [Irish](147575.md) "[Land War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_War)" and derives from Captain [Charles Boycott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Boycott), the [land agent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_agent) of an [absentee landlord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absentee_landlord), [Lord Erne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Crichton,_3rd_Earl_Erne), who lived in [County Mayo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Mayo), [Ireland](147575.md). Captain Boycott was the target of social ostracism organized by the [Irish Land League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Land_League) in 1880. As harvests had been poor that year, Lord Erne offered his tenants a ten percent reduction in their rents. In September of that year, protesting tenants demanded a twenty-five percent reduction, which Lord Erne refused. Boycott then attempted to evict eleven tenants from the land. [Charles Stewart Parnell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Stewart_Parnell), an Irish leader, proposed that when dealing with tenants who take farms where another tenant was evicted, rather than resorting to violence, everyone in the locality should shun them. While Parnell's speech did not refer to land agents or landlords, the tactic was first applied to Boycott when the alarm was raised about the evictions. Despite the short-term economic hardship to those undertaking this action, Boycott soon found himself isolated – his workers stopped work in the fields and stables, as well as in his house. Local businessmen stopped trading with him, and the local postman refused to deliver mail.[^cite_note-2]

The concerted action taken against him meant that Boycott was unable to hire anyone to harvest his crops in his charge.[^cite_note-3] After the harvest, the "boycott" was successfully continued and soon the new word was everywhere. A *[New-York Tribune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New-York_Tribune)* reporter, [James Redpath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Redpath), first wrote of the boycott in the international press. The Irish author [George Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Moore_\(novelist\)) reported: 'Like a comet the verb 'boycott' appeared.'[^cite_note-4] It was used by *[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times)* in November 1880 as a term for organized isolation. According to an account in the book *The Fall of Feudalism in Ireland* by [Michael Davitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Davitt), the term was promoted by Fr. John O'Malley of County Mayo to "signify ostracism applied to a landlord or agent like Boycott". *[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times)* first reported on November 20, 1880: "The people of New Pallas have resolved to 'boycott' them and refused to supply them with food or drink." The *Daily News* wrote on December 13, 1880: "Already the stoutest-hearted are yielding on every side to the dread of being 'Boycotted'." By January of the following year, the word was being used figuratively: "Dame Nature arose.... She 'Boycotted' [London](17867.md) from Kew to Mile End."[^cite_note-5]

### Girlcott

**Girlcott**, a [pun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pun) on "boycott", is a boycott intended to focus on the rights or actions of women. The term was coined in 1968 by American [Lacey O'Neal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lacey_O'Neal) during the [1968 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1968_Summer_Olympics) in the context of [protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1968_Olympics_Black_Power_salute) by male [African American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_American) athletes. The term was later used by retired [tennis](29773.md) player [Billie Jean King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billie_Jean_King) in 1999 in reference to [Wimbledon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Championships,_Wimbledon) while discussing [equal pay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equal_pay_for_equal_work) for women players.[^cite_note-6] The term "girlcott" was revived in 2005 by the [Women and Girls Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_and_Girls_Foundation) in [Allegheny County, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allegheny_County,_Pennsylvania) against [Abercrombie & Fitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abercrombie_&_Fitch).[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8]

## Application and uses



Protesters advocating boycott of [BP](18998720.md) due to the [Deepwater Horizon oil spill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deepwater_Horizon_oil_spill)

In studies of [political consumerism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_consumerism), boycotts are often distinguished from “buycotts”. A boycott involves withholding purchases or support from a target, while a buycott involves deliberately supporting a company, product, or service because of its perceived political, social, or ethical conduct.[^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31]

Boycotts are now much easier to successfully initiate due to the [Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet). Examples include the [gay and lesbian](5488304.md) boycott of advertisers of the *[Dr. Laura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dr._Laura)* [talk show](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talk_show), gun owners' similar boycott of advertisers of [Rosie O'Donnell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosie_O'Donnell)'s talk show and (later) magazine, and gun owners' boycott of [Smith & Wesson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smith_&_Wesson) following that company's March 2000 settlement with the [Clinton administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinton_administration). They may be initiated very easily using either websites (the *Dr. Laura* boycott), newsgroups (the Rosie O'Donnell boycotts), or even mailing lists. Internet-initiated boycotts "snowball" very quickly compared to other forms of organization.



African-Americans in Dallas boycotting a Korean owned [Kwik Stop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Shamrock_and_Kwik_Stop_Boycott) in a mostly black community.

*Viral Labeling* is a new boycott method using the new digital technology proposed by the Multitude Project and applied for the first time against [Walt Disney](37398.md) around Christmas time in 2009.[^cite_note-32]

Some boycotts center on particular businesses, such as recent protests regarding [Costco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Costco), [Walmart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walmart), [Ford Motor Company](30433662.md), or the diverse products of [Philip Morris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altria_Group#Food_.26_Beverage). Another form of boycott identifies a number of different companies involved in a particular issue, such as the Sudan Divestment campaign, the "Boycott Bush" campaign. The Boycott Bush website was set up by [Ethical Consumer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethical_Consumer) after U.S. President [George W. Bush](3414021.md) failed to ratify the [Kyoto Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto_Protocol) – the website identified Bush's corporate funders and the brands and products they produce. Historically boycotts have also targeted individual businesses. During the early decades of the twentieth century, hotels in Australia were regularly targeted over the cost of alcohol, accommodation and food, as well as mistreatment of employees.[^cite_note-33] [Pope Francis](1687680.md) refers to boycotting as a successful means of influencing businesses, "forcing them to consider their [environmental footprint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_footprint) and their patterns of production".[^cite_note-34]

Mobile applications have also been developed to support consumer boycotts by allowing users to scan barcodes or search brand names against campaign-specific boycott lists. Coverage of some such apps has raised questions about the reliability of the sources and criteria used to classify companies or products.[^cite_note-35][^cite_note-36]

As a response to consumer boycotts of large-scale and multinational businesses, some companies have marketed brands that do not bear the company's name on the packaging or in advertising. Activists such as Ethical Consumer produce information that reveals which companies own which brands and products so consumers can practice boycotts or moral purchasing more effectively. Another organization, [Buycott.com](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buycott.com), provides an Internet-based smart-phone application that scans [Universal Product Codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Product_Code) and displays corporate relationships to the user.[^cite_note-Forbes-37]

"Boycotts" may be formally organized by governments as well. In reality, government "boycotts" are just a type of [embargo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embargo). Notably, the first formal, nationwide act of the Nazi government against German Jews was a national [embargo of Jewish businesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_boycott_of_Jewish_businesses) on April 1, 1933.[^cite_note-38]

Where the target of a boycott derives all or part of its revenues from other businesses, as a newspaper does, boycott organizers may address the target's commercial customers.

## Collective behavior

The sociology of [collective behavior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collective_behavior) is concerned with causes and conditions pertaining to behavior carried out by a collective, as opposed to an individual (e.g., [riots](146704.md), [panics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_panic), [fads/crazes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fad), boycotts). Boycotts have been characterized by some as different from traditional forms of collective behavior in that they appear to be highly rational and dependent on existing norms and structures. Lewis Killian criticizes that characterization, pointing to the [Tallahassee bus boycott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tallahassee_bus_boycott) as one example of a boycott that aligns with traditional collective behavior theory.[^cite_note-39]

Philip Balsiger points out that political consumption (e.g., boycotts) tends to follow dual-purpose action repertoires, or scripts, which are used publicly to pressure boycott targets and to educate and recruit consumers. Balsiger finds one example in Switzerland, documenting activities of the [Clean Clothes Campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clean_Clothes_Campaign), a public [NGO](46539.md)-backed campaign, that highlighted and disseminated information about local companies' ethical practices.[^cite_note-40]

Dixon, Martin, and Nau analyzed 31 collective behavior campaigns against corporations that took place during the 1990s and 2000s. Protests considered successful included boycotts and were found to include a third party, either in the capacity of state intervention or of media coverage. State intervention may make boycotts more efficacious when corporation leaders fear the imposition of regulations. Media intervention may be a crucial contributor to a successful boycott because of its potential to damage the reputation of a corporation. Target corporations that were the most visible were found to be the most vulnerable to either market (protest causing economic loss) or mediated (caused by third-party) disruption. Third-party actors (i.e., the state or media) were more influential when a corporation had a high reputation—when third-party activity was low, highly reputable corporations did not make the desired concessions to boycotters; when third-party activity was high, highly reputable corporations satisfied the demands of boycotters. The boycott, a prima facie market-disruptive tactic, often precipitates mediated disruption. The researchers' analysis led them to conclude that when boycott targets are highly visible and directly interact with and depend on local consumers who can easily find substitutes, they are more likely to make concessions. Koku, Akhigbe, and Springer also emphasize the importance of boycotts' threat of reputational damage, finding that boycotts alone pose more of a threat to a corporation's reputation than to its finances directly.[^cite_note-41][^cite_note-42]

Philippe Delacote points out that a problem contributing to a generally low probability of success for any boycott is the fact that the consumers with the most power to cause market disruption are the least likely to participate; the opposite is true for consumers with the least power. Another collective behavior problem is the difficulty, or impossibility, of direct coordination amongst a dispersed group of boycotters. Yuksel and Mryteza emphasize the collective behavior problem of free riding in consumer boycotts, noting that some individuals may perceive participating to be too great an immediate personal utility sacrifice. They also note that boycotting consumers took the collectivity into account when deciding to participate, that is, consideration of joining a boycott as goal-oriented collective activity increased one's likelihood of participating. A corporation-targeted protest repertoire including boycotts and education of consumers presents the highest likelihood for success.This has been linked to the broader problem of substitution costs, since participation may be less likely when avoiding a target requires consumers to give up goods or services for which convenient alternatives are unavailable.[^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44]

## Legality



Protesters calling for a boycott of Israel

Boycotts are generally legal in developed countries. Occasionally, some restrictions may apply; for instance, in the United States, it may be unlawful for a union to engage in "[secondary boycotts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_boycott)" (to request that its members boycott companies that supply items to an organization already under a boycott, in the United States);[^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46] however, the union is free to use its right to speak freely to inform its members of the fact that suppliers of a company are breaking a boycott; its members then may take whatever action they deem appropriate, in consideration of that fact.

### United Kingdom

When the boycott first emerged in Ireland, it presented a serious dilemma for [William Ewart Gladstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Ewart_Gladstone)'s government. The individual actions that constituted a boycott were recognized by legislators as essential to a free society. However, overall a boycott amounted to a harsh, extrajudicial punishment. The [Prevention of Crime (Ireland) Act 1882](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prevention_of_Crime_\(Ireland\)_Act_1882?action=edit\&redlink=1) made it illegal to use "intimidation" to instigate or enforce a boycott, but not to participate in one.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELaird200534-47]

The 19th-century conservative jurist [James Fitzjames Stephen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Fitzjames_Stephen) justified laws against boycotting by claiming that the practice amounted to "usurpation of the functions of government" and ought therefore to be dealt with as "the modern representatives of the old conception of high treason".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELaird200536-48]

### United States



"Boycott Xinjiang Genocide Products! Also don't attack our Chinese neighbors. Just say no to xenophobia and racism!" sticker on New York University campus in 2020

Boycotts are legal under common law. The right to engage in commerce, social intercourse, and friendship includes the implied right not to engage in commerce, social intercourse, and friendship. Since a boycott is voluntary and nonviolent, the law cannot stop it. Opponents of boycotts historically have the choice of suffering under it, yielding to its demands, or attempting to suppress it through extralegal means, such as force and coercion.

In the United States, the antiboycott provisions of the [Export Administration Regulations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Export_Administration_Regulations) (EAR) apply to all "U.S. persons", defined to include individuals and companies located in the United States and their foreign affiliates. The antiboycott provisions are intended to prevent United States citizens and companies being used as instrumentalities of a foreign government's foreign policy. The EAR forbids participation in or material support of boycotts initiated by foreign governments; for example, the [Arab League](52625.md) boycott of [Israel](9282173.md). These persons are subject to the law when their activities relate to the sale, purchase, or transfer of goods or services (including the sale of information) within the United States or between the United States and a foreign country. This covers exports and imports, financing, forwarding and shipping, and certain other transactions that may take place wholly offshore.[^cite_note-49]

However, the EAR only applies to *foreign government initiated* boycotts: a domestic boycott campaign arising within the United States that has the same object as the foreign-government-initiated boycott appears to be lawful, assuming that it is an independent effort not connected with the foreign government's boycott.

Other legal impediments to certain boycotts remain. One set are [refusal to deal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refusal_to_deal) laws, which prohibit concerted efforts to eliminate competition by refusal to buy from or to sell to a party.[^cite_note-50] Similarly, boycotts may also run afoul of [anti-discrimination laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-discrimination_laws); for example, [New Jersey](21648.md)'s Law Against Discrimination prohibits any place that offers goods, services and facilities to the general public, such as a restaurant, from denying or withholding any accommodation to (i.e., not to engage in commerce with) an individual because of that individual's race (etc.).[^cite_note-51]

A boycott is typically a one-time affair intended to correct an outstanding single wrong. When extended for a long period of time, or as part of an overall program of awareness-raising or reforms to laws or regimes, a boycott is part of [moral purchasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_purchasing), and some prefer those economic or political terms. Most organized consumer boycotts today are focused on long-term change of buying habits, and so fit into part of a larger political program, with many techniques that require a longer structural commitment, e.g. reform to [commodity markets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodity_markets), or government commitment to [moral purchasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_purchasing), e.g. the longstanding boycott of [South African](17416221.md) businesses to protest [apartheid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apartheid) already alluded to. These stretch the meaning of a "boycott."

Another form of consumer boycotting is substitution for an equivalent product; for example, [Mecca Cola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecca_Cola) and [Qibla Cola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qibla_Cola) have been marketed as substitutes for [Coca-Cola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coca-Cola) among [Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim) populations.

A prime target of boycotts is [consumerism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumerism) itself, e.g. "[International Buy Nothing Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Buy_Nothing_Day)" celebrated globally on the Friday after [Thanksgiving Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thanksgiving) in the United States.[^cite_note-52]

Another version of the boycott is targeted divestment, or [disinvestment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disinvestment). Targeted divestment involves campaigning for withdrawal of investment; for example, the Sudan Divestment campaign involves putting pressure on companies, often through shareholder activism, to withdraw investment that helps the Sudanese government perpetuate genocide in Darfur. Only if a company refuses to change its behavior in response to shareholder engagement does the targeted divestment model call for divestment from that company. Such targeted divestment implicitly excludes companies involved in agriculture, the production and distribution of consumer goods, or the provision of goods and services intended to relieve human suffering or to promote health, religious and spiritual activities, or education.

When students are dissatisfied with a political or academic issue, a common tactic for [students' unions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Students'_union) is to start a boycott of classes (called a [student strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Student_strike) among faculty and students, since it is meant to resemble [strike action](155134.md) by [organized labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organized_labor)) to put pressure on the governing body of the institution, such as a university, vocational college or a school, since such institutions cannot afford to have a cohort miss an entire year.

## Sports events

Further information: [List of Olympic Games boycotts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Olympic_Games_boycotts)

The [1936 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1936_Summer_Olympics) in Berlin were held after the Nazis rose to power three years prior. Despite advocacy from numerous officials and activists, no country boycotted the games, although the United States was close to it. In the 1970s and 1980s South Africa became the target of a sports boycott.[^cite_note-53]

After the [Soviet Union invaded Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghan%E2%80%93Soviet_War) in 1979, the United States led a 66-nation [boycott of the 1980 Moscow Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980_Summer_Olympics_boycott) much to Soviet chagrin. The USSR then organized an [Eastern Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Bloc) [boycott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1984_Summer_Olympics_boycott) of the [1984 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1984_Summer_Olympics) in Los Angeles, which allowed the Americans to win far more medals than expected.[^cite_note-54]

In at least one case, a boycott has been documented due to on-field results of a game; the residents of [New Orleans](53842.md) boycotted television broadcasts of [Super Bowl LIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Bowl_LIII) after a controversial officiating call led to the hometown [New Orleans Saints](21720.md) losing the [NFC Championship Game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NFC_Championship_Game) and being denied a trip to the Super Bowl. Viewership of the game dropped in the city by half compared to [Super Bowl LII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Bowl_LII), contributing to a noticeable drop in the overall national ratings, but the boycott failed to achieve any meaningful remedy for the Saints or their fans.[^cite_note-55]

## Diplomatic boycott

Nations have from time to time used "diplomatic boycotts" to isolate other governments. Following the May Coup of 1903, Great Britain led the major powers in a [diplomatic boycott against Serbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_Coup_\(Serbia\)#International_outrage_and_boycott), which was a refusal to recognize the post-coup government of Serbia altogether by withdrawing ambassadors and other diplomatic officials from the country;[^cite_note-56] it ended three years later in 1906, when Great Britain renewed diplomatic relations through a decree signed by King [Edward VII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_VII).[^cite_note-Markovic-57]

A diplomatic boycott is when diplomatic participation is withheld from an event such as the Olympics but athletic participation is not limited.[^cite_note-58] In 2021, a number of [Western nations](21208200.md), led by the United States, Britain and Canada, protested the [2022 Beijing Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Winter_Olympics) through a diplomatic boycott, citing China's policies concerning the [persecution of Uyghurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persecution_of_Uyghurs_in_China) and [human rights violations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights_in_China) in the country.[^cite_note-59][^cite_note-60][^cite_note-61]
