**Armenians in Russia** or **Russian Armenians**[^cite_note-6] ([Armenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_language): Հայերը Ռուսաստանում, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Armenian): *Hayery Rrusastanum*; [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language): Армяне в России, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Russian): *Armyane v Rossii*) are one of the country's largest ethnic minorities and the largest [Armenian diaspora](214413.md) community outside [Armenia](10918072.md). The 2010 Russian census recorded 1,182,388 Armenians in the country. Various figures estimate that the ethnic [Armenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_people) population in [Russia](25391.md) is actually more than 2 million. Armenians populate various regions, including [Moscow](19004.md), [Saint Petersburg](24320051.md), [Krasnodar Krai](474125.md) in the [North Caucasus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Caucasus) and as far as [Vladivostok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladivostok) in the East.

## History

### Early period

There has been an [Armenian](387816.md) presence in [Russia](25391.md) since the [Late Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Middle_Ages), when various [merchants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_merchantry) and [artisans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artisans) ventured west to the [Crimea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_Peninsula) and the northern [Caucasus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caucasus) in order to set up trade ties and conduct commerce.

### Russian Empire



Portrait of Russian-Armenian General [Valerian Madatov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valerian_Madatov) by [George Dawe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Dawe) from the [Military Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Gallery), 1820

The relationship between Armenians and Russian imperial authorities was complex, shaped as often by parallel interests as competing objectives.[^cite_note-7] Large Armenian communities resided both in the Caucasus and in Russian cities well before the modern era. After the destruction of the last remaining independent Armenian states in the Middle Ages, the nobility disintegrated, leaving Armenian society composed of a mass of peasants plus a middle class who were either craftsmen or merchants. Such Armenians were to be found in most towns of Transcaucasia; indeed, at the beginning of the 19th century they formed the majority of the population in cities such as [Tbilisi](42298.md). Armenian merchants conducted their trade across the world and many had set up base within Russia. In 1778, [Catherine the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catherine_the_Great) invited Armenian merchants from the [Crimea](163045.md) to Russia and they established a settlement at [Nakhichevan-on-Don](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nakhichevan-on-Don), near [Rostov-on-Don](317562.md).[^cite_note-8] The Russian ruling classes welcomed the Armenians' entrepreneurial skills as a boost to the economy, but they also regarded them with some suspicion. The image of the Armenian as a "wily merchant" was already widespread. Russian nobles derived their income from their estates worked by serfs and, with their aristocratic distaste for engaging in business, they had little understanding or sympathy for the way of life of mercantile Armenians. Due to the [Armenian genocide](140376.md), over 300 thousand Armenians migrated to the [Russian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire), specifically within Georgia and Armenia.

Nevertheless, middle-class Armenians prospered under Russian rule and they were the first to seize the new opportunities and transform themselves into a prosperous [bourgeoisie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourgeoisie) when [capitalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalism) and [industrialisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrialisation) came to Transcaucasia in the later half of the 19th century. The Armenians more easily adapted to the new economic circumstances than their neighbours in Transcaucasia, the Georgians and the [Azeris](746.md). They became the most powerful element in the municipal life of Tbilisi, the heart of the tsarist administration of the Caucasus as well as its economic center. Armenian entrepreneurs were quick to engage the oil boom which began in Transcaucasia in the 1870s, making investments in the oil fields in [Baku](4566.md) in Azerbaijan and the refineries of [Batumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batumi) on the [Black Sea](3386.md) coast. All this meant that the tensions between Armenians, Georgians and Azeris in Russian Transcaucasia were not simply ethnic or religious in nature but also were shaped by social and economic considerations. Nevertheless, despite the stereotype of the typical Armenian as a successful businessman, at the end of the 19th century 80 percent of Russian Armenians were still peasants working the land.[^cite_note-9]

### Soviet Union

Many Armenians were deported around the Soviet Union states, Armenians were in many of the existing Soviet Republics, there still is a sizable population of Armenian's in post-Soviet countries, many whom still speak Russian.

A wave of Armenian's from Middle Eastern countries between 1946-1949 specifically from, [Syria](7515849.md), [Lebanon](17771.md), and [Iraq](7515928.md) were called to migrate to the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) for a better life, due to many of these people were ancestors and victims of the Armenian genocide and displaced them from their homelands in the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md). Many whom immigrated faced discrimination, many of these people have assimilated into the modern day Eastern Armenian population, and do not speak the Western Armenian dialect or now speak have a diglossic situation between Western Armenian dialects in informal usage and an Eastern Armenian standard.[^cite_note-10]

This includes [Iranian Armenians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Armenians), who left [Iran](14653.md) to [Soviet Armenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Soviet_Socialist_Republic), many have again assimilated into the Armenian population.[^cite_note-11]

### Present day



Commemoration of the [Armenian genocide](140376.md) in [Volgograd](32585.md), 2012

According to the Union of Russian Armenians, there are 2.5 million Armenians living in Russia today. According to the same source, about 850,000 are immigrants from Armenia, 350,000 from Azerbaijan and 250,000 from Georgia, including 100,000 from Abkhazia and 180,000 from Central Asia, mostly Tajikistan and Turkmenistan.[^cite_note-12]

The Russian government is encouraging Armenians to immigrate and settle in Russia and is providing financial and settlement incentives.[^cite_note-13]

Armenians in Russia have one of the highest rates of educational attainment. According to the 2002 census 21.4% of Armenians have [higher education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_education), 31.8% have "middle special" education (i.e. [vocational education](56551.md)), and 46.1% have secondary education.[^cite_note-14]

### Moscow



The [Armenian Cathedral of Moscow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Cathedral_of_Moscow), completed in 2011

The 2010 Russian census put the number of Moscow Armenians at 106,466. Another 63,306 Armenians lived in [Moscow region](372315.md) at the time. There are various estimates on the number of Armenians in Moscow: 400,000,[^cite_note-27] 600,000,[^cite_note-28] 1,000,000.[^cite_note-armeniadiaspora-29] Moscow is often regarded as the [largest Armenian community outside Armenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Largest_Armenian_diaspora_communities).



[Saint Catherine's Armenian Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Catherine's_Armenian_Church), Saint Petersburg

### Saint Petersburg

In 1708 the first Armenians came to St. Petersburg, and in 1710 in the city already existed "Armenian offices". In 1730, under the leadership of the priest Ivan Sheristanova organized the first parish of the Armenian Apostolic Church. Throughout the 20th century Armenian population of St. Petersburg has been steadily increasing. The number of Armenians in St. Petersburg increased from 1,759 in 1926 to 19,164[^cite_note-ReferenceA-19] in 2002.

According to [Soviet 1989 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Census_\(1989\)) 47% Armenians speak [Armenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_language) as native language, 52% speak [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language) as native language. At the same time almost all fluent in Russian language. About half of the Armenians have higher education and, consequently, higher social status.[^cite_note-30]

According to the head of Saint Petersburg's Armenian community Karen Mkrtchyan, currently about 100,000 Armenians are living in the region of Saint Petersburg. There are 2 Armenian churches, a Sunday school, "Havatamk" Armenian monthly and a printing house.[^cite_note-31]

### Krasnodar

See also: [Cherkesogai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherkesogai) and [Hamshenis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamshenis)

The Krasnodar Krai is one of the biggest communities of the [Armenian diaspora](214413.md).[^cite_note-32] According to the [Russian 2002 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Census_\(2002\)), there were 274,566 Armenians. 211,397 of them spoke [Armenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_language) as their native language and 6,948 had [Armenian](10918072.md) citizenship.

According to estimates some 500,000,[^cite_note-33][^cite_note-34] 700,000[^cite_note-35] or 1,000,000[^cite_note-ria-36] Armenians resided in Krasnodar.

They are chiefly concentrated in [Greater Sochi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sochi) (80,045[^cite_note-37]–125,000)[^cite_note-38] the city of [Krasnodar](474128.md) (28,022[^cite_note-40]–70,000),[^cite_note-41] the city of [Armavir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armavir,_Russia) (18,262[^cite_note-krasnodar-39]–50,000)[^cite_note-42] [Tuapse](1623160.md) (18,194)[^cite_note-43], [Novorossiysk](1190336.md) (12,092[^cite_note-krasnodar-39]–40,000)[^cite_note-44] [Apsheron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apsheronsky_District) (10,659),[^cite_note-krasnodar-39] and [Anapa](1194038.md) (8,201).[^cite_note-krasnodar-39]

### Rostov-on-Don

Historically, the Don region was home to the largest Armenian community on the territory of the modern Russian Federation. Armenians were resettled from Crimean Khanate in 1779 by orders of [Catherine the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catherine_the_Great) and founded several settlements around the territory of modern [Rostov-on-Don](317562.md). The largest of them, [Nakhichevan-on-Don](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nakhichevan-on-Don), was merged into the Rostov city in 1928. Armenians still constitute the majority (60%) of population in [Myasnikovsky District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myasnikovsky_District). In 2010, Rostov-on-Don had the third largest Armenian population of all Russian cities (after Moscow and [Sochi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sochi), Krasnodar Krai).

## Institutions

The Union of Armenians of Russia is a public organization founded in 2000 that unites Armenians in Russia and Russians of Armenian descent to strengthen the visibility of Armenians and Armenian culture in Russia. There are more than 700 regional and local branches of the union in Russia.[^cite_note-45]
