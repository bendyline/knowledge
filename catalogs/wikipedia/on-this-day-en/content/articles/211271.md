The Bogotá savanna is the high plateau in the Andes where Bogotá is located. The flatlands are clearly visible in the topography and the result of a [Pleistocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleistocene) lake; Lake Humboldt, that existed until around 30,000 years BP

**Bogotá** ([/ˌboʊɡəˈtɑː/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English),[^cite_note-15][^cite_note-16] also [UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_English): [/ˌbɒɡ-/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English), [US](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_English): [/ˈboʊɡətɑː/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English),[^cite_note-17] Spanish pronunciation: [\[boɣoˈta\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Spanish) [//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/8/89/ES-Bogota.ogg/ES-Bogota.ogg.mp3](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/8/89/ES-Bogota.ogg/ES-Bogota.ogg.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup>)[^cite_note-26] is the [capital](181337.md) and largest city of [Colombia](5222.md). The city is administered as the Capital District, as well as the capital of, though not politically part of, the surrounding department of [Cundinamarca](189405.md).[^cite_note-27] Bogotá is a territorial entity of the first order, with the same administrative status as the [departments of Colombia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Departments_of_Colombia). It is the main political, economic, administrative, industrial, cultural, aeronautical, technological, scientific, medical, educational and airport center of the country and northern [South America](26769.md).[^cite_note-28][^cite_note-29][^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31][^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33]

Bogotá was founded as the capital of the [New Kingdom of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Kingdom_of_Granada) on 6 August 1538 by Spanish [conquistador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conquistador) [Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonzalo_Jim%C3%A9nez_de_Quesada) after a harsh [expedition into the Andes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_conquest_of_the_Muisca) conquering the [Muisca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_people), the indigenous inhabitants of the [Altiplano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altiplano_Cundiboyacense). Santafé (its name after 1540) became the seat of the government of the Spanish Royal [Audiencia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_Audiencia) of the [New Kingdom of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Kingdom_of_Granada) (created in 1550), and then after 1717 it was the capital of the [Viceroyalty of New Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viceroyalty_of_New_Granada). After the [Battle of Boyacá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Boyac%C3%A1) on 7 August 1819, Bogotá became the capital of the [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colombian_Declaration_of_Independence) nation of [Gran Colombia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gran_Colombia). It was [Simón Bolívar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sim%C3%B3n_Bol%C3%ADvar) who rebaptized the city with the name of Bogotá, as a way of honoring the Muisca people and as an emancipation act towards the Spanish crown.[^cite_note-\:1-34] Hence, since the Viceroyalty of New Granada's independence from the [Spanish Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Empire) and during the formation of present-day Colombia, Bogotá has remained the capital of this territory.

The city is located in the center of Colombia, on a high plateau known as the [Bogotá savanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogot%C3%A1_savanna), part of the [Altiplano Cundiboyacense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altiplano_Cundiboyacense) located in the [Eastern Cordillera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cordillera_Oriental_\(Colombia\)) of the [Andes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andes). Its altitude averages 2,640 meters (8,660 ft) above sea level, making it the [third highest capital city in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_capital_cities_by_elevation). Subdivided into 20 localities, Bogotá covers an area of 1,587 square kilometers (613 square miles) and enjoys a consistently cool climate throughout the year.

The city is home to central offices of the executive branch ([Office of the President](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_Republic)), the legislative branch ([Congress of Colombia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_Colombia)) and the judicial branch ([Supreme Court of Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Justice_of_Colombia), [Constitutional Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_Court_of_Colombia), [Council of State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_State_\(Colombia\)) and the [Superior Council of Judicature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superior_Council_of_Judicature)) of the Colombian government. Bogotá stands out for its economic strength and associated financial maturity, its attractiveness to global companies and the quality of [human capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_capital). It is the financial and commercial heart of Colombia, with the most business activity of any city in the country.[^cite_note-Hotspots-35][^cite_note-TheTalentIssue-36] The capital hosts the main financial market in Colombia and the [Andean natural region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andean_natural_region), and is the leading destination for new foreign direct investment projects coming into [Latin America](18524.md) and Colombia.[^cite_note-investmentIBM-37] It has the highest nominal [GDP](12594.md) in the country, responsible for almost a quarter of the nation's total (24.7%).

The city's airport, [El Dorado International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Dorado_International_Airport), named after the [mythical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_mythology) *[El Dorado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Dorado)*, handles the largest cargo volume in [Latin America](18524.md), and is third in number of passengers.[^cite_note-38] Bogotá is home to the largest number of universities and research centers in the country,[^cite_note-TheTalentIssue-36] and is an important cultural center, with many theaters, libraries ([Virgilio Barco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgilio_Barco_Library), [Tintal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tintal_library), and [Tunal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunal_library) of [BibloRed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BibloRed), [BLAA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luis_%C3%81ngel_Arango_Library), [National Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Library_of_Colombia), among more than 1000) and museums.[^cite_note-39][^cite_note-Biblioteca_Digital_de_Bogotá-40] Bogotá ranks 52nd on the Global Cities Index 2014,[^cite_note-41] and is considered a [global city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_city) type "Alpha-" by [GaWC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GaWC).[^cite_note-42]

## Toponymy

The name of Bogotá corresponds to the Spanish pronunciation of the [Chibcha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chibcha_language) *[Bacatá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacat%C3%A1)* (or Muyquytá) which was the name of a neighboring settlement located between the modern towns of Funza and Cota. There are different opinions about the meaning of the word Muyquytá, the most accepted being that it means "walling of the farmland" in the Chibcha language.[^cite_note-\:0-43][^cite_note-44] Another popular translation argues that it means "The Lady of the [Andes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andes)".[^cite_note-\:1-34] Others suggest that Bacatá was the name of the Muisca [cacique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cacique) who governed the land before the Spaniards arrived.[^cite_note-\:2-45] [Jiménez de Quesada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonzalo_Jim%C3%A9nez_de_Quesada) gave the settlement the name of "Our Lady of Hope" but the Spanish crown gave it the name of Santafé (Holy Faith) in 1540 when it was appointed as a city.[^cite_note-\:0-43] The [Muisca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_people), the indigenous inhabitants of the region, called the place on which the city was founded "Thybzaca" or "Old Town".

The area of modern Bogotá was first populated by groups of indigenous people who migrated south based on the relation with the other [Chibcha languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chibchan_languages); the Bogotá savanna was the southernmost Chibcha-speaking group that exists from Nicaragua to the Andes in Colombia. The civilization built by the [Muisca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_people), who settled in the valleys and fertile highlands of and surrounding the [Altiplano Cundiboyacense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altiplano_Cundiboyacense) (modern-day [departments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Departments_of_Colombia) of [Cundinamarca](189405.md) and [Boyacá](185665.md) and small parts of [Santander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santander_Department)), was one of the great civilizations in the Americas. The name [Muisca Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_Confederation) has been given to a loose egalitarian society of various chiefs (*caciques*) who lived in small settlements of maximum 100 *[bohíos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_architecture)*. The [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_agriculture) and [salt-based](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_economy#Salt) society of the people was rich in goldworking, [trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_economy#Trade) and [mummification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_mummification). The [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_religion) of the Muisca consisted of various gods, mostly related to natural phenomena as the Sun ([Sué](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Su%C3%A9)) and his wife, the Moon; [Chía](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C3%ADa_\(goddess\)), rain [Chibchacum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chibchacum), rainbow [Cuchavira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuchavira) and with building and feasting ([Nencatacoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nencatacoa)) and wisdom ([Bochica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bochica)). Their complex [luni-solar calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_calendar), deciphered by [Manuel Izquierdo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuel_Arturo_Izquierdo_Pe%C3%B1a) based on work by [Duquesne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Domingo_Duquesne), followed three different sets of years, where the [sidereal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidereal_year) and [synodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synodic_period) months were represented. Their [astronomical knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_astronomy) is represented in one of the few extant landmarks of the [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_architecture) of the Muisca in *[El Infiernito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Infiernito)* outside [Villa de Leyva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villa_de_Leyva) to the north of Bogotá.

The first populations inhabiting the present-day [Metropolitan Area of Bogotá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Area_of_Bogot%C3%A1) were [hunter-gatherers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter-gatherer) in the late [Pleistocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleistocene). Dating to around 12,500 [BP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Before_Present), the oldest evidence of human activity was discovered in *[El Abra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Abra),* north of [Zipaquirá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zipaquir%C3%A1). Other excavations in a rock shelter southwest of the city in [Soacha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soacha) provided ages of \~11,000 BP; [Tequendama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tequendama). Since roughly 0 AD, the local Muisca people domesticated [guinea pigs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinea_pig) as a source of dietary meat.[^cite_note-46] The people inhabiting the [Bogotá savanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogot%C3%A1_savanna) in the late 15th century were the [Muisca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_people), speaking *[Muysccubun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chibcha_language)*, a member of the [Chibcha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chibcha_language#Comparison_to_other_Chibchan_languages) language family.[^cite_note-ElCarnero-47] Muisca means "people" or "person", making "Muisca people", how they are called, a [tautology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tautology_\(grammar\)). At the arrival of the [Spanish conquistadores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conquistador), the Muisca population was estimated to be half a million indigenous people on the Bogotá savanna, and up to two million in the [Muisca Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_Confederation). They occupied the highland and mild climate flanks between the [Sumapaz Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumapaz_Paramo) to the southwest and [Cocuy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Nevada_del_Cocuy)'s snowy peak to the northeast, covering an approximate area of 25,000 km<sup>2</sup> (9,653 sq mi), comprising Bogotá's high plain, a large portion of the modern-day department of [Boyacá department](185665.md) portion and a small area in the [Santander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santander_Department) region.

Trade was the most important activity of the Muisca with other Chibcha-speaking neighbours,[^cite_note-48] such as the [Guane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guane_people), [Lache](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lache_people) and [U'wa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U'wa_people) and with [Cariban-speaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cariban_languages) groups such as the [Muzo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muzo_people) or "Emerald People". Their knowledge of salt production from brines, a task devoted exclusively to [Muisca women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_Muisca_society), gave them the name of "Salt People".[^cite_note-Daza_2728-49] Tropical fruits that did not grow on the cool highlands, as well as [coca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coca), [cotton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton) and [gold](12240.md) were all traded at markets that took place every Muisca week; every four days. At these frequent markets, the Muisca obtained various luxury goods that appear worthless in a modern sense, as well as precious metals and gemstones that seem valuable to us and which became abundant and were used for various purposes.[^cite_note-50] The [Muisca warrior elite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_warfare) were allowed to wear feathered crowns, from parrots and macaws whose habitat was to the east of the Andes; the [Arawakan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arawakan_languages)-speaking [Guayupe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guayupe_people), [Tegua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tegua_people) and [Achagua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achagua_people).

The [Muisca cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_cuisine) consisted of a stable and varied diet of [tubers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tubers), [potatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potato) and fruits. [Maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize) was the main ingredient of the Muisca, cultivated on [elevated and irrigated terraces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_agriculture). Many words exist in Muysccubun for *maize*, *corn* and [the various types and forms of it](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_cuisine).[^cite_note-Daza_2728-49] The product was also the base for [chicha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicha#Colombia); the alcoholic beverage of the people, still sold in central Bogotá today. It was the beverage used to celebrate the construction of houses, harvests and sowing, ritual practices around the various sacred sites of the Altiplano, [music and dances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_music), trade at special fairs with farther away trading indigenous groups of Colombia and to inaugurate the new highest regarded member of the community; *zipas*, *zaques*, *caciques* and the religious ruler *[iraca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraca)* from [Sacred City of the Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Temple_\(Sogamoso\)) [Sugamuxi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sogamoso).[^cite_note-51]

### Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada expedition and Spanish conquest

Main article: [Spanish conquest of the Muisca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_conquest_of_the_Muisca)

From 1533, a belief persisted that the Río Grande de la [Magdalena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magdalena_River) was the trail to the [South Sea](23070.md), to [Peru](170691.md), legendary *[El Dorado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Dorado)*. Such was the target of [Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonzalo_Jim%C3%A9nez_de_Quesada), the Granadanian [conquistador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_conquistadors_in_Colombia) who left [Santa Marta](547214.md) on 6 April 1536 with 800 soldiers, heading towards the interior of current [Colombia](5222.md). The expedition divided into two groups, one under Quesada's command to move on land, and the other commanded by Diego de Urbino would go upriver in four brigantine ships to eventually meet Quesada's troops at the site named Tora de las Barrancas Bermejas. When they arrived, they heard news about Indians inhabiting the south and making large salt cakes used to trade for wild cotton and fish. Jiménez de Quesada decided to abandon the route to [Peru](170691.md) and cross the mountain in search of salt villages. They saw crops, trails, white salt cakes and then huts where they found corn, yucca and beans. From Tora, the expedition went up the Opón River and found indigenous people wearing very finely painted cotton mantles. When they arrived in Muisca territories in the Andean Plateau, on 9 March 1537, of the expedition leaving Santa Marta, only 162 men were left.[^cite_note-BancoList-52]

The *zipa* at the moment of [Spanish conquest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_conquest_of_the_Muisca) was [Tisquesusa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tisquesusa). His main *bohío* was in a small village called *Bacatá* with others in [Funza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funza) and [Cajicá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cajic%C3%A1), giving name to the present day capital of Colombia. *Bacatá* was actually located near to the modern location of the city of [Funza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funza). A prophecy in his life came true; *he would be dying, bathing in his own blood*. Defending Funza with a reduced army of [guecha warriors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_warfare) against the heavily exhausted but heavily armed strangers, his reign fell in the hands of Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada and his younger brother [Hernán Pérez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hern%C3%A1n_P%C3%A9rez_de_Quesada) on 20 April 1537. Upon his death, his brother [Sagipa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sagipa) became the last *zipa*, against the [inheritance tradition of the Muisca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_Muisca_society). Sagipa used to be a main captain for Tisquesusa but quickly submitted to the Spanish rulers. The first [encomenderos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encomienda) asked high prices in valuable products and agricultural production from the indigenous people. On top of that epidemics of European viruses razed through the population, of which in current [Boyacá](185665.md) 65–85 % of the Muisca were killed within 100 years.[^cite_note-53]

Jiménez de Quesada decided to establish a military campament in the area in 1538, in the site today known as the [Chorro de Quevedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chorro_de_Quevedo) square. The foundation was performed by the construction of 12 houses of reed, referring to the [Twelve Apostles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_Apostles), and the construction of a preliminary church, also of reed. With the celebration of the first [mass](5684685.md) in the campament, celebrated by [Dominican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominican_Order) friar [Domingo de las Casas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domingo_de_las_Casas?action=edit\&redlink=1) the city was founded with the name of Nuestra Señora de la Esperanza (Our Lady of Hope) on 6 August 1538.[^cite_note-\:0-43][^cite_note-\:2-45] Quesada placed his right foot on the bare earth and said simply, "I take possession of this land in the name of the most sovereign emperor, Charles V."



[Bolívar Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bol%C3%ADvar_Square) and the [cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primatial_Cathedral_of_Bogot%C3%A1)

This founding, however, was irregular as no town council was formed nor were town officials appointed, as well as lacking some other juridical requirements for an official founding. As a consequence, the official founding only occurred about eight months later, on 27 April 1539, in a site close to one of the recreational lands of the *zipa*, called Theusa or Theusaquillo.[^cite_note-\:0-43] This official founding involved an official ceremony appointing a council and officials, and the demarcation of streets and lands, and in it fellow conquistadores [Sebastián de Belalcázar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sebasti%C3%A1n_de_Belalc%C3%A1zar) and [Nikolaus Federmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolaus_Federmann) were present. While this was the official date of founding, traditionally it is 6 August 1538 that is considered the date of the actual foundation.

The village obtained the title of City by way of a decree from [Charles V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_V,_Holy_Roman_Emperor) on 27 July 1540, which changed the name of the city from Our Lady of Hope to Santa Fe (Holy Faith), after the name of a town nearby [Granada](38264.md) where Jiménez de Quesada grew up.[^cite_note-\:2-45] Jiménez de Quesada and conquerors [De Belalcázar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sebasti%C3%A1n_de_Belalc%C3%A1zar) and [Federmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolaus_Federmann) left for Spain in April 1539, founding [Guataquí](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guataqu%C3%AD) together on 6 April 1539. The rule over the newly created [New Kingdom of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Kingdom_of_Granada) was left to Jiménez de Quesada's brother, [Hernán Pérez de Quesada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hern%C3%A1n_P%C3%A9rez_de_Quesada). The first mayors of the city were captains [Pedro de Arévalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedro_de_Ar%C3%A9valo?action=edit\&redlink=1) and [Jerónimo de Inzar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jer%C3%B3nimo_de_Inzar?action=edit\&redlink=1). The city obtained the Title of Muy Noble y Muy Leal (Very Noble and Loyal) on 17 August 1575 by a decree from [Phillip II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_II_of_Spain). Bogotá, then called Santa Fe, later became the capital of the later [Viceroyalty of New Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viceroyalty_of_New_Granada).[^cite_note-ElCarnero-47]

### Spanish colonization



The [Plateresque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plateresque) Colonial [Colegio Mayor de San Bartolomé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colegio_Mayor_de_San_Bartolom%C3%A9), built between 1604 and 1622



[Santa Clara Church Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Clara_Church_Museum?action=edit\&redlink=1) built between 1629 and 1647. It has a large collection of colonial Colombian paintings.

The city mayor and the chapter formed by two councilmen, assisted by the constable and the police chief, governed the city. For better administration of these domains, in April 1550, the [Audiencia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_Audiencia) of Santafé was organized. Santa Fe (or Santafé) became the seat of the government of the [New Kingdom of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Kingdom_of_Granada). Fourteen years later in 1564, the Spanish Crown designated the first Royal Audiencia chairman, [Andrés Díaz Venero de Leyva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andr%C3%A9s_D%C3%ADaz_Venero_de_Leiva). The Chapter and the Royal Audience were located on the other side of what is today the [Plaza de Bolívar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plaza_de_Bol%C3%ADvar,_Bogot%C3%A1) (then called, Plaza Mayor or Major Square). The street connecting the Major Square and the Square of Herbs— now Santander Park— was named *Calle Real* (Royal Street), now *Carrera Séptima* (or "Seventh Street"; counted from the mountains to the east of the city). After 1717 Santafé became the capital of the [Viceroyalty of New Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viceroyalty_of_New_Granada).

Formed by Europeans, [mestizos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mestizo), indigenous people, and slaves, from the second half of the 16th century, the population began to grow rapidly. The 1789 census recorded 18,161 inhabitants, and by 1819 the city population reached 30,000 inhabitants distributed in 195 blocks. Importance grew when the [diocese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese) was established.

### Nineteenth century



Former colonial Plaza de [San Victorino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Victorino), in Bogotá, painted in 1824 by [François Désiré Roulin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_D%C3%A9sir%C3%A9_Roulin).



Royal Street (in 1869), today known as Seventh Avenue ([Carrera Séptima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrera_S%C3%A9ptima))



[Bogotá La Sabana railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogot%C3%A1_La_Sabana_railway_station)

Political unease over the Spanish monarchy and the rights of citizens born in the Americas had been felt throughout the Spanish colonies in America, and it was expressed in New Granada in many different ways, accelerating the movement to independence. One of the most transcendent was the [Insurrection of the Comuneros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolt_of_the_Comuneros_\(New_Granada\)), a riot by the locals that started in [Villa del Socorro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socorro,_Santander) —current Department of [Santander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santander_Department)—in March 1781.[^cite_note-54] Spanish authorities suppressed the riot, and [José Antonio Galán](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Antonio_Gal%C3%A1n), the leader, was executed. He left an imprint, though. One of the soldiers witnessing his execution was an intellectually curious, noble teenager named [Antonio Nariño](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_Nari%C3%B1o), who was deeply impressed by both the insurrection and the execution. Nariño went on to become a politician in Santafé, and he became acquainted with the liberal ideas in vogue in Europe. He started organizing clandestine meetings with other intellectuals and politicians to discuss and promote the independence of the American colonies from the Spanish crown. In 1794, Nariño clandestinely translated and published in Santa Fe the [Declaration of the Rights of Men and of the Citizen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declaration_of_the_Rights_of_Man_and_of_the_Citizen), and copies of his translation were distributed all over the continent and started creating a stirring in the political mentalities of the time. The Spanish government had banned the distribution of the pamphlet and soon discovered the material and burned any copy that they could find. Nariño was arrested on 29 August 1794, and sentenced to ten years of imprisonment and to have all of his properties confiscated, and was sent to exile the year after. Those suspected of being part of Nariño's intellectual circle were also persecuted, but his ideas had become widespread.[^cite_note-55]

In 1807, following the [French invasion of Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peninsular_War) and the subsequent [abdication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdications_of_Bayonne) of the [House of Bourbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Bourbon) in Spain, pressed by [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon) to give the crown to his brother [Joseph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Bonaparte), resulting in the destruction of the Spanish administration, many in Spain and in the American colonies created local resistance governments called [Juntas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junta_\(Peninsular_War\)). The dissolution of the [Supreme Central Junta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Central_and_Governing_Junta_of_the_Kingdom), following a series of military defeats in the Spanish troops, promoted the [creation of local juntas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junta_\(Spanish_American_Independence\)) all throughout Latin America, which very soon consolidated the independentist ideas already in vogue. After the establishment of a junta in [Cartagena de Indias](63927.md) on 22 May 1810, and in many other cities throughout the Viceroyalty, the **Junta de Santa Fe** was established on 20 July 1810, in what is often called the [Colombian Declaration of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colombian_Declaration_of_Independence). The Junta adopted the name of "Supreme Junta of the New Kingdom of Granada", and first swore allegiance to Viceroy [Antonio José Amar y Borbón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_Jos%C3%A9_Amar_y_Borb%C3%B3n), and appointed him as president, but then he was deposed and arrested five days later. After declaring independence from Spain the different juntas attempted to establish a congress of provinces, but they were unable to do so and military conflicts soon emerged.

The period between 1810 and 1816 was marked by intense conflict between federalist and centralist factions over the nature of the new government of the recently emancipated juntas, a period that has become known as *[la Patria Boba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foolish_Fatherland)*. The Province of Santafé became the [Free and Independent State of Cundinamarca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_and_Independent_State_of_Cundinamarca), which soon became embroiled in a civil war against other local juntas which banded together to form the [United Provinces of New Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Provinces_of_New_Granada) and advocated for a federalist government system. Following a failed military campaign against Quito, General [Simón Bolívar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sim%C3%B3n_Bol%C3%ADvar) of the United Provinces led a campaign that led to the surrender of the Cundinamarca province in December 1814.

In Spain, the war had ended and the [Spanish monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_monarchy) was restored on 11 December 1813. King [Ferdinand VII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_VII) declared the uprisings in the colonies illegal and sent a large army to quell the rebellions and reconquer the lost colonies, for which he appointed General [Pablo Morillo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pablo_Morillo). Morillo led a [successful military campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_reconquest_of_New_Granada) that culminated in the capture of Santafé on 6 May 1816.

In 1819, [Bolívar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sim%C3%B3n_Bol%C3%ADvar) initiated his [campaign to liberate New Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bol%C3%ADvar's_campaign_to_liberate_New_Granada). Following a series of battles, the last of which was the [Battle of Boyacá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Boyac%C3%A1), the republican army led by Bolívar cleared its way to Santafé, where he arrived victorious on 10 August 1819. It was [Simón Bolívar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sim%C3%B3n_Bol%C3%ADvar) who rebaptized the city with the name of Bogotá, to honor the Muisca people and to emphasize the emancipation from Spain. Bogotá then became the capital of the [Gran Colombia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gran_Colombia).

Between 1819 and 1849, there were no fundamental structural changes from the colonial period. By the mid-19th century, a series of fundamental reforms were enacted, some of the most important being slavery abolition and religious, teaching, print and speech industry and trade freedom, among others.  During the decade of the 70s, radicalism accelerated reforms and state and social institutions were substantially modified. However, during the second half of the century, the country faced permanent pronouncements, declarations of rebellions between states, and factions which resulted in civil wars: the last and bloodiest was the [Thousand Days' War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thousand_Days'_War) from 1899 to 1902.&#x20;

In 1823, a few years after the formation of Gran Colombia, the Public Library, now the National Library, was enlarged and modernized with new volumes and better facilities.  The National Museum was founded. Those institutions were of great importance to the new republic's cultural development.  The Central University was the first State school, precursor of the current [National University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_University_of_Colombia), founded in 1867 and domiciled in Bogotá.

### Regeneration

President Rafael Núñez declared the end of Federalism, and in 1886 the country became a centralist republic ruled by the constitution in force – save some amendments – up to 1991. In the middle of political and administration avatars, Bogotá continued as the capital and principal political center of the country.

From a base of only 20,000 people in 1793, the city grew to approximately 117,000 people in 1912. Population growth was rapid after 1870, largely because of emigration from the eastern highlands.[^cite_note-56]

### Twentieth century

Early in the 20th century, Colombia had to face devastating consequences from the [Thousand Days' War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thousand_Days'_War), which lasted from 1899 to 1902, and the loss of [Panama](22997.md).  Between 1904 and 1909, the lawfulness of the liberal party was re-established and President Rafael Reyes endeavored to implement a national government. Peace and state reorganization generated the increase of economic activities. Bogotá started deep architectural and urban transformation with significant industrial and artisan production increases.  In 1910, the Industrial Exposition of the Century took place at Park of Independence. Stands built evidenced industrial, artisan work, beaux arts, electricity and machinery progress achieved. The period from 1910 to 1930 is designated conservative hegemony.  Between 1924 and 1928, hard union struggles began, with oil fields and banana zone workers' strikes, leaving numerous people dead.

Bogotá had practically no industry. Production was basically artisan work grouped in specific places, similar to commercial sectors.  [Plaza de Bolívar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bol%C3%ADvar_Square) and surroundings lodged hat stores, at Calle del Comercio –current Carrera Seventh– and Calle Florián –now Carrera Eight– luxurious stores selling imported products opened their doors; at Pasaje Hernández, tailor's shops provided their services, and between 1870 and 1883, four main banks opened their doors: Bogotá, Colombia, Popular and Mortgage Credit banks.



[Bogotazo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogotazo) in 1948

Following the 1928 [Banana Massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banana_Massacre) of fruit pickers and [conservative party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colombian_Conservative_Party) division, [Enrique Olaya Herrera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrique_Olaya_Herrera) took office in 1930. The [liberal party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colombian_Liberal_Party) reformed during 16 years of the so-called Liberal Republic, agricultural, social, political, labor, educational, economic and administrative sectors. Unionism strengthened and education coverage expanded.&#x20;

The celebration produced a large number of infrastructure works, new construction and work sources. In 1948 liberal leader [Jorge Eliécer Gaitán](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jorge_Eli%C3%A9cer_Gait%C3%A1n) was assassinated, and subsequent riots in an event called *[El Bogotazo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogotazo)* resulted in Bogotá's downtown being virtually destroyed. From then, Bogotá's urban, architectural and population sectors were substantially reorganized. The violence sparked civil conflict, *[La Violencia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Violencia)*, that lasted ten years.

### Twenty-first century



Bogotá is the second largest city within city limits in South America by population, after [São Paulo](390875.md).

The city began the 21st century with important changes in its urban space and public transport, looking to plan demographic and economic growth, that would position it as a strategic hub for international business in Latin America. Some of the main interventions initiated looked to develop projects contained in the Plan of Territorial Ordering (POT), which aims to guide the development of the city for the next two centuries.

One of the most important interventions in the city was in its transportation system. In 1967, there were 2,679 urban buses in Bogotá that transported, on average, 1,630,000 passengers per day. The city had about 1 million inhabitants and 80 km<sup>2</sup> of area, the service was relatively reasonable. But as the city grew and reached more than five million and an area greater than 300 km<sup>2</sup>, not only did the car fleet increase substantially to more than 20,000 vehicles, but traffic complexity increased, as well as pollution and the inefficiency of the only existing transportation system.

By the end of the 20th century, the situation was difficult. There was no real urban public transport system that would serve as an alternative to the private vehicle – which further incentivized its use – and the city had low levels of competitiveness in Latin America, as well as an unsatisfactory quality of life for the vast majority of its inhabitants. The administrations of mayors [Andrés Pastrana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andr%C3%A9s_Pastrana) (1988–90) and Jaime Castro (1992–94), in addition to the first of [Antanas Mockus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antanas_Mockus) (1995–97), formulated proposals to solve the problem of public transport, with limited results. It was during the mayoralty of the latter when there was talk about the possibility of establishing a mass transportation system that would help remedy the problem of mobility.

Under the second administration of Antanas Mockus, Bogotá opened a 'zone of tolerancia' which legalized prostitution in a large swath of the center of the city in the Santa Fe neighborhood.[^cite_note-57]

Mayor [Enrique Peñalosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrique_Pe%C3%B1alosa) (whose first term was 1998–2000) included in his program as a priority a solution to public transport. Consequently, in the execution of the development plan "For the Bogotá we Want" in terms of mobility and in a mass transportation system project, the construction of special infrastructure exclusively for its operation was determined. This system would include specialized bus corridors, equipped with single-use lanes, stations, bridges, bike paths and special pedestrian access platforms, designed to facilitate the user's experience in the system. However, Peñalosa became infamous for his campaign against the poor, saying he would rather see robbers on the streets, than people selling candies. Peñalosa served a second term (2016–19).

After being elected in 2011, Mayor [Gustavo Petro](13391962.md) clashed with the conservative political establishment after remunicipalization of the city's garbage collection system. The inspector general Alejandro Ordoñez deposed Petro for alleged constitutional overreach when he tried to replace the city's private trash collectors. Petro was reinstated weeks later after a Bogotá court ruled that Ordoñez had overstepped his authority.

The *[TransMilenio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TransMilenio)* system of articulated buses opened its first phase in 2000 and has since grown to 12 lines.[^cite_note-58] As the city's population increased, the buses are often crowded, leading to the development of [Bogotá Metro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogot%C3%A1_Metro), construction on which began in 2021.[^cite_note-59]



Financial District

For its part, the cultural equipment plan of Bogotá has yielded significant results, including the construction of three large public libraries in different sectors. These libraries have not only expanded access to existing library resources but also serve as some of the 150 hubs for [BibloRed - Bogotá's Public Libraries Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BibloRed), including the Bogota Digital Library[^cite_note-pub113-60][^cite_note-61][^cite_note-Biblioteca_Digital_de_Bogotá-40] The new libraries were located in sectors that allow a wide coverage, have easy access by public transport and bike paths; and their projects were commissioned to distinguished architects of the city. They are those of [El Tunal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Tunal_library?action=edit\&redlink=1), in the south, projected by the architect [Suely Vargas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suely_Vargas?action=edit\&redlink=1); of [El Tintal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tintal_library), in the west, the work of the architect [Daniel Bermúdez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Berm%C3%BAdez); and the [Virgilio Barco Vargas library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgilio_Barco_Library), located in the Simón Bolívar park in the central area, work of the architect [Rogelio Salmona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogelio_Salmona). Out of the city's 150 libraries, these three, with their outstanding architecture, provide public and open-access spaces for the educational and cultural development of the citizens of Bogotá.[^cite_note-62][^cite_note-63][^cite_note-64]

As for 2019, the city's distribution is composed of nine main business centers (Av. El Dorado Business Corridor, Centro Internacional, Parque de la 93, El Lago, North Point, Calle 100, Santa Barbara Business Center, Zona Industrial Montevideo & Parque Industrial Zona Franca). Grittier sides sit south and southwest, where working-class barrios continue to battle their reputations for drugs and crime. In the ritzier north there are boutique hotels, corporate offices and well-heeled locals piling into chic entertainment districts such as the Zona Rosa and Zona G.

[Protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Javier_Ord%C3%B3%C3%B1ez_protests) against [police brutality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_brutality) started in Bogotá following the death of Javier Ordóñez while in police custody on 9 September 2020.[^cite_note-65] The National Police killed 13 people and injured over 400 in their response to the protests.[^cite_note-66]

## Geography



[Hailstorm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hail) in Bogotá



[Eastern Hills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Hills_\(Bogot%C3%A1\))

Bogotá is located in the southeastern part of the [Bogotá savanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogot%C3%A1_savanna) (*Sabana de Bogotá*) at an average altitude of 2,640 meters (8,660 ft) [above sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Above_mean_sea_level).[^cite_note-Alcaldia-4] The Bogotá savanna is popularly called "[savannah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savannah)" (*sabana*), but constitutes actually a high [plateau](538005.md) in the Andes mountains, part of an extended region known as the [Altiplano Cundiboyacense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altiplano_Cundiboyacense), which literally means "high plateau of Cundinamarca and Boyacá". Bogotá is the largest city in the world at its elevation; there is no urban area that is both higher and more populous than Bogotá.

In the extreme south of Bogotá's District, the world's largest continuous [paramo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C3%A1ramo) ecosystem can be found; [Sumapaz Páramo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumapaz_P%C3%A1ramo) in the locality [Sumapaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumapaz).[^cite_note-67] The [Bogotá River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogot%C3%A1_River) running NE-SW crosses the *sabana*, forming [Tequendama Falls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tequendama_Falls) (*Salto del Tequendama*) to the south. Tributary rivers form valleys with villages, whose economy is based on agriculture, livestock raising and artisanal production.

The *sabana* is bordered to the east by the [Eastern Cordillera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cordillera_Oriental,_Colombia) of the [Andes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andes) mountain range. The [Eastern Hills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Hills_\(Bogot%C3%A1\)), which limit city growth, run from south to north, forming east of the downtown the Guadalupe and [Monserrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monserrate) mountains. The western city limit is [Bogotá River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogot%C3%A1_River). The [Sumapaz Paramo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumapaz_Paramo) ([moorland](553383.md)) borders the south and to the north Bogotá extends over the plateau up to the towns of [Chía](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C3%ADa,_Cundinamarca) and [Sopó](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sop%C3%B3).

Most of the wetlands in the Bogotá region have disappeared. They covered nearly 50,000 hectares in the 1960s, compared to only 727 in 2019, for a disappearance rate of 98%.[^cite_note-68]

### Climate



[Sumapaz Páramo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumapaz_P%C3%A1ramo)



The city of Bogotá on an unusual clear day

Bogotá features a [subtropical highland climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtropical_highland_climate) with uniform precipitation ([Köppen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification): *Cfb*, [Trewartha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trewartha_climate_classification): *Cfll*).[^cite_note-Climate-Data.org-69] The average temperature is 14.5 °C (58 °F),[^cite_note-70] varying from 6 to 19 °C (43 to 66 °F) on sunny days to 10 to 18 °C (50 to 64 °F) on rainy days. Dry and rainy seasons alternate throughout the year. The driest months are December, January, July and August. The warmest month is March, bringing a maximum of 19.7 °C (67.5 °F). The coolest nights occur in January, with an average of 7.6 °C (45.7 °F) in the city; fog is very usual in early morning, 220 days per year,[^cite_note-SUNNY_AND_FOGGY_DAYS-71] whilst clear sky sunny full days are quite unusual.[^cite_note-SUNNY_AND_FOGGY_DAYS-71]

Hailstorms occur sporadically, averaging three times per year—between 1939 and 2008, 231 events were recorded. They occur in the afternoon during the rainy season, when cumulonimbus cloud cells develop rapidly, which are related to the formation of tornadoes. They also cause a noticeable drop in temperature in the affected areas, sometimes drastic, reaching almost 20 degrees in less than an hour.

The official highest temperature recorded within the city limits is 30.0 °C (86 °F), and the lowest temperature recorded is −7.1 °C (19 °F), both at the [Guaymaral Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guaymaral_Airport).[^cite_note-IDEAM3-72]

The rainiest months are April, May, September, October, and November, in which typical days are mostly [overcast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overcast), with low clouds and some winds, bringing maximum temperatures of 18 °C (64 °F) and lows of 7 °C (45 °F).

On 30 October 2012, the first snowfall in more than 150 years was recorded in the administrative area of Bogotá, specifically in [Sumapaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumapaz), followed by further significant snowfalls in 2016, 2017, 2020 and 2022.[^cite_note-73]

On 8 February 2017, Bogotá recorded a temperature of 25.1 °C, the highest in the city in sixty years, according to the [Institute of Hydrology, Meteorology and Environmental Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_Hydrology,_Meteorology_and_Environmental_Studies_\(Colombia\)) (IDEAM).[^cite_note-74] In February 2020, Bogotá recorded a temperature of −6.3 °C, one of the lowest in the city in recent years, according to the IDEAM.[^cite_note-75]

### Urban layout and nomenclature



Street arrangement of Bogotá based on the Cartesian coordinate system: North is to the right. (Typical maps for the city place the north on the left.)



View of Bogotá from the mountain [Monserrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monserrate)

The colonial city, from 1539 to 1810, barely changed its urban layout and culture. Santafe was slowly leaving behind the colonialism after the independence revolution from 1810 to 1819. Entering the 19th century, the city of Bogotá was still the political and demographic core of Nueva Granada but remained a small city as compared with similar cities, such as Lima and Buenos Aires.[^cite_note-\:3-79] At the year of 1801, the city had 173 blocks and 21,394 inhabitants marking a slow population growth during the 18th century.[^cite_note-\:3-79] In the beginning of the 19th century, city life was marked by the lack of cultural activity and public services as well as by the excessive Catholic religiosity in its inhabitants, which almost controlled the whole life of people, as a journalist traveler wrote in 1822 (Biblioteca Luis Angel Arango, 1990,2 ).

The city's urban shape remained the same in the 19th century as the 1557 urban landscape as a checkerboard with the Plaza as its core. In other words, the city grew in area adding new square blocks but kept the same urban fabric.[^cite_note-\:4-80] Buildings were low rise, as a traveler wrote in 1830 (Bibliotheca Luis Angel Arango, 1990,2), where most city buildings had one floor, and a few had no more than two. One special characteristic of those houses with two floors, which were the houses of the wealthiest families of Bogotá, were their balconies. The building facades of Bogotá were very simplistic without ornaments, meaning no more than a wall with windows and the entry door. However, due to the poor street conditions because of the potholes and waste, in addition to the lack of cultural and social activity at night, the ornaments were reserved to indoors where Bogotanos spent most of their time.[^cite_note-\:4-80] One of the few outdoor activities of the people of Bogotá during the first half of the 19th century was going to the Plaza or the "altozano" as the locals called it. The Plaza was the social core of the city, where fresh fruits were sold and where inhabitants from all socioeconomic backgrounds converged (Bibliotheca Luis Angel Arango, 1990,2).

The landscape of Bogotá was very similar from the 16th century to 19th century. Nonetheless, after the milestone fact of the independence from the Spanish, which was a process that lasted at least ten years from 1810 to 1819, some changes started to happen.[^cite_note-81] Those changes were happening slowly while the new republican order was getting power. Trying to make a difference from the colonial ages, the new Republic began changing the name of the plazas, streets and even the name of the city, from Santafe to Bogotá. The names of colonial streets were changed to numbers and the name of plazas were changed to the founding fathers of Colombia.[^cite_note-\:4-80] Thereafter, the empty plazas of the colonial ages turned into ornamented plazas with plenty of trees and civic statues. For instance, the first civil statue placed in a plaza in Colombia was the figure of Bolivar, the main founding father of Colombia. The statue of Bolivar was unveiled on 20 July 1846, which is the Independence Day of Colombia, trying to strengthen the patriotism of the new republic in people of Bogotá and Colombia.[^cite_note-\:4-80]

The last quarter of the 19th century, from 1870 to 1900, more clearly marked a new urban landscape of Bogotá. The constant rural migration to Bogotá had been one of the most important factors that allowed the city to maintain its influential power in the region both during the colonial ages and during the republic.[^cite_note-\:3-79] In 1847, the city governor and the council tried to expand the urban area of Bogotá beyond the colonial limits, whereas, only until the 1860s was that expansion encouraged by the president of Colombia Tomas Cipriano de Mosquera.[^cite_note-\:3-79] The Mosquera plan included lotting the western part of Bogotá, building bridges and wider roads and plazas, but that plan was only partially implemented.[^cite_note-\:3-79] In the following decade, other urban initiatives emerged but this time from the private sector. A group of businessmen, tired of the city's slow growth and development, proposed the construction of sewers, theaters, electric systems and new roads in order to hasten the development of Bogotá. Because of the 1876 civil war, the plan could not be implemented, but from that initiative, the council adopted the first urban code of Bogotá in 1875.[^cite_note-\:3-79] These initiatives tried to update the undeveloped city to the new technologies of the 1800s; however, the pace remained slow, and only after 1882, when the train and the trolley came to Bogotá, some urban development projects progressed more quickly.[^cite_note-\:3-79]

Today Bogotá has 20 localities, or districts, forming an extensive network of neighborhoods. Areas of higher economic status tend to be located in the north, close to the Eastern Hills in the districts of [Chapinero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapinero), [Usaquén](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usaqu%C3%A9n) and the east of [Suba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suba,_Bogot%C3%A1). The lower middle class inhabit the central, western and northwestern parts of the city.. The working-class neighborhoods are located in the south, some of them [squatter areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanty_towns).



Bogotá, Distrito Capital shown in red on a map of [districts of Colombia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Districts_of_Colombia).

Further information: [Restrepo, Bogotá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restrepo,_Bogot%C3%A1)

The urban layout in the center of the city is based on the focal point of a square or plaza, typical of Spanish-founded settlements, but the layout gradually becomes more modern in outlying neighborhoods. The types of roads are classified as *Calles* (streets), which run from west to east horizontally, with street numbers increasing towards the north, and also towards the south (with the suffix "Sur") from Calle 0 down south. *Carreras* (roads) run from north to south vertically, with numbering increasing from east to west. (with the suffix "Este" for roads east of Carrera 0). At the southeast of the city, the addresses are logically *sur-este*. Other types of roads more common in newer parts of the city may be termed *Eje* (Axis), *Diagonal* or *Transversal*. The numbering system for street addresses recently changed, and numbers are assigned according to street rank from main avenues to smaller avenues and local streets. Some of Bogotá's main roads, which also go by a proper name in addition to a number, are:

- Norte-Quito-Sur or NQS (North Quito South Avenue, from 9th Rd at north following railway to 30th Rd, or Quito City Avenue, and Southern Highway)

- [Autopista Norte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autopista_Norte)-Avenida Caracas (Northern Highway, or 45th Rd, joined to Caracas Avenue, or 14th Rd)

- Avenida Circunvalar (or 1st Rd)

- Avenida Suba (60th transversal from 100th St the Suba Hills; 145th St from Suba Hills westward)

- Avenida El Dorado (El Dorado Avenue, or 26th St)

- Avenida de las Américas (Avenue of the Americas, from 34th street at east to 6th street at west)

- Avenida Primero de Mayo (May First Avenue, or 22nd St South)

- Avenida Ciudad de Cali (Cali City Avenue, or 86th Rd)

- Avenida Boyacá (Boyacá Avenue, or 72nd Rd)

- Autopista Sur (Southern Highway)

The largest and most populous city in Colombia, Bogotá had 7,412,566 inhabitants within the city's limits (2018 census),[^cite_note-auto-10] with a population density of approximately 4,310 inhabitants per square kilometer. 25,166 people are located in rural areas of Capital District. 47.8% of the population are male and 52.2% women.[^cite_note-auto-10]



Bogotá future population (medium variant)

In Bogotá, as in the rest of the country, urbanization has accelerated due to industrialization as well as complex political and social reasons such as poverty and violence, which led to migration from rural to urban areas throughout the twentieth and twenty-first centuries. A dramatic example of this is the number of displaced people who have arrived in Bogotá due to the internal armed conflict.

Some estimates show that Bogotá's [floating population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating_population) may be as high as 4 million people, most of them being migrant workers from other departments and displaced people.[^cite_note-85] The majority of the displaced population lives in the [Ciudad Bolívar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ciudad_Bol%C3%ADvar_\(Bogot%C3%A1\)), [Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kennedy_\(Bogot%C3%A1\)), [Usme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usme), and [Bosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosa_\(Bogot%C3%A1\)) sections.

Colombia has experienced steady improvements in life expectancy over the past several years, reflecting broader gains in public health, medical access, and socioeconomic development. National data show that life expectancy rose to 78.21 years in 2025, following 78.04 years in 2024. This continued an upward trend from 77.73 years in 2023 and 76.51 years in 2022, the latter representing a notable rebound from pandemic-related mortality increases in 2021.[^cite_note-86]

### Crime

Bogotá has gone to great lengths to change its formerly notorious crime rate and its image with increasing success after having been considered in the 1990s to be one of the most violent cities in the world.[^cite_note-94] In 1993 there were 4,352 murders at a rate of 81 per 100,000 people;[^cite_note-95] in 2007, Bogotá suffered 1,401 murders at a rate of 20 per 100,000 inhabitants, and had a further reduction to 14 per 100,000 inhabitants in 2017 (the lowest since 1979).[^cite_note-96][^cite_note-97][^cite_note-98] This success was mainly the result of a participatory and integrated security policy, "Comunidad Segura", that was first adopted in 1995 and continues to be enforced.[^cite_note-99] 1.2 percent of street addresses account for 99 percent of homicides.[^cite_note-100]

#### Insecurity

Bogotá has a relatively low level of insecurity compared to other capitals in Latin America. Localities such as Kennedy, Bosa, Ciudad Bolívar, and Los Mártires are the areas where the highest levels of violence are reported. Citizens who feel the most insecure are residents of neighborhoods in the southeast and central areas of the city, particularly those belonging to socioeconomic strata 1 and 2. The population indicates that homicides are related to street robberies, the presence of gangs, and drug trafficking.[^cite_note-101]

As of 2020, Bogotá reported an average of 214 robberies per day, a decrease of 37% compared to the same period in 2019,[^cite_note-102] largely due to the quarantine decreed by the national government as a response to [COVID-19](62750956.md).[^cite_note-103] However, robberies increased again after this measure ended.[^cite_note-104]

A report by the Bogotá Metropolitan Police in 2013 indicated that thefts exceeded forecasts, reaching around 24,000 cases, not including incidents that were not reported due to fear or lack of infrastructure for filing complaints.[^cite_note-105] Street theft decreased slightly (from 51% to 49%), as did theft on public transportation (from 19% to 17%). However, robberies in commercial establishments doubled (from 8% to 16%) between 2013 and 2014. In the automotive sector, an average of four car thefts per day is reported, including vehicles belonging to entities such as the police.[^cite_note-106] The most vulnerable localities are Kennedy and Usaquén; authorities maintain that most cases are due to carelessness on the part of owners.[^cite_note-107]

The armed conflict in Colombia has made its capital the scene of several terrorist attacks. Since the 1980s, Bogotá has been a target of drug traffickers and illegal armed groups.[^cite_note-\:6-108]

On 7 February 2003, the explosion of a car bomb in a parking lot at the [El Nogal Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_El_Nogal_Club_bombing) in the northern part of the city left 36 people dead and more than 200 injured. The attack was claimed by the [FARC](26100.md) guerrilla group. That same year, a car bomb exploded in front of a shopping center, killing six people and injuring 12.[^cite_note-\:6-108]

On 12 August 2010, a car bomb was detonated in front of a complex of buildings that house, among other companies, [Caracol Radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caracol_Radio). The attack left nine people injured.[^cite_note-\:6-108]

On 15 May 2012, a magnetic bomb exploded at the intersection of 74th Street and Caracas Avenue, near the financial district. The attack targeted former [Minister of the Interior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_of_the_Interior_\(Colombia\)) [Fernando Londoño](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fernando_Londo%C3%B1o), who was injured; two people were killed.[^cite_note-\:6-108]

On 19 February 2017, an explosion in the La Macarena neighborhood left one police officer dead and 26 people injured, including 24 members of the Mobile Anti-Riot Squad. The [ELN](822032.md) guerrilla group claimed responsibility for the attack.[^cite_note-\:6-108]

On 17 January 2019, a car bomb exploded at the General Santander Police Cadet School located in the southern part of the city, resulting in the deaths of at least 21 people and 68 injuries.[^cite_note-109]

## Government

Bogotá is the capital of the [Republic of Colombia](5222.md), and houses the [Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_Colombia), [Supreme Court of Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Justice_of_Colombia) and the center of the executive administration as well as the residence of the [President](251302.md) ([Casa de Nariño](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_de_Nari%C3%B1o)).[^cite_note-110] These buildings, along with the Office of the Mayor, the Lievano Palace (Palacio Liévano), are located within a few meters from each other on the [Plaza de Bolívar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plaza_de_Bol%C3%ADvar,_Bogot%C3%A1). The square is located in the city's historical center, [La Candelaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Candelaria,_Bogot%C3%A1), which features architecture in [Spanish Colonial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Colonial_architecture) and [Spanish Baroque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Baroque_architecture) styles.

The [Mayor of Bogotá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_mayors_of_Bogot%C3%A1) and the [City Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogot%C3%A1_City_Council) – both elected by popular vote – are responsible for city administration. In 2023 [Carlos Fernando Galán](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlos_Fernando_Gal%C3%A1n) was elected Mayor; his term runs from 2024 to 2027.

## Economy

Bogotá is the main economic and industrial center of Colombia. The Colombian government fosters the import of capital goods, Bogotá being one of the main destinations of these imports.

### Tourism

The metro contributes to 38% of Colombia's GDP. (150 billion dollars in 2024) Travel and tourism's share of the city's overall GDP stands at 2.5%.[^cite_note-111] Bogotá is responsible for 56% of the tourism that arrives to Colombia and is home to 1,423 [multinational companies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multinational_companies). Bogotá also ranks highly as a global city for doing [business](39206.md) and holding meetings. Bogotá is a growing destination for international meetings.[^cite_note-BestCities-112][^cite_note-113]

In 2016, Bogotá has won 50 major international events, with 12 more world-class events in progress.[^cite_note-BestCities-112] The 16th [World Summit of Nobel Peace Laureates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Summit_of_Nobel_Peace_Laureates) took place from 2 to 5 February 2017 in Bogotá, Colombia.[^cite_note-114] One Young World is the preeminent global forum for young leaders, aged 18–30. Bogotá, Colombia is the host city for Summit 2017.[^cite_note-115]

In La Candelaria, there are many museums, including the [Botero Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botero_Museum) and the [Gold Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_Museum,_Bogot%C3%A1). Bogotá has a great cultural diversity, coming from different regions of the country, which allows tourists to know the multiculturalism of the country without the need to travel to other cities, this includes gastronomy and different festivals.

## Transport

In addition to TransMilenio, the Peñalosa administration and voter-approved referendums helped to establish travel restrictions on cars with certain license plate numbers during peak hours called [Pico y placa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pico_y_placa) (peak, as in hour, and plate, as in license plate); 121 kilometers (75 miles) of [Ciclovía](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ciclov%C3%ADa) on Sundays and major holidays; a massive system (376 km (234 mi) as of 2013) of bicycle paths and segregated lanes called *[ciclorrutas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogot%C3%A1's_Bike_Paths_Network)*; and the removal of thousands of parking spots in an attempt to make roads more pedestrian-friendly and discourage car use. *Ciclorrutas* is one of the most extensive dedicated bike path networks of any city in the world, with a total extension of 376 kilometers (234 miles). It extends from the north of the city, 170th Street, to the south, 27th Street, and from [Monserrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monserrate) on the east to the [Bogotá River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogot%C3%A1_River) on the west. The *ciclorruta* was started by the 1995–1998 [Antanas Mockus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antanas_Mockus) administration with a few kilometers, and considerably extended afterwards with the development of a Bicycle Master Plan and the addition of paths hundreds of kilometers in extent.[^cite_note-125] Since the construction of the *ciclorruta* bicycle use in the city has increased, and a car free week was introduced in 2014.[^cite_note-greenblog-126]

Map of the [TransMilenio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TransMilenio) system

In addition to this, the metropolis currently has over 650 kilometers of urban bicycle paths divided among its 20 boroughs, making it the city with the largest bicycle lane system on the planet. Bogotá's massive urban growth during the 20th and 21st centuries due to immigration and rapid urbanization of neighboring cities has placed a strain on the city's downtown avenues and highways, but since the creation of the Bogota Metropolitan area in 1990 significant efforts to upgrade the city's infrastructure have been undertaken, including the construction of several new avenues and boulevards, as well as the construction of the first two lines of the Bogota Metro. In recent years, the capital district has embarked on several city building projects including the expansion of the Avenida Boyaca and Autopista Norte, as well as the construction of the brand new Avenida Guayacanes, a 13-kilometer 8-lane avenue currently being constructed from scratch in the boroughs of Bosa and Kennedy, as well as the excavations for the city's second metro line.[^cite_note-127] Private car ownership forms a major part of the congestion, in addition to taxis, buses, and commercial vehicles.

### Buses

Buses remain the main means of mass transit. There are two bus systems: the traditional system and the [TransMilenio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TransMilenio) BRT system. The city's administration is currently in the process of replacing and updating the entire fleet for the [TransMilenio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TransMilenio) BRT system, adopting Euro VI-standard CNG-powered buses as well as electric buses. Once completed, Bogotá will have the largest electric bus fleet in the world outside China.

The traditional system runs a variety of bus types, operated by several companies on normal streets and avenues: *Bus* (large buses), *Buseta* (medium size buses) and *Colectivo* (vans or minivans). The bigger buses were divided into two categories: *Ejecutivo*, which was originally to be a deluxe service and was not to carry standing passengers, and *corriente* or normal service. Since May 2008, all buses run as *corriente* services. Bogotá is a [hub](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transportation_hub) for domestic and international bus routes. The Bogotá terminal serves routes to most cities and towns in Colombia[^cite_note-128] and is the largest in the country. There is international service to Ecuador, Peru and Venezuela.

The [TransMilenio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TransMilenio) system was created during [Enrique Peñalosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrique_Pe%C3%B1alosa)'s mayoral term,[^cite_note-129] and is a form of [bus rapid transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bus_rapid_transit) that has been deployed as a measure to compensate for the lack of a subway or rail system. TransMilenio combines articulated buses that operate on dedicated bus roads (busways) and smaller buses (feeders) that operate in residential areas, bringing passengers to the main grid. TransMilenio's main routes are: Caracas Avenue, Northern Highway (*[Autopista Norte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autopista_Norte)*), 80th Street, Americas Avenue, Jiménez Avenue, and 30th Avenue (also referred to as *Norte Quito Sur* or *N.Q.S.* for short). Routes for Suba Avenue and Southern Highway (*Autopista Sur*), the southern leg of the 30th Avenue, were opened in April 2006. The third phase of the system will cover 7th Avenue, 10th Avenue, and 26th Street (or *Avenida El Dorado*). The system is planned to cover the entire city by 2030. Although the Transmilenio carries commuters to numerous corners of the city, it is more expensive (US\$0.80 or 2300 COP) than any public transport, except taxis.

### Air



[El Dorado International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Dorado_International_Airport)

Bogotá's main airport is [El Dorado International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Dorado_International_Airport), with an approximate area of 6.9 km<sup>2</sup> (2.7 sq mi) located west of the city's downtown in the [Fontibón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fontib%C3%B3n) Locality. Globally known as The Hub of the Americas, it is the busiest airport in Latin America and Colombia. Construction of the airport was ordered by [Gustavo Rojas Pinilla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustavo_Rojas_Pinilla) (19th President of Colombia) in 1955 to replace the [Techo Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Techo_Airport). Due to its central location in Colombia and in Latin America, it is a hub for Colombia's Flagship Carrier [Avianca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avianca), [Copa Airlines Colombia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copa_Airlines_Colombia) and [LATAM Colombia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LATAM_Colombia). It is also serviced by a number of international airlines including American, Delta, United, Air France, KLM, Turkish Airlines, Edelweiss, Lufthansa, Emirates, Iberia and Air Canada. The national airport has begun to take more responsibility due to the congestion at the international airport. In response to the high demand of approximately 27 Million passengers per year,[^cite_note-130]

A secondary airport, [CATAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Transport_Air_Command_\(Colombian_Air_Force\)), serves as a base for Military and Police Aviation. This airport, which uses the runways of El Dorado will eventually move to Madrid, a nearby town in the region of Cundinamarca, leaving further space to expand El Dorado.[^cite_note-131]

[Guaymaral Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guaymaral_Airport) is another small airport located in the northern boundaries of Bogotá. It is used mainly for private aviation activities.

### Gondola lift

[TransMiCable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TransMiCable) is a [gondola lift](298681.md) system implemented by the city of Bogotá, with the purpose of providing a complementary transportation service to [TransMilenio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TransMilenio). Line T, with a length of 3,34 km\[2] and four stations, connects the [Portal del Tunal (TransMilenio)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portal_del_Tunal_\(TransMilenio\)) station to Mirador del Paraíso station in the steep hills of [Ciudad Bolívar district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ciudad_Bol%C3%ADvar,_Bogot%C3%A1) and was opened on 27 December 2018. It is part of the city's Integrated Public Transport System, along with TransMilenio and the urban, complementary and special bus services operating on neighbourhoods and main streets. 2 more lines are under development.

### Water

In 2024, the City Council approved a megaproject that would be carried out in three phases: the first phase would be implemented in the first year, with a main port, docks and vessels all along the [Bogotá River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogot%C3%A1_River), at a cost of 250 billion pesos and would contemplate the transport of up to 120,000 passengers per day, with seven possible routes that would cover distances such as from the boroughs of Suba to Bosa (with the [El Dorado International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Dorado_International_Airport) station in between) in a time no longer than 40 minutes. The next two phases, which would also require an investment of nearly 250 billion pesos, would be aimed at strengthening the system and increasing passenger capacity. With this measure, the Bogotá River is expected to become a vital artery for transport and regional connectivity, improving citizens' quality of life and fostering more balanced and sustainable urban development.[^cite_note-132]

### Rail

#### Urban rail

Main articles: [Bogotá Metro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogot%C3%A1_Metro) and [Trams in Bogotá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Bogot%C3%A1)

Bogotá has little railway transit infrastructure, following the collapse of the tram network, although a number of plans hope to change that. The [Bogotá Metro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogot%C3%A1_Metro) has been pushed forward by two successive governments, and construction began in 2020 with opening planned for 2028.[^cite_note-133]

#### Suburban rail

Plans to construct railways in and out of the city, replacing defunct routes, have been delayed due to the pressing need for transport within the city.[^cite_note-134] A [tram train](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tram_train) line using right-of-way from the defunct Bogotá Savannah Railway, known as [RegioTram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RegioTram) is being constructed.

#### History

On 25 December 1884, the first tramway pulled by mules was inaugurated and covered the route from [Plaza de Bolívar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bol%C3%ADvar_Square) to Chapinero,[^cite_note-PorupRaub2009-135] and in 1892, the line connecting Plaza de Bolívar and La Sabana Station started operating. The tramway ran over wooden rails and was easily derailed, so steel rails imported from Britain were eventually installed. In 1894, a tramway car ran the Bogotá–Chapinero line every 20 minutes.  The tram system eventually grew to cover most of the city and its surrounding suburbs. But during the [Bogotazo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogotazo) riots of 1948, the system suffered heavy damage and was forced to close. The economic effects of the [subsequent civil war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Violencia) that followed prevented the damage from being repaired. Parts of the system continued to operate in a reduced state until 1951, when they were replaced by buses. Most of the streetcar tracks were eventually paved over, but exposed tracks can still be seen on many of the older roads of the city, especially downtown and in the [La Candelaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Candelaria,_Bogot%C3%A1) area, although it has been about 70 years since any vehicles have run on them.

### Cycling

Main article: [Bike paths in Bogotá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bike_paths_in_Bogot%C3%A1)

Bogotá has most extensive and comprehensive network of bike paths in Colombia. Bogotá's bike paths network or Ciclorrutas de Bogotá in Spanish, designed and built during the administration of Mayors [Antanas Mockus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antanas_Mockus) and [Enrique Peñalosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrique_Pe%C3%B1alosa), is also one of the most extensive in the world and the most extensive in Latin America.[^cite_note-136] The network is integrated with the [TransMilenio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TransMilenio) bus system which has bicycle parking facilities, therefore rendering it feasible to cross the entire metropolitan area while riding a bike.

Bogotá implemented a healthy lifestyle program called "Ciclovía", through which principal highways are closed from 7\:00 a.m. until 2\:00 pm on Sundays and public holidays. Thanks to the "Ciclovía", the general population rides its bikes enjoying the city as well as exercising. In addition, cars do not circulate as heavily, so there is less pollution. Along the same lines, just during the month of December, the same activity is carried out at night; there are some special amenities and activities, such as fireworks, street theater performances, and street food vendors.

Since 4 April 2016 the [carrera 11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrera_11?action=edit\&redlink=1) has been reduced from four to three car lanes and a new bike lane (*ciclorruta*) has been inaugurated.[^cite_note-137]

### Public transport statistics

The average time spent commuting via public transit in Bogotá on a weekday (for example, to and from work) is 97 minutes total. 32% of riders spend more than 2 hours each day riding on public transportation. The average time spent waiting at a stop or station for public transit to arrive is 20 minutes, while 40% of riders wait for over 20 minutes every day. The average distance traveled via public transit is 8 kilometers, with 16% of trips being over 12 kilometers in a single direction.[^cite_note-138]

### Architecture



[National Capitol of Colombia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Capitol_of_Colombia) in [Bolivar Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolivar_Square).

The urban morphology and typology of colonial buildings in Bogotá have been maintained since the late nineteenth century, long after the independence of Colombia (1810).  This persistence of the colonial setting is still visible, particularly in La Candelaria, the historical center of Bogotá. Also kept up are the colonial houses of two stories, with courtyards, gabled roofs, ceramic tiles and balconies. In some cases, these balconies were enclosed with glass windows during the Republican period, a distinguishing feature of the architecture of the sector (for example, the House of Rafael Pombo).

Palace of San Francisco.

"Republican Architecture" was the style that prevailed between 1830 and 1930. Although there were attempts to consolidate a modern architectural language, the only examples seen are University City and White City at the National University of Colombia (constructed 1936–39). This work was developed by German architect [Leopold Rother](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopold_Rother), although architects of rationalist trends participated in the design of campus buildings. There are also architecture trends such as art deco, expressionism and organic architecture. This last trend was typified by Bogotan architects in the second half of the twentieth century such as [Rogelio Salmona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogelio_Salmona).

### Libraries and archives

In 2007, Bogotá was named [World Book Capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Book_Capital) by [UNESCO](21786641.md).[^cite_note-\:5-146] Bogotá is the first Latin American city to receive this recognition, and the second one in the Americas after [Montreal](7954681.md). It stood out in programs, the library network and the presence of organizations that, in a coordinated manner, are working to promote books and reading in the city. Several specific initiatives for the World Book Capital program have been undertaken with the commitment of groups, both public and private, engaged in the book sector.

The city is home to the [Biblored](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblored), a network which administers 146 small and four large public libraries (Biblioteca Virgilio Barco, Biblioteca El Tintal, Biblioteca El Tunal and Biblioteca Julio Mario Santodomingo).[^cite_note-pub113-60] It also has six branches of the Library Network of the Family Compensation Fund Colsubsidio and libraries and documentation centers attached to institutions like the Museo Nacional de Colombia (specializing in old books, catalogs and art), [Bogotá Museum of Modern Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogot%C3%A1_Museum_of_Modern_Art), the Alliance Française, and the Centro Colombo Americano.

Another set of libraries are the new collaborative initiatives between the state, city and international agencies. Examples are the Cultural Center Gabriel García Marquez, custom designed by the Fondo de Cultura Economica in Mexico, and the Spanish Cultural Center, which will begin construction with public funds and of the Spanish government in downtown Bogotá.&#x20;

The [National Library of Colombia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Library_of_Colombia) (1777), a dependence of the Ministry of Culture and the Biblioteca Luis Angel Arango (1958), a dependence of the Bank of the Republic are the two largest public libraries in the city. The first is the repository of more than two million volumes, with an important collection of ancient books. The latter has almost two million volumes, and with 45,000 m<sup>2</sup> (480,000 [sq ft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_foot)) in size, it hosts 10,000 visitors a day; the Library Alfonso Palacio Rudas is also a dependence of the Bank of the Republic, and is located at the north of the city, with about 50,000 volumes. Other large public libraries are the Library of Congress in Colombia (with 100,000 volumes), of the Instituto Caro y Cuervo (with nearly 200,000 volumes, the largest Latin American library in Philology and Linguistics), the Library of the Academy of History, the Library of the Academy of Language, the Library of the Colombian Institute of Anthropology and History ICANH, and many university libraries.

Bogotá is home to historical records housed in the General National Archive, a collection of about 60 million documents, one of the largest repositories of primary historical sources in Latin America. Bogotá is also home to the Musical Archive of the Cathedral of Bogotá (with thousands of books and choral song-colonial period), the Archdiocesan Archive, the Archive of the Conciliar Seminary of Bogotá, the Archive History National University of Colombia and the Archive of the Mint in Bogotá, under the Bank of the Republic.

### Museums and galleries

See also: [List of museums in Colombia § Capital District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_museums_in_Colombia#Capital_District)



[Colombian National Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colombian_National_Museum)

As of 2009, the city had 58 museums and 62 art galleries.[^cite_note-colombia.travel-140] The [National Museum of Colombia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Colombia) has acquisitions divided into four collections: art, history, archeology and ethnography. The [Gold Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_Museum,_Bogot%C3%A1), with 35,000 pieces of tumbaga gold, along with 30,000 objects in ceramic, stone and textiles, represents the largest collection of pre-Columbian gold in the world.

The [Museo Botero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museo_Botero) has 123 works of Fernando Botero and 87 works by international artists. The [Bogotá Museum of Modern Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogot%C3%A1_Museum_of_Modern_Art) has a collection of graphic arts, industrial design and photography. The [Museum of Colonial Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonial_Museum_\(Bogot%C3%A1\)) is home to an important collection of colonial art from Colombia. Fundación Gilberto Alzate Avendaño hosts activities related to the performing arts and shows temporary exhibits of art in its halls and galleries.

Among the scientific museums are the Archeological Museum – Casa del Marqués de San Jorge, which has about 30 thousand pieces of pre-Columbian art, Instituto de Ciencias Naturales (UN), one of the four largest museums of natural sciences in Latin America, and the Geological Museum, which has a collection specializing in Geology and Paleontology.

Bogotá has historical museums like the [Jorge Eliecer Gaitan Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jorge_Eliecer_Gaitan_Museum), the Museum of Independence (Museo de la Independencia), the [Quinta de Bolívar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinta_de_Bol%C3%ADvar) and the Casa Museo Francisco José de Caldas, as well as the headquarters of Maloka and the [Children's Museum of Bogotá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Children's_Museum_of_Bogot%C3%A1). New museums include the Art Deco and the Museum of Bogotá.

### Theater and arts



[Ibero-American Theater Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibero-American_Theater_Festival)



*[Teatro de Cristóbal Colón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teatro_de_Crist%C3%B3bal_Col%C3%B3n)* (Christopher Columbus Theater)

Besides the [Ibero-American Theater Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibero-American_Theater_Festival), the largest theater festival in the world, the city has forty-five theaters; the principal ones are the [Colon Theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teatro_de_Crist%C3%B3bal_Col%C3%B3n), the newly built [Teatro Mayor Julio Mario Santo Domingo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julio_Mario_Santo_Domingo_Cultural_Center?action=edit\&redlink=1), the National Theater with its two venues, the traditional TPB Hall, the Theater of La Candelaria, the Camarin del Carmen (over 400 years old, formerly a convent), the Colsubsidio, and a symbol of the city, the renovated [Teatro Jorge Eliécer Gaitán](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teatro_Jorge_Eli%C3%A9cer_Gait%C3%A1n?action=edit\&redlink=1), León de Greiff Auditorium (home of the Bogotá Philharmonic Orchestra), and the Open Air Theater "La Media Torta", where musical events are also held.

The Ibero-American Theater Festival, is not the only acclaimed festival. There are many other regional and local theater festivals that are celebrated and maintain the city active year-round. Amongst these is the "Alternative Theater Festival".

Bogotá has its own film festival, the [Bogotá Film Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogot%C3%A1_Film_Festival), and many theaters, showing both contemporary films and art cinema. Bogotá's international art fair, [ArtBo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ArtBo), takes place in October of every year and showcases thousands of works covering arts across all formats, movements, and concepts.[^cite_note-157]

The main cultural center of the city is the La Candelaria, historic center of the city, with a concentration of universities and museums. In 2007 Bogotá was designated the Ibero-American cultural Capital of Iberoamerica.

### Religion

Before the [Spanish conquest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_conquest_of_the_Muisca), the beliefs of the inhabitants of Bogotá formed part of the [Muisca religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_religion). From the colonial period onwards, the city has been predominantly Roman Catholic. Proof of this religious tradition is the number of churches built in the historic city center. The city has been seat of the [Roman Catholic Archdiocese of Bogotá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Bogot%C3%A1) since 22 March 1564. The seat of the Archbishop is the [Primatial Cathedral of Bogotá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primatial_Cathedral_of_Bogot%C3%A1); the archdiocese itself is located in new buildings in the north of the city.

As of 2023, Bogotá had six mosques,[^cite_note-\:22-158] including [Abou Bakr Al-Siddiq Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abou_Bakr_Al-Siddiq_Mosque) on Calle 80 and Carrera 30,[^cite_note-159] Estambul mosque in Chapinero,[^cite_note-160] and Centro de Estudios Al-Qurtubi on Calle 60.[^cite_note-161]

There are a total of four synagogues in Bogotá, the main [Ashkenazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashkenazi) Jewish synagogue is located on 94th street.

An Eastern Orthodox church and the San Pablo Anglican Cathedral, the mother church of the Episcopal Church in Colombia, are both located in Chapinero. The [Bogotá Colombia Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogot%C3%A1_Colombia_Temple) of [the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Church_of_Jesus_Christ_of_Latter-day_Saints) is located in the Niza neighborhood. There are four Buddhist centers located in the north of the city. There is also a wide variety of Protestant churches in different parts of the city, including the Bogotá Baptist Chapel, the non-denominational Union Church, and the St. Matthaus Evangelical Lutheran Church which holds services in German as well as Spanish for the German-Colombian community.

### Cuisine

There is a broad array of restaurants in Bogotá that serve typical and international food. Parque de la 93, Usaquén, Zona T, The [G Zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G_Zone?action=edit\&redlink=1), La Macarena, La Candelaria, The parkway and the International Center are some of the main sectors where a number of international restaurants are found, ranging from Argentinian, Peruvian, Venezuelan, Brazilian, Mexican, American establishments to Arabic, Asian, French, Italian, Russian and British bistros, rotisseries, steakhouses and pubs, just to name a few. Typical dishes of Bogotá include the [ajiaco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ajiaco),[^cite_note-162] a soup prepared with chicken, a variety of potatoes, corn on the cob, and *[guascas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galinsoga_parviflora)* (an herb), usually served with sour cream and capers, and accompanied by avocado and rice.

[Tamales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamale) is a very traditional Bogotá dish. Colombian tamal is a paste made with rice, beef, pork and/or chicken (depending on the region), chickpea, carrot, and spices, wrapped in [plantain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plantain_\(cooking\)) leaves and steam-cooked.

Figs with [arequipe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dulce_de_leche), strawberries with cream, *postre de natas* and *cuajada con melao* are some of the main desserts offered in the city. [Canelazo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canelazo) is a hot drink from the Altiplano prepared with [aguapanela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aguapanela), cinnamon and [aguardiente](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aguardiente). Another hot beverage is the carajillo, made with coffee (tinto as it is known in Colombia) and aguardiente.

### Parks and recreation

There are numerous parks in Bogotá, with facilities for concerts, plays, movies, storytellers, and other activities.

- [Simón Bolívar Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sim%C3%B3n_Bol%C3%ADvar_Park) is a large park regularly used to stage free concerts (such as the annual [Rock al Parque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_al_Parque) festival).

- The public [*Parque Nacional* (National Park)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Park_\(Bogot%C3%A1\)) has green spaces, ponds, games for children, foot and bicycle paths, and venues for entertainment such as public screenings of movies and concerts and events organized by the Council of Bogotá

- Mundo Aventura is an [amusement park](137327.md), with an entry charge and charges for the different attractions. It has rides for adults and children, a [petting zoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petting_zoo), and even a small track for animal races.

- [Salitre Mágico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salitre_M%C3%A1gico) is another amusement park with rides and attractions. The park is near the Simón Bolívar park, where concerts are held throughout the year.

- Parque del Chicó has trees, gardens, artificial creeks and ponds, and a colonial-style house converted into a museum; [Museo del Chicó](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museo_del_Chic%C3%B3?action=edit\&redlink=1)

- To the north of Bogotá, in the municipality of [Tocancipá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tocancip%C3%A1); [Jaime Duque Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaime_Duque_Park) has rides, a giant map of Colombia, various exhibits, a zoo, and a big hand holding the world, symbolizing God. There is a reproduction of the [Taj Mahal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taj_Mahal) in the park with a collection of reproductions of famous paintings. The park is also used for large concerts, mainly electronic music.

- [Maloka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maloka_Museum) is an interactive science museum, in the style of similar venues around the world.

- [Tourist train](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogot%C3%A1_Savannah_Railway) is a sightseeing train, popular with Bogotá residents, which runs to outlying towns [Zipaquirá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zipaquir%C3%A1), [Cajicá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cajic%C3%A1) and [Nemocón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nemoc%C3%B3n) along the lines of the former [Bogotá Savannah Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogot%C3%A1_Savannah_Railway) on weekends. The route to Zipaquirá (known for its [salt cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_Cathedral_of_Zipaquir%C3%A1)) is 53 kilometers (33 miles) long. Another line goes towards the north for 47 km (29 mi) and ends at [Briceño](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brice%C3%B1o,_Boyac%C3%A1).

- The [Usaquén Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usaqu%C3%A9n_Park?action=edit\&redlink=1) is another of the most important parks in the city several of the best restaurants in this city are located there, is recognized to have street performers such as storytellers, magicians, jugglers, etc., and also for being one of the most decorated parks in the city during Christmas time.

## Sports



[Estadio El Campín](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estadio_El_Camp%C3%ADn)

The District Institute for Recreation and Sport promotes recreation, sports and use of the parks in Bogotá.

[Football](10568.md) has been declared a symbol of Bogotá, and is widely played in the city. There are three professional clubs in the city, [Santa Fe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Club_Santa_Fe), [Millonarios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millonarios_F%C3%BAtbol_Club), and [La Equidad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Equidad). The main stadium in the city is The Campín Stadium (Estadio Nemesio Camacho El Campín) home of the local teams [Santa Fe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Club_Santa_Fe) and [Millonarios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millonarios), In 2001 The Campín Stadium was the place for the [2001 Copa América](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001_Copa_Am%C3%A9rica) final between the [Colombia national football](5222.md) and the [Mexico national football](3966054.md), with a final score 1–0 in favor of the home team, which finally obtained its first continental cup. The other soccer venue is the multi-use Techo Metropolitan Stadium which is the home of [La Equidad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Equidad).

Other major sporting venues are the covered Coliseum El Campín, the Simón Bolívar Aquatic Complex, the Sports Palace, the El Salitre Sports Venue which includes the [Luis Carlos Galán Velodrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luis_Carlos_Gal%C3%A1n_Velodrome) (which hosted the [1995 UCI Track Cycling World Championships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1995_UCI_Track_Cycling_World_Championships)), the El Salitre Diamond Ballpark and the [BMX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BMX) track "Mario Andrés Soto".

Bogotá hosted the first Bolivarian Games held in 1938. The city hosted the National Games in 2004, winning the championship. It was a sub-venue Bolivarian Pan American Games. In addition, the city is on the route of the Tour of Colombia.

After being a major venue city for the [2011 FIFA U-20 World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_FIFA_U-20_World_Cup) that was held in [Colombia](5222.md), Bogotá was one of the venue cities hosting the [2016 FIFA Futsal World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_FIFA_Futsal_World_Cup).[^cite_note-163]

## Symbols

### Flag



Flag of Bogotá

The flag originated with the insurgency movement against the colonial authorities which began on 20 July 1810, during which the rebels wore armbands with yellow and red bands, as these colors were those of the [Spanish flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_Spain) used as the flag for the New Kingdom of Granada.[^cite_note-\:7-164]

On 9 October 1952, exactly 142 years after these events, decree 555 of 1952 officially adopted the patriotic armband as the flag of Bogotá.[^cite_note-165] The flag of Cundinamarca follows the same pattern, plus a light blue tile which represents the Virgin Mary's cape.

The flag itself is a yellow band above a red one. The yellow denotes the gold from the earth, as well as the virtues of justice, clemency, benevolence, the so-called "mundane qualities" (defined as nobility, excellence, richness, generosity, splendor, health, steadfastness, joy and prosperity), long life, eternity, power and constancy. The red denotes the virtue of charity, as well as the qualities of bravery, nobility, values, audacity, victory, honor and furor, Colombians call it the blood of their people.&#x20;

### Coat of arms



Coat of arms of Bogotá

The coat of arms of the city was granted by emperor [Charles V (Charles I of Spain)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_V,_Holy_Roman_Emperor) to the [New Kingdom of Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Kingdom_of_Granada), by royal decree given in [Valladolid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valladolid), Spain on 3 December 1548. It contains a black eagle in the center, which symbolizes steadfastness. The eagle is also a symbol of the Habsburgs, which was the ruling family of the Spanish empire at the time. The eagle is crowned with gold and holds a red pomegranate inside a golden background. The border contains olive branches with nine golden pomegranates on a blue background. The two red pomegranates symbolize audacity, and the nine golden ones represent the nine states which constituted the New Kingdom of Granada at the time. In 1932 the coat of arms was officially recognized and adopted as the symbol of Bogotá.[^cite_note-\:7-164]

### Anthem

Main article: [Anthem of Bogotá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthem_of_Bogot%C3%A1)

Bogotá's anthem lyrics were written by [Pedro Medina Avendaño](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedro_Medina_Avenda%C3%B1o); the melody was composed by Roberto Pineda Duque. The song was officially declared the anthem by decree 1000 31 July 1974, by then Mayor of Bogotá, Aníbal Fernandez de Soto.

### Others



*Odontoglossum luteopurpureum*

The walnut tree (*[Juglans neotropica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juglans_neotropica)*) was declared the emblematic tree of the city by District Agreement No. 069 of 2002.[^cite_note-\:7-164]

By initiative of the Bogotá City Council through Project 088 of 2003, and by Agreement No. 109 29 December 2003, the orchid *[Odontoglossum luteopurpureum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oncidium_luteopurpureum)* (Lindl.) was adopted as the emblematic flower of Bogotá, highlighting this species—found in the hills surrounding the Bogotá savanna—for its protection.[^cite_note-166]

The symbolic sport of the city is futsal, according to City Council Agreement 133 of 2004.[^cite_note-167]

The patron saint of the city is [Saint Elizabeth of Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_of_Hungary), by consecration of the [Archdiocese of Bogotá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archdiocese_of_Bogot%C3%A1) since the late 19th century.
