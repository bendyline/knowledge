**Methane** ([US](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_English): [/ˈmɛθeɪn/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [*METH-ayn*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key), [UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_English): [/ˈmiːθeɪn/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [*MEE-thayn*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key)) is a [chemical compound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_compound) that has the [chemical formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_formula) CH<sub>4</sub> (one [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon) atom bonded to four [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) atoms). It is a [group-14 hydride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group-14_hydride), the simplest [alkane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkane), and the main constituent of [natural gas](22131.md). The abundance of methane on [Earth](9228.md) makes it an economically attractive [fuel](21188370.md), although capturing and storing it is difficult because it is a [gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas) at [standard temperature and pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_temperature_and_pressure). In the Earth's atmosphere methane is transparent to visible light but absorbs [infrared radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared_radiation), acting as a [greenhouse gas](21350772.md). Methane is an [organic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_chemistry) [hydrocarbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon), and among the simplest of organic compounds.

Naturally occurring methane is found both below ground and under the [seafloor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seafloor) and is formed by both geological and biological processes. The largest [reservoir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reservoir) of methane is under the seafloor in the form of [methane clathrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane_clathrate). When methane reaches the surface and the [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_Earth), it is known as [atmospheric methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_methane).[^cite_note-Khalil-1999-10]

Methane has also been detected on other planets, including [Mars](14640471.md), which has implications for [astrobiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrobiology) research.[^cite_note-Etiope-2013-11]

## Properties and bonding



[Covalently bonded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covalently_bonded) hydrogen and carbon in a molecule of methane.

Methane is a [tetrahedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrahedral_molecular_geometry) molecule with four equivalent [C–H bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon%E2%80%93hydrogen_bond). Its [electronic structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_configuration) is described by two bonding [molecular orbitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_orbital) (MOs) resulting from the overlap of the valence orbitals on [C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon) and [H](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen). The lowest-energy MO is the result of the overlap of the [2s orbital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_orbital) on carbon with the in-phase combination of the 1s orbitals on the four hydrogen atoms. Above this energy level is a triply degenerate MO that overlaps with various linear combinations of the 1s orbitals on hydrogen atom. The resulting "three-over-one" bonding scheme is consistent with photoelectron spectroscopic measurements.

Methane is an odorless, colourless and transparent gas at [standard temperature and pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_temperature_and_pressure).[^cite_note-12] It does absorb visible light, especially at the red end of the spectrum, due to [overtone bands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overtone_band), but the effect is only noticeable if the light path is very long. This is what gives [Uranus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranus) and [Neptune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neptune) their blue or bluish-green colors, as light passes through their atmospheres containing methane and is then scattered back out.[^cite_note-13]

The familiar smell of natural gas as used in homes is achieved by the addition of an [odorant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odorizer), usually blends containing [*tert*-butylthiol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tert-butylthiol), as a safety measure. Methane has a boiling point of −161.5 [°C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_Celsius) at a pressure of one [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_\(unit\)).[^cite_note-crc-3] As a gas, it is [flammable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flammable) over a range of concentrations (5.4%–17%) in air at [standard pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_pressure).

Solid methane exists in several [modifications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymorphism_\(materials_science\)), of which nine are known.[^cite_note-BiniPratesi-14] Cooling methane at normal pressure results in the formation of methane I. This substance crystallizes in the cubic system ([space group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_group) Fm3m). The positions of the hydrogen atoms are not fixed in methane I, i.e. methane molecules may rotate freely. Therefore, it is a [plastic crystal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_crystal).[^cite_note-15]

## Chemical reactions

The primary chemical reactions of methane are [combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion), [steam reforming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_reforming) to [syngas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syngas), and [halogenation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halogenation). In general, methane reactions are difficult to control.

### Acid–base reactions

Like other [hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon), methane is an extremely [weak acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_acid). Its [p*K*<sub>a</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PKa) in [DMSO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimethyl_sulfoxide) is estimated to be 56.[^cite_note-16] It cannot be [deprotonated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deprotonated) in solution, but the [conjugate base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_base) is known in forms such as [methyllithium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methyllithium).

A variety of [positive ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_ion) derived from methane have been observed, mostly as unstable species in low-pressure gas mixtures. These include [methenium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methenium) or methyl cation CH<sub>3</sub><sup>+</sup>, methane cation CH<sub>4</sub><sup>+</sup>, and [methanium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methanium) or protonated methane CH<sub>5</sub><sup>+</sup>. Some of these have been [detected in outer space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_interstellar_and_circumstellar_molecules). Methanium can also be produced as diluted solutions from methane with [superacids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superacid). [Cations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cation) with higher charge, such as CH<sub>6</sub><sup>2+</sup> and CH<sub>7</sub><sup>3+</sup>, have been studied theoretically and conjectured to be stable.[^cite_note-Rasul-17]

Despite the [strength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bond_strength) of its [C–H bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C%E2%80%93H_bond), there is intense interest in [catalysts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalysts) that facilitate [C–H bond activation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C%E2%80%93H_bond_activation) in methane (and other lower numbered [alkanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkanes)).[^cite_note-18]

Industrial scale oxidation has never achieved commercial success, even with an insufficient supply of [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen). Some homogeneously [catalyzed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalysis) systems and heterogeneous systems have been demonstrated, but no such processes has proven economical. These generally operate by generating protected products which are shielded from overoxidation. Examples include the [Catalytica system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane_functionalization#The_Catalytica_system), copper [zeolites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeolite), and iron zeolites stabilizing the [alpha-oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha-oxygen) active site.[^cite_note-22]

### Methane radical reactions

Given appropriate conditions, methane reacts with [halogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halogen) [radicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_\(chemistry\)) as follows:

•X + CH<sub>4</sub> → HX + •CH<sub>3</sub>

•CH<sub>3</sub> + X<sub>2</sub> → CH<sub>3</sub>X + •X

where X is a [halogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halogen): [fluorine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorine) (F), [chlorine](5667.md) (Cl), [bromine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bromine) (Br), or [iodine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iodine) (I). This mechanism for this process is called [free radical halogenation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_radical_halogenation). It is initiated when [UV light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UV_light) or some other [radical initiator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_initiator) (like [peroxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peroxides)) produces a halogen [atom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom). A two-step [chain reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain_reaction) ensues in which the halogen atom abstracts a hydrogen atom from a methane molecule, resulting in the formation of a [hydrogen halide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_halide) molecule and a [methyl radical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methyl_radical) (•CH<sub>3</sub>). The methyl radical then reacts with a molecule of the halogen to form a molecule of the halomethane, with a new halogen atom as byproduct.[^cite_note-23] Similar reactions can occur on the halogenated product, leading to replacement of additional hydrogen atoms by halogen atoms with [dihalomethane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dihalomethane), [trihalomethane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trihalomethane), and ultimately, [tetrahalomethane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrahalomethane) structures, depending upon reaction conditions and the halogen-to-methane ratio.

This reaction is commonly used with chlorine to produce [dichloromethane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dichloromethane) and [chloroform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroform) via [chloromethane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloromethane). [Carbon tetrachloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_tetrachloride) can be made with excess chlorine.

## Uses

Methane may be transported as a refrigerated liquid (liquefied natural gas, or [LNG](832128.md)). While leaks from a refrigerated liquid container are initially heavier than air due to the increased density of the cold gas, the gas at ambient temperature is lighter than air. [Gas pipelines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipeline_transport) distribute large amounts of natural gas, of which methane is the principal component.

### Fuel

Methane is used as a [fuel](21188370.md) for ovens, homes, water heaters, kilns, automobiles,[^cite_note-24][^cite_note-Cornell-2008-25] rockets, turbines, etc.

As the major constituent of [natural gas](22131.md), methane is important for [electricity generation](9540.md) by burning it as a fuel in a [gas turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_turbine) or [steam generator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boiler_\(power_generation\)). Compared to other [hydrocarbon fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel), methane produces less [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) for each unit of heat released. At about 891 kJ/mol, methane's [heat of combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_of_combustion) is lower than that of any other hydrocarbon, but the ratio of the heat of combustion (891 kJ/mol) to the molecular mass (16.0 g/mol, of which 12.0 g/mol is carbon) shows that methane, being the simplest hydrocarbon, produces more heat per mass unit (55.7 kJ/g) than other complex hydrocarbons. In many areas with a dense enough population, methane is piped into homes and businesses for [heating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heating), cooking, and industrial uses. In this context it is usually known as [natural gas](22131.md), which is considered to have an energy content of 39 [megajoules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megajoule) per cubic meter, or 1,000 [BTU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_thermal_unit) per [standard cubic foot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_cubic_foot). [Liquefied natural gas](832128.md) (LNG) is predominantly methane converted into liquid form for ease of storage or transport.

#### Rocket propellant

See also: [Liquid rocket propellant § Methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_rocket_propellant#Methane)



New-generation, [reusable launch vehicles](336014.md) such as [SpaceX Starship](59118602.md) prefer using liquid methane ([in combination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bipropellant_rocket) with [liquid oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_oxygen), collectively known as [methalox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methalox)) as their primary propellant.

Refined **liquid methane** as well as LNG is [used as](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_rocket_propellants#Bipropellants) a [rocket fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_fuel),[^cite_note-aiaa2004-26] when combined with [liquid oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_oxygen), as in the [TQ-12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TQ-12), [BE-4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BE-4), [Raptor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raptor_\(rocket_engine_family\)), [YF-215](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/YF-215), and [Aeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativity_Space#Rocket_engines) engines.[^cite_note-27] Due to the similarities between methane and LNG such engines are commonly grouped together under the term *methalox*.

As a [liquid rocket propellant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_rocket_propellant), a methane/[liquid oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_oxygen) combination offers the advantage over [kerosene](16992.md)/[liquid oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_oxygen) combination, or kerolox, of producing small exhaust molecules, reducing coking or deposition of [soot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soot) on engine components. Methane is easier to store than hydrogen due to its higher boiling point and density, as well as its lack of [hydrogen embrittlement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_embrittlement).[^cite_note-pbt20140219-28][^cite_note-nsf20140307-29] The lower [molecular weight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_weight) of the exhaust also increases the fraction of the heat energy which is in the form of kinetic energy available for propulsion, increasing the [specific impulse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_impulse) of the rocket. Compared to [liquid hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_hydrogen), the [specific energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_energy) of methane is lower but this disadvantage is offset by methane's greater density and temperature range, allowing for smaller and lighter tankage for a given fuel mass. Liquid methane has a temperature range (91–112 K) nearly compatible with liquid oxygen (54–90 K). The fuel currently sees use in operational launch vehicles such as [Zhuque-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhuque-2), [Vulcan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulcan_Centaur) and [New Glenn](51585988.md) as well as in-development launchers such as [Starship](59118602.md), [Neutron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_Lab_Neutron), [Terran R](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terran_R), [Nova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoke_Space_Nova), and [Long March 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_March_9).[^cite_note-30]

### Chemical feedstock

[Natural gas](22131.md), which is mostly composed of methane, is used to produce hydrogen gas on an industrial scale. [Steam methane reforming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_reforming) (SMR), or simply known as steam reforming, is the standard industrial method of producing commercial bulk hydrogen gas. More than 50 million metric tons are produced annually worldwide (2013), principally from the SMR of natural gas.[^cite_note-31] Much of this hydrogen is used in [petroleum](23195.md) [refineries](339530.md), in the production of chemicals and in food processing. Very large quantities of hydrogen are used in the [industrial synthesis of ammonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonia_production).

At high temperatures (700–1100 °C) and in the presence of a [metal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal)-based [catalyst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalyst) ([nickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel)), steam reacts with methane to yield a mixture of [CO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide) and [H<sub>2</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dihydrogen), known as "water gas" or "[syngas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syngas)":

CH<sub>4</sub> + H<sub>2</sub>O ⇌ CO + 3 H<sub>2</sub>

This reaction is strongly [endothermic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endothermic) (consumes heat, Δ<sub>r</sub>*H* = 206 kJ/mol). Additional hydrogen is obtained by the reaction of [CO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide) with water via the [water-gas shift reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water-gas_shift_reaction):

CO + H<sub>2</sub>O ⇌ CO<sub>2</sub> + H<sub>2</sub>

This reaction is mildly [exothermic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exothermic) (produces heat, Δ<sub>r</sub>*H* = −41 kJ/mol).

Methane is also subjected to free-radical [chlorination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorination_reaction) in the production of chloromethanes, although [methanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methanol) is a more typical precursor.[^cite_note-Ullmann-32]

Hydrogen can also be produced via the direct decomposition of methane, also known as methane [pyrolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrolysis), which, unlike steam reforming, produces no [greenhouse gases](21350772.md) (GHG). The heat needed for the reaction can also be GHG emission free, e.g. from concentrated sunlight, renewable electricity, or burning some of the produced hydrogen. If the methane is from [biogas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biogas) then the process can be a [carbon sink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_sink). Temperatures in excess of 1200 °C are required to break the bonds of methane to produce hydrogen gas and solid carbon.[^cite_note-33] Through the use of a suitable catalyst the reaction temperature can be reduced to between 550 and 900 °C depending on the chosen catalyst. Dozens of catalysts have been tested, including unsupported and supported metal catalysts, carbonaceous and metal-carbon catalysts.[^cite_note-34]

The reaction is moderately endothermic as shown in the reaction equation below.[^cite_note-35]

CH<sub>4</sub>(g) → C(s) + 2 H<sub>2</sub>(g)

(Δ*H*° = 74.8 [kJ/mol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joule_per_mole))

### Refrigerant

As a [refrigerant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refrigerant), methane has the [ASHRAE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASHRAE) designation [R-50](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_refrigerants#:~\:text=R-50).

## Generation



Global methane budget (2017). Shows natural sources and sinks (green), anthropogenic sources (orange), and mixed natural and anthropogenic sources (hatched orange-green for 'biomass and biofuel burning').

Methane can be generated through geological, biological or industrial routes.

### Geological routes

See also: [Biogeochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biogeochemistry)



Abiotic sources of methane have been found in more than 20 countries and in several deep ocean regions so far.

The two main routes for geological methane generation are (i) organic (thermally generated, or thermogenic) and (ii) inorganic ([abiotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abiotic_component)).[^cite_note-Etiope-2013-11] Thermogenic methane occurs due to the breakup of organic matter at elevated temperatures and pressures in deep sedimentary [strata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratum). Most methane in sedimentary basins is thermogenic; therefore, thermogenic methane is the most important source of natural gas. Thermogenic methane components are typically considered to be relic (from an earlier time). Generally, formation of thermogenic methane (at depth) can occur through organic matter breakup, or organic synthesis. Both ways can involve microorganisms ([methanogenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methanogenesis)), but may also occur inorganically. The processes involved can also consume methane, with and without microorganisms.

The more important source of methane at depth (crystalline bedrock) is abiotic. Abiotic means that methane is created from inorganic compounds, without biological activity, either through magmatic processes or via water-rock reactions that occur at low temperatures and pressures, like [serpentinization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serpentinite).[^cite_note-Kietäväinen-2015-36][^cite_note-Cramer-2005-37]

### Biological routes

Main article: [Methanogenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methanogenesis)

Most of Earth's methane is [biogenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biogenic_substance) and is produced by [methanogenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methanogenesis),[^cite_note-Lessner-2009-38][^cite_note-Thiel-2018-39] a form of anaerobic respiration only known to be conducted by some members of the domain [Archaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaea).[^cite_note-Dean-2018-2-40] Methanogens occur in [landfills](211285.md) and [soils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_gas),[^cite_note-Serrano-Silva-2014-41] [ruminants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruminants) (for example, [cattle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cattle)),[^cite_note-Sirohi-2010-42] the guts of termites, and the [anoxic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anoxic_waters) sediments below the seafloor and the bottom of lakes.

This multistep process is used by these microorganisms for energy. The net reaction of methanogenesis is:

CO<sub>2</sub> + 4 H<sub>2</sub> → CH<sub>4</sub> + 2 H<sub>2</sub>O

&#x20;The final step in the process is catalyzed by the enzyme [methyl coenzyme M reductase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coenzyme-B_sulfoethylthiotransferase) (MCR).[^cite_note-43]



Testing Australian sheep for exhaled methane production (2001), [CSIRO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CSIRO)



This image represents a ruminant, specifically a sheep, producing methane in the four stages of hydrolysis, acidogenesis, acetogenesis, and methanogenesis.

#### Wetlands

See also: [Greenhouse gas emissions from wetlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_gas_emissions_from_wetlands)

Wetlands are the largest natural sources of methane to the atmosphere,[^cite_note-44] accounting for approximately 20–30% of atmospheric methane.[^cite_note-phys.org-2015-45] Climate change is increasing the amount of [methane released from wetlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquatic_methane) due to increased temperatures and altered rainfall patterns. This phenomenon is called *wetland methane feedback*.[^cite_note-Boswell-2011-46]

[Rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice) cultivation generates as much as 12% of total global methane emissions due to the long-term flooding of rice fields.[^cite_note-47]



Frozen methane bubbles on a lake

#### Ruminants

Ruminants such as cattle belch out methane, accounting for about 22% of the U.S. annual methane emissions to the atmosphere.[^cite_note-Inventory_of_U.S._Greenhouse_Gas_Emissions_and_Sinks:_1990-20142-48] One study reported that the livestock sector in general (primarily cattle, chickens, and pigs) produces 37% of all human-induced methane.[^cite_note-FAO-2006-49] A 2013 study estimated that livestock accounted for 44% of human-induced methane and about 15% of human-induced greenhouse gas emissions.[^cite_note-Tempio-2013-50] Many efforts are underway to reduce livestock methane production, such as medical treatments and dietary adjustments,[^cite_note-Roach-2002-51][^cite_note-52] and to trap the gas to use its combustion energy.[^cite_note-Silverman-2007-53]

#### Seafloor sediments

Most of the subseafloor is [anoxic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anoxic_waters) because oxygen is removed by [aerobic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerobic_respiration) microorganisms within the first few centimeters of the [sediment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seafloor_sediment). Below the oxygen-replete seafloor, [methanogens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methanogen) produce [methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquatic_methane) that is either used by other organisms or becomes trapped in [gas hydrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clathrate_hydrate).[^cite_note-Dean-2018-2-40] These other organisms that utilize methane for energy are known as [methanotrophs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methanotroph) ('methane-eating'), and are the main reason why little methane generated at depth reaches the sea surface.[^cite_note-Dean-2018-2-40] Consortia of Archaea and Bacteria have been found to oxidize methane via [anaerobic oxidation of methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaerobic_oxidation_of_methane) (AOM); the organisms responsible for this are anaerobic [methanotrophic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methanotroph) Archaea (ANME) and [sulfate-reducing bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfate-reducing_microorganism) (SRB).[^cite_note-Knittel-2019-54]

### Industrial routes



This diagram shows a method for producing methane sustainably. See: [electrolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrolysis), [Sabatier reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabatier_reaction)

Given its cheap abundance in natural gas, there is little incentive to produce methane industrially. Methane can be produced by [hydrogenating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogenation) carbon dioxide through the [Sabatier process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabatier_process). Methane is also a side product of the hydrogenation of carbon monoxide in the [Fischer–Tropsch process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fischer%E2%80%93Tropsch_process), which is practiced on a large scale to produce longer-chain molecules than methane.

An example of large-scale coal-to-methane gasification is the [Great Plains Synfuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Plains_Synfuels) plant, started in 1984 in [Beulah, North Dakota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beulah,_North_Dakota) as a way to develop abundant local resources of low-grade [lignite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lignite), a resource that is otherwise difficult to transport for its weight, [ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_assay#Ash) content, low calorific value and propensity to [spontaneous combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_combustion) during storage and transport. A number of similar plants exist around the world, although mostly these plants are targeted towards the production of long chain alkanes for use as [gasoline](23639.md), [diesel](81761.md), or feedstock to other processes.

[Power to methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_to_gas#Power_to_methane) is a technology that uses [electrical power](9550.md) to produce hydrogen from water by [electrolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrolysis) and uses the [Sabatier reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabatier_reaction) to combine hydrogen with [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) to produce methane.

#### Laboratory synthesis

Methane can be produced by [protonation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protonation) of [methyl lithium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methyl_lithium) or a methyl [Grignard reagent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grignard_reagent) such as [methylmagnesium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methylmagnesium_chloride). It can also be made from anhydrous [sodium acetate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_acetate) and dry [sodium hydroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_hydroxide), mixed and heated above 300 °C (with [sodium carbonate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_carbonate) as byproduct). In practice, a requirement for pure methane can easily be fulfilled by steel gas bottle from standard gas suppliers.

## Occurrence

Methane is the major component of natural gas, about 87% by volume. The major source of methane is extraction from geological deposits known as [natural gas fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas_fields), with [coal seam gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_seam_gas) extraction becoming a major source (see [coal bed methane extraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_bed_methane_extraction), a method for extracting methane from a [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal) deposit, while [enhanced coal bed methane recovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enhanced_coal_bed_methane_recovery) is a method of recovering methane from non-mineable coal seams). It is associated with other [hydrocarbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon) fuels, and sometimes accompanied by [helium](13256.md) and [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen). Methane is produced at shallow levels (low pressure) by [anaerobic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaerobic_organism) [decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decomposition) of [organic matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_matter) and reworked methane from deep under the Earth's surface. In general, the [sediments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sediment) that generate natural gas are buried deeper and at higher temperatures than those that contain [oil](23195.md).

Methane is generally transported in bulk by [pipeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipeline_transport) in its natural gas form, or by LNG carriers in its liquefied form; few countries transport it by truck.

## Atmospheric methane and climate change

Main article: [Atmospheric methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_methane)



Methane (CH<sub>4</sub>) measured by the Advanced Global Atmospheric Gases Experiment ([AGAGE](http://agage.mit.edu/)) in the lower atmosphere ([troposphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troposphere)) at stations around the world. Abundances are given as pollution free monthly mean mole fractions in [parts-per-billion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parts-per_notation).

Methane is an important [greenhouse gas](21350772.md), responsible for around 30% of the rise in global temperatures since the industrial revolution.[^cite_note-55]

Methane has a [global warming potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming_potential) (GWP) of 29.8 ± 11 compared to CO<sub>2</sub> (potential of 1) over a 100-year period, and 82.5 ± 25.8 over a 20-year period.[^cite_note-ar6-56] This means that, for example, a [leak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fugitive_gas_emissions) of one tonne of methane is equivalent to emitting 82.5 tonnes of carbon dioxide. Burning methane and producing carbon dioxide also reduces the greenhouse gas impact compared to simply venting methane to the atmosphere.



Sources of global methane emissions

As methane is gradually converted into carbon dioxide (and water) in the atmosphere, these values include the climate forcing from the carbon dioxide produced from methane over these timescales.

Annual global methane emissions are currently approximately 580 Mt,[^cite_note-57] 40% of which is from natural sources and the remaining 60% originating from human activity, known as anthropogenic emissions. The largest anthropogenic source is [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture), responsible for around one quarter of emissions, closely followed by the [energy sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_industry), which includes emissions from coal, oil, natural gas and biofuels.[^cite_note-58]

[Historic methane concentrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keeling_Curve) in the world's atmosphere have ranged between 300 and 400 nmol/mol during glacial periods commonly known as [ice ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_ages), and between 600 and 700 nmol/mol during the warm [interglacial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interglacial) periods. A 2012 NASA website said the oceans were a potential important source of Arctic methane,[^cite_note-NASA_2012-59] but more recent studies associate increasing methane levels as caused by human activity.[^cite_note-UNEP_2022-60]

Global monitoring of atmospheric methane concentrations began in the 1980s.[^cite_note-UNEP_2022-60] The Earth's atmospheric methane concentration has increased 160% since preindustrial levels in the mid-18th century.[^cite_note-UNEP_2022-60] In 2013, atmospheric methane accounted for 20% of the total [radiative forcing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiative_forcing) from all of the long-lived and globally mixed greenhouse gases.[^cite_note-61] Between 2011 and 2019 the annual average increase of methane in the atmosphere was 1866 ppb.[^cite_note-Technical_summary2-62] From 2015 to 2019 sharp rises in levels of atmospheric methane were recorded.[^cite_note-63][^cite_note-64]

In 2019, the atmospheric methane concentration was higher than at any time in the last 800,000 years. As stated in the [AR6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPCC_Sixth_Assessment_Report) of the [IPCC](15030.md), "Since 1750, increases in CO<sub>2</sub> (47%) and CH<sub>4</sub> (156%) concentrations far exceed, and increases in N<sub>2</sub>O (23%) are similar to, the natural multi-millennial changes between glacial and interglacial periods over at least the past 800,000 years (very high confidence)".[^cite_note-Technical_summary2-62][^cite_note-65][^cite_note-IPCC_2013-66]

In February 2020, it was reported that [fugitive emissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fugitive_emissions) and [gas venting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_venting) from the [fossil fuel industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel_industry) may have been significantly underestimated.[^cite_note-Hmiel_2020-67] [^cite_note-Harvey_2020-68] The largest annual increase occurred in 2021 with the overwhelming percentage caused by human activity.[^cite_note-UNEP_2022-60]

Climate change can increase atmospheric methane levels by increasing methane production in natural ecosystems, forming a [climate change feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_feedback).[^cite_note-Dean-2018-2-40][^cite_note-69] Another explanation for the rise in methane emissions could be a slowdown of the chemical reaction that removes methane from the atmosphere.[^cite_note-70]

Over 100 countries have signed the Global Methane Pledge, launched in 2021, promising to cut their methane emissions by 30% by 2030.[^cite_note-71] This could avoid 0.2 °C of warming globally by 2050, although there have been calls for higher commitments in order to reach this target.[^cite_note-72] The [International Energy Agency](285292.md)'s 2022 report states "the most cost-effective opportunities for methane abatement are in the energy sector, especially in oil and gas operations".[^cite_note-73]

According to the IEA's 2026 Global Methane Tracker, there is "no sign that global energy-related methane emissions declined in 2025", with these remaining "at very high levels". Record fossil fuel production was responsible for 35% of human-caused methane emissions, estimated at 124 Mt, a slight increase from 121 Mt in 2024.[^cite_note-74]

### Clathrates

[Methane clathrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane_clathrate) (also known as methane hydrates) are solid cages of water molecules that trap single molecules of methane. Significant reservoirs of methane clathrates have been found in arctic permafrost and along [continental margins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_margin) beneath the [ocean floor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seabed) within the [gas clathrate stability zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_hydrate_stability_zone), located at high pressures (1 to 100 MPa; lower end requires lower temperature) and low temperatures (< 15 °C; upper end requires higher pressure).[^cite_note-Bohrmann-2006-75] Methane clathrates can form from biogenic methane, thermogenic methane, or a mix of the two. These deposits are both a potential source of methane fuel as well as a potential contributor to global warming.[^cite_note-Miller-2007-76][^cite_note-Dean-2018-77] The global mass of carbon stored in gas clathrates is still uncertain and has been estimated as high as 12,500 [Gt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gigaton) carbon and as low as 500 Gt carbon.[^cite_note-Boswell-2011-46] The estimate has declined over time with a most recent estimate of ≈1800 Gt carbon.[^cite_note-Ruppel-2017-78] A large part of this uncertainty is due to our knowledge gap in sources and sinks of methane and the distribution of methane clathrates at the global scale. For example, a source of methane was discovered relatively recently in an [ultraslow spreading ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid-ocean_ridge) in the Arctic.[^cite_note-phys.org-2015-45] Some climate models suggest that today's methane emission regime from the ocean floor is potentially similar to that during the period of the [Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleocene%E2%80%93Eocene_Thermal_Maximum) ([PETM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PETM)) around 55.5 million years ago, although there are no data indicating that methane from clathrate dissociation currently reaches the atmosphere.[^cite_note-Ruppel-2017-78] [Arctic methane release](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_methane_release) from [permafrost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permafrost) and seafloor methane clathrates is a potential consequence and further cause of [global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming); this is known as the [clathrate gun hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clathrate_gun_hypothesis).[^cite_note-NSF-2010-79][^cite_note-Connor-2011-80][^cite_note-NSIDC-2012-81][^cite_note-UN_Environment-82] Data from 2016 indicate that Arctic permafrost thaws faster than predicted.[^cite_note-83]

## Public safety and the environment



An International Energy Agency graphic showing the potential of various emission reduction policies for addressing global methane emissions.

Methane "degrades air quality and adversely impacts human health, agricultural yields, and ecosystem productivity".[^cite_note-Schindell_2012-84]

The 2015–2016 [methane gas leak in Aliso Canyon, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aliso_Canyon_gas_leak) was considered to be the worst in terms of its environmental effect in American history.[^cite_note-LATimes_2016-85][^cite_note-86][^cite_note-87] It was also described as more damaging to the environment than [Deepwater Horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deepwater_Horizon_oil_spill)'s leak in the Gulf of Mexico.[^cite_note-88]

In May 2023 *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)* published a report blaming [Turkmenistan](198149.md) as the worst in the world for methane *super emitting*. The data collected by Kayrros researchers indicate that two large Turkmen fossil fuel fields [leaked](73651871.md) 2.6 million and 1.8 million [metric tonnes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_tonne) of methane in 2022 alone, pumping the [CO<sub>2</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) equivalent of 366 million tonnes into the atmosphere, surpassing the annual CO<sub>2</sub> emissions of the [United Kingdom](31717.md).[^cite_note-89]

### Interstellar medium

### Mars



[Methane](4548379.md#Methane) (CH<sub>4</sub>) on Mars –  potential sources and sinks

Main article: [Natural methane on Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_methane_on_Mars)

The [*Curiosity* rover](36645032.md) has documented seasonal fluctuations of [atmospheric methane](4548379.md) levels on Mars. These fluctuations peaked at the end of the Martian summer at 0.6 parts per billion.[^cite_note-NASA-20180607-95][^cite_note-NASA-20180607vid-96][^cite_note-SPC-20180607-97][^cite_note-NYT-20180607-98][^cite_note-SCI-20180607-99][^cite_note-SCI-20180608a-100][^cite_note-SCI-20180608b-101][^cite_note-SCI-20180608c-102] Methane could be produced by a non-biological process called [serpentinization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serpentinite)[^cite_note-serpentinization-103] involving water, carbon dioxide, and the mineral [olivine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olivine), which is known to be common on Mars.[^cite_note-olivine-104] There has been some speculation that [methanogenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methanogen) microbes, such as bacteria in the sub-surface, could also be responsible for methane on Mars; however there is no other evidence that such lifeforms exist.[^cite_note-105]

Methane has been proposed as a possible [rocket propellant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_propellant) on future [Mars missions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_mission_to_Mars) due in part to the possibility of synthesizing it on the planet by [in situ resource utilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_situ_resource_utilization#Mars).[^cite_note-106] An adaptation of the [Sabatier methanation reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabatier_reaction) may be used with a mixed catalyst bed and a [reverse water-gas shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water-gas_shift_reaction#Reverse_water-gas_shift) in a single reactor to produce methane and [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen) from the raw materials available on Mars, utilizing water from the [Martian subsoil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martian_soil) and [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) in the [Martian atmosphere](4548379.md).[^cite_note-zubrin20121215-90]

### Titan



Titan lakes (September 11, 2017)

Main article: [Atmosphere of Titan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_Titan)

Methane has been detected in vast abundance on [Titan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titan_\(moon\)), the largest moon of [Saturn](44474.md). It comprises a significant portion of [its atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_Titan) and also exists in a liquid form on its surface, where it comprises the majority of the liquid in Titan's [vast lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakes_of_Titan) of hydrocarbons, the [second largest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ligeia_Mare) of which is believed to be almost pure methane in composition.[^cite_note-MethaneJPL2016-107]

The presence of stable lakes of liquid methane on Titan, as well as the surface of Titan being highly chemically active and rich in organic compounds, has led scientists to consider the [possibility of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_on_Titan#Hydrocarbons_as_solvents) existing within Titan's lakes, using methane as a solvent in the place of water for Earth-based life[^cite_note-methanesolvent-108] and using hydrogen in the atmosphere to derive energy with [acetylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetylene).[^cite_note-mckay-109]

## History



[Alessandro Volta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alessandro_Volta)

The discovery of methane is credited to [Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_people) physicist [Alessandro Volta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alessandro_Volta), who characterized numerous properties including its [flammability limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flammability_limit) and origin from decaying organic matter.[^cite_note-Volta-110]

Volta was initially motivated by reports of "inflammable air" present in marshes by his friend Father Carlo Giuseppe Campi. While on a fishing trip to [Lake Maggiore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Maggiore) straddling [Italy](14532.md) and [Switzerland](26748.md) in November 1776, he noticed the presence of bubbles in the nearby marshes and decided to investigate. Volta collected the [gas rising from the marsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsh_gas) and showed that the gas would catch fire if exposed to a flame or spark.[^cite_note-Volta-110][^cite_note-111]

Volta notes similar observations of "inflammable air" were present previously in scientific literature, including a letter written by [Benjamin Franklin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Franklin).[^cite_note-112]

Following the [Felling mine disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felling_mine_disasters#1812_disaster) of 1812 in which 92 men perished, Sir [Humphry Davy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humphry_Davy) established that the feared [firedamp](505455.md) was in fact largely methane.[^cite_note-113]

The name "methane" was coined in 1866 by the German chemist [August Wilhelm von Hofmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Wilhelm_von_Hofmann).[^cite_note-114][^cite_note-115] The name was derived from [methanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methanol#History).

## Etymology

Etymologically, the word *methane* is coined from the chemical suffix "*[-ane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/-ane)*", which denotes substances belonging to the alkane family; and the word *methyl*, which is derived from the German *Methyl* (1840) or directly from the French *méthyle*, which is a back-formation from the French *méthylène* (corresponding to English "methylene"), the root of which was coined by [Jean-Baptiste Dumas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Dumas) and [Eugène-Melchior Péligot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eug%C3%A8ne-Melchior_P%C3%A9ligot) in 1834 from the Greek μέθυ *méthy* (wine) (related to English "mead") and ὕλη *hýlē* (meaning "wood"). The radical is named after this because it was first detected in [methanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methanol), an alcohol first isolated by distillation of wood. The chemical suffix *-ane* is from the coordinating chemical suffix *-ine* which is from Latin feminine suffix *-ina* which is applied to represent abstracts. The coordination of "-ane", "[-ene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/-ene)", "[-one](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/-one)", etc. was proposed in 1866 by German chemist [August Wilhelm von Hofmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Wilhelm_von_Hofmann).[^cite_note-116]

### Abbreviations

The abbreviation CH<sub>4</sub>-C can mean the mass of carbon contained in a mass of methane, and the mass of methane is always 1.33 times the mass of CH<sub>4</sub>-C.[^cite_note-methane-c-117][^cite_note-epa-ag-118] CH<sub>4</sub>-C can also mean the methane-carbon ratio, which is 1.33 by mass.[^cite_note-mcratio-119] Methane at scales of the atmosphere is commonly measured in teragrams (Tg CH<sub>4</sub>) or millions of metric tons (MMT CH<sub>4</sub>), which mean the same thing.[^cite_note-epa90-20-120] Other standard units are also used, such as nanomole (nmol, one billionth of a mole), [mole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_\(unit\)) (mol), [kilogram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilogram), and [gram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gram).

## Safety

Methane is an [asphyxiant gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asphyxiant_gas), meaning that it is non-toxic and the primary health hazard is displacement of oxygen in high enough concentrations, potentially causing death by [asphyxiation](98534.md). No systemic toxicity has been detected at 5% concentration in air.[^cite_note-PubChem-121]

Methane is an extremely flammable gas at normal ambient temperature.[^cite_note-PubChem-121] It may form [explosive](10192.md) mixtures with air. Methane gas explosions are responsible for many deadly mining disasters.[^cite_note-122] A methane gas explosion was the cause of the [Upper Big Branch coal mine disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Big_Branch_Mine_disaster) in [West Virginia](32905.md) on April 5, 2010, killing 29.[^cite_note-123] [Natural gas accidental release](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas_accidental_release) has also been a major focus in the field of [safety engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safety_engineering), due to past accidental releases that concluded in the formation of [jet fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_fire) disasters.[^cite_note-124][^cite_note-125]
