The **politics of the Republic of Peru** takes place in a framework of a [unitary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_state) [semi-presidential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-presidential_republic) [representative democratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representative_democracy) [republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic),[^cite_note-Draft-1][^cite_note-Dual-2] whereby the [President of Peru](239109.md) is both [head of state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_of_state) and [head of government](37570.md), and of a [pluriform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diversity_\(politics\)) [multi-party system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-party_system). [Executive power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_power) is exercised by the President and the Government. [Legislative power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislative_power) is vested in both the [Government](12229.md) and the bicameral [Congress](1571007.md). The [Judiciary](8900001.md) is independent of the executive and the legislature. Due to its ability to [remove presidents without cause](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacancy_due_to_moral_incapacity_\(Peru\)) and its protection from judicial oversight, the Congress holds most political control of institutional functions.[^cite_note-PROFE92-3][^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5][^cite_note-\:7-6] The [Economist Intelligence Unit](15303116.md) rated Peru a "[hybrid regime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy_Index#Definitions)" in 2024.[^cite_note-democracy-index-7] By 2026, following years of political conflict, Peru has been described as one of the most politically unstable nations in [Latin America](18524.md).[^cite_note-\:8-8]

Peru has a historically unstable political history with dictatorships, coups, military meddling in politics, and [internal conflict.](9624647.md)[^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11] Peru transitioned to democracy in 1980 before backsliding with Alberto Fujimori's regime (1990–2000) before transitioning to democracy again in 2000.[^cite_note-\:9-12][^cite_note-13] While democratic institutions have improved since 2000, Peruvian politics is characterized by political instability and dissatisfaction with the political system.[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15][^cite_note-\:9-12] Peru has been characterized by rapid leadership turnover and corruption scandals.[^cite_note-\:8-8]

Traditionally weak [political parties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_party) saw their support collapse further in Peru since 2000, paving the way for the rise of personalist leaderships.[^cite_note-\:5-16][^cite_note-emol11nov-17] The political parties in the congress of Peru are, according to political scientist [Lucía Dammert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luc%C3%ADa_Dammert), "agglomerations of individual and group interests more than solid and representative parties".[^cite_note-emol11nov-17]

The historian [Antonio Zapata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gast%C3%B3n_Antonio_Zapata_Velasco) describes Peru as a "[right-wing](56522.md) country"; the only [left-wing](18499.md) government in contemporary history until the election of [Pedro Castillo](49637220.md) in 2021 was that of [Juan Velasco Alvarado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Velasco_Alvarado) (1968-1975), author of an agrarian reform and the nationalization of strategic sectors.[^cite_note-\:6-18] Peru is also one of the most [socially conservative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socially_conservative) nations in [Latin America](18524.md).[^cite_note-19]

Currently, almost all major media and political parties in the country are in favour of economic liberalism.[^cite_note-\:6-18] Those opposed to the [neoliberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoliberal) *[status quo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Status_quo)* or involved in left-wing politics are often targeted with [fear mongering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fear_mongering) attacks called *[terruqueos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terruqueo)*, where individuals or groups are associated with [terrorists](30636.md) involved with the [internal conflict in Peru](9624647.md).[^cite_note-\:0-20][^cite_note-\:1-21]

## History

### 19th century

The weakness of [political parties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_parties) in Peruvian politics has been recognized throughout the nation's history, with competing leaders fighting for power following the collapse of the [Spanish Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Empire)'s [Viceroyalty of Peru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viceroyalty_of_Peru).[^cite_note-\:2-22][^cite_note-\:3-23][^cite_note-\:5-16] The [Peruvian War of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peruvian_War_of_Independence) saw [aristocrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristocrats) with land and wealthy [merchants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merchants) cooperate to fight the Spanish Empire, though the aristocrats would later obtain greater power and lead an [oligarchy](22315.md) headed by *[caudillos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caudillos)* that defended the existing [feudalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feudalist) [*haciendas*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hacienda).[^cite_note-\:5-16] During the time of the [Chincha Islands War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chincha_Islands_War), [guano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guano) extraction in Peru led to the rise of an even wealthier aristocracy that established a [plutocracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutocracy).[^cite_note-\:5-16] [Anarchist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anarchist) politician [Manuel González Prada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuel_Gonz%C3%A1lez_Prada) accurately detailed that parties in Peru shortly after the [War of the Pacific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_Pacific) were controlled by a wealthy oligarchy that used candidate-based political parties to control economic interests[^cite_note-24]; a practice that continues to the present day.[^cite_note-\:5-16] This oligarchy was supported by the [Catholic Church](606848.md), which would ignore inequalities in Peru and instead assist governments with appeasing the impoverished majority.[^cite_note-\:5-16] At this time, the [armed forces of Peru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armed_Forces_of_Peru) were seen by the public as ensuring territorial sovereignty and order, granting military leaders the ability to blame political parties and justify [coup d'états](25914154.md) against established leaders of the nation who were facing socioeconomic difficulties.[^cite_note-\:3-23] This led to a pattern throughout Peru's political history of an elected leader drafting and proposing a policy while the military would later overthrow the said leader, adopting and implementing the elected official's proposals.[^cite_note-\:3-23] Combatting ideologies of *[indigenismo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenismo)* of the majority and the elite holding [Europhile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europhile) values would also arise at the end of the nineteenth century and into the twentieth century.[^cite_note-\:5-16]

### 20th century

Following industrialization and [World War I](4764461.md), economic expansion in Peru resulted with rural groups demanding more interaction with the wealthy urban areas and embracing *indigenismo*.[^cite_note-\:5-16] Labor and student movements – especially the [anarcho-syndicalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anarcho-syndicalist) [Peruvian Regional Workers' Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peruvian_Regional_Workers'_Federation) – would arise at this time while nearly overtaking the existing oligarchical structure, though the coup and subsequent dictatorship of [Augusto B. Leguía](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augusto_B._Legu%C3%ADa) for the next decade would quash hopes for further progress.[^cite_note-\:5-16] During the Leguía dictatorship emerged two political thinkers inspired by González Prada; [José Carlos Mariátegui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Carlos_Mari%C3%A1tegui) and [Víctor Raúl Haya de la Torre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%ADctor_Ra%C3%BAl_Haya_de_la_Torre).[^cite_note-\:5-16] In 1924 from [Mexico](3966054.md), university reform leaders in Peru who had been forced into exile by the government founded the [American People's Revolutionary Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_People's_Revolutionary_Alliance), which had a major influence on the country's political life. APRA is thus largely a political expression of the university reform and workers' struggles of the years 1918–1920. The movement draws its influences from the [Mexican Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Revolution) and its [1917 Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1917_Constitution) – particularly on issues of [agrarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agrarianism) and [indigenism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenism) – and to a lesser extent from the [Russian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution). Its leader, [Haya de la Torre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haya_de_la_Torre), declares that APRA as a "Marxist interpretation of the American reality", it nevertheless moves away from it on the question of class struggle and on the importance given to the struggle for the political unity of Latin America.[^cite_note-25]

With the emergence of [communism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communism) internationally, dictators and elites in Peru utilized [political polarization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_polarization), aligning themselves with both the [far-left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Far-left) and [far-right](221220.md), in order to undermine democratic movements of moderate political bodies.[^cite_note-\:02-26] In 1928, the Peruvian Socialist Party was founded, notably under the leadership of José Carlos Mariátegui, himself a spectator of the European socialist movements who maintained relationships with the [Communist Party of Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_Party_of_Italy), including the leadership of [Palmiro Togliatti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palmiro_Togliatti) and [Antonio Gramsci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_Gramsci). Shortly afterwards in 1929, the party created the General Confederation of Workers. Following the assassination of President [Luis Miguel Sánchez Cerro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luis_Miguel_S%C3%A1nchez_Cerro) in 1933 by an Aprista, APRA was persecuted in Peru. Persecution of APRA persisted until about 1956 when it became allied with the elite in Peru.[^cite_note-\:5-16]

Following [World War II](32927.md), the military's ideology began to distance itself from the wealthy elite when the Center of High Military Studies began to promote studies of Manuel González Prada and José Carlos Mariátegui, creating officers that viewed the elite as sacrificing national sovereignty in order to acquire foreign capital and resulted with an undeveloped, reliant nation.[^cite_note-\:5-16] Thus in 1963, [Fernando Belaúnde Terry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fernando_Bela%C3%BAnde_Terry) was elected president and proposed the first pro-worker and peasant policies for Peru.[^cite_note-\:3-23] Belaúnde's first administration was tolerant of the political left, and a variety of Marxist parties expanded during his time in office.[^cite_note-\:23233-27]<sup>: 132–133 </sup>

Belaúnde was overthrown by General [Juan Velasco Alvarado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Velasco_Alvarado) in 1968, who implemented Belaúnde's policies in his own unique manner.[^cite_note-\:3-23] The [Shining Path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shining_Path) guerilla group emerged in 1968 led by [Abimael Guzmán](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abimael_Guzm%C3%A1n), beginning the [internal conflict in Peru](9624647.md) between the state and Shining Path forces. The 1979 [Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Peru) established universal suffrage in Peru and resulted in the return of democracy at the national level.[^cite_note-\:23233-27]<sup>: 133 </sup>

During the [Lost Decade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_Decade_\(Peru\)) of the 1980s and internal conflict, political parties became weaker once again.[^cite_note-\:2-22][^cite_note-\:3-23] Fernando Belaúnde was once again elected in 1980 and strongly confronted Shining Path, with him and his military commanders engaging in human rights violations against populations caught in between the fighting.[^cite_note-28] Angered by Belaúnde's successor, [Alan García](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Garc%C3%ADa), and his inability to combat the crises in the nation, the armed forces began planning a coup to establish a [neoliberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoliberal) government in the late 1980s with [Plan Verde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plan_Verde).[^cite_note-\:4-29][^cite_note-Alfredo-30] Peruvians shifted their support for [authoritarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authoritarian) leader [Alberto Fujimori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberto_Fujimori), who was supported by the military following his win in the [1990 Peruvian general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1990_Peruvian_general_election).[^cite_note-\:2-22][^cite_note-\:3-23]

Fujimori essentially adopted the policies outlined in the military's Plan Verde and turned Peru into a neoliberal nation.[^cite_note-Alfredo-30][^cite_note-\:10-31] Fujimori's civil-military government established sentiments in Peru that politics were slower than brute military force while governing.[^cite_note-\:3-23] The 1979 Constitution was changed after the [Fujimori's self-coup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1992_Peruvian_constitutional_crisis) where the president dissolved the Congress and established the new 1993 Constitution. One of the changes to the 1979 Constitution was the possibility of the president's immediate re-election (Article 112) which made possible the re-election of Fujimori in the following years.&#x20;

### 21st century

After Fujimori's resignation, the transitional government of [Valentín Paniagua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valent%C3%ADn_Paniagua) changed Article 112 and called for [new elections in 2001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001_Peruvian_general_election) where [Alejandro Toledo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alejandro_Toledo) was elected. Peru still lacked strong political parties following Fujimori's exit, leaving the nation vulnerable to populist outsider politicians lacking experience.[^cite_note-\:2-22] Regional parties then grew to become more popular as foreign investment increased during the 21st century, though their service to the elites sowed public distrust.[^cite_note-\:3-23] On 28 July 2021, left-wing candidate [Pedro Castillo](49637220.md) was sworn in as the new [President of Peru](239109.md) after a narrow win in a tightly contested run-off [election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Peruvian_general_election).[^cite_note-32] On 7 December 2022, the congress removed President Castillo from office. He was replaced by Vice President [Dina Boluarte](67870874.md), the country's first female president.[^cite_note-33] In 2024, reforms were passed that moved the Congress from a unicameral body to a bicameral one. On 10 October 2025, Peru’s congress removed President Dina Boluarte from office and [Jose Jeri](80709159.md) was sworn in as Peru’s interim president.[^cite_note-34] On 17 February 2026, Peru's Congress ousted interim President José Jerí after four months in office, making him the third consecutive president to be ousted.[^cite_note-35] On 18 February 2026, Peru's Congress elected [José María Balcázar](82451696.md) as interim President, making him the eighth president to lead Peru since 2016.[^cite_note-36]

In the first round of the [2026 general election](69679018.md), Keiko Fujimori and [Roberto Sánchez](72806167.md) advanced to the second round after ballot counting delays. In Peru's Congress, Fujimori's [Popular Force](15247524.md) emerged as the single-largest party in both chambers. The second round, held on 7 June, saw another tight contest, with Fujimori emerging as the victor after weeks of counting.[^cite_note-37] On 28 July 2026, Keiko Fujimori was sworn in as Peru’s president.[^cite_note-38]

#### Allegations of corruption in politics

Exceptionally many [Presidents of Peru](239109.md) have been ousted from office or imprisoned on [allegations of corruption](41862074.md) over the past three decades. [Alberto Fujimori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberto_Fujimori) is serving a 25-year sentence in prison for commanding [death squads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_squad) that killed civilians in a counterinsurgency campaign during his tenure (1990-2000). He was later also found guilty of corruption. Former president [Alan García](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Garc%C3%ADa) (1985-1990 and 2006–2011) committed suicide in April 2019 when Peruvian police arrived to arrest him over allegations he participated in [Odebrecht bribery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odebrecht_Case) scheme. Former president [Alejandro Toledo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alejandro_Toledo) is accused of allegedly receiving bribe from Brazilian construction firm [Odebrecht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odebrecht) during his government (2001-2006). Former president [Ollanta Humala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ollanta_Humala) (2011-2016) is also under investigation for allegedly receiving bribe from Odebrecht during his presidential election campaign. Humala's successor [Pedro Pablo Kuczynski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedro_Pablo_Kuczynski) (2016-2018) remains under house arrest while prosecutors investigate him for favoring contracts with Odebrecht. Former president [Martín Vizcarra](51682201.md) (2018-2020) was ousted by Congress after media reports alleged he had received bribes while he was a regional governor years earlier.[^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40]

Under the current constitution, the president is the [head of state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_of_state) and [government](37570.md). The president is elected for a five-year term and may not immediately be re-elected.[^cite_note-41] All citizens above the age of eighteen are entitled and in fact [compelled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compulsory_voting) to vote. The first and second vice presidents also are popularly elected but have no constitutional functions unless the president is unable to discharge his duties.

The President appoints the [Prime Minister](1900295.md) *(Primer Ministro)* and the [Council of Ministers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_of_Peru) *(Consejo de Ministros,* or Cabinet), which is individually and collectively responsible both to the president and the legislature.[^cite_note-Draft-1][^cite_note-Dual-2] All presidential decree laws or draft bills sent to Congress must be approved by the Council of Ministers.

## Legislative branch



[Congress of the Republic of Peru](1571007.md).

The legislative branch consists of a bicameral [Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_Peru) (*Congreso*), consisting of a [Chamber of Deputies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamber_of_Deputies_\(Peru\)) (lower house) and a [Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senate_of_Peru) (upper house). In addition to passing laws, Congress ratifies treaties, authorizes government loans, and approves the government budget. The president has the power to block legislation with which the executive branch does not agree.

## Political parties and elections

For other political parties, see [List of political parties in Peru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_political_parties_in_Peru). An overview on elections and election results is included in [Elections in Peru](1442449.md).

Like other Latin American nations, political parties in Peru, since their revolutionary period, have been weak and centred around a candidate instead of policy, with parties selecting a candidate with the most wealth that they can bring to support the organization.[^cite_note-\:2-22][^cite_note-\:3-23][^cite_note-\:5-16] The lack of popular political parties led to the rise of [populist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Populist) authoritarian leaders.[^cite_note-\:2-22] With the growth of media and a large informal population, Peru has continued to ignore the need for political parties.[^cite_note-\:2-22] Political parties exist mainly through conflict, holding a goal to damage opposing parties while ignoring policy.[^cite_note-\:3-23] Elections in Peru are usually held every five years, but the legislature can be dissolved by the president.

## Judicial branch



[Supreme Court of Justice of Peru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Justice_of_Peru).

The judicial branch of government is headed by a 16-member [Supreme Court](4913027.md) seated in [Lima](85423.md). The National Council of the Judiciary appoints judges to this court.

The [Constitutional Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_Court_of_Peru) *(Tribunal Constitucional)* interprets the constitution on matters of individual rights. Superior courts in regional capitals review appeals from decisions by lower courts. Courts of first instance are located in provincial capitals and are divided into civil, penal, and special chambers. The judiciary has created several temporary specialized courts in an attempt to reduce the large backlog of cases pending final court action.

Peru's legal system is based on civil law system. Peru has not accepted compulsory [ICJ](14918.md) jurisdiction. In 1996 a [human rights](13831.md) [ombudsman](26289198.md)'s office *(defensor del pueblo)* was created to address [human rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_right) issues.

## Administrative divisions

Peru's territory, according to the Regionalization Law which was passed on 18 November 2002, is divided into 25 [regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Region) (regiones). These regions are subdivided into [provinces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province), which are composed of [districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/District). There are a total of 180 provinces and 1747 districts in Peru.

[Lima Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lima_Province) is **not** part of any political region.

## Organizations

### Armed groups

Leftist guerrilla groups include [Shining Path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shining_Path), the [Túpac Amaru Revolutionary Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C3%BApac_Amaru_Revolutionary_Movement) (MRTA). Both Shining Path and MRTA are considered [terrorist](30636.md) organizations.

### Regional groups

Regional groups representing peasant and indigenous groups exist in the outlying provinces, often working to promote autonomy.[^cite_note-PROFE22-42] Groups promoting autonomy agreements with larger states possibly existed since the [Inca Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_Empire) and such sentiments of independence have continued among local communities to current times.[^cite_note-PROFE22-42]

### Non-governmental organizations (NGOs)

In the early 1970s and 1980s, many grassroots organizations emerged in Peru. They were concerned with the problems of local people and poverty reduction. Organizations such as Solaris Peru, Traperos de Emus San Agustin, APRODE PERU, Cáritas del Perú, and the American organization CARE, with their Peruvian location, fought to address poverty in their communities with different approaches, depending on the organization. In 2000, these organizations played an important role in the [decentralization](49139.md) process. Their hope was that power would be clearly divided between national and local governments, and the latter would be able to address social justice and the concerns of local people better than the national government could. Some [NGO](46539.md) members even became part of local governments. There is a debate about the extent to which this engagement in politics contributes to the attainment of their original goals.[^cite_note-43]
