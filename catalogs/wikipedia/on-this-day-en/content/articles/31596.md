**Typhoid fever**, also known as **typhoid**, is an orally contracted disease caused by *[Salmonella enterica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmonella_enterica)* [serotype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serotype) Typhi bacteria, also called ***Salmonella* Typhi**.[^cite_note-Wain_2015-2][^cite_note-3] Symptoms vary from mild to severe, and usually begin six to 30 days after exposure.[^cite_note-Newton_2014-4][^cite_note-CDC2013-5] Often there is a gradual onset of a high [fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fever) over several days.[^cite_note-Newton_2014-4] This is commonly accompanied by [weakness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weakness), [abdominal pain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdominal_pain), [constipation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constipation), [headaches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Headache), and mild [vomiting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vomiting).[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-CDC2013-5][^cite_note-CDC2013Tech-7] Some people develop a [skin rash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skin_rash) with [rose colored spots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rose_spots).[^cite_note-CDC2013-5] In severe cases, people may experience [confusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confusion).[^cite_note-CDC2013Tech-7] Without treatment, symptoms may last weeks or months.[^cite_note-CDC2013-5] [Diarrhea](53951.md) may be severe, but is uncommon.[^cite_note-CDC2013Tech-7] Other people may carry it without being affected, but are still contagious.[^cite_note-WHO2008-8] Typhoid fever is a type of [enteric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enteric) fever, along with [paratyphoid fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paratyphoid_fever).[^cite_note-Wain_2015-2] *[Salmonella](42114.md) enterica* Typhi is believed to infect and replicate only within humans.[^cite_note-Pitzer_2019-9]

Typhoid is caused by the bacterium [*Salmonella enterica* subsp. *enterica*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmonella_enterica_subsp._enterica) [serovar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serovar) Typhi growing in the [intestines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intestine), [Peyer's patches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peyer's_patch), [mesenteric lymph nodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superior_mesenteric_lymph_nodes), [spleen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spleen), [liver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liver), [gallbladder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallbladder), [bone marrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bone_marrow) and [blood](3997.md).[^cite_note-CDC2013-5][^cite_note-CDC2013Tech-7] Typhoid is spread by ingesting food or water contaminated with the [feces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feces) of an infected person.[^cite_note-WHO2008-8] Risk factors include limited access to clean drinking water and poor [sanitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanitation).[^cite_note-Wain_2015-2] Those who have not yet been exposed to it and ingest contaminated drinking water or food are most at risk for developing symptoms.[^cite_note-CDC2013Tech-7] Only humans can be infected; there are no known animal reservoirs.[^cite_note-WHO2008-8] Salmonella typhi which causes typhoid fever is different from the other *Salmonella* bacteria that usually cause [salmonellosis](1046292.md), a common type of food poisoning.[^cite_note-10]

Diagnosis is performed by culturing and identifying *S.* typhi from patient samples or [detecting an immune response to the pathogen from blood samples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Widal_test).[^cite_note-CDC2013-5][^cite_note-Wain_2015-2][^cite_note-Crump_2010-11] Recently, new advances in large-scale data collection and analysis have allowed researchers to develop better diagnostics, such as detecting changing abundances of small molecules in the blood that may specifically indicate typhoid fever.[^cite_note-12] Diagnostic tools in regions where typhoid is most prevalent are quite limited in their accuracy and specificity, and the time required for a proper diagnosis, the increasing spread of [antibiotic resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimicrobial_resistance), and the cost of testing are also hardships for under-resourced healthcare systems.[^cite_note-Pitzer_2019-9]

A [typhoid vaccine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhoid_vaccine) can prevent about 40–90% of cases during the first two years.[^cite_note-Milligan_2018-13] The vaccine may have some effect for up to seven years.[^cite_note-Wain_2015-2] For those at high risk or people traveling to areas where it is common, vaccination is recommended.[^cite_note-WHO2008-8] Other efforts to prevent it include providing clean [drinking water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drinking_water), good [sanitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanitation), and [handwashing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handwashing).[^cite_note-CDC2013-5][^cite_note-WHO2008-8] Until an infection is confirmed as cleared, the infected person should not prepare food for others.[^cite_note-CDC2013-5] Typhoid can be treated with [antibiotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibiotic) such as [azithromycin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azithromycin), [fluoroquinolones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluoroquinolones), or [third-generation cephalosporins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third-generation_cephalosporin).[^cite_note-Wain_2015-2] Resistance to these antibiotics has been developing, which has made treatment more difficult.[^cite_note-Wain_2015-2][^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15]

In 2015, 12.5 million new typhoid cases were reported.[^cite_note-Vos_2016-16] Children are most commonly affected.[^cite_note-Wain_2015-2][^cite_note-WHO2008-8] Typhoid decreased in the [developed world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developed_world) in the 1940s as a result of improved sanitation and the use of antibiotics.[^cite_note-WHO2008-8] Every year about 400 cases are reported in the U.S. and an estimated 6,000 people have typhoid.[^cite_note-CDC2013Tech-7][^cite_note-17] In 2015, it resulted in about 149,000 deaths worldwide – down from 181,000 in 1990.[^cite_note-Wang_2016-18][^cite_note-Abubakar_2015-19] Without treatment, the risk of death may be as high as 20%.[^cite_note-WHO2008-8] With treatment, it is between 1% and 4%.[^cite_note-Wain_2015-2][^cite_note-WHO2008-8]

[Typhus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhus) is a different disease, caused by unrelated species of bacteria.[^cite_note-20] Owing to their similar symptoms, they were not recognized as distinct diseases until the 1800s. "Typhoid" means "resembling typhus".[^cite_note-21]

## Signs and symptoms

Classically, the progression of untreated typhoid fever has three distinct stages, each lasting about a week. Over the course of these stages, the patient becomes exhausted and emaciated.[^cite_note-22]

- In the first week, the body temperature rises slowly, and fever fluctuations are seen with relative [bradycardia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bradycardia) ([Faget sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faget_sign)), [malaise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaise), headache, and cough. A bloody nose ([epistaxis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistaxis)) is seen in a quarter of cases, and abdominal pain is also possible. A decrease in the number of circulating white blood cells ([leukopenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leukopenia)) occurs with [eosinopenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eosinopenia) and relative [lymphocytosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lymphocytosis); blood cultures are positive for *S. enterica* subsp. enterica serovar Typhi. The [Widal test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Widal_test) is usually negative.

  [^cite_note-Kumar_2017-23]

- In the second week, the person is often too tired to get up, with high fever in plateau around 39–40.5 °C (102.2–104.9 °F) and bradycardia (sphygmothermic dissociation or Faget sign), classically with a [dicrotic pulse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dicrotic_pulse) wave. [Delirium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delirium) can occur, where the patient is often calm, but sometimes becomes agitated. This delirium has given typhoid the nickname "nervous fever". [Rose spots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rose_spots) appear on the lower chest and abdomen in around a third of patients. [Rhonchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhonchi) (rattling breathing sounds) are heard in the base of the lungs. The abdomen is distended and painful in the right lower quadrant, where a rumbling sound can be heard. Diarrhea can occur in this stage, but constipation is also common. The spleen and liver are enlarged ([hepatosplenomegaly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepatosplenomegaly)) and tender, and liver [transaminases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transaminases) are elevated. The Widal test is strongly positive, with antiO and antiH antibodies. Blood cultures are sometimes still positive.

- In the third week of typhoid fever, possible complications include:&#x20;

  - The fever is still very high and oscillates very little over 24 hours. [Dehydration](155056.md) ensues along with malnutrition, and the patient is delirious. A third of affected people develop a macular rash on the trunk.

  - [Intestinal haemorrhage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastrointestinal_bleeding) due to bleeding in congested [Peyer's patches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peyer's_patches) occurs; this can be very serious but is usually not fatal.

  - Intestinal perforation in the distal [ileum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ileum) is a critical complication and often fatal. It may occur without alarming symptoms until [sepsis](158400.md) or diffuse [peritonitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peritonitis) sets in.

  - Respiratory diseases such as [pneumonia](52135.md) and [acute bronchitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_bronchitis)

  - [Encephalitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encephalitis)

  - Neuropsychiatric symptoms (described as "muttering delirium" or "coma vigil"), with picking at bedclothes or imaginary objects.

  - Metastatic abscesses, [cholecystitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cholecystitis), [endocarditis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endocarditis), and [osteitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osteitis).

  - Low platelet count ([thrombocytopenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrombocytopenia)) is sometimes seen.

    [^cite_note-24]

## Causes



A 1939 conceptual illustration showing various ways that typhoid bacteria can contaminate a [water well](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_well) (center)

### Bacteria

The [Gram-negative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gram_stain) bacterium that causes typhoid fever is *Salmonella enterica* subsp. enterica serovar Typhi. Based on the MLST subtyping scheme, the two main sequence types of the *S.* typhi are ST1 and ST2, which are widespread globally.[^cite_note-Yap_2016-25] Global phylogeographical analysis showed dominance of a haplotype 58 (H58), which probably originated in India during the late 1980s and is now spreading through the world with [multi-drug resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_drug_resistance).[^cite_note-26] A more detailed genotyping scheme was reported in 2016 and is now widely used. This scheme reclassified the nomenclature of H58 to genotype 4.3.1.[^cite_note-27]

### Transmission

Unlike other strains of *[Salmonella](42114.md)*, humans are the only known typhoid carriers.[^cite_note-Eng_2015-28] *[S. enterica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S._enterica)* subsp. enterica serovar Typhi is spread by the [fecal-oral route](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fecal-oral_route) from people who are infected and from [asymptomatic carriers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptomatic_carriers) of the bacterium.[^cite_note-Eng_2015-28] An asymptomatic human carrier is someone who is still excreting typhoid bacteria in stool a year after the acute stage of the infection, also known as a chronic excretor.[^cite_note-Eng_2015-28]

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis is made by any [blood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_culture), [bone marrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bone_marrow), or [stool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_feces) [cultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_culture) and with the [Widal test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Widal_test) (demonstration of [antibodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibody) against *Salmonella* [antigens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antigens) O-somatic and H-flagellar). In [epidemics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epidemics) and less wealthy countries, after excluding [malaria](20423.md), [dysentery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dysentery), or [pneumonia](52135.md), a therapeutic trial time with [chloramphenicol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloramphenicol) is generally undertaken while awaiting the results of the Widal test and blood and stool cultures.[^cite_note-Ryan_2004-29]

### Widal test

Main article: [Widal test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Widal_test)



Widal test card

The [Widal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Widal) test is used to identify specific [antibodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibodies) in the serum of people with typhoid by using antigen-antibody interactions.[^cite_note-30]

In this test, the serum is mixed with a dead bacterial suspension of *Salmonella* with specific antigens. If the patient's serum contains antibodies against those antigens, they get attached to them, forming clumps. If clumping does not occur, the test is negative. The Widal test is time-consuming and prone to significant false positives. It may also be falsely negative in recently infected people. But unlike the Typhidot test, the Widal test quantifies the specimen with [titres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titer).[^cite_note-31]

### Rapid diagnostic tests

Rapid diagnostic tests such as Tubex, Typhidot, and Test-It have shown moderate diagnostic accuracy.[^cite_note-32]

#### Typhidot

Typhidot is based on the presence of specific IgM and IgG antibodies to a specific 50[Kd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissociation_constant) OMP antigen. This test is carried out on a cellulose nitrate membrane where a specific *S. typhi* outer membrane protein is attached as fixed test lines. It separately identifies IgM and IgG antibodies. IgM shows recent infection; IgG signifies remote infection.[^cite_note-Mehmood_2015-33]The sample pad of this kit contains colloidal gold-anti-human IgG or gold-anti-human IgM. If the sample contains IgG and IgM antibodies against those antigens, they will react and turn red. The typhidot test becomes positive within 2–3 days of infection.[^cite_note-Mehmood_2015-33]

Two colored bands indicate a positive test. A single control band indicates a negative test. A single first fixed line or no band at all indicates an invalid test. Typhidot's biggest limitation is that it is not quantitative, just positive or negative.[^cite_note-34]

#### Tubex test

The Tubex test contains two types of particles: brown magnetic particles coated with antigen and blue indicator particles coated with O9 antibody. During the test, if antibodies are present in the serum, they will attach to the brown magnetic particles and settle at the base, while the blue indicator particles remain in the solution, producing a blue color, which means the test is positive.

If the serum does not have an antibody in it, the blue particles attach to the brown particles and settle at the bottom, producing a colorless solution, which means the test is negative.[^cite_note-35]

## Prevention



Doctor administering a typhoid [vaccination](32473.md) at a school in [San Augustine County, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Augustine_County,_Texas), 1943

Sanitation and hygiene are important to prevent typhoid. It can spread only in environments where human feces can come into contact with food or drinking water. Careful food preparation and washing of hands are crucial to prevent typhoid. Industrialization contributed greatly to the elimination of typhoid fever, as it eliminated the public health hazards associated with having horse manure in public streets, which led to a large number of flies,[^cite_note-36] which are [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_\(epidemiology\)) of many pathogens, including *Salmonella* spp.[^cite_note-37] According to statistics from the U.S. [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention](6811.md), the [chlorination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_chlorination) of drinking water has led to dramatic decreases in the transmission of typhoid fever.[^cite_note-38]

### Vaccination

Two [typhoid vaccines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhoid_vaccine) are licensed for use in the prevention of typhoid:[^cite_note-Milligan_2018-13] the live, oral [Ty21a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ty21a) vaccine (sold as Vivotif by [Crucell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crucell) Switzerland AG) and the injectable [typhoid polysaccharide vaccine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhoid_polysaccharide_vaccine) (sold as Typhim Vi by [Sanofi Pasteur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanofi_Pasteur) and Typherix by [GlaxoSmithKline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GlaxoSmithKline)). Both are efficacious and recommended for travelers to areas where typhoid is endemic. Boosters are recommended every five years for the oral vaccine and every two years for the injectable form.[^cite_note-Milligan_2018-13] An older, killed [whole-cell vaccine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whole-cell_vaccine) is still used in countries where the newer preparations are not available, but this vaccine is no longer recommended for use because it has more side effects (mainly pain and inflammation at the site of the injection).[^cite_note-39]



Vivotif – oral typhoid vaccine of live-attenuated *S. enterica* Typhi strain Ty21a

To help decrease rates of typhoid fever in [developing nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developing_nation), the [World Health Organization](33583.md) (WHO) endorsed the use of a vaccination program starting in 1999. Vaccination has proven effective at controlling outbreaks in high-incidence areas and is also very cost-effective: prices are normally less than US\$1 per dose. Because the price is low, poverty-stricken communities are more willing to take advantage of the vaccinations.[^cite_note-Date_2015-40] Although vaccination programs for typhoid have proven effective, they alone cannot eliminate typhoid fever.[^cite_note-Date_2015-40] Combining vaccines with public health efforts is the only proven way to control this disease.[^cite_note-Date_2015-40]

Since the 1990s, the WHO has recommended two typhoid fever vaccines. The ViPS vaccine is given by injection and the Ty21a by capsules. Only people over age two are recommended to be vaccinated with the ViPS vaccine, and it requires a revaccination after 2–3 years, with a 55–72% efficacy. The Ty21a vaccine is recommended for people five and older, lasting 5–7 years with 51–67% efficacy. The two vaccines have proved safe and effective for epidemic disease control in multiple regions.[^cite_note-Date_2015-40]

A version of the vaccine combined with a [hepatitis A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepatitis_A) vaccine is also available.[^cite_note-41]

Results of a phase 3 trial of typhoid [conjugate vaccine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_vaccine) (TCV) in December 2019 reported 81% fewer cases among children.[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43]

## Treatment

Treatment for typhoid fever is most effective if started early.

### Oral rehydration therapy

The rediscovery of [oral rehydration therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_rehydration_therapy) in the 1960s provided a simple way to prevent many of the deaths of [diarrheal](53951.md) diseases in general.[^cite_note-44]

### Antibiotics

Where resistance is uncommon, the treatment of choice is a [fluoroquinolone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluoroquinolone) such as [ciprofloxacin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ciprofloxacin).[^cite_note-Parry_2009-45][^cite_note-Effa_2011-46] Otherwise, a third-generation cephalosporin such as [ceftriaxone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceftriaxone) or [cefotaxime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cefotaxime) is the first choice.[^cite_note-47][^cite_note-48][^cite_note-49][^cite_note-50] [Cefixime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cefixime) is a suitable oral alternative.[^cite_note-51][^cite_note-52]

Properly treated, typhoid fever is not fatal in most cases. Antibiotics such as [ampicillin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampicillin), [chloramphenicol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloramphenicol), [trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole), [amoxicillin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amoxicillin), and ciprofloxacin have been commonly used to treat it.[^cite_note-53] Treatment with antibiotics reduces the case-fatality rate to about 1%.[^cite_note-54]

Without treatment, some patients develop sustained fever, bradycardia, hepatosplenomegaly, abdominal symptoms, and occasionally pneumonia. In white-skinned patients, pink spots, which fade on pressure, appear on the skin of the trunk in up to 20% of cases. In the third week, untreated cases may develop gastrointestinal and cerebral complications, which may prove fatal in 10–20% of cases. The highest case fatality rates are reported in children under 4. Around 2–5% of those who contract typhoid fever become chronic carriers, as bacteria persist in the [biliary tract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biliary_tract) after symptoms have resolved.[^cite_note-55]

### Surgery

Surgery is usually indicated if [intestinal perforation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intestinal_perforation) occurs. One study found a 30-day mortality rate of 9% (8/88), and surgical site infections at 67% (59/88), with the disease burden borne predominantly by low-resource countries.[^cite_note-Anyomih_2018-56]

For surgical treatment, most surgeons prefer simple closure of the perforation with drainage of the [peritoneum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peritoneum). Small bowel [resection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surgery#Resection) is indicated for patients with multiple perforations. If antibiotic treatment fails to eradicate the [hepatobiliary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepatobiliary) carriage, the [gallbladder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallbladder) should be resected. [Cholecystectomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cholecystectomy) is sometimes successful, especially in patients with [gallstones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallstone), but is not always successful in eradicating the carrier state because of persisting [hepatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepatic) infection.[^cite_note-57][^cite_note-Gonzalez-Escobedo_2011-58]

### Resistance

As [resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimicrobial_resistance) to ampicillin, chloramphenicol, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and [streptomycin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streptomycin) is now common, these agents are no longer used as first-line treatment of typhoid fever.[^cite_note-59] Typhoid resistant to these agents is known as multidrug-resistant typhoid.[^cite_note-60]

Ciprofloxacin resistance is an increasing problem, especially in the [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent) and [Southeast Asia](28741.md). Many centres are shifting from ciprofloxacin to [ceftriaxone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceftriaxone) as the first line for treating suspected typhoid originating in South America, India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Thailand, or Vietnam. Also, it has been suggested that [azithromycin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azithromycin) is better at treating resistant typhoid than both fluoroquinolone drugs and ceftriaxone.[^cite_note-Effa_2011-46] Azithromycin can be taken by mouth and is less expensive than ceftriaxone, which is given by injection.[^cite_note-61]

A separate problem exists with laboratory testing for reduced susceptibility to ciprofloxacin; current recommendations are that isolates should be tested simultaneously against ciprofloxacin (CIP) and against [nalidixic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nalidixic_acid) (NAL), that isolates sensitive to both CIP and NAL should be reported as "sensitive to ciprofloxacin", and that isolates sensitive to CIP but not to NAL should be reported as "reduced sensitivity to ciprofloxacin". But an analysis of 271 isolates found that around 18% of isolates with reduced susceptibility to [fluoroquinolones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluoroquinolones), the class to which CIP belongs ([MIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum_inhibitory_concentration) 0.125–1.0 mg/L), would not be detected by this method.[^cite_note-62]

## Epidemiology

Main article: [Epidemiology of typhoid fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epidemiology_of_typhoid_fever)



Death rate typhoid paratyphoid fever children under 5

In 2000, typhoid fever caused an estimated 21.7 million illnesses and 217,000 deaths.[^cite_note-Crump_2010-11] It occurs most often in children and young adults between 5 and 19 years old.[^cite_note-who-63] In 2013, it resulted in about 161,000 deaths – down from 181,000 in 1990.[^cite_note-Abubakar_2015-19] Infants, children, and adolescents in south-central and Southeast Asia have the highest rates of typhoid.[^cite_note-Crump_2004-64] Outbreaks are also often reported in sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia.[^cite_note-Muyembe-Tamfum_2009-65][^cite_note-Baddam_2012-66][^cite_note-Yap_2012a-67] In 2000, more than 90% of morbidity and mortality due to typhoid fever occurred in Asia.[^cite_note-68] In the U.S., about 400 cases occur each year, 75% of which are acquired while traveling internationally.[^cite_note-69][^cite_note-70]

Before the antibiotic era, the [case fatality rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_fatality_rate) of typhoid fever was 10–20%. Today, with prompt treatment, it is less than 1%,[^cite_note-71] but 3–5% of people who are infected develop a chronic infection in the gall bladder.[^cite_note-Levine_1982-72] Since *S. enterica* subsp. enterica serovar Typhi is human-restricted, these chronic carriers become the crucial reservoir, which can persist for decades for further spread of the disease, further complicating its identification and treatment.[^cite_note-Gonzalez-Escobedo_2011-58] Lately, the study of *S. enterica* subsp. enterica serovar Typhi associated with a large outbreak and a carrier at the genome level provides new insight into the pathogenesis of the pathogen.[^cite_note-Yap_2012b-73][^cite_note-Yap_2014-74]

In industrialized nations, water sanitation and food handling improvements have reduced the number of typhoid cases.[^cite_note-75] Third world nations have the highest rates. People in these areas often lack access to clean water, proper sanitation systems, and proper healthcare facilities. In these areas, such access to basic public-health needs is not expected in the near future.[^cite_note-76]

In 2004–2005, an outbreak in the [Democratic Republic of Congo](76762.md) resulted in more than 42,000 cases and 214 deaths.[^cite_note-who-63] Since November 2016, [Pakistan](23235.md) has had an outbreak of extensively [drug-resistant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drug-resistant) (XDR) typhoid fever.[^cite_note-77]

In Europe, a report based on data for 2017 retrieved from The European Surveillance System (TESSy) on the distribution of confirmed typhoid and [paratyphoid fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paratyphoid_fever) cases found that 22 EU/EEA countries reported a total of 1,098 cases, 90.9% of which were travel-related, mainly acquired during travel to [South Asia](21566765.md).[^cite_note-78]

## History

### Early descriptions

The [plague of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Athens), during the [Peloponnesian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peloponnesian_War), was most likely an outbreak of typhoid fever.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] During the war, [Athenians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenians) retreated into a walled-in city to escape attack from the [Spartans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparta). This massive influx of humans into a concentrated space overwhelmed the water supply and waste infrastructure, likely leading to unsanitary conditions as fresh water became harder to obtain and waste became more difficult to collect and remove beyond the city walls.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] In 2006, examining the remains of a mass burial site from Athens from around the time of the plague (\~430 B.C.) revealed that fragments of DNA similar to that of modern-day [*S.* Typhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmonella_enterica_subsp._enterica) were detected, whereas *[Yersinia pestis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yersinia_pestis)* (plague), *[Rickettsia prowazekii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rickettsia_prowazekii)* (typhus), *[Mycobacterium tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycobacterium_tuberculosis)*, [cowpox virus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cowpox), and *[Bartonella henselae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartonella_henselae)* were not detected in any of the remains tested.[^cite_note-Papagrigorakis_2006-79]

### Definition and evidence of transmission

The French doctors [Pierre-Fidele Bretonneau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Bretonneau) and [Pierre-Charles-Alexandre Louis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Charles_Alexandre_Louis) are credited with describing typhoid fever as a specific disease, unique from [typhus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhus). Both doctors performed autopsies on individuals who died in Paris due to fever – and indicated that many had lesions on the [Peyer's patches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peyer's_patch) which correlated with distinct symptoms before death.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] British medics were skeptical of the differentiation between typhoid and typhus because both were endemic to Britain at that time. However, in France, only typhoid was present circulating in the population.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] Pierre-Charlles-Alexandre Louis also performed case studies and statistical analysis to demonstrate that typhoid was contagious – and that persons who already had the disease seemed to be protected.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] Afterward, several American doctors confirmed these findings, and then [Sir William Jenner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_William_Jenner,_1st_Baronet) convinced any remaining skeptics that typhoid is a specific disease recognizable by lesions in the Peyer's patches by examining sixty-six autopsies from fever patients and concluding that the symptoms of headaches, diarrhea, rash spots, and abdominal pain were present only in patients who were found to have intestinal lesions after death; these observations solidified the association of the disease with the intestinal tract and gave the first clue to the route of transmission.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82]

In 1847, [William Budd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Budd) learned of an epidemic of typhoid fever in Clifton, and identified that all 13 of 34 residents who had contracted the disease drew their drinking water from the same well.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] Notably, this observation was two years before [John Snow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Snow) first published an early version of his theory that contaminated water was the central conduit for transmitting [cholera](7591.md). Budd later became health officer of [Bristol](36741.md) ensured a clean water supply, and documented further evidence of typhoid as a water-borne illness throughout his career.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82]

### Cause

Polish scientist [Tadeusz Browicz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tadeusz_Browicz) described a short bacillus in the organs and feces of typhoid victims in 1874.[^cite_note-91] Browicz was able to isolate and grow the bacilli but did not go as far as to insinuate or prove that they caused the disease.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82]

In April 1880, three months before Eberth's publication, [Edwin Klebs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Klebs) described short and [filamentous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filamentation) bacilli in the [Peyer's patches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peyer's_patch) in typhoid victims.[^cite_note-92] The bacterium's role in disease was speculated but not confirmed.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82]

In 1880, [Karl Joseph Eberth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Joseph_Eberth) described a bacillus that he suspected was the cause of typhoid.[^cite_note-93][^cite_note-94][^cite_note-95] Eberth is given credit for discovering the bacterium definitively by successfully isolating the same bacterium from 18 of 40 typhoid victims and failing to discover the bacterium present in any "control" victims of other diseases.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] In 1884, pathologist [Georg Theodor August Gaffky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Theodor_August_Gaffky) (1850–1918) confirmed Eberth's findings.[^cite_note-96] Gaffky isolated the same bacterium as Eberth from the spleen of a typhoid victim, and was able to grow the bacterium on solid media.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] The organism was given names such as Eberth's bacillus, *Eberthella* Typhi, and Gaffky-Eberth bacillus.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] Today, the bacillus that causes typhoid fever goes by the scientific name *[Salmonella enterica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmonella_enterica)* serovar Typhi.[^cite_note-97]

### Chlorination of water

Most developed countries had declining rates of typhoid fever throughout the first half of the 20th century due to vaccinations and advances in public sanitation and hygiene. In 1893, attempts were made to chlorinate the water supply in [Hamburg](13467.md), Germany, and in 1897 [Maidstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maidstone), England, was the first town to have its entire water supply chlorinated.[^cite_note-98] In 1905, following an outbreak of typhoid fever, the City of [Lincoln, England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln,_England), instituted permanent water chlorination.[^cite_note-99] The first permanent disinfection of drinking water in the US was made in 1908 to the [Jersey City, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jersey_City,_New_Jersey), water supply. Credit for the decision to build the chlorination system has been given to [John L. Leal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_L._Leal).[^cite_note-100] The [chlorination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_chlorination) facility was designed by [George W. Fuller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Fuller).[^cite_note-101]

Outbreaks in traveling military groups led to the creation of the Lyster bag in 1915: a bag with a faucet that can be hung from a tree or pole, filled with water, and comes with a chlorination tablet to drop into the water.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] The Lyster bag was essential for the survival of American soldiers in the [Vietnam War](32611.md).[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82]

### Direct transmission and carriers



[Mary Mallon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Mallon) ("Typhoid Mary") in a hospital bed (foreground): She was forcibly quarantined as a carrier of typhoid fever in 1907 for three years and then again from 1915 until she died in 1938.

There were several occurrences of [milk delivery men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milk_delivery) spreading typhoid fever throughout the communities they served. Although typhoid is not spread through milk itself, there were several examples of milk distributors in many locations watering their milk down with contaminated water, or cleaning the glass bottles the milk was placed in with contaminated water.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82][^cite_note-Remember_Jamaica_Plain?_2007-85] [Boston](24437894.md) had two such cases around the turn of the 20th century.[^cite_note-Remember_Jamaica_Plain?_2007-85] In 1899, there were 24 cases of typhoid traced to a single milkman, whose wife had died of typhoid fever a week before the outbreak.[^cite_note-Remember_Jamaica_Plain?_2007-85] In 1908, J.J. Fallon, who was also a milkman, died of typhoid fever.[^cite_note-Remember_Jamaica_Plain?_2007-85] Following his death and confirmation of the typhoid fever diagnosis, the city conducted an investigation of typhoid symptoms and cases along his route and found evidence of a significant outbreak. A month after the outbreak was first reported, the [*Boston Globe*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Boston_Globe) published a short statement declaring the outbreak over, stating "\[a]t Jamaica Plain there is a slight increase, the total being 272 cases. Throughout the city, there is a total of 348 cases."[^cite_note-Remember_Jamaica_Plain?_2007-85] There was at least one death reported during this outbreak: Mrs. Sophia S. Engstrom, aged 46.[^cite_note-Remember_Jamaica_Plain?_2007-85] Typhoid continued to ravage the [Jamaica Plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamaica_Plain) neighborhood in particular throughout 1908, and several more people were reported dead due to typhoid fever, although these cases were not explicitly linked to the outbreak.[^cite_note-Remember_Jamaica_Plain?_2007-85] The Jamaica Plain neighborhood at that time was home to many working-class and poor immigrants, mostly from [Ireland](147575.md).[^cite_note-102]

The most notorious carrier of typhoid fever, but by no means the most destructive, was [Mary Mallon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Mallon), known as Typhoid Mary.[^cite_note-Nova-103][^cite_note-Apple_Podcasts-87] Although other cases of human-to-human spread of typhoid were known at the time, the concept of an asymptomatic carrier, who was able to transmit disease, had only been hypothesized and not yet identified or proven.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] Mary Mallon became the first known example of an [asymptomatic carrier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptomatic_carrier) of an infectious disease, making typhoid fever the first known disease being transmissible through asymptomatic hosts.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] The cases and deaths caused by Mallon were mainly upper-class families in New York City.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] At the time of Mallon's tenure as a personal cook for upper-class families, New York City reported 3,000 to 4,500 cases of typhoid fever annually.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] In the summer of 1906, two daughters of a wealthy family and maids working in their home became ill with typhoid fever.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] After investigating their home water sources and ruling out water contamination, the family hired civil engineer [George Soper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Soper), who himself as an "epidemic fighter", to conduct an investigation of the possible source of typhoid fever in the home.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] His investigation ruled out many sources of food, and led him to question if the cook the family hired just prior to their household outbreak, Mallon, was the source.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] Since she had already left and begun employment elsewhere, he proceeded to track her down in order to obtain a stool sample.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] Ultimately, 51 cases and 3 deaths were suspected to be caused by Mallon.[^cite_note-104][^cite_note-Apple_Podcasts-87]

In 1924, the city of [Portland, Oregon](23503.md), experienced an outbreak of typhoid fever, consisting of 26 cases and 5 deaths, all deaths due to intestinal [hemorrhage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemorrhage).[^cite_note-Sears_1924-105] All cases were concluded to be due to a single milk farm worker, who was shedding large amounts of the typhoid pathogen in his urine.[^cite_note-Sears_1924-105] Misidentification of the disease, due to inaccurate Widal test results, delayed identification of the carrier and proper treatment.[^cite_note-Sears_1924-105] Ultimately, it took four samplings of different secretions from all of the dairy workers in order to successfully identify the carrier.[^cite_note-Sears_1924-105] Upon discovery, the dairy worker was forcibly quarantined for seven weeks, and regular samples were taken, most of the time the stool samples yielding no typhoid and often the urine yielding the pathogen.[^cite_note-Sears_1924-105] The carrier was reported as being 72 years old and appearing in excellent health with no symptoms.[^cite_note-Sears_1924-105] Pharmaceutical treatment decreased the amount of bacteria secreted, however, the infection was never fully cleared from the urine, and the carrier was released "under orders never again to engage in the handling of foods for human consumption."[^cite_note-Sears_1924-105] At the time of release, the authors noted "for more than fifty years he has earned his living chiefly by milking cows and knows little of other forms of labor, it must be expected that the closest surveillance will be necessary to make certain that he does not again engage in this occupation."[^cite_note-Sears_1924-105]

Overall, in the early 20th century the medical profession began to identify disease carriers and evidence of transmission independent of water contamination.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] In a 1933 [American Medical Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Medical_Association) publication, physicians' treatment of asymptomatic carriers is best summarized by the opening line "Carriers of typhoid bacilli are a menace".[^cite_note-Journal_of_the_American_Medical_Association_1933-106] Within the same publication, the first official estimate of typhoid carriers is given: 2–5% of all typhoid patients, and distinguished between temporary carriers and chronic carriers.[^cite_note-Journal_of_the_American_Medical_Association_1933-106] The authors further estimate that there are four to five chronic female carriers to every one male carrier, although offered no data to explain this assertion of a gender difference in the rate of typhoid carriers.[^cite_note-Journal_of_the_American_Medical_Association_1933-106] As far as treatment, the authors suggest: "When recognized, carriers must be instructed as to the disposal of excreta as well as to the importance of personal cleanliness. They should be forbidden to handle food or drink intended for others, and their movements and whereabouts must be reported to the public health officers".[^cite_note-Journal_of_the_American_Medical_Association_1933-106] Today, typhoid [carriers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptomatic_carrier) exist all over the world, but the highest incidence of [asymptomatic](602790.md) infection is likely to occur in South/Southeast Asian and Sub-Saharan countries.[^cite_note-LA_county_typhoid_2016-1][^cite_note-107] The [Los Angeles County department of public health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Angeles_County_Department_of_Public_Health) tracks typhoid carriers and reports the number of carriers identified within the county yearly; between 2006 and 2016 0–4 new cases of typhoid carriers were identified per year.[^cite_note-LA_county_typhoid_2016-1] Cases of typhoid fever [must be reported](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infectious_disease_reporting) within one working day from identification. As of 2018, chronic typhoid carriers must sign a "Carrier Agreement" and are required to test for typhoid shedding twice yearly, ideally every 6 months.[^cite_note-Los_Angeles_Department_of_Public_Health_2018-108] Carriers may be released from their agreements upon fulfilling "release" requirements, based on completion of a personalized treatment plan designed with medical professionals.[^cite_note-Los_Angeles_Department_of_Public_Health_2018-108] Fecal or gallbladder carrier release requirements: 6 consecutive negative feces and urine specimens submitted at 1-month or greater intervals beginning at least 7 days after completion of therapy.[^cite_note-Los_Angeles_Department_of_Public_Health_2018-108] Urinary or kidney carrier release requirements: 6 consecutive negative urine specimens submitted at 1-month or greater intervals beginning at least 7 days after completion of therapy.[^cite_note-Los_Angeles_Department_of_Public_Health_2018-108]

Due to the nature of asymptomatic cases, many questions remain about how individuals can tolerate infection for long periods, how to identify such cases, and efficient options for treatment. Researchers are working to understand asymptomatic infection with *[Salmonella](42114.md)* species by studying infections in laboratory animals, which will ultimately lead to improved prevention and treatment options for typhoid carriers. In 2002, John Gunn described the ability of *Salmonella* sp. to form [biofilms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biofilm) on [gallstones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallstone) in mice, providing a model for studying carriage in the gallbladder.[^cite_note-109] [Denise Monack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denise_Monack) and [Stanley Falkow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Falkow) described a mouse model of asymptomatic intestinal and systemic infection in 2004, and Monack went on to demonstrate that a subpopulation of [superspreaders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superspreaders) are responsible for the majority of transmission to new hosts, following the [80/20 rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pareto_principle) of disease transmission, and that the intestinal microbiota likely plays a role in transmission.[^cite_note-Monack_2004-110][^cite_note-111] Monack's mouse model allows long-term carriage of *Salmonella* in [mesenteric lymph nodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesenteric_lymph_nodes), [spleen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spleen) and [liver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liver).[^cite_note-Monack_2004-110]

### Vaccine development



[Almroth Edward Wright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almroth_Edward_Wright) developed the first effective typhoid vaccine.

British bacteriologist [Almroth Edward Wright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almroth_Edward_Wright) first developed an effective typhoid vaccine at the Army Medical School in [Netley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netley), [Hampshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hampshire). It was introduced in 1896 and used successfully by the British during the [Second Boer War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Boer_War) in South Africa.[^cite_note-112] At that time, typhoid often killed more soldiers at war than were lost due to enemy combat. Wright further developed his vaccine at a newly opened research department at [St Mary's Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Mary's_Hospital_\(London\)) Medical School in London in 1902, where he established a method for measuring protective substances ([opsonin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opsonin)) in human blood.[^cite_note-113] Wright's version of the typhoid vaccine was produced by growing the bacterium at [body temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermoregulation) in broth, then heating the bacteria to 60 °C to "heat inactivate" the pathogen, killing it, while keeping the surface [antigens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antigen) intact. The heat-killed bacteria was then injected into a patient.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] To show evidence of the vaccine's efficacy, Wright then collected serum samples from patients several weeks post-vaccination, and tested their serum's ability to [agglutinate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agglutination_\(biology\)) live typhoid bacteria. A "positive" result was represented by clumping of bacteria, indicating that the body was producing anti-serum (now called [antibodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibody)) against the pathogen.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82]

Citing the example of the Second Boer War, during which many soldiers died from easily preventable diseases, Wright convinced the [British Army](4887.md) that 10 million vaccine doses should be produced for the troops being sent to the [Western Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Front_\(World_War_I\)), thereby saving up to half a million lives during [World War I](4764461.md).[^cite_note-114] The British Army was the only combatant at the outbreak of the war to have its troops fully immunized against the bacterium. For the first time, their casualties due to combat exceeded those from disease.[^cite_note-115]

In 1909, [Frederick F. Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_F._Russell), a [U.S. Army](32087.md) physician, adopted Wright's typhoid vaccine for use with the Army, and two years later, his vaccination program became the first in which an entire army was immunized. It eliminated typhoid as a significant cause of morbidity and mortality in the U.S. military.[^cite_note-USAMRMC-116] Typhoid vaccination for members of the American military became mandatory in 1911.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] Before the vaccine, the rate of typhoid fever in the military was 14,000 or greater per 100,000 soldiers. By World War I, the rate of typhoid in American soldiers was 37 per 100,000.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82]

During the Second World War, the United States Army authorized the use of a trivalent vaccine – containing heat-inactivated Typhoid, [Paratyphi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paratyphoid_fever) A and [Paratyphi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paratyphoid_fever) B pathogens.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82]

In 1934, the discovery of the [Vi capsular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vi_capsular_polysaccharide_vaccine) antigen by [Arthur Felix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Felix) and [Miss S. R. Margaret Pitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_S._R._Margaret_Pitt?action=edit\&redlink=1) enabled the development of the safer Vi Antigen vaccine – which is widely in use today.[^cite_note-117] Arthur Felix and Margaret Pitt also isolated the strain Ty2, which became the parent strain of [Ty21a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ty21a), the strain used as a live-attenuated vaccine for typhoid fever today.[^cite_note-118]

### Antibiotics and resistance

[Chloramphenicol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloramphenicol) was isolated from *[Streptomyces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streptomyces)* by [David Gotlieb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Gottlieb_\(biologist\)) during the 1940s.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] In 1948, American army doctors tested its efficacy in treating typhoid patients in [Kuala Lumpur](16854.md), Malaysia.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] Individuals who received a full course of treatment cleared the infection, whereas patients given a lower dose had a relapse.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] Asymptomatic carriers continued to shed bacilli despite chloramphenicol treatment – only ill patients were improved with chloramphenicol.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82] Resistance to chloramphenicol became frequent in Southeast Asia by the 1950s, and today chloramphenicol is only used as a last resort due to the high prevalence of resistance.[^cite_note-Adler_2016-82]

## Terminology

The disease has been referred to by various names, often associated with symptoms, such as gastric fever, enteric fever, abdominal typhus, infantile remittent fever, slow fever, nervous fever, phytogenic fever,[^cite_note-119] drain fever, and low fever.[^cite_note-120]
