**Denali** ([/dəˈnɑːli/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [*də-NAH-lee*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key);[^cite_note-5] [Koyukon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koyukon_language): *Deenaalee*), federally designated as **Mount McKinley**,[^cite_note-Friedman2025-6][^cite_note-7] is the highest [mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain) peak in [North America](21139.md), with a summit [elevation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevation) of 20,310 feet (6,190 m) above [sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level). Its vertical relief above the surrounding plateaus is one of the greatest on Earth, measuring about 18,000 feet (5,500 meters).[^cite_note-Helman2005-8] With a [topographic prominence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topographic_prominence) of 20,156 feet (6,144 m)[^cite_note-PeakVisor-3] and a [topographic isolation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topographic_isolation) of 4,621.1 miles (7,436.9 km),[^cite_note-PeakVisor-3] Denali is the [third most prominent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_peaks_by_prominence) and [third-most isolated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_peaks_by_isolation) peak on Earth, after [Mount Everest](42179.md) and [Aconcagua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aconcagua). Located in the [Alaska Range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaska_Range) in the interior of the [U.S. state](18618239.md) of [Alaska](624.md), Denali is the centerpiece of [Denali National Park and Preserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denali_National_Park_and_Preserve).

The [Koyukon people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koyukon_people) who inhabit the area around the mountain have referred to the peak as "Denali" for centuries. In 1896, a gold prospector named it "Mount McKinley" in support of then presidential candidate [William McKinley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_McKinley), who later became the 25th president; McKinley's name was the official name recognized by the [federal government of the United States](195149.md) from 1917 until 2015. In 1975, the [State of Alaska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_of_Alaska) officially renamed the mountain Denali and requested the federal government follow suit. The [United States Department of the Interior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_the_Interior) changed the [official federal name of the mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denali%E2%80%93Mount_McKinley_naming_dispute) to Denali on August 29, 2015.[^cite_note-DenaliNameChangeDOI-9][^cite_note-denalirestored-10][^cite_note-McKinley_Name-11] In January 2025, the Department of the Interior under the [Trump administration](78298108.md) reverted the mountain's official federal name to Mount McKinley.[^cite_note-\:8-12][^cite_note-Friedman2025-6]

In 1903, [James Wickersham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Wickersham) recorded the first attempt at climbing Denali, which was unsuccessful. In 1906, [Frederick Cook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Cook) claimed the [first ascent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_ascent), but this ascent is unverified and its legitimacy questioned. The first verifiable ascent to Denali's summit was achieved on June 7, 1913, by climbers [Hudson Stuck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hudson_Stuck), [Harry Karstens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Karstens), [Walter Harper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Harper), and [Robert Tatum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Tatum), who went by the South Summit. In 1951, [Bradford Washburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bradford_Washburn) pioneered the West Buttress route, considered to be the safest and easiest route, and therefore the most popular.[^cite_note-roberts-13]

On September 2, 2015, the [U.S. Geological Survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Geological_Survey) measured the mountain at 20,310 feet (6,190 m) high,[^cite_note-ADN-1] 10 ft lower than the 20,320 feet (6,194 m) measured in 1952 using [photogrammetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photogrammetry).

## Geology and features

Denali is a [granitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granitic) [pluton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pluton), mostly pink [quartz monzonite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartz_monzonite), lifted by [tectonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tectonic) pressure from the [subduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subduction) of the [Pacific plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_plate) beneath the [North American plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_plate); at the same time, the [sedimentary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedimentary) material above and around the mountain was stripped away by [erosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erosion).[^cite_note-brease-14][^cite_note-15] The forces that lifted Denali also caused many deep [earthquakes](10106.md) in Alaska and the [Aleutian Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleutian_Islands). The Pacific Plate is seismically active beneath Denali, a tectonic region that is known as the "McKinley cluster".[^cite_note-hanson-16]

### Structural geology

The high topography of Denali is related to the complex structural relationships created by the right-lateral [Denali Fault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denali_Fault) and Denali Fault Bend. The Denali Fault is caused by stresses created by the low-angle subduction of the [Yakutat microplate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakutat_Block) underneath Alaska. The Denali Fault Bend is characterized as a gentle restraining bend.[^cite_note-\:3-17] The Denali Fault Bend represents a curvature in the Denali Fault that is approximately 75 km long. This curvature creates what is known as a "space problem". As the right-lateral movement along the Denali Fault continues, high compressional forces created at the fault bend essentially push the crust up in a vertical fashion. The longer the crust stays within the restraining bend, the higher the topography will be. Several active normal faults north of the restraining bend have recently been mapped with slip rates of approximately 2–6 mm/year.[^cite_note-\:3-17] These normal faults help to accommodate the unusual curvature of the restraining bend.

### Elevation

Denali has a summit [elevation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevation) of 20,310 feet (6,190 m) above [sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level), making it the highest peak in North America and the northernmost mountain above 19,685 feet (6,000 m) elevation in the world.[^cite_note-ADN-1] Measured from base to peak at some 18,000 ft (5,500 m), it is among the largest mountains situated entirely above sea level. Denali rises from a sloping plain with elevations from 1,000 to 3,000 ft (300 to 910 m), for a base-to-peak height of 17,000 to 19,000 ft (5,000 to 6,000 m).[^cite_note-18] By comparison, [Mount Everest](42179.md) rises from the [Tibetan Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibetan_Plateau) at a much higher base elevation. Base elevations for Everest range from 13,800 ft (4,200 m) on the south side to 17,100 ft (5,200 m) on the Tibetan Plateau, for a base-to-peak height in the range of 12,000 to 15,300 ft (3,700 to 4,700 m).[^cite_note-washburn_map-19] Denali's base-to-peak height is little more than half the 33,500 ft (10,200 m) of the volcano [Mauna Kea](267366.md), which lies mostly under water.[^cite_note-natgeo-20]

### Geography of the mountain

Denali has two significant summits: the South Summit is the higher one, while the North Summit has an elevation of 19,470 ft (5,934 m)[^cite_note-brease-14] and a [prominence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prominence) of approximately 1,270 ft (387 m).[^cite_note-pbnorth-21] The North Summit is sometimes counted as a separate peak (see e.g., [fourteener](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourteener)) and sometimes not; it is rarely climbed, except by those doing routes on the north side of the [massif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massif).

Five large glaciers flow off the slopes of the mountain. The [Peters Glacier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peters_Glacier_\(Alaska_Range\)) lies on the northwest side of the massif, while the [Muldrow Glacier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muldrow_Glacier) falls from its northeast slopes. Just to the east of the Muldrow, and abutting the eastern side of the massif, is the [Traleika Glacier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traleika_Glacier). The [Ruth Glacier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruth_Glacier) lies to the southeast of the mountain, and the [Kahiltna Glacier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kahiltna_Glacier) leads up to the southwest side of the mountain.[^cite_note-areaparks-22][^cite_note-planetware-23] With a length of 44 mi (71 km), the Kahiltna Glacier is the longest glacier in the [Alaska Range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaska_Range).

The [Koyukon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koyukon_people) [Athabaskans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaskan_Athabaskans) who inhabit the area around the mountain have, for centuries, referred to the peak as *Dinale* or *Denali*. The name is based on a Koyukon word for 'high' or 'tall'.[^cite_note-Martinson2015-24] During the [Russian ownership of Alaska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_America), the common name for the mountain was *Bolshaya Gora* ([Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language): Большая Гора; *bolshaya* 'big'; *gora* 'mountain'), which is the Russian translation of *Denali*.[^cite_note-DictionaryOFAlaskaPlaceNames-25] It was briefly called Densmore's Mountain in the late 1880s and early 1890s[^cite_note-norrisv1-1-26] after Frank Densmore, a gold prospector who was the first non-native Alaskan to reach the base of the mountain.[^cite_note-27]

### William A. Dickey

In 1896, gold prospector William Dickey[^cite_note-28][^cite_note-Brooks1911-29] named it *McKinley* as political support for then-presidential candidate [William McKinley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_McKinley), who became president the following year.

> One, however, W. A. Dickey, of a different type from the rest, recognized the surpassing height of the peak and its geographic import and gave it the name Mount McKinley. His exploration in 1896 was probably one of the first extensive journeys in this district since that of the Russian Malakoff in 1834. With three other men he ascended Susitna River to the trading station at the head of the delta; then constructed boats of whipsawed lumber and continued his journey up the main Susitna to the mouth of Indian Creek. From this place he explored a part of the upper canyon of the Susitna and also made a journey westward to the Chulitna, reaching the foot of the glacier which discharges into this river and has its source on the slopes of Mount McKinley. It was after this journey that he published his description of the mountain, in which he named it and stated its altitude as over 20,000 feet. He told the writer that he had no instruments, but made his estimate, which has proved to be remarkably accurate, with careful consideration of the atmospheric conditions, as well as of the probable distance to the base of the peak. In 1897 he made a second trip into this region, with one companion, and extended his previous explorations. He was the first to call attention to the great lowland drained by Tokichitna River and to the low divide which separates it from the Kuskokwim drainage, later explored by Spurr, Herron, and the writer.

— [Alfred H. Brooks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Hulse_Brooks), "The Mount McKinley Region, Alaska", *Department of the Interior, United States Geological Survey, Professional Paper 70*[^cite_note-Brooks1911-29]

The United States formally recognized the name Mount McKinley after President [Woodrow Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodrow_Wilson) signed the Mount McKinley National Park Act of February 26, 1917.[^cite_note-30] In 1965, President [Lyndon B. Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson) declared the north and south peaks of the mountain the "Churchill Peaks", in honor of British statesman [Winston Churchill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winston_Churchill).[^cite_note-31] The Alaska Board of Geographic Names changed the state name of the mountain to *Denali* in 1975, which was how it was referred to locally.[^cite_note-McKinley_Name-11][^cite_note-32] However, a request in 1975 from the Alaska state legislature to the [United States Board on Geographic Names](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Board_on_Geographic_Names) to do the same at the federal level was blocked by Ohio congressman [Ralph Regula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Regula), whose district included McKinley's home town of [Canton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canton,_Ohio).[^cite_note-monmonier-33]

On August 28, 2015, just ahead of a presidential visit to Alaska, the [Barack Obama administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidency_of_Barack_Obama) changed the mountain's official federal name to Denali,[^cite_note-DenaliNameChangeDOI-9] bringing it in line with the Alaska Geographic Board's designation.[^cite_note-denalirestored-10][^cite_note-34] [U.S. secretary of the interior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Secretary_of_the_Interior) [Sally Jewell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sally_Jewell) said the change had been "a long time coming".[^cite_note-ADNDenali-35] The renaming of the mountain received praise from Alaska's senior U.S. senator, Republican [Lisa Murkowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisa_Murkowski),[^cite_note-KNOM-36] who had previously introduced legislation to accomplish the name change,[^cite_note-LATimes0830-37] but it drew criticism from several politicians from President McKinley's home state of Ohio, such as [Governor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_governors_of_Ohio) [John Kasich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Kasich), [U.S. senator](24909346.md) [Rob Portman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rob_Portman), [U.S. House speaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speaker_of_the_United_States_House_of_Representatives) [John Boehner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Boehner), and [Representative](19468510.md) [Bob Gibbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Gibbs), who described Obama's action as "constitutional overreach" because he said an act of [Congress](31756.md) was required to rename the mountain.[^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40] The *[Alaska Dispatch News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaska_Dispatch_News)* reported that the secretary of the interior has authority under federal law to change geographic names when the Board of Geographic Names does not act on a naming request within a "reasonable" period of time. Jewell told the *Alaska Dispatch News* that "I think any of us would think that 40 years is an unreasonable amount of time."[^cite_note-Martinson2015-24]

In December 2024, [President-elect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President-elect_of_the_United_States) [Donald Trump](4848272.md) stated that he planned to revert the mountain's official federal name back to Mount McKinley during his [second term](78298108.md), in honor of President William McKinley. Trump had previously proposed changing the name in 2017, drawing opposition from Alaska's [Republican](32070.md) governor [Mike Dunleavy](40933016.md).[^cite_note-41] His 2017 proposal was strongly opposed by both Republican U.S. senators from Alaska, Murkowski and [Dan Sullivan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Sullivan_\(U.S._senator\)),[^cite_note-42] who, along with Alaska state senator [Scott Kawasaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scott_Kawasaki), a [Democrat](5043544.md), again expressed their preference for Denali in 2024.[^cite_note-43] On January 20, 2025, shortly after his [second inauguration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_inauguration_of_Donald_Trump), Trump signed an [executive order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restoring_Names_That_Honor_American_Greatness) requiring the secretary of the interior to revert the Obama-era name change within 30 days of signing, renaming Denali back to Mount McKinley in official maps and communications from the American federal government.[^cite_note-\:7-44] The executive order does not change the name of [Denali National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denali_National_Park).[^cite_note-45] On January 23, 2025, the [Department of the Interior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_the_Interior) changed the mountain's official federal name back to Mount McKinley.[^cite_note-\:8-12][^cite_note-Friedman2025-6] The same day, the [Associated Press](18935732.md) announced that it would use Mount McKinley instead of Denali, with the reasoning that as president, Trump has the authority to change federal geographical names of features lying within national borders.[^cite_note-46]

[Hudson Stuck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hudson_Stuck), the organizer of the Stuck-Karstens Expedition, which was the first confirmed team to summit the mountain, preferred the name Denali over McKinley, referring to it as Denali nearly exclusively in his account of the expedition.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStuck1914vii-47]

According to the [Alaska Department of Commerce, Community, and Economic Development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaska_Department_of_Commerce,_Community,_and_Economic_Development)'s official database of [business licenses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_license), businesses named after Denali outnumber those named after McKinley by a margin of six to one.[^cite_note-48]

Indigenous names for Denali can be found in seven different Alaskan languages. The names fall into two categories. To the south of the Alaska Range in the [Dena'ina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dena'ina_language) and [Ahtna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahtna_language) languages the mountain is known by names that are translated as 'big mountain'. To the north of the Alaska Range in the [Lower Tanana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Tanana_language), [Koyukon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koyukon_language), [Upper Kuskokwim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Kuskokwim_language), [Holikachuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holikachuk_language), and [Deg Xinag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deg_Xinag_language) languages the mountain is known by names that are translated as 'the high one',[^cite_note-49] 'the tall one' (Koyukon, Lower and Middle Tanana, Upper Kuskokwim, Deg Xinag, and Holikachuk).[^cite_note-karijamden-50]

Asked about the importance of the mountain and its name, Will Mayo, former president of the [Tanana Chiefs Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanana_Chiefs_Conference), an organization that represents 42 Athabaskan tribes in the Alaskan interior, said: "It's not one homogeneous belief structure around the mountain, but we all agree that we're all deeply gratified by the acknowledgment of the importance of Denali to Alaska's people."[^cite_note-mayochiefsconference-51]

## History

In a grayscale photograph, two men stand in front of a tent and snowy evergreen trees

[Hudson Stuck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hudson_Stuck) and [Harry Karstens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Karstens), co-leaders of the first successful expedition of four to reach the summit of Denali in 1913, the other members of the expedition being Robert G. Tatum and Walter Harper

The [Koyukon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koyukon) [Athabaskans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athabaskan_languages), living in the [Yukon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yukon_River), [Tanana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanana_River) and [Kuskokwim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuskokwim_River) basins, were the first Native Americans with access to the flanks of the mountain.[^cite_note-gnis-4] A British naval captain and explorer, [George Vancouver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Vancouver), is the first [European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europeans) on record to have sighted Denali, when he noted "distant stupendous mountains" while surveying the [Knik Arm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knik_Arm) of the [Cook Inlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cook_Inlet) on May 6, 1794.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBeckey199342-52] The Russian explorer [Lavrenty Zagoskin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lavrenty_Zagoskin) explored the Tanana and Kuskokwim rivers in 1843 and 1844, and was likely the first European to sight the mountain from the other side.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBeckey199344-53]

William Dickey, a [New Hampshire](21134.md)-born resident of [Seattle](11388236.md), Washington, who had been digging for gold in the sands of the [Susitna River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susitna_River), wrote, after his returning from Alaska, an account in the New York *[Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sun_\(New_York_City\))* that appeared on January 24, 1897.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBeckey199347-54] His report drew attention with the sentence "We have no doubt that this peak is the highest in North America, and estimate that it is over 20,000 feet (6,100 m) high." Until then, [Mount Logan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Logan) in Canada's [Yukon Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yukon_Territory) was believed to be the continent's highest point. Though later praised for his estimate, Dickey admitted that other prospector parties had also guessed the mountain to be over 20,000 feet (6,100 m).[^cite_note-55] These estimates were confirmed in 1898 by the surveyor [Robert Muldrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Muldrow), who measured its elevation as 20,300 feet (6,200 m).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStuck1914159-56]

On November 5, 2012, the [United States Mint](358818.md) released a [twenty-five cent piece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarter_\(United_States_coin\)) depicting Denali National Park. It is the fifteenth of the [America the Beautiful Quarters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/America_the_Beautiful_Quarters) series. The [reverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obverse_and_reverse) features a [Dall sheep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dall_sheep) with the peak of Denali in the background.[^cite_note-parkquarters-57]

### Climbing history

During the summer of 1902 scientist [Alfred Brooks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Hulse_Brooks) explored the flanks of the mountain as a part of an exploratory surveying party conducted by the [U.S. Geological Survey](23814944.md). The party landed at Cook Inlet in late May, then traveled east, paralleling the Alaska Range, before reaching the slopes of Denali in early August. Camped on the flank of the mountain on August 3, Brooks noted later that while "the ascent of Mount McKinley had never been part of our plans", the party decided to delay one day so "that we might actually set foot on the slopes of the mountain". Setting off alone, with good weather, on August 4, Brooks aimed to reach a 10,000 feet (3,048 m) shoulder. At 7,500 feet (2,286 m), Brooks found his way blocked by sheer ice and, after leaving a small cairn as a marker, descended.[^cite_note-58] After the party's return, Brooks co-authored a "Plan For Climbing Mt McKinley", published in *[National Geographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Geographic)* magazine in January 1903, with fellow party-member and topographer D. L. Raeburn, in which they suggested that future attempts at the summit should approach from the north, not the south.[^cite_note-59] The report received substantial attention, and within a year, two climbing parties declared their intent to summit.[^cite_note-Sfraga1997-60]

During the early summer of 1903, Judge [James Wickersham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Wickersham), then of [Eagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eagle,_Alaska), Alaska, made the first recorded attempt to climb Denali, along with a party of four others. The group attempted to get as close to the mountain as possible via the [Kantishna river](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kantishna_River) by [steamer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steamboat), before offloading and following Chitsia Creek with a poling boat, mules and backpacks, a route suggested to them by [Tanana Athabaskan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanana_Athabaskans) people they met along the way. The party received further navigational assistance at Anotoktilon, an Athabaskan hunting camp, where residents gave the group detailed directions to reach the glaciers at the foot of Denali. On reaching the mountain, the mountaineers set up base camp on the lower portion of Peters Glacier. Aiming for the northwest buttress of Denali's north peak, they attempted to ascend directly; however, [crevasses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crevasse), ice fall and the lack of a clear passage caused them to turn and attempt to follow a spur via [Jeffery Glacier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeffery_Glacier) where they believed they could see a way to the summit. After a dangerous ascent, at around 10,000 feet (3,048 m), Wickersham found that the route did not connect as it had appeared from below, instead discovering "a tremendous precipice beyond which we cannot go. Our only line of further ascent would be to climb the vertical wall of the mountain at our left, and that is impossible." This wall, now known as the Wickersham Wall, juts 15,000 feet (4,572 m) upwards from the glacier to the north peak of Denali.[^cite_note-\:0-61] Because of the route's history of avalanche danger, it was not successfully climbed until 1963.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBeckey1993139-62]

Later in the summer of 1903, Dr. [Frederick Cook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Cook) directed a team of five men on another attempt at the summit. Cook was already an experienced explorer and had been a party-member on successful arctic expeditions commanded both by [Robert Peary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Peary) and [Roald Amundsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roald_Amundsen).[^cite_note-\:0-61][^cite_note-\:1-63] Yet he struggled to obtain funding for his own expedition, eventually organizing it "on a shoestring budget"[^cite_note-\:2-64] without any other experienced climbers.[^cite_note-\:1-63] The party navigated up the Cook inlet and followed the path of the 1902 Brooks party towards Denali. Cook approached the mountain via the Peters Glacier, as Wickersham had done; however, he was able to overcome the ice fall that had caused the previous group to turn up the spur towards the Wickersham Wall. Despite avoiding this obstacle, on August 31, having reached an elevation of about 10,900 feet (3,322 m) on the northwest buttress of the north peak, the party found they had reached a dead end and could make no further progress. On the descent, the group completely circumnavigated the mountain, the first climbing party to do so.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBeckey1993295-65] Although Cook's 1903 expedition did not reach the summit, he received acclaim for the accomplishment – a 1,000-mile (1,609 km) trek in which he not only circled the entire mountain but also found, on the descent, an accessible pass northeast of the Muldrow Glacier following the headwaters of the [Toklat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toklat_River) and [Chulitna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chulitna_River_\(Susitna_River\)) rivers.[^cite_note-\:0-61]

In 1906, Cook initiated another expedition to Denali with co-leader [Herschel Parker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herschel_Parker), a [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University) professor of electrical engineering with extensive mountaineering experience. [Belmore Browne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belmore_Browne), an experienced climber and five other men comprised the rest of the group. Cook and Parker's group spent most of the summer season exploring the southern and southeastern approaches to the mountain, eventually reaching a high point on [Tokositna glacier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokositna_Glacier), 25 miles (40 km) from the summit.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBeckey1993295-65] During their explorations the party mapped out many of the tributaries and glaciers of the Susitna river along the mountain's south flank.[^cite_note-\:0-61] As the summer ended, the team retreated to the coast and began to disperse. In September 1906, Cook and a single party-member, horseman Robert Barrill, journeyed towards the summit again, in what Cook later described as "a last desperate attempt" in a telegram to his financial backers.[^cite_note-\:0-61] Cook and Barrill spent 12 days in total on the attempt, and claimed to have reached the summit via the Ruth Glacier.[^cite_note-\:2-64]

Upon hearing Cook's claims, Parker and Browne were immediately suspicious. Browne later wrote that he knew Cook's claims were lies, just as "any New Yorker would know that no man could walk from the Brooklyn Bridge to Grant's Tomb \[a distance of eight miles] in ten minutes."[^cite_note-\:2-64] In May 1907, *[Harper's Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harper's_Magazine)* published Cook's account of the climb along with a photograph of what appeared to be Barrill standing on the summit. By 1909, Barrill had recanted at least part of his story about the climb, and others publicly questioned the account; however, Cook continued to assert his claim.[^cite_note-66] The controversy continued for decades. In 1956, mountaineers [Bradford Washburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bradford_Washburn) and Walter Gonnason tried to settle the matter, with Gonnason attempting to follow Cook's purported route to the summit. Washburn noted inconsistencies between Cook's account of locations of glaciers and found a spot, at 5,400 feet (1,646 m) and 19 miles (31 km) southeast of the summit that appeared identical to the supposed summit image. Gonnason was not able to complete the climb, but because he was turned back by poor weather, felt that this did not definitely disprove Cook's story.[^cite_note-67] In 1998, historian Robert Bryce discovered an original and un-cropped version of the ["fake peak"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fake_Peak) photograph of Barrill standing on the promontory. It showed a wider view of surrounding features, appearing to definitively discount Cook's claim.[^cite_note-68]

Several tents are pitched near the edge of a snow-covered cliff

High camp (17,200 ft or 5,200 m) of the West Buttress Route pioneered by [Bradford Washburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bradford_Washburn), photographed in 2001

Given the skepticism concerning Cook's story, interest in claiming the first ascent remained. Miners and other Alaskans living in Kantishna and Fairbanks wanted the honors to go to local men. In 1909, four Alaska residents – Tom Lloyd, Peter Anderson, Billy Taylor, and Charles McGonagall – set out from [Fairbanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairbanks,_Alaska), Alaska during late December with supplies and dogs that were in part paid for by bettors in a Fairbanks tavern. By March 1910, the men had established a base camp near one of the sites where the Brooks party had been and pressed on from the north via the Muldrow glacier. Unlike some previous expeditions, they discovered a pass, since named McGonagall Pass, which allowed them to bypass the Wickersham Wall and access the higher reaches of the mountain. At roughly 11,000 feet (3,353 m), Tom Lloyd, old and less physically fit than the others, stayed behind. According to their account, the remaining three men pioneered a route following Karstens Ridge around the Harper Icefall, then reached the upper basin before ascending to Pioneer Ridge. The three men carried a 14-foot-long (4.3 m) spruce pole. Around 19,000 feet (5,791 m), Charles McGonagall, older and having exhausted himself carrying the spruce pole, remained behind. On April 3, 1910, Billy Taylor and Peter Anderson scrambled the final few hundred feet to reach the north peak of Denali, at 19,470 feet (5,934 m) high, the shorter of the two peaks. The pair erected the pole near the top, with the hope that it would be visible from lower reaches to prove they had made it.[^cite_note-\:4-69]

After the expedition, Tom Lloyd returned to Fairbanks, while the three others remained in Kantishna to mine. In Lloyd's recounting, all four men made it to the top of not only the north peak, but the higher south peak as well. When the remaining three men returned to town with conflicting accounts, the entire expedition's legitimacy was questioned.[^cite_note-70] Several years later, another climbing group would claim to have seen the spruce pole in the distance, supporting their north peak claim.[^cite_note-\:4-69] However, some continue to doubt they reached the summit. Outside of the single later climbing group, who were friendly with some of the Sourdough expedition men, no other group would ever see it. [Jon Waterman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Waterman), author of the book *Chasing Denali*, which explored the controversy, outlined several reasons to doubt the claim: There was never any photographic evidence. The four men climbed during the winter season, known for much more difficult conditions, along a route that has never been fully replicated. They were inexperienced climbers, ascending without any of the usual safety gear or any care for altitude sickness. They claimed to have ascended from 11,000 feet (3,353 m) to the top in less than 18 hours, unheard of at a time when siege-style alpinism was the norm.[^cite_note-\:5-71] Yet Waterman says "these guys were men of the trail. They didn't care what anybody thought. They were just tough SOBs."[^cite_note-72] He noted that the men were largely unlettered and that some of the ensuing doubt was related to their lack of sophistication in dealing with the press and the contemporary climbing establishment.[^cite_note-\:5-71]

In 1912, the [Parker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herschel_Clifford_Parker)-Browne expedition nearly reached the summit, turning back within just a few hundred yards/meters of it due to harsh weather. On July 7, the day after their descent, a 7.4-magnitude earthquake shattered the glacier they had ascended.[^cite_note-73][^cite_note-74][^cite_note-75]

The first ascent of the main summit of Denali came on June 7, 1913, by a party directed by [Hudson Stuck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hudson_Stuck) and [Harry Karstens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Karstens), along with [Walter Harper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Harper) and [Robert Tatum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Tatum). Karstens relocated to Alaska in the gold rush of 1897, and in subsequent years became involved in a variety of endeavors beyond mining, including helping establish dog mushing routes to deliver mail across vast swathes of territory and supporting expeditions led by naturalist [Charles Sheldon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Alexander_Sheldon) near the base of Denali.[^cite_note-76] Stuck was an English-born Episcopal priest who came to Alaska by chance. He became acclimated to the often harsh Alaskan environment because of his many travels between far-flung outposts within his district, climbing mountains as a hobby.[^cite_note-\:6-77] At 21 years old, Harper was already known as a skilled and strong outdoorsman, the Alaska-born son of a Koyukon-Athabascan mother and Irish gold prospector father.[^cite_note-78][^cite_note-79] Tatum, also 21 years old, was a theology student working at a Tanana mission, and the least experienced of the team. His primary responsibility on the trip was as a cook.[^cite_note-80]

The team approached the peak from the north via the Muldrow glacier and McGonagall pass. While ferrying loads up to a camp at around 10,800 feet (3,292 m), they suffered a setback when a stray match accidentally set fire to some supplies, including several tents. The prior year's earthquake had left what had previously been described by the Parker-Browne expedition as a gentle slope ascended in no more than three days as a dangerous, ice-strewn morass on a knife-edged ridge (later named Karstens ridge). It would take the team three weeks to cover the same ground, as Karstens and Harper laboriously cut steps into the ice. On May 30, the team, with the help of some good weather, ascended to a new high camp, situated at 17,500 feet (5,334 m) in the Grand Basin between the north and south peaks. On June 7, the team made the summit attempt. Temperatures were below −20 °F (−29 °C) at times. Every man, and particularly Stuck, suffered from altitude sickness. By midday, Harper became the first climber to reach the summit, followed seconds later by Tatum and Karstens. Stuck arrived last, falling unconscious on the summit.[^cite_note-81]

Using the mountain's contemporary name, Tatum later commented, "The view from the top of Mount McKinley is like looking out the windows of Heaven!"[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECoombsWashburn199726-82] During the climb, Stuck spotted, via binoculars, the presence of a large pole near the North Summit; this report confirmed the Sourdough ascent, and it is widely believed presently that the Sourdoughs did succeed on the North Summit. However, the pole was never seen before or since, so there is still some doubt. Stuck also discovered that the Parker-Browne party were only about 200 feet (61 m) of elevation short of the true summit when they turned back. Stuck and Karstens' team achieved the uncontroversial first ascent of Denali's south peak; however, the news was met with muted interest by the wider climbing community. *Appalachia Journal*, then the official journal of the [American Alpine Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Alpine_Club), published a small notice of the accomplishment a year later.[^cite_note-\:6-77]

The mountain is climbed regularly nowadays. In 2003, around 58% of climbers reached the top. But by that time, the mountain had claimed the lives of nearly 100 mountaineers.[^cite_note-glickman-83] The vast majority of climbers use the West Buttress Route, pioneered in 1951 by Bradford Washburn,[^cite_note-roberts-13] after an extensive aerial photographic analysis of the mountain. Climbers typically take two to four weeks to ascend Denali. It is one of the [Seven Summits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Summits); summiting all of them is a challenge for mountaineers.

### Accidents

From 1947 to 2018 in the United States "2,799 people were reported to be involved in mountaineering accidents"[^cite_note-14_June_2022-84] and 11% of these accidents occurred on Denali.[^cite_note-14_June_2022-84] Of these 2,799 accidents, 43% resulted in death and 8% of these deaths occurred on Denali.[^cite_note-14_June_2022-84]

## Weather station

Denali seen through a green field

The east side viewed from Denali National Park and Preserve, which surrounds the mountain

The [Japanese Alpine Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_Alpine_Club) installed a meteorological station on a ridge near the summit of Denali at an elevation of 18,733 feet (5,710 m) in 1990.[^cite_note-rozell-117] In 1998, this weather station was donated to the [International Arctic Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Arctic_Research_Center) at the [University of Alaska Fairbanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Alaska_Fairbanks).[^cite_note-rozell-117] In June 2002, a weather station was placed at the 19,000-foot (5,800 m) level. This weather station was designed to transmit data in real-time for use by the climbing public and the science community. Since its establishment, annual upgrades to the equipment have been performed with instrumentation custom built for the extreme weather and altitude conditions. This weather station is the third-highest weather station in the world.[^cite_note-japantimes-118]

The weather station recorded a temperature of −75.5 °F (−59.7 °C) on December 1, 2003. On the previous day of November 30, 2003, a temperature of −74.4 °F (−59.1 °C) combined with a wind speed of 18.4 miles per hour (29.6 km/h) to produce a North American record windchill of −118.1 °F (−83.4 °C).

Even in July, this weather station has recorded temperatures as low as −22.9 °F (−30.5 °C) and windchills as low as −59.2 °F (−50.7 °C).

### Historical record

According to the [National Park Service](161535.md), in 1932 the Liek-Lindley expedition recovered a [self-recording minimum thermometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six's_thermometer) left near Browne's Tower, at about 15,000 feet (4,600 m), on Denali by the Stuck-Karstens party in 1913. The spirit thermometer was calibrated down to −95 °F (−71 °C), and the lowest recorded temperature was below that point. Harry J. Liek took the thermometer back to Washington, D.C. where it was tested by the [United States Weather Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Weather_Bureau) and found to be accurate. The lowest temperature that it had recorded was found to be approximately −100 °F (−73 °C).[^cite_note-dixon-119] Another thermometer was placed at the 15,000-foot (4,600 m) level by the [U.S. Army Natick Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combat_Capabilities_Development_Command_Soldier_Center), and was there from 1950 to 1969. The lowest temperature recorded during that period was also −100 °F (−73 °C).[^cite_note-120]

## Subpeaks and nearby mountains

The top of Denali is shrouded in clouds

Denali, here shrouded in clouds, is large enough to create its own localized weather.

Besides the North Summit mentioned above, other features on the massif which are sometimes included as separate peaks are:

- South Buttress, 15,885 feet (4,842 m); mean prominence: 335 feet (102 m)

- East Buttress high point, 14,730 feet (4,490 m); mean prominence: 380 feet (120 m)

- East Buttress, most topographically prominent point, 14,650 feet (4,470 m); mean prominence: 600 feet (180 m)

- Browne Tower, 14,530 feet (4,430 m); mean prominence: 75 feet (23 m)

## Taxonomic honors



Ice sheets on Denali

- *denaliensis*&#x20;

  - *[Ceratozetella denaliensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceratozetella_denaliensis)* (formerly *Cyrtozetes denaliensis* Behan-Pelletier, 1985) is a species of [moss mite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oribatida) in the family Mycobatidae

  - *[Magnoavipes denaliensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnoavipes_denaliensis)* Fiorillo et al., 2011 (literally "bird with large feet found in Denali") is a *[Magnoavipes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnoavipes)* [ichnospecies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ichnospecies) of bird footprint from the Upper Cretaceous of Alaska and was a large heron-like bird (as large as a [sandhill crane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandhill_crane)) with three toes and toe pads.

- *denali*&#x20;

  - *[Cosberella denali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosberella_denali)* (Fjellberg, 1985) is a [springtail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Springtail) in the genus *[Cosberella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosberella)*.

  - *[Proclossiana aphirape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proclossiana_aphirape) denali* Klots, 1940 is a *[Boloria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boloria)* butterfly subspecies in the family [Nymphalidae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nymphalidae).

  - *[Symplecta denali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplecta_denali)* (Alexander, 1955) is a species of crane fly in the family [Limoniidae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limoniidae).

  - *[Tipula denali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tipula_denali)* Alexander, 1969 is a species of crane fly in the family [Tipulidae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tipulidae).

- *denalii*&#x20;

  - *[Erigeron denalii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erigeron_denalii)* A. Nelson, 1945 or [Denali fleabane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denali_fleabane) is an *[Erigeron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erigeron)* fleabane species.

- *mckinleyensis* or *mackinleyensis*&#x20;

  - *[Erebia mackinleyensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erebia_mackinleyensis)* (Gunder, 1932) or [Mt. McKinley alpine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mt._McKinley_alpine) is a butterfly species of the subfamily [Satyrinae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satyrinae) of family [Nymphalidae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nymphalidae).

  - *[Oeneis mackinleyensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oeneis_mackinleyensis)* Dos Passos 1965 or *[Oeneis mckinleyensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oeneis_mckinleyensis)* Dos Passos 1949 is a butterfly species of the subfamily [Satyrinae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satyrinae) of family [Nymphalidae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nymphalidae) (synonym of *[Oeneis bore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oeneis_bore)*)

  - *[Uredo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uredo) mckinleyensis* Cummins 1952 (sometimes spelled *Uredo mackinleyensis*) is a [rust fungus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rust_\(fungus\)) species.

- others:&#x20;

  - *[Papaver mcconnellii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papaver_mcconnellii?action=edit\&redlink=1)* Hultén 1945, synonym *Papaver denalii* Gjaerevoll 1963, is a [poppy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poppy) species.
