Since 2024, [Sudan](27421.md) has been experiencing an [epidemic](66981.md) of [cholera](7591.md) that has also spread to neighbouring [Chad](5488.md) and [South Sudan](32350676.md). It is reportedly the nation's worst cholera outbreak in years.[^cite_note-1] [UNICEF](19698110.md) has reported the epidemic is being exacerbated by the [Sudanese civil war](73556699.md).[^cite_note-2] [Khartoum](17320.md), [North Kordofan](767669.md), [White Nile](767622.md) and [Darfur](605837.md) were among the hardest-hit areas.[^cite_note-sudrep-3] The cholera epidemic in Sudan is part of a wider outbreak; as of 31 August 2025, 462,890 cases and 5,869 deaths had been reported from 32 countries across five WHO regions.[^cite_note-4] By December 2025, Sudan and Chad had recorded 127,248 suspected cholera cases and 3,522 deaths across all 23 [states](579458.md).[^cite_note-sudrep-3] By August 2026, 191,213 cholera cases and 2,772 deaths across all 61 counties in Sudan, Chad, and South Sudan.[^cite_note-5]

## Background

[Cholera](7591.md) is a disease resulting from a bacterial infection and most often transmitted through polluted water. The illness leads to intense [diarrhoea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diarrhoea) and rapid loss of fluids. Without timely treatment it can be fatal within a few hours, even in people who were in good health beforehand.[^cite_note-whahas-6] Cholera has a long history in Sudan, with repeated outbreaks linked to conflict, environmental conditions and insufficient [WASH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WASH) and health infrastructure.[^cite_note-acaps-7]

[The war in Sudan](73556699.md) created conditions that allowed the disease to spread quickly. Water and sewage systems have been damaged or destroyed, and many medical facilities have been closed due to a lack of supplies.[^cite_note-whahas-6] By November 2025, more than 11.7 million people have been [displaced](477535.md), including around 7.26 million people within Sudan,[^cite_note-8] forcing many into overcrowded areas with very poor sanitation.[^cite_note-whahas-6] Limited access to clean water, open defecation and poor waste management make faecal contamination of water supplies common, enabling the bacteria to circulate rapidly.[^cite_note-brinot-9] The heavy rain season in 2025 further worsened conditions by flooding roads, slowing aid deliveries and leaving communities without clean water. These combined factors created an environment in which cholera could move rapidly through the population.[^cite_note-whahas-6]

Surveillance of the epidemic was hindered by security constraints and limited access, which also disrupted vaccine delivery and routine immunisation. Cholera vaccines provided temporary protection during the outbreak but could not address the underlying drivers of transmission, and were intended mainly to buy time while improvements to water and sanitation systems were pursued.[^cite_note-acaps-7]

## History

In August 2023, three suspected cholera cases were detected in [Al Qadarif State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Qadarif_State) in Sudan, but financial constraints and a concurrent [dengue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dengue) outbreak hindered a proper response.[^cite_note-10]

In August 2024, cholera was declared in Al Qadarif, [Kassala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kassala) and [River Nile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Nile) states. By mid-December, more than 47,000 cases had been recorded across 81 localities in 11 of Sudan’s 18 states, with about 1,235 deaths and a [case fatality rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_fatality_rate) of 2.6 percent, well above the WHO benchmark of under 1 percent. Because access to many areas is limited and reporting is constrained, actual numbers are believed to be higher, with reduced community referrals further contributing to undercounting.[^cite_note-acaps-7]

In October 2024, a cholera outbreak was declared in [South Sudan](32350676.md),[^cite_note-11] with the epidemic centred in [Khartoum](17320.md).[^cite_note-12] As of August 2025, Khartoum State continued to be the most affected area,[^cite_note-contow-13] yet the epidemic had spread throughout Sudan's refugee camps,[^cite_note-14] and vaccination programmes had been rolled out.[^cite_note-15]

In October 2025, [CARE International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CARE_International) reported that Sudan’s cholera outbreak was accelerating, with infection and [fatality rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_fatality_rate) that far exceeded the emergency threshold.[^cite_note-16] The organization noted that the collapse of health services had left more than three-quarters of facilities destroyed, severely limiting the response capacity. Shortages of medical supplies, chlorine and clean water, combined with insecurity and insufficient funding, further deepened the crisis as the rainy season continued.[^cite_note-17]

Cholera infections were highest among young adults and adolescents, with the 20–29 age group reporting the greatest number of infections, followed by those aged 10–19. Fatalities, however, were concentrated at both ends of the age spectrum: people aged 70 and above recorded the most deaths, closely followed by children under five. There was no notable disparity between male and female cases or deaths.[^cite_note-contow-13] Malnourished children, especially those with severe acute [malnutrition](258979.md), faced a much higher risk of serious complications from cholera.[^cite_note-brinot-9]

By October 2025, the outbreak in South Sudan had become the worst in the country’s history, with 95,450 reported cases and 1,587 deaths, resulting in a case fatality rate of 1.7%.[^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19]

By November 2025, Sudan had recorded 124,269 suspected cholera cases and 3,355 deaths across all 18 states. Of these, 71,373 suspected cases and 1,996 deaths were reported in 2025, the highest numbers in Africa during that period. Khartoum, [North Kordofan](767669.md), [White Nile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Nile) and [Darfur](605837.md) were among the hardest-hit areas.[^cite_note-sudrep-3] Although case numbers had fallen by about 44 percent over the previous four weeks, overall vulnerability remained high because of the ongoing conflict and collapsed infrastructure.[^cite_note-sudrep-3]

The [funding cuts by USAID](82201872.md) starting in 2025 weakened the cholera response in South Sudan during the ongoing epidemic. They led to disrupted surveillance, the rationing of treatment, and the loss of essential services in affected communities. According to humanitarian and former US officials, these reductions coincided with a reversal of declining case trends and contributed to a renewed surge in cholera cases and preventable deaths.[^cite_note-20]

On 17 June 2026, the TASIS-aligned Peace Government officially declared a [cholera](7591.md) outbreak in the [Kordofan](1127161.md) region, with laboratory tests in [South Sudan](32350676.md) confirming the disease, after weeks of [acute watery diarrhoea cases](53951.md#Definition) in [West Kordofan](767647.md).[^cite_note-21] Later in June, on 29 June, the Sudanese [health ministry](83226660.md) and [World Health Organization](33583.md) [chief](63815422.md) [Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus](39712205.md) declared a new cholera outbreak in [West Kordofan](767647.md) [state](579458.md), four months after declaring an end to a previous outbreak that started in July 2024.[^cite_note-22][^cite_note-23][^cite_note-24]

## Effect on health care

By January 2025, the cholera epidemic had placed an additional burden on Sudan’s wider humanitarian response, overwhelming a health system already strained by increasing child malnutrition, war injuries and other preventable diseases. The outbreak led health actors to redirect limited medical resources toward cholera treatment, reducing the capacity to address illnesses such as [malaria](20423.md) and [dengue fever](39669.md), which continued to pose major risks. Response efforts were further hampered by the deterioration and understaffing of health facilities in conflict-affected areas, as well as by critical shortages of cholera kits and rehydration supplies.[^cite_note-acaps-7]
