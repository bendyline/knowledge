**Oklahoma** ([/ˌoʊkləˈhoʊmə/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/c/c9/En-us-Oklahoma.ogg/En-us-Oklahoma.ogg.mp3](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/c/c9/En-us-Oklahoma.ogg/En-us-Oklahoma.ogg.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup> [*OH-klə-HOH-mə*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key);[^cite_note-18] [Choctaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choctaw_language): *Oklahumma*, pronounced [\[oklahómma\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA))[^cite_note-19], is a landlocked [state](18618239.md) in the [South Central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Central_United_States), [Southern](179553.md), and [Southwestern](179571.md) regions of the [United States](3434750.md).[^cite_note-regdiv-20] It borders [Texas](29810.md) to the southwest, [Kansas](16716.md) to the north, [Missouri](19571.md) to the northeast, [Arkansas](1930.md) to the southeast, [New Mexico](21649.md) to the west, and [Colorado](5399.md) to the northwest. Partially in the western extreme of the [Upland South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upland_South), it is the [20th-most extensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_area) and the [28th-most populous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_population) of the 50 United States. Its residents are known as Oklahomans, and its capital and largest city is [Oklahoma City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_City).

The state's name is derived from the [Choctaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choctaw_language) words *[okla](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/okla#Choctaw)*, 'people' and *humma*, which translates as 'red'.[^cite_note-cno-21] Oklahoma is also known informally by its [nickname](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U.S._state_and_territory_nicknames), "The Sooner State", in reference to the [Sooners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sooners), [American settlers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_pioneer) who staked their claims in formerly American Indian-owned lands until the [Indian Appropriations Act of 1889](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Appropriations_Act#1889_Act) authorized the [Land Rush of 1889](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_Rush_of_1889) opening the land to settlement.

With ancient mountain ranges, prairie, [mesas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesa), and eastern forests, most of Oklahoma lies in the [Great Plains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Plains), [Cross Timbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_Timbers), and the [U.S. Interior Highlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Interior_Highlands), all regions prone to severe weather.[^cite_note-Oklahoma_Terrain-22] Oklahoma is at a confluence of three major American [cultural regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_area). Historically, it served as a government-sanctioned [territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territories_of_the_United_States) for American Indians moved from east of the Mississippi River, a route for cattle drives from Texas and related regions, and a destination for Southern settlers. There are currently 25 [Indigenous languages spoken in Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category\:Indigenous_languages_of_Oklahoma).[^cite_note-languages-23] According to the 2020 U.S. census, 14.2 percent of Oklahomans identify as [American Indians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_Americans_in_the_United_States), the highest Indigenous population by percentage out of any state.[^cite_note-24]

A major producer of natural gas, oil, and agricultural products, Oklahoma relies on an economic base of aviation, energy, telecommunications, and biotechnology.[^cite_note-Oklahoma's_Economy_1-25] Oklahoma City and [Tulsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulsa,_Oklahoma) serve as Oklahoma's primary economic anchors, with nearly two-thirds of Oklahomans living within their [metropolitan statistical areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_statistical_area).[^cite_note-metros-26]

## Etymology

The name *Oklahoma* comes from the [Choctaw language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choctaw_language) phrase *okla*, 'people', and *humma*, translated as 'red'.[^cite_note-cno-21][^cite_note-27] [Choctaw Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choctaw_Nation) Chief [Allen Wright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allen_Wright) suggested the name in 1865 during [treaty negotiations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Smith_Council) with the [federal US government](195149.md) on the use of [Indian Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Territory). He envisioned an [all exclusive American Indian state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_barrier_state) controlled by the [United States bureau of Indian Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_of_Indian_Affairs). A painting of the 1865 Council now hangs in the Oklahoma Senate.[^cite_note-28] *Oklahoma* later became the de facto name for [Oklahoma Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_Territory), and it was officially approved in 1890, two years after that area was opened to [American settlers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_pioneer).[^cite_note-Oklahoma's_Name-29][^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31]

## History

Main article: [History of Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Oklahoma)

### Pre-Columbian

[Indigenous peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples) were present in what is now Oklahoma by the [last ice age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternary_glaciation).[^cite_note-32] Ancestors of the [Wichita and Affiliated Tribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wichita_people) (including [Teyas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teyas_Indians) and [Escanjaques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escanjaque_Indians) and [Tawakoni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tawakoni)), [Tonkawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonkawa),[^cite_note-33] and [Caddo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caddo) (including [Kichai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kichai_people)) lived in what is now Oklahoma. [Southern Plains villagers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Plains_villagers) lived in the central and west of the state, with a subgroup, the [Panhandle culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panhandle_culture) people, living in the panhandle region. [Caddoan Mississippian culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caddoan_Mississippian_culture) peoples lived in the eastern part of the state. [Spiro Mounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiro_Mounds), in what is now [Spiro, Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiro,_Oklahoma), was a major [Mississippian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippian_culture) mound complex that flourished between AD 850 and 1450.[^cite_note-34][^cite_note-35] [Plains Apache](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plains_Apache) people settled in the Southern Plains and in Oklahoma between 1300 and 1500.[^cite_note-36]

### European exploration and colonization

The expedition of Spaniard [Francisco Vázquez de Coronado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_V%C3%A1zquez_de_Coronado) traveled through the area in 1541,[^cite_note-OK_History-37] but French explorers claimed the area in the early 18th century.[^cite_note-38] By the 18th century, Comanche and [Kiowa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiowa) entered the region from the west and Quapaw and Osage peoples moved into what is now eastern Oklahoma. French colonists claimed the region until 1803, when all the French territory west of the Mississippi River was acquired by the United States in the [Louisiana Purchase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_Purchase).[^cite_note-OK_History-37] The territory was a part of the [Arkansas Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arkansas_Territory) from 1819 until 1828.[^cite_note-39]

### 19th century

During the 19th century, the [U.S. federal government](195149.md) forcibly removed tens of thousands of American Indians from their ancestral homelands from across North America and transported them to the area including and surrounding present-day Oklahoma. The Choctaw was the first of the [Five Civilized Tribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Civilized_Tribes) to be removed from the [Southeastern United States](816925.md). The phrase "[Trail of Tears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trail_of_Tears)" originated from a description of the removal of the [Choctaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choctaw) Nation in 1831, although the term is usually used for the [Cherokee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherokee) removal.[^cite_note-len_green-40]

Seventeen thousand Cherokees and 2,000 of their black slaves were deported.[^cite_note-41] The area, already occupied by [Osage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osage_Nation) and [Quapaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quapaw) tribes, was called for the [Choctaw Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choctaw_Nation_of_Oklahoma) until revised Native American and then later American policy redefined the boundaries to include other Native Americans. By 1890, more than 30 Native American nations and tribes had been concentrated on land within [Indian Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Territory) or "Indian Country".[^cite_note-42]

All Five Civilized Tribes signed treaties with the Confederate military during the [American Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War).[^cite_note-43] The [Cherokee Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherokee_Nation_\(1794%E2%80%931907\)) had an internal [civil war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creek_War).[^cite_note-Native_American_Participation_Numbers-44] Slavery in Indian Territory was not abolished until 1866.[^cite_note-45]

Between 1866 and 1899,[^cite_note-OK_History-37] cattle ranches in Texas strove to meet the demands for food in eastern cities and railroads in Kansas promised to deliver in a timely manner. [Cattle trails](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cattle_drive) and cattle ranches developed as [cowboys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cowboy) either drove their product north or settled illegally in Indian Territory.[^cite_note-OK_History-37] In 1881, four of five major cattle trails on the western frontier traveled through Indian Territory.[^cite_note-46]



Indian reservations in Oklahoma before the Dawes Act of 1887.

As white settlers arrived in Indian Territory and demanded land owned and guaranteed to Indian tribes by treaties, the U.S. government responded by enacting the [Dawes Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dawes_Act) in 1887 and the [Curtis Act of 1898](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtis_Act_of_1898). The acts abolished tribal governments, eliminated tribal ownership of land, and allotted 160 acres (65 ha) of land to each head of an Indian family. An objective of the acts was the forced assimilation of Indians into white society. Land not allotted to individual Indians was owned by the U.S. government and sold or distributed to settlers and railroads. The proceeds of the land sales were used to educate Indian children and advance the policy of assimilation. As a result of the two acts, about one-half of the land previously owned by Indian tribes was owned by whites by 1900.[^cite_note-47] Moreover, much of the land allotted to individual Indian heads of families became white-owned. Allottees often sold or were fraudulently deprived of their land.[^cite_note-Case-48]

The acquisition of tribal lands by the U.S. government led to [land runs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_run), also called *land rushes*, from 1887 and 1895. Major land runs, including the [Land Rush of 1889](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_Rush_of_1889), opened up millions of acres of formerly tribal lands to white settlement. The rushes began at precise times as each prospective settler literally raced with other prospective settlers to claim ownership of 160 acres (65 ha) of land under the [Homestead Act of 1862](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homestead_Act_of_1862). Usually land was claimed by settlers on a first come, first served basis.[^cite_note-49] Those who broke the rules by crossing the border into the territory before the official opening time were said to have been crossing the border *sooner*, leading to the term *[sooners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sooners)*, which eventually became the state's official nickname.[^cite_note-50] [George Washington Steele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington_Steele) was appointed the first governor of the territory of Oklahoma in 1890.

Attempts to create an all-Indian state named *Oklahoma* and a later attempt to create an all-Indian state named *[Sequoyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_of_Sequoyah)* failed, but the Sequoyah Statehood Convention of 1905 eventually laid the groundwork for the Oklahoma Statehood Convention, which took place two years later.[^cite_note-51] On June 16, 1906, Congress enacted [a statute authorizing the people of the Oklahoma and Indian Territories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_Enabling_Act), as well as [Arizona](21883824.md) and [New Mexico](21649.md), to form a constitution and state government in order to be admitted as a state.[^cite_note-52] On November 16, 1907, President [Theodore Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Roosevelt) issued [Presidential Proclamation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidential_proclamation_\(United_States\)) no. [780](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Proclamation%20780), establishing Oklahoma as the 46th state in the Union.[^cite_note-53]

The new state became a focal point for the emerging [oil industry](994887.md), as discoveries of oil pools prompted towns to grow rapidly in population and wealth. Tulsa eventually became known as the "[Oil Capital of the World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_Capital_of_the_World)" for most of the 20th century and oil investments fueled much of the state's early economy.[^cite_note-54] In 1927, Oklahoman businessman [Cyrus Avery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_Avery), known as the "Father of Route 66", began the campaign to create [U.S. Route 66](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Route_66). Using a stretch of highway from [Amarillo, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amarillo,_Texas) to Tulsa, Oklahoma to form the original portion of Highway 66, Avery spearheaded the creation of the [U.S. Highway 66 Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Highway_66_Association) to oversee the planning of Route 66, based in his hometown of Tulsa.[^cite_note-55]

In late September 1918, the first cases of the [Spanish flu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_flu) appeared in Oklahoma. Although public health authorities statewide had some early indication that the disease being reported on the East coast had traveled westward, the turmoil caused by the rapid increase in cases in Oklahoma quickly overwhelmed both health workers and local governing bodies. In Oklahoma City, shortages of both supplies and personnel were mitigated, in part, by the mobilization of the American Red Cross. Rough estimates based on contemporary reports indicate that approximately 100,000 people fell ill with the disease before the pandemic ebbed in 1919. Of those 100,000 cases, it is estimated that around 7,500 proved fatal, placing total mortality rates for the state at roughly 7.5%.[^cite_note-56]

Oklahoma also has a rich [African-American history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Americans_in_Oklahoma). Many Black towns, founded by the Freedmen of the Five Tribes during Reconstruction, thrived in the early 20th century with the arrival of Black [Exodusters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exodusters) who migrated from neighboring states, especially Kansas. The politician [Edward P. McCabe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_P._McCabe) encouraged Black settlers to come to what was then Indian Territory. McCabe discussed with President Theodore Roosevelt the possibility of making Oklahoma a majority-Black state.[^cite_note-57]

By the early 20th century, the [Greenwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenwood,_Tulsa) district of [Tulsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulsa,_Oklahoma) was one of the most prosperous African-American communities in the United States.[^cite_note-58] [Jim Crow laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Crow_laws) had established [racial segregation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_segregation) since before the start of the 20th century, but Tulsa's Black residents had created a thriving area.[^cite_note-59][^cite_note-60][^cite_note-\:0-61]

Social tensions were exacerbated by the revival of the [Ku Klux Klan](16779.md) after 1915. The [Tulsa race massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulsa_race_massacre) occurred in 1921, with White mobs attacking Black people and carrying out a [pogrom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pogrom) in Greenwood. In one of the costliest episodes of [racist violence in American history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_racial_violence_in_the_United_States), sixteen hours of rioting resulted in the destruction of 35 city blocks, \$1.8 million in property damage, and an estimated death toll of between 75 and 300 people.[^cite_note-62] By the late 1920s, the Ku Klux Klan had declined to negligible influence within the state.[^cite_note-63]

During the 1930s, parts of the state began to suffer from the consequences of poor farming practices. This period was known as the [Dust Bowl](59749.md), throughout which areas of Kansas, Texas, New Mexico, and [northwestern Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwestern_Oklahoma) were hampered by long periods of little rainfall, strong winds, abnormally high temperatures, and most notably, severe [dust storms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_storm) sending thousands of farmers into poverty and forcing them to relocate to more fertile areas of the western United States.[^cite_note-64] Over a twenty-year period ending in 1950, the state saw its only historical decline in population, dropping 6.9 percent as impoverished families migrated out of the state after the Dust Bowl.

[Soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_conservation) and [water conservation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_conservation) projects markedly changed practices in the state, leading to the construction of massive flood control systems and dams to supply water for domestic needs and agricultural irrigation.[^cite_note-Oklahoma_Terrain-22][^cite_note-65] As of 2024, Oklahoma had more than 4,700 dams, about 20% of all dams in the U.S.[^cite_note-66]

Murrah Federal Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_P._Murrah_Federal_Building), killed 168 people, including 19 children. ## Geography

Main article: [Geography of Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Oklahoma)

See also: [List of Oklahoma tri-points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Oklahoma_tri-points)



[Köppen climate types](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification) of Oklahoma

Oklahoma is the 20th-largest state in the United States, covering an area of 69,895 square miles (181,030 km<sup>2</sup>), with 68,591 square miles (177,650 km<sup>2</sup>) of land, and 1,304 square miles (3,380 km<sup>2</sup>) of water.[^cite_note-2010census-74] It lies partly in the [Great Plains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Plains) near the geographical center of the 48 [contiguous states](83759.md). It is bordered on the east by [Arkansas](1930.md) and [Missouri](19571.md), on the north by [Kansas](16716.md), on the northwest by [Colorado](5399.md), on the far west by [New Mexico](21649.md), and on the south and near-west by [Texas](29810.md).

### Borders

Oklahoma's border with Kansas was defined as the 37th Parallel in the 1854 [Kansas-Nebraska Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kansas%E2%80%93Nebraska_Act). This was disputed with the Cherokee and Osage Nations, which claimed their border extended north of this line and could not be part of the [Kansas Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kansas_Territory). This was resolved in 1870 with the [Drum Creek Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drum_Creek_Treaty), which reestablished Kansas's southern border as the 37th parallel. This also applied to the then No-Man's Land that became the [Oklahoma Panhandle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_Panhandle).

The Oklahoma-Texas border consists of the [Red River of the South](194242.md) in the south and the 100th meridian west as the western border between Oklahoma and the Texas Panhandle. These were first established in the 1819 [Adams–Onís Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adams%E2%80%93On%C3%ADs_Treaty) between the United States and Spain.

The Oklahoma panhandle was originally part of the Panhandle of the Republic of Texas, but when Texas joined the Union as a slave state, it could not retain any lands north of 36 degrees 30 minutes, as specified in the [Missouri Compromise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missouri_Compromise). The Panhandle existed as a no-man's land until 1907 when Oklahoma acquired the territory upon gaining statehood.

Oklahoma's Eastern border is divided between Missouri and Arkansas. The Missouri-Oklahoma border is defined as the Meridian passing through the Kawsmouth,[^cite_note-75] where the Kansas River meets the Missouri River. This is the same Meridian as the Kansas-Missouri border.

The Oklahoma-Arkansas border was originally defined by two lines: the borders between Arkansas and the Cherokee and Choctaw Reservations. This formed two diagonal lines meeting at the western edge of Fort Smith Arkansas, with one line running northeast from the Red River and the other running southeast from the Oklahoma-Arkansas-Missouri border. The Choctaw-Arkansas border was established in the 1820 [Treaty of Doak's Sand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Doak's_Stand), and later refined in the 1830 [Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Dancing_Rabbit_Creek). These treaties left a 57-acre [exclave](4848945.md) of the Choctaw reservation bounded by Arkansas, the Arkansas River and the Poteau River. This became the site of a smuggling camp called "Coke Hill", noted mostly for its importance in cocaine smuggling.[^cite_note-76]

After petitioning Congress to hand over jurisdiction, the 57 acres was given to Arkansas in 1905. The 1985 US Supreme Court Case Oklahoma v. Arkansas decided the land would remain Arkansas, even though the Choctaw had not been notified or asked about the territory being handed over.[^cite_note-77] Therefore, the Poteau River serves as the Oklahoma-Arkansas boundary for approximately 1 mile, reducing the Choctaw Reservation and later Oklahoma by 57 acres as established in the treaties of the early 1800s.

### Topography

See also: [List of lakes in Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_lakes_in_Oklahoma)

Oklahoma is between the [Great Plains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Plains) and the [Ozark Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozarks) in the [Gulf of Mexico](21076367.md) watershed,[^cite_note-Geography_Map-78] generally sloping from the high plains of its western boundary to the low wetlands of its southeastern boundary.[^cite_note-The_Geography_of_Oklahoma-79][^cite_note-Topography_of_Oklahoma-80] Its highest and lowest points follow this trend, with its highest peak, [Black Mesa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Mesa_\(Oklahoma\)), at 4,973 feet (1,516 m) above sea level, situated near its far northwest corner in the [Oklahoma Panhandle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_Panhandle). The state's lowest point is on the Little River near its far southeastern boundary near the town of [Idabel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idabel,_Oklahoma), which dips to 289 feet (88 m) above sea level.[^cite_note-The_Climate_of_Oklahoma-81]

Among the most geographically diverse states, Oklahoma is one of four to harbor more than 10 distinct [ecological regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecoregion), with 11 in its borders—more per square mile than in any other state.[^cite_note-Oklahoma_Terrain-22] Its western and eastern halves, however, are marked by extreme differences in geographical diversity: Eastern Oklahoma touches eight ecological regions and its western half contains three. Although having fewer ecological regions Western Oklahoma contains many rare, relic species.[^cite_note-Oklahoma_Terrain-22]

Oklahoma has four primary mountain ranges: the [Ouachita Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouachita_Mountains), the [Arbuckle Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arbuckle_Mountains), the [Wichita Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wichita_Mountains), and the [Ozark Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozarks).[^cite_note-The_Geography_of_Oklahoma-79] Contained within the [U.S. Interior Highlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Interior_Highlands) region, the Ozark and Ouachita Mountains are the only major mountainous region between the [Rocky Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocky_Mountains) and the [Appalachians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appalachian_Mountains).[^cite_note-82] A portion of the [Flint Hills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flint_Hills) stretches into north-central Oklahoma, and near the state's eastern border, The Oklahoma Tourism & Recreation Department regards [Cavanal Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavanal_Hill) as the world's tallest hill; at 1,999 feet (609 m), it fails their definition of a mountain by one foot.[^cite_note-TravelOK-83]

The [semi-arid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-arid_climate) [high plains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Plains_\(United_States\)) in the state's [northwestern corner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwestern_Oklahoma) harbor few natural forests; the region has a rolling to flat landscape with intermittent [canyons](78478.md) and [mesa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesa) ranges like the [Glass Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glass_Mountains). Partial plains interrupted by small, [sky island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky_island) mountain ranges like the [Antelope Hills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antelope_Hills,_Oklahoma) and the [Wichita Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wichita_Mountains) dot [southwestern Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwestern_Oklahoma); transitional prairie and [oak savannas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oak_savanna) cover the [central portion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Oklahoma) of the state. The Ozark and Ouachita Mountains rise from west to east over the state's eastern third, gradually increasing in elevation in an eastward direction.[^cite_note-Topography_of_Oklahoma-80][^cite_note-forests_1-84]

More than 500 named creeks and rivers make up Oklahoma's waterways, and with 200 lakes created by dams, it holds the nation's highest number of artificial reservoirs.[^cite_note-TravelOK-83] Most of the state lies in two primary [drainage basins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage_basin) belonging to the [Red](194242.md) and [Arkansas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arkansas_River) Rivers, though the Lee and Little Rivers also contain significant drainage basins.[^cite_note-forests_1-84]

### Flora and fauna

See also: [List of fauna of Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fauna_of_Oklahoma)



Populations of [American bison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_bison) inhabit Oklahoma's prairie ecosystems.

Due to Oklahoma's location at the confluence of many geographic regions, Oklahoma's climatic regions have a high rate of biodiversity. Forests cover 24 percent of Oklahoma[^cite_note-TravelOK-83] and [prairie grasslands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prairie) composed of shortgrass, mixed-grass, and [tallgrass prairie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tallgrass_prairie), harbor expansive ecosystems in the state's central and western portions, although [cropland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_land) has largely replaced native grasses.[^cite_note-ecology-85] Where rainfall is sparse in the state's western regions, shortgrass prairie and [shrublands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shrubland) are the most prominent ecosystems, though [pinyon pines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinyon_pine), red cedar ([junipers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juniper)), and [ponderosa pines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinus_ponderosa) grow near rivers and creek beds in the panhandle's far western reaches.[^cite_note-ecology-85] [Southwestern Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwestern_Oklahoma) contains many rare, [disjunct species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disjunct_distribution), including [sugar maple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acer_saccharum), [bigtooth maple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acer_grandidentatum), [nolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nolina), and [Texas live oak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quercus_fusiformis).[^cite_note-86]

[Marshlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsh), [cypress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cypress) forests, and mixtures of [shortleaf pine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinus_echinata), [loblolly pine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinus_taeda), [blue palmetto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabal_minor), and deciduous forests dominate the state's [southeastern quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiamichi_Country), while mixtures of largely [post oak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quercus_stellata), [elm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elm), red cedar (*[Juniperus virginiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juniperus_virginiana)*), and [pine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pine) forests cover [northeastern Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Country).[^cite_note-forests_1-84][^cite_note-ecology-85][^cite_note-OK_forests-87]



The scissor-tailed flycatcher (*Tyrannus forficatus*) is Oklahoma's State Bird

Oklahoma has populations of [white-tailed deer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White-tailed_deer), [mule deer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mule_deer), [antelope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pronghorn), [coyotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coyote), [mountain lions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cougar), [bobcats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobcat), [elk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elk), and birds such as [quail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quail), [doves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbidae), [cardinals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_cardinal), [bald eagles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bald_eagle), [red-tailed hawks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red-tailed_hawk), and [pheasants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pheasant). In prairie ecosystems, [American bison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_bison), [greater prairie-chickens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_prairie-chicken), [badgers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Badger), and [armadillo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armadillo) are common, and some of the nation's largest [prairie dog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prairie_dog) towns inhabit shortgrass prairie in the state's panhandle. The [Cross Timbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_Timbers), a region transitioning from prairie to woodlands in Central Oklahoma, harbors 351 [vertebrate species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertebrate). The Ouachita Mountains are home to [black bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_black_bear), [red fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_fox), [gray fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gray_fox), and [river otter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_river_otter) populations, which coexist with 328 vertebrate species in southeastern Oklahoma. Also in southeastern Oklahoma lives the [American alligator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_alligator).[^cite_note-ecology-85]

### Protected lands

Oklahoma has fifty-one [state parks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_park),[^cite_note-88] six [national parks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_park) or protected regions,[^cite_note-Oklahoma_national_parks-89] two [national protected forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_National_Forest) or [grasslands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grassland),[^cite_note-90] and a network of wildlife preserves and conservation areas. Six percent of the state's 10 million acres (40,000 km<sup>2</sup>) of forest is public land,[^cite_note-OK_forests-87] including the western portions of the [Ouachita National Forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouachita_National_Forest), the largest and oldest national forest in the [Southern United States](179553.md).[^cite_note-91]

With 39,000 acres (160 km<sup>2</sup>), the [Tallgrass Prairie Preserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tallgrass_Prairie_Preserve) in north-central Oklahoma is the largest protected area of [tallgrass prairie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tallgrass_prairie) in the world and is part of an [ecosystem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem) that encompasses only ten percent of its former land area, once covering fourteen states.[^cite_note-92] In addition, the [Black Kettle National Grassland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Kettle_National_Grassland) covers 31,300 acres (127 km<sup>2</sup>) of prairie in southwestern Oklahoma.[^cite_note-93] The [Wichita Mountains Wildlife Refuge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wichita_Mountains_Wildlife_Refuge) is the oldest and largest of nine [National Wildlife Refuges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Wildlife_Refuge) in the state[^cite_note-94] and was founded in 1901, encompassing 59,020 acres (238.8 km<sup>2</sup>).[^cite_note-95]

Of Oklahoma's federally protected parks or recreational sites, the [Chickasaw National Recreation Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chickasaw_National_Recreation_Area) is the largest, with 9,898.63 acres (4,005.83 ha).[^cite_note-96] Other sites include the [Santa Fe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Fe_Trail) and [Trail of Tears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trail_of_Tears) national historic trails, the [Fort Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Smith_National_Historic_Site) and [Washita Battlefield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washita_Battlefield_National_Historic_Site) national historic sites, and the [Oklahoma City National Memorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_City_National_Memorial).[^cite_note-Oklahoma_national_parks-89]

### Climate

Further information: [Climate change in Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_in_Oklahoma) and [Tornadoes in Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tornadoes_in_Oklahoma)



Oklahoma's climate is prime for the generation of [thunderstorms](70807.md).



Winter at the [Oklahoma Baptist University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_Baptist_University) campus



Most of Oklahoma has a [humid subtropical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humid_subtropical) climate.

Oklahoma is in a humid subtropical region[^cite_note-Climate_of_OK-97] that lies in a transition zone between semiarid further to the west, humid continental to the north, and humid subtropical to the east and southeast. Most of Oklahoma lies in an area known as [Tornado Alley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tornado_Alley) characterized by frequent interaction between cold, dry air from Canada, warm to hot, dry air from Mexico and the Southwestern U.S., and warm, moist air from the Gulf of Mexico. The interactions between these three contrasting air currents produces [severe weather](11593538.md) (severe thunderstorms, damaging thunderstorm winds, large hail and tornadoes) with a frequency virtually unseen anywhere else on planet Earth.[^cite_note-The_Climate_of_Oklahoma-81] An average 62 [tornadoes](37530.md) [strike the state per year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tornadoes_in_Oklahoma)—one of the highest rates in the world.[^cite_note-Annual_average_number_of_tornadoes-98]

Because of Oklahoma's position between zones of differing prevailing temperature and winds, weather patterns within the state can vary widely over relatively short distances, and they can change drastically in a short time.[^cite_note-The_Climate_of_Oklahoma-81] On November 11, 1911, the temperature at Oklahoma City reached 83 °F (28 °C) (the record high for that date), then [a cold front of unprecedented intensity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Blue_Norther_of_November_11,_1911) slammed across the state, causing the temperature to reach 17 °F (−8 °C) (the record low for that date) by midnight.[^cite_note-99] This type of phenomenon is also responsible for many of the tornadoes in the area, such as the [1912 Oklahoma tornado outbreak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tornado_outbreak_of_April_27%E2%80%9329,_1912) when a warm front traveled along a stalled cold front, resulting in an average of about one tornado per hour.[^cite_note-1912_tornadoes-100]

The [humid subtropical climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humid_subtropical_climate) (Köppen *Cfa*) of central, southern, and eastern Oklahoma is influenced heavily by southerly winds bringing moisture from the [Gulf of Mexico](21076367.md). Traveling westward, the climate transitions progressively toward a [semiarid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiarid) zone (Köppen *BSk*) in the high plains of the Panhandle and other western areas from about [Lawton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawton,_Oklahoma) westward, less frequently touched by southern moisture.[^cite_note-Climate_of_OK-97] Precipitation and temperatures decline from east to west. Areas in the southeast average an annual temperature of 62 °F (17 °C) and an annual rainfall of generally over 40 in (1,020 mm) and up to 56 in (1,420 mm). Areas of the higher-elevation panhandle average 58 °F (14 °C), with annual rainfall under 17 in (430 mm).[^cite_note-101]

Over almost all of Oklahoma, winter is the driest season. Average monthly precipitation increases dramatically in the spring, to a peak in May, the wettest month over most of the state, with its frequent and not uncommonly severe thunderstorm activity. Early June can still be wet, but most years see a marked decrease in rainfall during June and early July. Mid-summer (July and August) represents a secondary dry season over much of Oklahoma, with long stretches of hot weather with only sporadic thunderstorm activity not uncommon many years.[^cite_note-The_Climate_of_Oklahoma-81]

Severe drought is common in the hottest summers, such as those of 1934, 1954, 1980 and 2011, all of which featured weeks on end of virtual rainlessness and highs well over 100 °F (38 °C). Average precipitation rises again from September to mid-October, representing a secondary wetter season, then declines from late October through December.[^cite_note-The_Climate_of_Oklahoma-81]

The entire state frequently experiences temperatures above 100 °F (38 °C) or below 0 °F (−18 °C),[^cite_note-Climate_of_OK-97] though below-zero temperatures are rare in south-central and southeastern Oklahoma. Snowfall ranges from an average of less than 4 in (102 mm) in the south to just over 20 in (508 mm) on the border of [Colorado](5399.md) in the panhandle.[^cite_note-The_Climate_of_Oklahoma-81] Oklahoma is home to the [Storm Prediction Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storm_Prediction_Center), the [National Severe Storms Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Severe_Storms_Laboratory), the [Warning Decision Training Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warning_Decision_Training_Division), and the [Radar Operations Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_Operations_Center), all part of the [National Weather Service](286989.md) and in [Norman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman,_Oklahoma).[^cite_note-102]

### Cities and towns

See also: [List of cities in Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cities_in_Oklahoma)

Oklahoma had 598 incorporated places in 2010, including four cities over 100,000 in population and 43 over 10,000.[^cite_note-ok_commerce-105] Two of the [fifty largest cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_United_States_cities_by_population) in the United States are in Oklahoma, [Oklahoma City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_City) and [Tulsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulsa,_Oklahoma). Sixty-five percent of Oklahomans live within their metropolitan areas, or spheres of economic and social influence defined by the United States Census Bureau as a [metropolitan statistical area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_statistical_area). Oklahoma City, the state's capital and largest city, had the [largest metropolitan area in the state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_City_Metroplex) in 2020, with 1,425,695 people. The [metropolitan area of Tulsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulsa_Metropolitan_Area) had 1,015,331 residents.[^cite_note-106] Between 2000 and 2010, the leading cities in population growth were [Blanchard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blanchard,_Oklahoma) (172.4%), [Elgin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elgin,_Oklahoma) (78.2%), [Jenks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jenks,_Oklahoma) (77.0%), [Piedmont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piedmont,_Oklahoma) (56.7%), [Bixby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bixby,_Oklahoma) (56.6%), and [Owasso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Owasso,_Oklahoma) (56.3%).[^cite_note-ok_commerce-105]

In descending order of population, Oklahoma's largest cities in 2010 were: Oklahoma City (579,999, +14.6%), Tulsa (391,906, −0.3%), Norman (110,925, +15.9%), [Broken Arrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broken_Arrow,_Oklahoma) (98,850, +32.0%), Lawton (96,867, +4.4%), [Edmond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmond,_Oklahoma) (81,405, +19.2%), [Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore,_Oklahoma) (55,081, +33.9%), [Midwest City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midwest_City,_Oklahoma) (54,371, +0.5%), [Enid](130198.md) (49,379, +5.0%), and [Stillwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stillwater,_Oklahoma) (45,688, +17.0%). Of the state's ten largest cities, three are outside the metropolitan areas of Oklahoma City and Tulsa, and only Lawton has a metropolitan statistical area of its own as designated by the United States Census Bureau, though the metropolitan statistical area of [Fort Smith, Arkansas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Smith,_Arkansas) extends into the state.[^cite_note-Ok_cities-107]

Under Oklahoma law, municipalities are divided into two categories: cities, defined as having more than 1,000 residents, and towns, with under 1,000 residents. Both have [legislative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislature), [judicial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judiciary), and public power within their boundaries, but cities can choose between a [mayor–council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor%E2%80%93council_government), [council–manager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council%E2%80%93manager_government), or [strong mayor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor%E2%80%93council_government) form of government, while towns operate through an elected officer system.[^cite_note-citygov-108]

From a [1920 U.S. census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1920_United_States_census) population of 2,028,283, Oklahoma's population has continued to increase. At the [2010 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_United_States_census), its population was 3,751,675. The [2020 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_United_States_census) revealed its population to be was 3,959,353, an 5.5% increase since 2010.[^cite_note-112] A 2022 [American Community Survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Community_Survey) estimate found that the population had surpassed 4 million residents for the first time.[^cite_note-113] Among the states of the [South Central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Central_United_States) region, Oklahoma had the second-largest population increase from 2010 to 2020, behind [Texas](29810.md).[^cite_note-114][^cite_note-115]

In 2018, of Oklahoma's total resident population, approximately 236,882 were [immigrants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigrants_\(United_States\)), making up 6% of Oklahoma's population at the time. Most of its immigrant population came from Mexico (45%), Vietnam (5%), India (5%), Germany (3%), and Guatemala (3%). In the state, 246,550 residents were native-born Americans who had at least one immigrant parent. An estimated 85,000 immigrants were undocumented with 125,989 Oklahomans having lived with at least one undocumented family member between 2010 and 2014. Immigrants to Oklahoma have contributed more than a billion U.S. dollars in taxes in 2018.[^cite_note-116]

In 2011, the U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey data from 2005 to 2009 indicated about 5% of Oklahoma's residents were born outside the United States. This was lower than the national figure of about 12.5% of U.S. residents being foreign-born.[^cite_note-117]

In 2010, the [center of population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_population) of Oklahoma was in [Lincoln County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln_County,_Oklahoma), near the town of [Sparks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparks,_Oklahoma).[^cite_note-118]

According to [HUD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Housing_and_Urban_Development)'s 2022 [Annual Homeless Assessment Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annual_Homeless_Assessment_Report_to_Congress), there were an estimated 3,754 [homeless](19394651.md) people in Oklahoma.[^cite_note-119][^cite_note-120]

#### English

The English language has been official in the state of Oklahoma since 2010.[^cite_note-136] The variety of [North American English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_English) spoken is called Oklahoma English, which is "quite diverse with its uneven blending of features of North Midland, South Midland, and [Southern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_American_English) dialects".[^cite_note-Languages-137] In 2000, 2,977,187 Oklahomans—92.6% of the resident population, five years or older—spoke only English at home, a decrease from 95% in 1990.[^cite_note-Languages-137] 238,732 Oklahoma residents reported speaking a language other than English at home in the 2000 census, about 7.4% of the state's population.[^cite_note-Languages-137]

#### Native American languages

The two most commonly spoken native North American languages are [Cherokee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherokee_language) and [Choctaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choctaw_language), with 10,000 Cherokee speakers living within the [Cherokee Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherokee_Nation) tribal jurisdiction area of eastern Oklahoma and another 10,000 Choctaw speakers living in the [Choctaw Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choctaw_Nation_of_Oklahoma) directly south of the Cherokees.[^cite_note-Cherokee-138] Cherokee is an official language in the Cherokee Nation tribal jurisdiction area and in the [United Keetoowah Band of Cherokee Indians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Keetoowah_Band_of_Cherokee_Indians).[^cite_note-official-6][^cite_note-constitution-7][^cite_note-CARLA-8]

Twenty-five [Native American languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_languages_of_the_Americas) are spoken in Oklahoma,[^cite_note-languages-23] second only to [California](5407.md). However, only Cherokee, if any, exhibits some language vitality at present. *[Ethnologue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnologue)* sees Cherokee as [moribund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endangered_language) because the only remaining active users of the language are members of the grandparent generation and older.

### Religion

See also: [The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints in Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Church_of_Jesus_Christ_of_Latter-day_Saints_in_Oklahoma)



The [Boston Avenue Methodist Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston_Avenue_Methodist_Church) in Tulsa is a [National Historic Landmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Historic_Landmark).

Oklahoma is part of a geographical region characterized by conservative and Evangelical Protestant Christianity known as the "[Bible Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible_Belt)". Spanning the southern and eastern parts of the United States, the area is known for [politically and socially](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideology) conservative views, with the [Republican Party](32070.md) having the greater number of voters registered between the two major parties.[^cite_note-ok.gov-139] Tulsa, the state's second-largest city, home to [Oral Roberts University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_Roberts_University), is sometimes called the "[buckle of the Bible Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible_Belt#Buckle)".[^cite_note-140][^cite_note-141]

In 2000, there were about 5,000 [Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_synagogues_in_Oklahoma) and 6,000 Muslims, with ten congregations to each group.[^cite_note-religion-142]

According to the [Pew Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pew_Research_Center) in 2008, the majority of Oklahoma's religious adherents were [Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian), accounting for about 80% of the population. The percentage of Catholics was half the national average, while the percentage of [Evangelical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelical) Protestants was more than twice the national average, tied with Arkansas for the largest percentage of any state.[^cite_note-religion2-143]



The [Cathedral of Our Lady of Perpetual Help](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_of_Our_Lady_of_Perpetual_Help_\(Oklahoma_City\)) in Oklahoma City

In 2010, the state's largest church memberships were in the [Southern Baptist Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Baptist_Convention) (886,394 members), the [United Methodist Church](32304.md) (282,347), the [Roman Catholic Church](606848.md) (178,430), and the [Assemblies of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assemblies_of_God_USA) (85,926) and [Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Church_of_Jesus_Christ_of_Latter-day_Saints) (LDS Church)[^cite_note-144] (47,349). Other religions represented in the state include [Buddhism](3267529.md), [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism), and [Islam](6037917.md).\[^cite_note-[www.thearda.com-145](http://www.thearda.com-145/)]

According to the Pew Research Center in 2014, the majority of Oklahoma's religious adherents remained Christian accounting for 79% of the population, 9 percent higher than the national average.[^cite_note-Pew2014national-146] The percentage of Evangelical Protestants declined since the last study, but they remain the largest religious group in the state at 47% over 20 percent higher than the national average.[^cite_note-Pew2014national-146] The largest growth over the six years between Pew's 2008 and 2014 survey was in the number of people who identify as [unaffiliated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreligious) in the state with an increase of 6% of the total population.

By the 2020 [Public Religion Research Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Religion_Research_Institute)'s survey, 73% of the population were Christian.[^cite_note-147] Evangelicalism made up 29% of the state population, followed by [Mainline Protestantism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainline_Protestant) at 19%. [Historically and predominantly African-American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_church) and Latino churches collectively made up 8% of the religious demographic. An estimated 13% of the state's religious population were Roman Catholic. About 22% of the population had no religious affiliation.

In April 2025, the [Oklahoma Legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_Legislature) passed a non-binding concurrent resolution proclaiming [Christ the King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ_the_King) and recognize "the enduring influence of Christian faith in the lives of its people."[^cite_note-148]

### Incarceration

Oklahoma has been described as "the world's prison capital", with 1,079 of every 100,000 residents imprisoned in 2018, the highest incarceration rate of any American state, and by comparison, higher than the [incarceration rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_incarceration_rate) of any country in the world.[^cite_note-152][^cite_note-153]

### Gender issues

In an April 2023 report, [The Sentencing Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sentencing_Project) highlighted Oklahoma's Failure to Protect law, which has "been used primarily against women" and has "sometimes resulted in survivors of abuse facing longer sentences for allegedly failing to protect their children from harm than the person who committed the abuse". The report states that "since the law went into effect in 2009, 139 women in Oklahoma have been imprisoned solely for failure-to-protect charges".[^cite_note-154] The [Human Rights Campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Rights_Campaign) has also pointed out cases of the Oklahoma legislature's actions against the LGBTQ population and censuring of a nonbinary lawmaker.[^cite_note-155]

## Economy

Main article: [Economy of Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Oklahoma)

See also: [Oklahoma locations by per capita income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_locations_by_per_capita_income) and [List of Oklahoma counties by socio-economic factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Oklahoma_counties_by_socio-economic_factors)



The [BOK Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BOK_Tower) in Tulsa, Oklahoma's second-tallest building, serves as the world headquarters for the [Williams Companies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Williams_Companies).

In 2025, Oklahoma's [gross domestic product](12594.md) was \$274.4 billion and the state had a [per capita personal income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Per_capita_personal_income) of \$66,660.[^cite_note-156] Its [median household income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Median_household_income) ranked 46th at \$59,673 in 2023.[^cite_note-u858-157] Additionally, Oklahoma ranks consistently among the lowest states in cost of living index.[^cite_note-158]

Oklahoma is host to a diverse range of sectors including [aviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviation), energy, transportation equipment, [food processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_processing), [electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronics), and telecommunications. Oklahoma is an important producer of natural gas, aircraft, and [food](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture).[^cite_note-Oklahoma's_Economy_1-25] The state ranks third in the nation for production of natural gas, is the 27th-most agriculturally productive state, and also ranks 5th in production of wheat.[^cite_note-agriculture-159] Four [Fortune 500](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortune_500) companies and six [Fortune 1000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortune_1000) companies are headquartered in Oklahoma,[^cite_note-Fortune_500-160] and it has been rated one of the most business-friendly states in the nation,[^cite_note-Pro-Business-161] with the 7th-lowest tax burden in 2007.[^cite_note-162]

- Total employment (2018): 1,385,228

- Number of employer establishments: 93,561

  [^cite_note-163]

In 2010, Oklahoma City-based [Love's Travel Stops & Country Stores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Love's_Travel_Stops_&_Country_Stores) ranked 18th on the Forbes list of largest private companies, Tulsa-based [QuikTrip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QuikTrip) ranked 37th, and Oklahoma City-based [Hobby Lobby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hobby_Lobby) ranked 198th in 2010 report.[^cite_note-164]

Though oil has historically dominated the state's economy, a [collapse in the energy industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980s_oil_glut) during the 1980s led to the loss of nearly 90,000 energy-related jobs between 1980 and 2000, severely damaging the local economy.[^cite_note-165] Oil accounted for 35 billion dollars in Oklahoma's economy in 2007,[^cite_note-oerb2008-166] and employment in the state's oil industry was outpaced by five other industries in 2007.[^cite_note-employment-167] As of May 2025, the state's unemployment rate was 3.1%.[^cite_note-168] In 2025, small businesses made up 99.4% of businesses in Oklahoma and employed 50.8% of the state's work force.[^cite_note-169]

### Industry

In mid-2011, Oklahoma had a civilian labor force of 1.7 million and non-farm employment fluctuated around 1.5 million.[^cite_note-employment-167] The government sector provides the most jobs, with 339,300 in 2011, followed by the transportation and utilities sector, providing 279,500 jobs, and the sectors of education, business, and [manufacturing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing), providing 207,800, 177,400, and 132,700 jobs, respectively.[^cite_note-employment-167] Among the state's largest industries, the aerospace sector generates \$11 billion annually.[^cite_note-Pro-Business-161]

Tulsa is home to the largest airline maintenance base in the world, which serves as the global maintenance and engineering headquarters for [American Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Airlines).[^cite_note-170] In total, aerospace accounts for more than 10 percent of Oklahoma's industrial output, and it is one of the top 10 states in aerospace engine manufacturing.[^cite_note-Oklahoma's_Economy_1-25] Because of its position in the center of the United States, Oklahoma is also among the top states for logistic centers, and a major contributor to weather-related research.[^cite_note-Pro-Business-161]

The state is the top manufacturer of tires in North America and contains one of the fastest-growing [biotechnology](4502.md) industries in the nation.[^cite_note-Pro-Business-161] In 2005, international exports from Oklahoma's manufacturing industry totaled \$4.3 billion, accounting for 3.6 percent of its economic impact.[^cite_note-171] Tire manufacturing, meat processing, oil and gas equipment manufacturing, and air conditioner manufacturing are the state's largest manufacturing industries.[^cite_note-172]

### Energy



A major oil-producing state, Oklahoma is the fifth-largest producer of crude oil in the United States.

[^cite_note-oerb2008-166]

Oklahoma is the nation's third-largest producer of [natural gas](22131.md), and its fifth-largest producer of crude oil. The state also has the second-greatest number of active [drilling rigs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drilling_rig),[^cite_note-oerb2008-166][^cite_note-173] and it is even ranked fifth in crude oil reserves.[^cite_note-174] While the state was ranked eighth for installed [wind energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_power) capacity in 2011,[^cite_note-175] it still was at the bottom of states in usage of [renewable energy](25784.md) in 2009, with 94% of its electricity being generated by [non-renewable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-renewable_energy) sources in 2009, including 25% from coal and 46% from natural gas.[^cite_note-176]

In 2019, 53.5% of electricity was produced from [natural gas](22131.md) and 34.6% from [wind power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_power).[^cite_note-177]

Ranking 13th for total energy consumption per capita in 2009,[^cite_note-178] the state's energy costs were eighth-lowest in the nation.[^cite_note-179]

#### Oil, gas, and coal

As a whole, the oil energy industry contributes \$35 billion to Oklahoma's gross domestic product (GDP), and employees of the state's oil-related companies earn an average of twice the state's typical yearly income.[^cite_note-oerb2008-166] In 2009, the state had 83,700 commercial oil wells churning 65.374 million barrels (10,393,600 m<sup>3</sup>) of crude oil.[^cite_note-okcorpcommish-180] A tabulated 8.5% of the nation's natural gas supply is held in Oklahoma, with 1.673 trillion cubic feet (47.4 km<sup>3</sup>) being produced in 2009.[^cite_note-okcorpcommish-180]

The Oklahoma Stack Play is a geographic referenced area in the Anadarko Basin. The oil field "Sooner Trend", Anadarko basin and the counties of Kingfisher and Canadian make up the basis for the "Oklahoma STACK". Other Plays such as the Eagle Ford are geological rather than geographical.[^cite_note-181]

All of the Fortune 500 companies based in Oklahoma are energy-related, including some of the largest companies in the [petroleum industry](994887.md) in the U.S.[^cite_note-Fortune_500-160] Tulsa's [ONEOK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ONEOK) and the [Williams Companies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Williams_Companies) are the state's largest and second-largest companies respectively, also ranking as the nation's second- and third-largest companies in the field of energy, according to [*Fortune* magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortune_\(magazine\)).[^cite_note-Devon-182]

Oklahoma Gas & Electric, commonly referred to as OG\&E (NYSE: OGE) operates four base electric power plants in Oklahoma. Two of them are coal-fired power plants: one in [Muskogee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muskogee,_Oklahoma), and the other in [Red Rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Rock,_Oklahoma). Two are gas-fired power plants: one in [Harrah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harrah,_Oklahoma) and the other in [Konawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konawa,_Oklahoma). OG\&E was the first electric company in Oklahoma to generate electricity from wind farms in 2003.[^cite_note-OG\&E_Energy-183]

#### Nuclear power

Oklahoma had no operational nuclear power plants as of March 2021.[^cite_note-184]

In 1973, the [Public Service Company of Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Service_Company_of_Oklahoma) proposed the [Black Fox Nuclear Power Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Fox_Nuclear_Power_Plant) near [Inola, Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inola,_Oklahoma).[^cite_note-185] Protestors disrupted project construction in 1979, several months after the [Three Mile Island accident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Mile_Island_accident), and the project was cancelled in 1982 after nine years of legal challenges.[^cite_note-186][^cite_note-187]

### Agriculture

The 27th-most agriculturally productive state, Oklahoma is fifth in cattle production and fifth in production of wheat.[^cite_note-agriculture-159][^cite_note-ag-188] Approximately 5.5 percent of American beef comes from Oklahoma, while the state produces 6.1 percent of American wheat, 4.2 percent of American pig products, and 2.2 percent of dairy products.[^cite_note-agriculture-159]

The state had 85,500 farms in 2012, collectively producing \$4.3 billion in animal products and fewer than one billion dollars in crop output with more than \$6.1 billion added to the state's gross domestic product.[^cite_note-agriculture-159] Poultry and swine are its second- and third-largest agricultural industries.[^cite_note-ag-188]

## Education

See also: [List of school districts in Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_school_districts_in_Oklahoma), [List of high schools in Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_high_schools_in_Oklahoma), and [List of colleges and universities in Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_colleges_and_universities_in_Oklahoma)



Oklahoma's system of public regional universities includes [Northeastern State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeastern_State_University) in [Tahlequah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tahlequah,_Oklahoma).



[Bizzell Memorial Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bizzell_Memorial_Library), the heart of the [University of Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oklahoma) in [Norman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman,_Oklahoma)

With an educational system made up of [public school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_school) districts and independent [private institutions](612857.md), Oklahoma had 638,817 students enrolled in 1,845 public primary, secondary, and [vocational](56551.md) schools in 533 [school districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School_districts) as of 2008.[^cite_note-OK_Education-189] Oklahoma has the highest enrollment of Native American students in the nation with 126,078 students in the 2009–10 school year.[^cite_note-NCES-190] Oklahoma spent \$7,755 for each student in 2008, and was 47th in the nation in expenditures per student,[^cite_note-OK_Education-189] though its growth of total education expenditures between 1992 and 2002 ranked 22nd.[^cite_note-191]

The state is among the best in [pre-kindergarten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-kindergarten) education, and the National Institute for Early Education Research rated it first in the United States with regard to standards, quality, and access to pre-kindergarten education in 2004, calling it "a model for [early childhood schooling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_childhood_education)".[^cite_note-192] High school dropout rate decreased from 3.1 to 2.5 percent between 2007 and 2008 with Oklahoma ranked among 18 other states with 3 percent or less dropout rate.[^cite_note-193] In 2004, the state ranked 36th in the nation for the relative number of adults with [high school diplomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_school_diploma), though at 85.2 percent, it had the highest rate among Southern states.[^cite_note-194][^cite_note-195] According to a study conducted by the Pell Institute, Oklahoma ranks 48th in college-participation for low-income students.[^cite_note-196]

The [University of Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oklahoma), [The University of Tulsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_University_of_Tulsa), [Oklahoma State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_State_University), the [University of Central Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Central_Oklahoma), and [Northeastern State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeastern_State_University) are the largest institutions of higher education in Oklahoma, each operating through one primary campus and satellite campuses throughout the state. The two state universities, along with [Oklahoma City University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_City_University) and the [University of Tulsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tulsa), rank among the country's best universities.[^cite_note-197]

[Oklahoma City University School of Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_City_University_School_of_Law), [University of Oklahoma College of Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oklahoma_College_of_Law), and [University of Tulsa College of Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tulsa_College_of_Law) are the state's only ABA-accredited institutions. Both University of Oklahoma and University of Tulsa are Tier 2 institutions, with the University of Oklahoma ranked 55th and the University of Tulsa ranked 120th in the nation.[^cite_note-198]

Oklahoma holds eleven public regional universities,[^cite_note-199] including [Northeastern State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeastern_State_University), the second-oldest institution of higher education west of the [Mississippi River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippi_River),[^cite_note-NSU_demographics-200] also containing the only College of [Optometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optometry) in Oklahoma[^cite_note-201] and the largest enrollment of [Native American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_United_States) students in the nation by percentage and amount.[^cite_note-NSU_demographics-200][^cite_note-202] [Langston University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langston_University) is Oklahoma's only historically black college. Six of the state's universities were placed in the [Princeton Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_Review)'s list of best 122 regional colleges in 2007,[^cite_note-203] and three made the list of top colleges for best value. The state has 55 post-secondary technical institutions operated by [Oklahoma's CareerTech program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_Department_of_Career_and_Technology_Education) for training in specific fields of industry or trade.[^cite_note-OK_Education-189]

In the 2007–2008 school year, there were 181,973 undergraduate students, 20,014 graduate students, and 4,395 first-professional degree students enrolled in Oklahoma colleges. Of these students, 18,892 received a bachelor's degree, 5,386 received a master's degree, and 462 received a first professional degree. This means the state of Oklahoma produces an average of 38,278-degree-holders per completions component (i.e. July 1, 2007 – June 30, 2008). National average is 68,322 total degrees awarded per completions component.[^cite_note-204]

Beginning on April 2, 2018, tens of thousands of K–12 public school teachers [went on strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Oklahoma_teachers'_strike) due to lack of funding. According to the National Education Association, teachers in Oklahoma had ranked 49th out of the 50 states in terms of teacher pay in 2016. The Oklahoma Legislature had passed a measure a week earlier to raise teacher salaries by \$6,100, but it fell short of the \$10,000 raise for teachers, \$5,000 raise for other school employees, and \$200 million increase in extra education funding many had sought.[^cite_note-205] A survey in 2019 found that the pay raise obtained by the strike lifted the State's teacher pay ranking to 34th in the nation.[^cite_note-206]

### Cherokee Language education



Writing in [Cherokee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherokee_syllabary)

The [Cherokee Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherokee_Nation) implemented a ten-year plan in 2005 that involved growing new speakers of the [Cherokee language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherokee_language) from childhood as well as speaking it exclusively at home.[^cite_note-207] The plan was part of an ambitious goal that in fifty years would have at least 80% of their people fluent.[^cite_note-preservation-208] The [Cherokee Preservation Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherokee_Preservation_Foundation) has invested \$3 million into opening schools, training teachers, and developing curricula for language education, as well as initiating community gatherings where the language can be actively used.[^cite_note-preservation-208] A Cherokee language immersion school in [Tahlequah, Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tahlequah,_Oklahoma) educates students from pre-school through eighth grade.[^cite_note-209]

## Culture



The *[Pioneer Woman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pioneer_Woman)* statue in [Ponca City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ponca_City,_Oklahoma), by [Bryant Baker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryant_Baker) (1930).

Oklahoma is placed in the South by the [United States Census Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Census_Bureau),[^cite_note-regdiv-20] but other definitions place the state at least partly in the [Southwest](179571.md), [Midwest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midwestern_United_States),[^cite_note-210] [Upland South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upland_South),[^cite_note-211] and [Great Plains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Plains).[^cite_note-212] Oklahomans have a high rate of [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Americans), [Scotch-Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotch-Irish_Americans), [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Americans), and [Native American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_Americans_in_the_United_States) ancestry,[^cite_note-213] with 25 different Indigenous languages spoken in the state.[^cite_note-languages-23]

Because many American Indians were forced to move to [Indian territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Territory) (modern day Oklahoma) when [American settlement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_pioneer) within North America had increased, Oklahoma has much linguistic diversity. Mary Linn, an associate professor of anthropology at the [University of Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oklahoma) and the associate curator of Native American languages at the [Sam Noble Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Noble_Oklahoma_Museum_of_Natural_History), notes Oklahoma also has high levels of language endangerment.[^cite_note-214]

Sixty-seven Native American tribes and bands are represented in Oklahoma,[^cite_note-OK_History-37] including 38 [federally recognized tribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federally_recognized_tribes),[^cite_note-JusticeIC-133] who are headquartered and have [tribal jurisdictional areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_Tribal_Statistical_Area) or [Indian reservations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_reservations) in the state.[^cite_note-215] Native American tribes, Western ranchers, Southern settlers, and Eastern oil barons have shaped the state's cultural predisposition, and its largest cities have been named among the most underrated cultural destinations in the United States.[^cite_note-216]

Residents of Oklahoma are associated with traits of [Southern hospitality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_hospitality)—the 2006 Catalogue for Philanthropy (with data from 2004) ranks Oklahomans 7th in the nation for overall generosity.[^cite_note-217] The state has also been associated with a negative cultural [stereotype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereotype) first popularized by [John Steinbeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Steinbeck)'s 1939 novel *[The Grapes of Wrath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Grapes_of_Wrath)*, which described the plight of uneducated, poverty-stricken Dust Bowl-era farmers deemed "[Okies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okie)".[^cite_note-okies-218][^cite_note-219] While the term is often used in a positive manner by Oklahomans,[^cite_note-okies-218] it is still considered a derogatory term by many.[^cite_note-220]

### Arts

Further information: [List of Native American artists from Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Native_American_artists_from_Oklahoma)



[Philbrook Museum of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philbrook_Museum_of_Art), one of the nation's top fifty

[^cite_note-philbrook-221]

In the state's largest urban areas, pockets of [jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz) culture flourish,[^cite_note-ok_arts-222] and [African American](2154.md), [Mexican American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Americans), and [Asian American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_Americans) communities produce music and art of their respective cultures.[^cite_note-223] The Oklahoma Mozart Festival in [Bartlesville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartlesville) is one of the largest classical music festivals on the Southern Plains,[^cite_note-224] and Oklahoma City's Festival of the Arts has been named one of the top fine arts festivals in the nation.[^cite_note-ok_arts-222]

The state has a rich history in ballet with five Native American ballerinas attaining worldwide fame. These were [Yvonne Chouteau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yvonne_Chouteau), sisters [Marjorie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marjorie_Tallchief) and [Maria Tallchief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Tallchief), [Rosella Hightower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosella_Hightower) and [Moscelyne Larkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscelyne_Larkin), known collectively as the [Five Moons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Moons). *[The New York Times](30680.md)* rates the [Tulsa Ballet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulsa_Ballet) as one of the top ballet companies in the United States.[^cite_note-ok_arts-222] The [Oklahoma City Ballet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_City_Ballet) and University of Oklahoma's dance program were formed by ballerina Yvonne Chouteau and husband [Miguel Terekhov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miguel_Terekhov). The university program was founded in 1962 and was the first fully accredited program of its kind in the United States.[^cite_note-225][^cite_note-226]

In [Sand Springs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sand_Springs,_Oklahoma), an outdoor amphitheater called "Discoveryland!" (since closed) is the official performance headquarters for the musical *[Oklahoma!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma!)*[^cite_note-227] [Ridge Bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ridge_Bond), native of [McAlester, Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McAlester,_Oklahoma),[^cite_note-tulsaworld.com-228] starred in the [Broadway](725252.md) and International touring productions of *Oklahoma!*,[^cite_note-digital.library.okstate.edu-229][^cite_note-230][^cite_note-231][^cite_note-232] playing the role of "Curly McClain" in more than 2,600 performances.[^cite_note-digital.library.okstate.edu-229][^cite_note-okhistory.org-233] In 1953 he was featured along with the *[Oklahoma!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma!)* cast on a [CBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CBS) [Omnibus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omnibus_\(U.S._TV_series\)) television broadcast.[^cite_note-okhistory.org-233] Bond was instrumental in the [Oklahoma! title song](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_\(Rodgers_and_Hammerstein_song\)) becoming the Oklahoma state song[^cite_note-tulsaworld.com-228][^cite_note-234] and is also featured on the U.S. [postage stamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postage_stamp) commemorating the musical's 50th anniversary.[^cite_note-digital.library.okstate.edu-229][^cite_note-235]

Historically, Oklahoma has produced musical styles such as [The Tulsa Sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tulsa_Sound) and [western swing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_swing), which was popularized at [Cain's Ballroom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cain's_Ballroom) in Tulsa. The building, known as the "Carnegie Hall of Western Swing",[^cite_note-236] served as the performance headquarters of [Bob Wills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Wills) and the [Texas Playboys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Playboys) during the 1930s.[^cite_note-237] Stillwater is known as the epicenter of [Red Dirt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Dirt_\(music\)) music, the best-known proponent of which is the late [Bob Childers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Childers).

Prominent theatre companies in Oklahoma include, in the capital city, Lyric Theatre of Oklahoma, Oklahoma City Theatre Company, Carpenter Square Theatre, [Oklahoma Shakespeare in the Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_Shakespeare_in_the_Park), and CityRep. CityRep is a professional company affording equity points to those performers and technical theatre professionals. In Tulsa, Oklahoma's oldest resident professional company is American Theatre Company, and [Theatre Tulsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_Tulsa) is the oldest [community theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community_theatre) company west of the Mississippi. Other companies in Tulsa include [Heller Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heller_Theatre) and Tulsa Spotlight Theater. The cities of Norman, Lawton, and Stillwater, among others, also host well-reviewed community theatre companies.

Oklahoma is in the nation's middle percentile in per capita spending on the arts, ranking 17th, and contains more than 300 museums.[^cite_note-ok_arts-222] The [Philbrook Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philbrook_Museum) of Tulsa is considered one of the top 50 [fine art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine_art) museums in the United States,[^cite_note-philbrook-221] and the [Sam Noble Oklahoma Museum of Natural History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Noble_Oklahoma_Museum_of_Natural_History) in Norman, one of the largest university-based art and history museums in the country, documents the natural history of the region.[^cite_note-ok_arts-222] The collections of [Thomas Gilcrease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Gilcrease) are housed in the [Gilcrease Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilcrease_Museum) of Tulsa, which also holds the world's largest, most comprehensive collection of art and artifacts of the American West.[^cite_note-238]

The Egyptian art collection at the [Mabee-Gerrer Museum of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mabee-Gerrer_Museum_of_Art) in Shawnee is considered to be the finest Egyptian collection between [Chicago](6886.md) and Los Angeles.[^cite_note-239] The [Oklahoma City Museum of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_City_Museum_of_Art) contains the most comprehensive collection of glass sculptures by artist [Dale Chihuly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dale_Chihuly) in the world,[^cite_note-240] and Oklahoma City's [National Cowboy & Western Heritage Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Cowboy_&_Western_Heritage_Museum) documents the heritage of the American Western frontier.[^cite_note-ok_arts-222] With remnants of the [Holocaust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Holocaust) and artifacts relevant to Judaism, the Sherwin Miller Museum of Jewish Art of Tulsa preserves the largest collection of Jewish art in the Southwest United States.[^cite_note-241]

### Festivals and events



Oklahoma [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance) Festival, 2017



Oklahoma [Regatta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regatta) Festival on the [North Canadian River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Canadian_River), 2012&#x20;

Oklahoma's centennial celebration was named the top event in the United States for 2007 by the [American Bus Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Bus_Association),[^cite_note-242] and consisted of multiple celebrations saving with the 100th anniversary of [statehood](18618239.md) on November 16, 2007. Annual ethnic festivals and events take place throughout the state such as ceremonial events, include festivals (as examples) in [Scottish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Americans), [Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Americans), [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Americans), [Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Americans), [Vietnamese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_Americans), [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Americans), [Czech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czech_Americans), [Jewish](1131183.md), [Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Americans), [Mexican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Americans) and African-American communities depicting cultural heritage or traditions.

Oklahoma City is home to a few reoccurring events and festivals. During a ten-day run in Oklahoma City, the [State Fair of Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_State_Fair) attracts roughly one million people[^cite_note-243] along with the annual Festival of the Arts. Such as various Latin American and [Asian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Asia) heritage festivals, and cultural festivals such as the [Juneteenth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juneteenth) celebrations are held in Oklahoma City each year. The Oklahoma City [Pride Parade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pride_Parade) has been held annually in late June since 1987 in the gay district of Oklahoma City on [39th and Penn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NW_39th_Street_Enclave).[^cite_note-244] The First Friday Art Walk in the [Paseo Arts District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paseo_Arts_District) is an art appreciation festival held the first Friday of every month.[^cite_note-245] Additionally, an annual art festival is held in the Paseo on Memorial Day Weekend.[^cite_note-246]

The [Tulsa State Fair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulsa_State_Fair) attracts more than a million people each year during its ten-day run.[^cite_note-247] The city also hosts the annual Mayfest festival which entertained more than 375,000 in four days during 2007.[^cite_note-248] In 2006, Tulsa's annual [Oktoberfest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oktoberfest) was named one of the top 10 in the world by *[USA Today](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USA_Today)*.

Norman plays host to the [Norman Music Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Music_Festival), a festival that highlights native Oklahoma bands and musicians. Norman is also host to the Medieval Fair of Norman, which has been held annually since 1976 and was Oklahoma's first medieval fair. The Fair was held first on the south oval of the University of Oklahoma campus and in the third year moved to the Duck Pond in Norman until the Fair became too big and moved to Reaves Park in 2003. The Medieval Fair of Norman is Oklahoma's "largest weekend event and the third-largest event in Oklahoma, and was selected by Events Media Network as one of the top 100 events in the nation".[^cite_note-249]

### Sports

The [Oklahoma City Thunder](18256220.md) of the [National Basketball Association](22093.md) (NBA) is the state's only [major league sports franchise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_professional_sports_leagues_in_the_United_States_and_Canada). The state had a team in the [Women's National Basketball Association](57721.md), the [Tulsa Shock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulsa_Shock), from 2010 through 2015, but the team relocated to [Dallas–Fort Worth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dallas%E2%80%93Fort_Worth_metroplex) after that season[^cite_note-250] and became the [Dallas Wings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dallas_Wings).[^cite_note-251]

Oklahoma has teams in several minor leagues, including [Minor League Baseball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_League_Baseball) at the [Triple-A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triple-A_\(baseball\)) and [Double-A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double-A_\(baseball\)) levels (the [Oklahoma City Comets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_City_Comets) and [Tulsa Drillers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulsa_Drillers), respectively), hockey's [ECHL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ECHL) with the [Tulsa Oilers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulsa_Oilers), and a number of indoor football leagues. In the last-named sport, the state's most notable team was the [Tulsa Talons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulsa_Talons), which played in the [Arena Football League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arena_Football_League) until 2012, when the team was moved to [San Antonio](53848.md), [Texas](29810.md). The [Oklahoma Defenders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_Defenders) replaced the Talons as Tulsa's only professional arena football team, playing the [CPIFL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CPIFL). The [Oklahoma City Blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_City_Blue), of the [NBA G League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NBA_G_League), relocated to Oklahoma City from Tulsa in 2014, where they were formerly known as the Tulsa 66ers. Tulsa is the base for the [Tulsa Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulsa_Revolution), which plays in the [American Indoor Soccer League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Indoor_Soccer_League).[^cite_note-252] Enid and Lawton host professional basketball teams in the [USBL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USBL) and the [CBA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_Basketball_Association).



The [Oklahoma City Thunder](18256220.md) moved to Oklahoma City in 2008, becoming the state's first (and so far only) permanent major-league team in any sport.

[Collegiate athletics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_athletics_in_the_United_States) are a popular draw in the state. The state has four schools that compete at the highest level of college sports, [NCAA Division I](669931.md). The [Oklahoma Sooners](965018.md) participate in the [Southeastern Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeastern_Conference),[^cite_note-253] and the [Oklahoma State Cowboys and Cowgirls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_State_Cowboys_and_Cowgirls) participate in the [Big 12 Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_12_Conference).[^cite_note-254] The Big 12 and SEC are two of the so-called [Power Four conferences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_Five_conferences) of the top tier of college football, [Division I FBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCAA_Division_I_Football_Bowl_Subdivision). The Sooners and Cowboys average well over 50,000 fans attending their football games, and Oklahoma's football program ranked 12th in attendance among American colleges in 2010, with an average of 84,738 people attending its home games.[^cite_note-255] The two universities meet several times each year in rivalry matches known as the [Bedlam Series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bedlam_Series), which are some of the greatest sporting draws to the state. *[Sports Illustrated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sports_Illustrated)* magazine rates Oklahoma and Oklahoma State among the top colleges for athletics in the nation.[^cite_note-top_sports-256][^cite_note-257]

Two private institutions in Tulsa, the [University of Tulsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulsa_Golden_Hurricane) and [Oral Roberts University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_Roberts_Golden_Eagles); are also Division I members. Tulsa competes in FBS football and other sports in the [American Athletic Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Athletic_Conference),[^cite_note-258] while Oral Roberts, which does not sponsor football,[^cite_note-259] is a member of the [Summit League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summit_League).[^cite_note-260] In addition, 12 of the state's smaller colleges and universities compete in [NCAA Division II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCAA_Division_II) as members of three different conferences,[^cite_note-261][^cite_note-262][^cite_note-263] and eight other Oklahoma institutions participate in the [NAIA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Association_of_Intercollegiate_Athletics), mostly within the [Sooner Athletic Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sooner_Athletic_Conference).[^cite_note-264]

Regular [LPGA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LPGA) tournaments are held at Cedar Ridge Country Club in Tulsa, and [major championships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Men's_major_golf_championships) for the [PGA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professional_Golfers'_Association_of_America) or LPGA have been played at [Southern Hills Country Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Hills_Country_Club) in Tulsa, Oak Tree Country Club in Oklahoma City, and Cedar Ridge Country Club in Tulsa.[^cite_note-265] Rated one of the top golf courses in the nation, Southern Hills has hosted five [PGA Championships](224616.md), including one in 2022, and three [U.S. Opens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Open_\(golf\)), the most recent in 2001.[^cite_note-266] [Rodeos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodeo) are popular throughout the state, and [Guymon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guymon,_Oklahoma), in the state's panhandle, hosts one of the largest in the nation.[^cite_note-267]

[ESPN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ESPN) called Oklahoma City "the center of the [softball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Softball) universe", specifically referring to the fast-pitch version, in a 2020 story. [Oklahoma City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_City) is home to the governing body of the sport in the United States, [USA Softball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USA_Softball), which has its headquarters in a complex that also includes [Devon Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devon_Park_\(stadium\)). It annually hosts the [Women's College World Series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women's_College_World_Series), the eight-team final round of the [NCAA Division I softball tournament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCAA_Division_I_softball_tournament).[^cite_note-268] Devon Park will host softball at the [2028 Summer Olympics](12695048.md), and the Riversport OKC complex will host canoe slalom at the Games.[^cite_note-269]

[College wrestling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collegiate_wrestling) has strong tradition in Oklahoma. [Oklahoma State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_State_Cowboys_wrestling) has the most [NCAA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCAA_Division_I_Wrestling_Championships) national championships of any [collegiate team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collegiate_wrestling) with 34, with the Oklahoma Sooners having 7 NCAA wrestling titles. The [National Wrestling Hall of Fame and Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Wrestling_Hall_of_Fame_and_Museum) is headquartered in [Stillwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stillwater,_Oklahoma).[^cite_note-270]

A [teqball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teqball) competition was held in [Tulsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulsa) June 14–16, 2024.[^cite_note-271]

## Health



Cancer Treatment Centers of America at Southwestern Regional Medical Center, Tulsa

Oklahoma was the 21st-largest recipient of medical funding from the federal government in 2005, with health-related federal expenditures in the state totaling \$75,801,364; [immunizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunization), [bioterrorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioterrorism) preparedness, and health education were the top three most funded medical items.[^cite_note-oklahoma_health_2-272] Instances of major diseases are near the national average in Oklahoma, and the state ranks at or slightly above the rest of the country in percentage of people with [asthma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asthma), [diabetes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diabetes_mellitus), cancer, and [hypertension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypertension).[^cite_note-oklahoma_health_2-272]

In 2000, Oklahoma ranked 45th in physicians per capita and slightly below the national average in nurses per capita, but was slightly above the national average in hospital beds per 100,000 people and above the national average in net growth of health services over a twelve-year period.[^cite_note-273] One of the worst states for percentage of insured people, nearly 25 percent of Oklahomans between the age of 18 and 64 did not have health insurance in 2005, the fifth-highest rate in the nation.[^cite_note-274]

Oklahomans are in the upper half of Americans in terms of [obesity prevalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obesity_in_the_US). In 2009, Oklahoma was the 5th most obese in the nation, with 30.3 percent of its population at or near obesity.[^cite_note-Oklahoma_Health-275] Oklahoma ranked last among the 50 states in a 2007 study by the [Commonwealth Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_Fund) on health care performance.[^cite_note-276]

The [OU Medical Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oklahoma_Health_Sciences_Center), Oklahoma's largest collection of hospitals, is the only hospital in the state designated a Level I [trauma center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trauma_center) by the [American College of Surgeons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_College_of_Surgeons). OU Medical Center is on the grounds of the Oklahoma Health Center in Oklahoma City, the state's largest concentration of medical research facilities.[^cite_note-277][^cite_note-278]

The Cancer Treatment Centers of America at Southwestern Regional Medical Center in Tulsa is one of four such regional facilities nationwide, offering cancer treatment to the entire southwestern United States, and is one of the largest cancer treatment hospitals in the country.[^cite_note-279] The largest [osteopathic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osteopathic_medicine_in_the_United_States) teaching facility in the nation, [Oklahoma State University Medical Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_State_University_Medical_Center) at Tulsa, also rates as one of the largest facilities in the field of [neuroscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroscience).[^cite_note-280][^cite_note-281] On June 26, 2018, Oklahoma made [marijuana legal for medical purposes](175440.md), making it one of the most conservative states to approve medical marijuana.[^cite_note-282]

### Life expectancy

The residents of Oklahoma have [a lower life expectancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_U.S._states_and_territories_by_life_expectancy) than the U.S. national average. In 2014, males in Oklahoma lived an average of 73.7 years compared to a male national average of 76.7 years and females lived an average of 78.5 years compared to a female national average of 81.5 years. Increases in life expectancy have been below the national average. Male life expectancy in Oklahoma between 1980 and 2014, increased by an average of 4.0 years, compared to a male national average of a 6.7 year increase. Life expectancy for females in Oklahoma between 1980 and 2014, increased by 1.0 years, compared to a female national average of a 4.0 year increase.[^cite_note-283]

Using 2016–2018 data, the [Robert Wood Johnson Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Wood_Johnson_Foundation) calculated that life expectancy (all sexes) for Oklahoma counties ranged from 71.2 years for [Okfuskee County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okfuskee_County) to 79.7 years for [Cimarron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cimarron_County) and [Logan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logan_County,_Oklahoma) counties. Life expectancy for the state as a whole was 76.0 years.[^cite_note-Robert_Wood_Johnson-284]

### Impact of COVID-19

As of December 22, 2022, Oklahoma has been impacted more by the [COVID-19 pandemic](62750956.md) (2020–2023) than the average U.S. state. Statistics for the U.S. as a whole are 331 deaths per 100,000 population with 68 percent of the population fully vaccinated. The comparable statistics for Oklahoma are 405 deaths per 100,000 population with 59 percent of the population fully vaccinated; 16,041 deaths from COVID-19 have been recorded in Oklahoma. A wide variation in deaths from COVID-19 exists among Oklahoma counties. [Greer County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greer_County,_Oklahoma) recorded the highest death rate of .00753 (753 deaths per 100,000 residents). [Payne County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Payne_County,_Oklahoma) recorded the lowest death rate of .00231 (231 deaths per 100,000 residents).[^cite_note-Tracking_Coronavirus-285]

## Media

Main articles: [List of newspapers in Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_newspapers_in_Oklahoma), [List of radio stations in Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_radio_stations_in_Oklahoma), and [List of television stations in Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_television_stations_in_Oklahoma)



The second-largest newspaper in Oklahoma, the *Tulsa World*, has a circulation of 189,789.

[^cite_note-world-286]

Oklahoma City and Tulsa are the 45th- and 61st-largest [media markets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_market) in the United States as ranked by [Nielsen Media Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nielsen_Media_Research). The state's third-largest media market, Lawton-[Wichita Falls, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wichita_Falls,_Texas), is ranked 149th nationally by the agency.[^cite_note-287] [Broadcast television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrestrial_television) in Oklahoma began in 1949 when [KFOR-TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KFOR-TV) (then WKY-TV) in Oklahoma City and [KOTV-TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KOTV-TV) in Tulsa began broadcasting a few months apart.[^cite_note-288] Currently, all major American [broadcast networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_network) have affiliated television stations in the state.[^cite_note-289]

Oklahoma has two primary newspapers. *[The Oklahoman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Oklahoman)*, based in Oklahoma City, is the largest newspaper in the state and 54th-largest in the nation by circulation, with a weekday readership of 138,493 and a Sunday readership of 202,690. The *[Tulsa World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulsa_World)*, the second-most widely circulated newspaper in Oklahoma and 79th in the nation, holds a Sunday circulation of 132,969 and a weekday readership of 93,558.[^cite_note-world-286] Oklahoma's first newspaper was established in 1844, called the *Cherokee Advocate*, and was written in both [Cherokee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherokee_language) and English.[^cite_note-ok_newspapers-290] In 2006, there were more than 220 newspapers in the state, including 177 with weekly publications and 48 with daily publications.[^cite_note-ok_newspapers-290]

Oklahoma's first radio station, [WKY](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WKY) in Oklahoma City, began broadcasting in 1920.[^cite_note-291] In 2006, there were more than 500 radio stations in Oklahoma broadcasting with various local or nationally owned networks. Five universities in Oklahoma operate non-commercial, public radio stations/networks.[^cite_note-292]

Oklahoma has a few ethnic-oriented TV stations broadcasting in Spanish and [Asian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_Americans) languages, and there is some Native American programming. [TBN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_Broadcasting_Network), a Christian religious television network, has a studio in Tulsa, and built its first entirely TBN-owned affiliate in Oklahoma City in 1980.[^cite_note-293]

## Transportation



Road network and waterways of Oklahoma from the 1970 edition of the [National Atlas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Atlas)

Transportation in Oklahoma is generated by an anchor system of [Interstate Highways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_Highway_System), [inter-city rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inter-city_rail) lines, airports, [inland ports](1341936.md), and [mass transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_transport) networks. Situated along an integral point in the Interstate Highway system, Oklahoma contains three [three primary and four auxiliary Interstate Highways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Interstate_Highways_in_Oklahoma). In Oklahoma City, [Interstate 35](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_35_in_Oklahoma) (I-35) intersects with [I-44](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_44_in_Oklahoma) and [I-40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_40_in_Oklahoma), forming one of the most important intersections along the United States highway system.[^cite_note-roads_okla-294]

More than 12,000 miles (19,000 km) of roads make up the state's major highway skeleton, including state-operated highways, ten [turnpikes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turnpikes_of_Oklahoma) or major toll roads,[^cite_note-roads_okla-294] and the longest drivable stretch of [Route 66](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Route_66_in_Oklahoma) in the nation.[^cite_note-295] In 2008, I-44 in Oklahoma City was Oklahoma's busiest highway, with a daily traffic volume of 123,300 cars.[^cite_note-296] In 2010, the state had the nation's third-highest number of bridges classified as structurally deficient, with nearly 5,212 bridges in disrepair, including 235 National Highway System bridges.[^cite_note-297]

Oklahoma's largest commercial airport is [Will Rogers World Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will_Rogers_World_Airport) in Oklahoma City, averaging a yearly passenger count of more than 3.5 million (1.7 million boardings) in 2010.[^cite_note-298] [Tulsa International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulsa_International_Airport), the state's second-largest commercial airport, served more than 1.3 million boardings in 2010.[^cite_note-299] Between the two, six airlines operate in Oklahoma.[^cite_note-300][^cite_note-301] In terms of traffic, [R. L. Jones Jr. (Riverside) Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Lloyd_Jones_Jr._Airport) in Tulsa is the state's busiest airport, with 335,826 takeoffs and landings in 2008.[^cite_note-302] Oklahoma has more than 150 public-use airports.[^cite_note-303]

Oklahoma is connected to the nation's rail network via [Amtrak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amtrak)'s *[Heartland Flyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heartland_Flyer)*, its only regional passenger rail line. It currently stretches from [Oklahoma City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_City) to [Fort Worth, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Worth,_Texas). Lawmakers began seeking funding in early 2007 to connect the *Heartland Flyer* to [Tulsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulsa),[^cite_note-304] but nothing came of this. In June 2023, following studies and negotiations, Oklahoma and Kansas state officials began seeking federal approval and funding to extend the *Heartland Flyer* from Oklahoma City to [Newton, Kansas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton,_Kansas). The two locations are currently connected by an Amtrak Thruway Bus route that includes a stop in [Wichita, Kansas](114932.md).[^cite_note-Newton-305] In November 2023, KDOT said the service would start in 2029 if approved, but could begin sooner were the project to be fast tracked.[^cite_note-306]

Two inland ports on rivers serve Oklahoma: the [Port of Muskogee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Muskogee) and the [Tulsa Port of Catoosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulsa_Port_of_Catoosa). The Tulsa Port of Catoosa is one of the United States' most inland international ports, at head of navigation of the [McClellan–Kerr Arkansas River Navigation System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McClellan%E2%80%93Kerr_Arkansas_River_Navigation_System), which connects [barge](4177.md) traffic from Tulsa and Muskogee to the Mississippi River.[^cite_note-Tulsa_Port_News-307] The port ships over two million tons of goods annually and is a designated [foreign trade zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free-trade_zone).

## Law and government

Main article: [Government of Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Oklahoma)



The [Oklahoma State Capitol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_State_Capitol) in Oklahoma City

Oklahoma is a constitutional republic with a government modeled after the [federal government of the United States](195149.md), with executive, legislative, and judicial branches.[^cite_note-308] Oklahoma has [77 counties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_counties_in_Oklahoma) with jurisdiction over most local government functions within each respective domain,[^cite_note-Topography_of_Oklahoma-80] [five congressional districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma's_congressional_districts), and a voting base with a majority in the [Republican Party](32070.md).[^cite_note-Current_Registration_Statistics_by_County-309] State officials are elected by [plurality voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plurality_voting) in the state of Oklahoma.

Oklahoma has [capital punishment](412425.md) as a legal sentence. Between 1976 and mid-2011, Oklahoma had the highest per capita execution rate in the nation.[^cite_note-310] Authorized methods of execution include the [electric chair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_chair), the [gas chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_chamber) and the [firing squad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Execution_by_firing_squad).[^cite_note-311]

In a 2020 study, Oklahoma was ranked as the 14th most difficult state for citizens to vote in.[^cite_note-312] In May 2020, it became the first state to enact [an anti–red flag law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_flag_law#Oklahoma), prohibiting the acceptance of any grants or funding to enact red flag laws.[^cite_note-313][^cite_note-314] [Abortion in Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abortion_in_Oklahoma) is illegal in nearly all circumstances.[^cite_note-315]

### State government

See also: [Governor of Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governor_of_Oklahoma), [Oklahoma Legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_Legislature), and [Oklahoma Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_Supreme_Court)

The [Legislature of Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_Legislature) consists of the [Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_Senate) and the [House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_House_of_Representatives). As the lawmaking branch of the state government, it is responsible for raising and distributing the money necessary to run the government. The Senate has 48 members serving four-year terms, while the House has 101 members with two-year terms. The state has a [term limit](505825.md) for its legislature that restricts any one person to twelve cumulative years service between both legislative branches.[^cite_note-Oklahoma_Government_2-316][^cite_note-317]

Oklahoma's judicial branch consists of the [Oklahoma Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_Supreme_Court), the [Oklahoma Court of Criminal Appeals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_Court_of_Criminal_Appeals), and 77 District Courts that each serve one county. The Oklahoma judiciary contains two independent courts: a Court of [Impeachment](1795376.md) for [impeachment trials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impeachment_trial), and the [Oklahoma Court on the Judiciary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_Court_on_the_Judiciary). Oklahoma has two courts of last resort: the state Supreme Court hears civil cases, and the state Court of Criminal Appeals hears criminal cases. This split system exists only in Oklahoma and neighboring Texas. Judges of those two courts, as well as the Court of Civil Appeals are appointed by the governor upon the recommendation of the state Judicial Nominating Commission, and are subject to a [non-partisan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-partisan) retention vote on a six-year rotating schedule.[^cite_note-Oklahoma_Government_2-316]

The executive branch consists of the [governor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governor_of_Oklahoma), their staff, and other elected officials. The principal head of government, the governor is the chief executive of the Oklahoma executive branch. He serves as the [ex officio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Latin_phrases_\(E\)) [Commander-in-chief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commander-in-chief) of the [Oklahoma National Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_National_Guard) when not called into [Federal](195149.md) use and reserving the power to veto bills passed through the legislature. The responsibilities of the Executive branch include submitting the budget, ensuring state laws are enforced, and ensuring peace within the state is preserved.[^cite_note-318]

### Local government

See also: [List of counties in Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_counties_in_Oklahoma)

Oklahoma is divided into 77 [counties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_\(United_States\)) that govern locally, each headed by a three-member council of elected commissioners, a tax assessor, clerk, [court clerk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_clerk), treasurer, and [sheriff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheriffs_in_the_United_States).[^cite_note-319] Each municipality operates as a separate and independent local government with executive, legislative and judicial power. County governments maintain jurisdiction over both incorporated cities and non-incorporated areas within their boundaries, and have executive power but no legislative or judicial power.[^cite_note-citygov-108]

Both county and municipal governments collect taxes, employ a separate police force, hold elections, and operate emergency response services within their jurisdiction.[^cite_note-citygov-108][^cite_note-320] Other local government units include [school districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School_district), technology center districts, community college districts, rural fire departments, rural water districts, and other special use districts.

Thirty-nine Native American tribal governments are based in Oklahoma, each holding limited powers within designated areas. While [Indian reservations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_reservation) are typical in most of the United States, they are not present in Oklahoma. Tribal governments hold land granted during the Indian Territory era, but with limited jurisdiction, and no control over state governing bodies such as municipalities and counties. Tribal governments are recognized by the United States as quasi-sovereign entities with executive, judicial, and legislative powers over tribal members and functions, but are subject to the authority of the [United States Congress](31756.md) to revoke or withhold certain powers. The tribal governments are required to submit a constitution and any subsequent amendments to the United States Congress for approval.[^cite_note-321][^cite_note-322]

Oklahoma has 11 substate districts, including the two large Councils of Governments, INCOG in Tulsa (Indian Nations Council of Governments) and ACOG (Association of Central Oklahoma Governments).

### National politics

Further information: [Political party strength in Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_party_strength_in_Oklahoma)

Main article: [Politics of Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics_of_Oklahoma)

During the first half-century of statehood, Oklahoma was considered a [Democratic](5043544.md) stronghold, being carried by the [Republican Party](32070.md) in only two presidential elections, [1920](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1920_United_States_presidential_election) and [1928](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1928_United_States_presidential_election). After the [1948 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1948_United_States_presidential_election), the state turned firmly Republican. Although registered Republicans were a minority in the state until 2015,[^cite_note-voters-323] Oklahoma has been carried by Republican presidential candidates in all but one election since 1952: [Lyndon B. Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson)'s [1964 landslide victory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_United_States_presidential_election).

Every single county in Oklahoma has been won by the Republican candidate in each election since [2004](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_presidential_election,_2004). Oklahoma was the only state where [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama) failed to carry any counties in [2008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_United_States_presidential_election). Oklahoma City was the largest city in the United States carried by Republican [Donald Trump](4848272.md) in both the 2016 and 2020 elections.

Democrats are strongest in urban areas, such as the inner parts of Oklahoma City and Tulsa, as well as the [college towns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_town) of Norman and Stillwater, and areas which are most heavily [African American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_American). The Democrats once held dominance in the eastern part of Oklahoma and [Little Dixie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Dixie_\(Oklahoma\)) before the area gradually shifted Republican in the late 2000s. As of the 2020 election, Native American voters, 16% of the state's population, are split, with urban populations supporting the Democrats, and rural reservation populaces favoring the Republicans.[^cite_note-324]

Following the [2000 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Census_Bureau), the Oklahoma delegation to the [U.S. House of Representatives](19468510.md) was reduced from six to five representatives, each serving one [congressional district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congressional_district). Oklahoma has had an all-Republican congressional delegation since 2021 and previously from 2013 to 2019.
