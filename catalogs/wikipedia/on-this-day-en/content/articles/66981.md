# Epidemic

For other uses, see [Epidemic (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epidemic_\(disambiguation\)).



Example of an epidemic showing the number of new infections over time.

An **epidemic** (from [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) ἐπί *epi* "upon or above" and δῆμος *demos* "people") is the rapid spread of [disease](8072.md) to a large number of [hosts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Host_\(biology\)) in a given population within a short period of time. For example, in [meningococcal infections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meningococcal_infection), an [attack rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attack_rate) in excess of 15 cases per 100,000 people for two consecutive weeks is considered an epidemic.[^cite_note-Principles_of_Epidemiology-1][^cite_note-Green-2]

Epidemics of infectious disease are generally caused by several factors including a change in the ecology of the host population (e.g., increased stress or increase in the density of a vector species), a genetic change in the pathogen reservoir or the introduction of an emerging pathogen to a host population (by movement of pathogen or host). Generally, an epidemic occurs when host [immunity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunity_\(medicine\)) to either an established pathogen or newly emerging [novel pathogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novel_pathogen) is suddenly reduced below that found in the [endemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endemic_\(epidemiology\)) equilibrium and the transmission threshold is exceeded.[^cite_note-3]

An epidemic may be restricted to one location; however, if it spreads to other countries or continents and affects a substantial number of people, it may be termed as a [pandemic](24255.md).[^cite_note-Principles_of_Epidemiology-1]<sup>: §1\:72 </sup> The declaration of an epidemic usually requires a good understanding of a [baseline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baseline_\(medicine\)) rate of [incidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incidence_\(epidemiology\)); epidemics for certain diseases, such as [influenza](19572217.md), are defined as reaching some defined increase in incidence above this baseline.[^cite_note-Green-2] A few cases of a very rare [disease](8072.md) may be classified as an epidemic, while many cases of a common disease (such as the [common cold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_cold)) would not. An epidemic can cause enormous damage through financial and economic losses in addition to impaired health and loss of life.

## Definition



*The Plague of Athens* (c. 1652–1654) by [Michiel Sweerts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michiel_Sweerts), illustrating the [devastating epidemic that struck Athens in 430 BC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Athens), as described by the historian [Thucydides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thucydides)

The United States [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention](6811.md) defines epidemic broadly: "Epidemic refers to an increase, often sudden, in the number of cases of a disease above what is normally expected in that population in that area." The term "outbreak" can also apply, but is usually restricted to smaller events.[^cite_note-Principles_of_Epidemiology-1]<sup>: §1\:72 </sup>[^cite_note-Green-2]

Any sudden increase in disease prevalence may generally be termed an epidemic. This may include [contagious disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contagious_disease) (i.e. easily spread between persons) such as [influenza](19572217.md); [vector-borne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disease_vector) diseases such as [malaria](20423.md); [water-borne diseases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterborne_diseases) such as [cholera](7591.md); and [sexually transmitted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexually_transmitted_infection) diseases such as [HIV/AIDS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS). The term can also be used for non-communicable health issues such as [obesity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obesity_epidemic).[^cite_note-Green-2][^cite_note-4][^cite_note-Martin-5]

The term *epidemic* derives from a word form attributed to [Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer)'s *[Odyssey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odyssey)*, which later took its medical meaning from the *[Epidemics](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Of%20the%20Epidemics),* a treatise by [Hippocrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocrates).[^cite_note-Martin-5] Before Hippocrates, *epidemios*, *epidemeo*, *epidamos*, and other variants had meanings similar to the current definitions of "[indigenous](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/indigenous)" or "[endemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endemic_\(epidemiology\))".[^cite_note-Martin-5] [Thucydides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thucydides)' description of the [Plague of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_of_Athens) is considered one of the earliest accounts of a disease epidemic.[^cite_note-Martin-5] By the early 17th century, the terms *endemic* and *epidemic* referred to contrasting conditions of population-level disease, with the endemic condition a "*common sicknesse*" and the epidemic "*hapning in some region, or countrey, at a certaine time, ....... producing in all sorts of people, one and the same kind of sicknesse*".[^cite_note-Lodge1603-6]

The term "epidemic" is often applied to diseases in non-human animals, although "[epizootic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epizootic)" is technically preferable.[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8]

## Causes

There are several factors that may contribute (individually or in combination) to causing an epidemic. There may be changes in a [pathogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathogen), in the population that it can infect, in the environment, or in the interaction between all three. Factors include the following:[^cite_note-Principles_of_Epidemiology-1]<sup>: §1\:72 </sup>

### Antigenic change



Illustration depicting the different mechanisms of antigenic shift and antigenic drift

An [antigen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antigen) is a [protein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein) on the [virus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virus)' surface that host [antibodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibody) can recognize and attack. Changes in the [antigenic characteristics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antigenic_variation) of the agent make it easier for the changed virus to spread throughout a previously immune population. There are two natural mechanisms for change - [antigenic drift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antigenic_drift) and [antigenic shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antigenic_shift). Antigenic drift arises over a period of time as an accumulation of [mutations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutation) in the [virus genes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virus#Genome), possibly through a series of hosts, and eventually gives rise to a new strain of virus which can evade existing immunity. Antigenic shift is abrupt - in this, two or more different strains of a virus, [coinfecting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coinfection) a single host, combine to form a new subtype having a mixture of characteristics of the original strains. The best known and best documented example of both processes is [influenza](19572217.md).[^cite_note-9] [SARS-CoV2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SARS-CoV-2) has demonstrated antigenic drift and possibly shift as well.[^cite_note-10]

### Drug resistance

[Antibiotic resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimicrobial_resistance) applies specifically to [bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteria) that become resistant to [antibiotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibiotic).[^cite_note-WHO2014-11] Resistance in bacteria can arise naturally by [genetic mutation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_mutation), or by one species acquiring resistance from another through [horizontal gene transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_gene_transfer).[^cite_note-12] Extended use of antibiotics appears to encourage selection for mutations which can render antibiotics ineffective. This is especially true of [tuberculosis](30653.md), with increasing occurrence of [multiple drug-resistant tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-drug-resistant_tuberculosis) (MDR-TB) worldwide.[^cite_note-WHO2015Fact-13][^cite_note-14]

### Changes in transmission



Anopheles mosquito, the vector of malaria

[Pathogen transmission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathogen_transmission) is a term used to describe the mechanisms by which a disease-causing agent (virus, bacterium, or parasite) spreads from one host to another. Common modes of transmission include:[^cite_note-15] -

- [airborne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airborne_transmission) (as with influenza and COVID-19),

- [fecal-oral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fecal%E2%80%93oral_route) (as with cholera and typhoid),

- [vector-borne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector-borne) (malaria, Zika) and

- [sexual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexually_transmitted_infection) (syphilis, HIV)

The first three of these require that pathogen must survive away from its host for a period of time; an evolutionary change which increases survival time will result in increased virulence.[^cite_note-16]

Another possibility, although rare, is that a pathogen may adapt to take advantage of a new mode of transmission[^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18]

### Seasonality

Seasonal diseases arise due to the change in the environmental conditions, especially such as humidity and temperature, during different seasons. Many diseases display [seasonality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seasonality),[^cite_note-19][^cite_note-20] This may be due to one or more of the following underlying factors: -[^cite_note-21]

- The ability of the pathogen to survive outside the host - e.g. [water-borne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterborne_diseases) cholera

  &#x20;which becomes prevalent in [tropical wet](57630.md) seasons, or influenza which peaks in temperate regions during winter.

- The behaviour of people susceptible to the disease - such as spending more time in close contact indoors.

- Changes in immune function during winter - one possibility is a reduction in vitamin D, and another is the effect of cold on mucous membranes in the nose.

### Human behaviour



*A Court for King Cholera.* Illustration from *[Punch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punch_\(magazine\))* (1852).

Changes in behaviour can affect the likelihood, extent, severity and longevity of epidemics. The classic example is the [1854 Broad Street cholera outbreak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1854_Broad_Street_cholera_outbreak), in which a cholera outbreak was mitigated by removing a supply of contaminated water - an event now regarded as the foundation of the science of [epidemiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epidemiology).[^cite_note-29] Urbanisation and overcrowding (e.g. in [refugee camps](438046.md)) increase the likelihood of disease outbreaks.[^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31] A factor which contributed to the initial rapid increase in the [2014 Ebola virus epidemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_African_Ebola_virus_epidemic) was [ritual bathing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bathing_the_dead) of (infective) corpses; one of the control measures was an education campaign to change behaviour around funeral rites.[^cite_note-32]

### Changes in the host population

The level of immunity to a disease in a population - [herd immunity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herd_immunity) - is at its peak after a disease outbreak or a vaccination campaign. In the following years, immunity will decline, both within individuals and in the population as a whole as older individuals die and new individuals are born. Eventually, unless there is another vaccination campaign, an outbreak or epidemic will recur.[^cite_note-33]

It's also possible for disease which is endemic in one population to become epidemic if it is introduced into a novel setting where the host population is not immune. An example of this was the introduction European diseases such as smallpox into indigenous populations during the 16th century.[^cite_note-34]

### Zoonosis



Possibilities for zoonotic disease transmissions

A [zoonosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoonosis) is an [infectious disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infectious_disease) of humans caused by a pathogen that can [jump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-species_transmission) from a non-human host to a human.[^cite_note-35] Major diseases such as [Ebola virus disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebola_virus_disease) and [salmonellosis](1046292.md) are zoonoses. [HIV](14170.md) was a zoonotic disease transmitted to humans in the early part of the 20th century, though it has now evolved into a separate human-only disease.[^cite_note-Orgin2011-36] Some strains of [bird flu](1019908.md) and [swine flu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Influenza_A_virus_subtype_H1N1) are zoonoses; these viruses occasionally recombine with human strains of the flu and can cause [pandemics](24255.md) such as the [1918 Spanish flu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1918_Spanish_flu) or the [2009 swine flu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_swine_flu).[^cite_note-37]

### Common source outbreak

In a common source outbreak epidemic, the affected individuals had an exposure to a common agent. If the exposure is singular and all of the affected individuals develop the disease over a single exposure and incubation course, it can be termed as a point source outbreak. If the exposure was continuous or variable, it can be termed as a continuous outbreak or intermittent outbreak, respectively.[^cite_note-Principles_of_Epidemiology-1]<sup>: 56 </sup>

### Propagated outbreak

In a propagated outbreak, the disease spreads person-to-person. Affected individuals may become independent reservoirs leading to further exposures.[^cite_note-Principles_of_Epidemiology-1]<sup>: 56 </sup>Many epidemics will have characteristics of both common source and propagated outbreaks (sometimes referred to as **mixed outbreak**).

For example, secondary person-to-person spread may occur after a common source exposure or an environmental vector may spread a [zoonotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoonotic) diseases agent.[^cite_note-Principles_of_Epidemiology-1]<sup>: 56–58 </sup>

## Preparation

Preparations for an epidemic include having a disease surveillance system; the ability to quickly dispatch emergency workers, especially local-based emergency workers; and a legitimate way to guarantee the safety and health of health workers.[^cite_note-gerson-38][^cite_note-39]

Effective preparations for a response to a pandemic are multi-layered. The first layer is a disease surveillance system. [Tanzania](30118.md), for example, runs a national lab that runs testing for 200 health sites and tracks the spread of infectious diseases. The next layer is the actual response to an emergency. According to U.S.-based columnist Michael Gerson in 2015, only the U.S. military and [NATO](21133.md) have the global capability to respond to such an emergency.[^cite_note-gerson-38] Still, despite the most extensive preparatory measures, a fast-spreading pandemic may easily exceed and overwhelm existing health-care resources.[^cite_note-ACAIM-WACEM_COVID-19_Consensus_Paper-40] Consequently, early and aggressive mitigation efforts, aimed at the so-called "epidemic curve flattening" need to be taken.[^cite_note-ACAIM-WACEM_COVID-19_Consensus_Paper-40] Such measures usually consist on non-pharmacological interventions such as social/physical distancing, aggressive contact tracing, "stay-at-home" orders, as well as appropriate personal protective equipment (i.e., masks, gloves, and other physical barriers to spread).[^cite_note-ACAIM-WACEM_COVID-19_Consensus_Paper-40]

Moreover, India has taken significant strides in its efforts to prepare for future respiratory pandemics through the development of the National Pandemic Preparedness Plan for Respiratory Viruses using a multisectoral approach.[^cite_note-41]

Preceding this national effort, a regional workshop on the Preparedness and Resilience for Emerging Threats (PRET) initiative was organized by WHO's South-East Asia Regional Office on October 12–13, 2023. Recognizing that the same capacities and capabilities can be leveraged and applied for groups of pathogens based on their mode of transmission, the workshop aimed to facilitate pandemic planning efficiency for countries in the region. The participating countries, in the aftermath of the workshop, outlined their immediate next steps and sought support from WHO and its partners to bolster regional preparedness against respiratory pathogen pandemics.[^cite_note-42]
