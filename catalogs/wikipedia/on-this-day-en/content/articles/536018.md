The **NCAA Division I women's basketball tournament**, sometimes referred to as **Women's March Madness**,[^cite_note-1] is a [single-elimination tournament](939680.md) played each spring in the United States, currently featuring 76 women's [college basketball](768856.md) teams from the [Division I](669931.md) level of the [National Collegiate Athletic Association](60706.md) (NCAA), to determine the national championship.

The tournament was preceded by the [AIAW women's basketball tournament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AIAW_women's_basketball_tournament), which was organized by the [Association for Intercollegiate Athletics for Women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_for_Intercollegiate_Athletics_for_Women) (AIAW) from 1972 to 1982. Basketball was one of 12 women's sports added to the NCAA championship program for the 1981–82 school year, as the NCAA engaged in battle with the AIAW for sole governance of women's collegiate sports. The AIAW continued to conduct its established championship program in the same 12 (and other) sports; however, after a year of dual women's championships, the NCAA prevailed, while the AIAW disbanded.

As of the upcoming 2027 edition, the tournament follows almost exactly the same format and [selection process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCAA_basketball_tournament_selection_process) as its [men's counterpart](507399.md), with 32 [automatic bids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automatic_bid) awarded to the champions of the Division I conferences, and 44 "[at-large bids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/At-large_bid)" extended by the NCAA Selection Committee, which are placed into four regional divisions and seeded from 1 to 16. The 12 lowest-seeded automatic bids, and the 12 lowest-seeded at-large bids, compete in the Opening Round games to advance to the 64-team [bracket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bracket_\(tournament\)) in the first round. The national semi-finals, branded as the **Women's Final Four**, are traditionally scheduled on the same weekend as the men's Final Four, but in a different host city. Presently, the Women's Final Four uses a Friday/Sunday scheduling, with its games occurring one day prior to the men's Final Four and championship, respectively.[^cite_note-\:1-2]

Attendance and interest in the women's championship have grown over the years, especially from 2003 to 2016, when the final championship game was moved to the Tuesday following the Monday [men's championship](507399.md) game.[^cite_note-\:1-2] The tournament is often overshadowed by the more prominent men's tournament; after a [gender equality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_equality) review following the [2021 tournament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament), the NCAA expanded it to the then-current 68-team format of the men's tournament and extended the "March Madness" branding to the tournament as well. Both tournaments were expanded to 76 teams starting with the 2027 editions. The [2024 women's championship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_championship_game) was the first to receive higher viewership than the men's championship the same year. Still, the tournament receives a smaller amount of funding from broadcast rights (which are held by [ESPN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ESPN), and are pooled with those of other NCAA Division I championships besides golf and men's basketball) and sponsorship (which are sold by [CBS and Turner Sports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCAA_March_Madness_\(TV_program\))) than the men's tournament.

With 12 national titles, the [UConn Huskies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UConn_Huskies_women's_basketball) hold the record for the most NCAA Women's Division I Basketball Championships, which includes a record four consecutive championships from 2013 through 2016.[^cite_note-3] The team had also made the semi-finals for a record 14 consecutive tournaments. The [Tennessee Lady Volunteers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tennessee_Lady_Volunteers_basketball) are the only team to make an appearance in every tournament since its founding in 1982.[^cite_note-FF_Record_book-4]

## Tournament format

From 1982 to 1990, 1996 to 2002, 2017 to 2019, and since 2021, the Women's Final Four is usually played on the Friday before the Men's Final Four or the hours before the men played on the final Saturday of the tournament. The final, since 2023, is played the Sunday afternoon following the Men's Final Four; from 2017 to 2019, 2021 and 2022, it was Sunday evening.

&#x20;The [tournament bracket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tournament_bracket) is made up of champions from each [Division I](669931.md) conference, which are automatic bids. The remaining slots are [at-large bids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/At-large_bid), with teams chosen by an NCAA selection committee. The [selection process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCAA_basketball_tournament_selection_process) and tournament seedings are based on several factors, including team rankings, win–loss records, and [NET](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCAA_basketball_tournament_selection_process) data.

Main article: [NCAA basketball tournament selection process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCAA_basketball_tournament_selection_process)

Since 2026, 76 teams qualify for the tournament played in March and April. Of these teams, 32 earn automatic bids by winning their respective conference tournaments. Since 2017, the [Ivy League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivy_League) conducts its own [post-season](690345.md#Postseason) tournament. The remaining teams are granted "at-large" bids, which are extended by the [NCAA Selection Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCAA_basketball_tournament_selection_process). Dr. Marilyn McNeil, vice president/director of athletics at [Monmouth University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monmouth_University) is the current chairwoman. On March 1, 2011, [Bowling Green State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowling_Green_State_University)'s director of intercollegiate athletics, Greg Christopher, was appointed chair of the NCAA Division I Women's Basketball Committee during the 2011–12 academic year.[^cite_note-new_chair-5]

The tournament begins with 12 games known as the Opening Round. From 2022–2026, the women's tournament had four opening round games branded as the First Four. Under both the 68- and 76-team formats, the men's and women's First Four/Opening Round have used essentially identical formats. In the First Four era, the four lowest-ranked conference champions played for 16 seeds in the round of 64, and the four lowest-ranked at-large teams played for their own spots in the round of 64.[^cite_note-68teams-6] Starting in 2027, the 12 lowest-ranked conference champions play for seeds 14 through 16, and the 12 lowest-ranked at-large teams will play for their own spots in the round of 64.

The tournament is split into four regional tournaments, and each regional has teams seeded from 1 to 16, with the committee ostensibly making every region as comparable to the others as possible. The top-seeded team in each region plays the #16 team, the #2 team plays the #15, etc. (meaning that all first-round games involve teams whose seeds add up to 17).

## Number of teams, and seeding

The first NCAA women's basketball tournament was held in [1982](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1982_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament). The [AIAW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AIAW) also held a basketball tournament in 1982, but most of the top teams, including defending AIAW champion [Louisiana Tech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_Tech_Lady_Techsters_basketball), decided to participate in the NCAA tournament.

The championship consisted of 32 teams from 1982 to 1985 (in 1983, 36), 40 teams from 1986 to 1988, and 48 teams from 1989 to 1993. From 1994 to 2021, 64 teams competed in each tournament. From 2022 to 2026, the tournament featured 68 teams, matching the size of the D-I men's tournament. From 2027, both the men's and women's tournaments involve 76 teams.

Prior to 1996, seeding was conducted on a regional basis. The top teams (eight in the 32-, 40-, and 48-team formats, and 16 in the 64-team format) were ranked and seeded on a national basis. The remaining teams were then seeded based on their geographic region. Teams were moved outside of its geographic region only if it was necessary to balance the bracket, or if the proximity of an opponent outside of its region would be comparable and a more competitive game would result. In 1993, all teams except for the top four were explicitly unseeded. The regional seeding resumed in 1994. In 1996, seeds were assigned on a national basis using an "S-Curve" format similar to the process used in selecting the field for the men's tournament.

The most significant difference between bracketing for the men's and women's tournaments was introduced to the women's tournament for 2027 and beyond. The top 16 teams in the selection committee's ranking will be placed in regionals based solely on their actual seeding, with no regard for conference affiliation.[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8]

## Selection process

Further information: [NCAA basketball tournament selection process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCAA_basketball_tournament_selection_process)

A special selection committee appointed by the NCAA determines which 76 teams will enter the tournament, and where they will be seeded and placed in the bracket. Because of the automatic bids, only 44 teams (the at-large bids) rely on the selection committee to secure them a spot in the tournament.

## Tournament trends

### Top-ranked teams

Since the women's tournament began in 1982, 20 teams have entered the tournament ranked #1 in at least 1 poll and gone on to win the tournament:

- 1982: Louisiana Tech

- 1983: USC

- 1986: Texas

- 1989: Tennessee

- 1995: UConn

- 1998: Tennessee

- 1999: Purdue

- 2000: UConn

- 2002: UConn

- 2003: UConn

- 2009: UConn

- 2010: UConn

- 2012: Baylor

- 2014: UConn

- 2015: UConn

- 2016: UConn

- 2019: Baylor

- 2021: Stanford

- 2022: South Carolina

- 2024: South Carolina

### Champions excluded the next year

Only once has the reigning champion (the previous year's winner) not made it to the tournament the next year.

- [1985 champion Old Dominion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1985_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) went 15–13 in 1986.

### No. 1 seeds

Since 1982, at least one #1 seed has made the Final Four every year.

Under coach Geno Auriemma, Connecticut has been seeded #1 a record 23 times. Tennessee is second with 21 #1 seeds.

All four #1 seeds have made it to the Final Four 5 times (champion in bold):

- [1989](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1989_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) Auburn, Louisiana Tech, Maryland, **Tennessee**

- [2012](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) **Baylor**, UConn, Notre Dame, Stanford

- [2015](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) **UConn**, Maryland, Notre Dame, South Carolina

- [2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) UConn, Mississippi State, **Notre Dame**, Louisville

- [2026](76476380.md) UConn, **UCLA**, South Carolina, Texas

The championship game has matched two #1 seeds 16 times:

- [1983](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1983_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) USC beat Louisiana Tech

- [1986](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1986_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) Texas beat USC

- [1989](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1989_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) Tennessee beat Auburn

- [1991](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1991_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) Tennessee beat Virginia

- [1995](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1995_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) UConn beat Tennessee

- [2000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2000_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) UConn beat Tennessee

- [2002](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2002_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) UConn beat Oklahoma

- [2003](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) UConn beat Tennessee

- [2010](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) UConn beat Stanford

- [2012](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) Baylor beat Notre Dame

- [2014](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) UConn beat Notre Dame

- [2015](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) UConn beat Notre Dame

- [2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) Notre Dame beat Mississippi State

- [2019](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) Baylor beat Notre Dame

- [2024](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) South Carolina beat Iowa

- [2026](76476380.md) UCLA beat South Carolina

Four teams have beaten three #1 seeds during the course of a tournament, the largest number of such teams that can be faced. This can only happen by a lower seed advancing to the final four over a #1 seed, then winning tournament over two more #1 seeds.

- [1987](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1987_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) #2 Tennessee (beat Auburn, Long Beach State, Louisiana Tech)

- [1988](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) #2 Louisiana Tech (beat Auburn, Tennessee, Texas)

- [2005](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) #2 Baylor (beat LSU, Michigan State, North Carolina)

- [2025](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) #2 UConn (beat USC, UCLA, South Carolina)

Prior to the expansion of the tournament to 64 teams, all four #1 seeds advanced to the Sweet Sixteen with three exceptions. Notably, the first two times this occurred were at the hands of the same school:

- [1986](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1986_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) East #1 seed Virginia lost to #8 seed James Madison

- [1991](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1991_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) East #1 seed Penn State lost to #8 seed James Madison

- [1992](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1992_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) Midwest #1 seed Iowa lost to #8 seed Southwest Missouri State

### High seeds

- [1999](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1999_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament) was the first time in tournament history (since the expansion to 64 teams) that all top seeds (1, 2, 3, and 4 seeds) made it to the Sweet Sixteen.

#### Undefeated teams not in the tournament

The NCAA tournament has undergone dramatic expansion since its first edition in 1982, and only one unbeaten team has failed to qualify for the tournament—[California Baptist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Baptist_Lancers_women's_basketball) in 2021, which was 24–0 after winning the [Western Athletic Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Athletic_Conference) [Tournament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_WAC_women's_basketball_tournament).[^cite_note-22] As, by definition, a team would have to win its conference tournament, and thus secure an automatic bid to the tournament, to be undefeated in a season, the only way a team could finish undefeated and not reach the tournament is if the team is banned from postseason play. (Other possibilities are that the team is independent, or is from a conference not yet eligible for an automatic bid.) Postseason bans can come about for one of two reasons:

- The team is serving a postseason ban due to NCAA sanctions.

- The team is transitioning from a lower NCAA division, during which time it is barred by NCAA rule from participation in NCAA-sponsored postseason play. This is the case for California Baptist, which began a transition from Division II in 2018 and thus could not play in the NCAA tournament until 2023. California Baptist was eligible for the [WNIT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Women's_National_Invitation_Tournament) because that tournament is not operated by the NCAA, unlike the [men's version](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Invitation_Tournament) (or the [Women's Basketball Invitation Tournament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women's_Basketball_Invitation_Tournament) introduced in 2024); the Lancers lost in the quarterfinals to eventual champion [Rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020%E2%80%9321_Rice_Owls_women's_basketball_team).

### Home state

Only one team has ever played the Final Four on its home court. Two other teams have played the Final Four in their home cities, and seven others have played the Final Four in their home states.

The only team to play on its home court was Texas in 1987, which lost its semifinal game at the now-defunct [Frank Erwin Special Events Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Erwin_Center).

Old Dominion enjoyed nearly as large an advantage in 1983 when the Final Four was played at the [Norfolk Scope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norfolk_Scope) in its home city of [Norfolk, Virginia](57898.md), but also lost its semifinal. The Scope has never been the Monarchs' regular home court. ODU has always used on-campus arenas, first the [ODU Fieldhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Dominion_University_Fieldhouse) and since 2002 [Chartway Arena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chartway_Arena). The following year, USC won the national title at [Pauley Pavilion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauley_Pavilion), the home court of its [Los Angeles](18110.md) archrival UCLA.

Of the other teams to play in their home states, Stanford (1992) won the national title; Notre Dame (2011) lost in the championship game; and Western Kentucky (1986), Penn State (2000), Missouri State (2001), LSU (2004), and Baylor (2010) lost in the semifinals.

## Television coverage and revenues

Main article: [List of NCAA Division I women's basketball tournament Final Four broadcasters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_NCAA_Division_I_women's_basketball_tournament_Final_Four_broadcasters)

Broadcast rights to the NCAA women's basketball tournament are included in a larger package covering most NCAA Division I championships,[^cite_note-APKaplan-25][^cite_note-KaplanReport-26] outside of men's basketball (which is held by [CBS and TNT Sports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCAA_March_Madness_\(TV_program\))),[^cite_note-27] and golf (which is held by [Golf Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golf_Channel)).[^cite_note-28] [ESPN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ESPN_College_Basketball) has held exclusive rights to the tournament since 1996; beginning with an 11-year, \$200 million contract renewal in 2003, ESPN would televise all 63 games in the tournament on television (increasing from 23), with games in the first and second rounds airing regionally on [ESPN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ESPN) and [ESPN2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ESPN2). Out-of-market games were carried via [pay-per-view](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pay-per-view).[^cite_note-NCAA_FFRecord75-29] Coverage later expanded to include ESPN's college sports-oriented network [ESPNU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ESPNU), and [ESPN360](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ESPN360) for streaming.[^cite_note-\:0-30] In 2011, ESPN renewed this agreement through the 2023–24 season, in a deal reported to be worth \$500 million in total. The deal also included rights to the men's tournament outside of the United States for [ESPN International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ESPN_International).[^cite_note-31] In 2024, ESPN renewed the contract again through 2032 (aligned with the end of the media rights for the men's tournament), in an agreement valued at \$920 million over eight years.[^cite_note-32]

In the first two rounds, one channel (typically ESPN or ESPN2's [high-definition feed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-definition_television)) typically aired "whiparound" coverage during each window, carrying rolling coverage of all games in progress. ESPN's standard definition channels were used to broadcast games on a regional basis, while games could also be viewed in their entirety on ESPN3 or alternate channels.[^cite_note-\:0-30] In 2021, ESPN adopted a broadcast arrangement similar to the men's tournament, with all games airing nationally in their entirety on either an ESPN linear channel or, for the first time, [ABC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ESPN_on_ABC). The Women's Final Four and championship remained exclusive to ESPN.[^cite_note-33] Beginning in 2023, the national championship game has aired on ABC.[^cite_note-\:2-34][^cite_note-\:3-35]

In data issued by the NCAA in 2021, it was stated that 15.9% of the value of the contract was allocated to the women's tournament, or approximately \$6.1 million annually. In comparison, the contract for the men's tournament is valued at over \$700 million annually. Amid scrutiny of inequality between the men's and women's tournaments that year, it has been suggested by critics that the structure of the NCAA's contract undervalues the media rights to the women's tournament.[^cite_note-36][^cite_note-37] Based on average viewership, Emily Caron and Eben Novy-Williams of *Sportico* estimated that the women's tournament could fetch at least \$20 million per year if its media rights were sold separately. [America East Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/America_East_Conference) commissioner Amy Huchthausen argued that the ESPN contract "provides a measure of financial certainty, but it does not provide women's basketball (or any of the other sports, for that matter) an incentive to grow".[^cite_note-38]

Following major media criticism of inequities between the 2021 men's and women's tournaments, the NCAA commissioned a comprehensive gender equity review of its championships by the law firm Kaplan Hecker & Fink. Among the report's findings was that U.S. television rights for the women's tournament would be worth at least \$81 million annually by the time the current broadcast contract with ESPN expires in 2024 (in comparison to the \$34 million value of the NCAA package as a whole).[^cite_note-APKaplan-25][^cite_note-KaplanReport-26]

The UCLA Bruins won the 2026 NCAA Division I women's basketball tournament, defeating South Carolina 78–74 in the final on April 5, 2026. Center Lauren Betts was named the tournament's Most Outstanding Player.[^cite_note-NCAAW_2026-39]

In an interview on NBC's *[Meet the Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meet_the_Press)* on the day of the 2023 national championship, new NCAA president [Charlie Baker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlie_Baker) implicated that the media rights to the women's basketball tournament may be sold separately in the next rights cycle, stating that "we do have an opportunity to put it out separately, and we're going to work really hard to make sure that those student-athletes, those schools, those programs get what I describe as what they should get."[^cite_note-40] Interest in [Caitlin Clark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caitlin_Clark)'s tournament run had led to record viewership of Iowa's Women's Final Four and championship games on ESPN and ABC, respectively.[^cite_note-\:2-34][^cite_note-41] The 2024 National Championship even peaked at 24 million viewers, being the first time in history that the women's final drew more viewership than the men.[^cite_note-42]

Nevertheless, the NCAA renewed its existing agreements with ESPN in January 2024 under an eight-year agreement, with ESPN paying approximately \$115 million per season, and the NCAA having valued the media rights to the Division I women's basketball tournament at \$65 million. The agreement also includes expanded rights for ESPN to sell sponsorships (although CBS/WBD will still administer the NCAA Corporate Champion and Partner Program sponsorships per its rights to the men's tournament), and guarantees that the national championship will air on ABC annually.[^cite_note-\:3-35]
