A **berry** is a small, pulpy, and often edible [fruit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fruit). Typically, berries are juicy, rounded, brightly colored, sweet, sour or tart, and do not have a [stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stone_fruit) or [pit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pit_\(fruit\)) although many [pips](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/pip#Etymology_2) or seeds may be present.‍[^cite_note-Encyclopædia_Britannica-1] Common examples of berries in the culinary sense are [strawberries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strawberries), [raspberries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raspberry), [blueberries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blueberry), [blackberries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackberry), [white currants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_currant), [blackcurrants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackcurrant), and [redcurrants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redcurrant).‍[^cite_note-2] In Britain, **soft fruit** is a [horticultural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horticulture) term for such fruits.‍[^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5]

The common usage of the term "berry" is different from the scientific or [botanical definition of a berry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berry_\(botany\)), which refers to a [fleshy fruit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fleshy_fruit) produced from the [ovary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ovary_\(botany\)) of a single flower where the outer layer of the ovary wall develops into an edible fleshy portion ([pericarp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pericarp)). The botanical definition includes many fruits that are not commonly known or referred to as berries,‍[^cite_note-6] such as [grapes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grape), [tomatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomato), [cucumbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cucumber), [eggplants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eggplant), [bananas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banana), and [chili peppers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chili_peppers). Fruits commonly considered berries but excluded by the botanical definition include strawberries, raspberries, and [blackberries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackberries), which are [aggregate fruits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aggregate_fruit), and [mulberries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morus_\(plant\)), which are [multiple fruits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_fruit). [Watermelons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watermelons) and [pumpkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pumpkins) are giant berries that fall into the category "[pepos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pepo_\(botany\))". A plant bearing berries is said to be *[bacciferous](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/bacciferous#English)* or *[baccate](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/baccate#English)*.

Berries are eaten worldwide and often used in [jams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jam), [preserves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fruit_preserves), [cakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cake), or [pies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pie). Some berries are commercially important. The berry industry varies from country to country as do types of berries cultivated or growing in the wild. Some berries such as raspberries and strawberries have been bred for hundreds of years and are distinct from their wild counterparts, while other berries, such as [lingonberries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lingonberry) and [cloudberries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloudberries), grow almost exclusively in the wild.

While many berries are edible, some are [poisonous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisonous_plant) to humans, such as those of [deadly nightshade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deadly_nightshade) and [pokeweed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pokeweed). Others, such as the [white mulberry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_mulberry), [red mulberry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_mulberry),‍[^cite_note-7] and [elderberry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elderberry),‍[^cite_note-8] are poisonous when [unripe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unripe), but edible when ripe.

## History

blackthorn bush

Blackthorn, *[Prunus spinosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prunus_spinosa)*

Berries have been valuable as a food source for humans since before the start of agriculture, and remain a food source for other [primates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primate). They were a seasonal staple for early [hunter-gatherers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter-gatherer) for thousands of years, and wild berry gathering remains a popular activity in Europe and North America today. In time, humans learned to store berries so that they could be used in the winter. They may be made into fruit preserves, and among Native Americans, mixed with meat and fats as [pemmican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pemmican).‍[^cite_note-cambridge-9]

Berries also began to be cultivated in Europe and other countries. Some species of blackberries and raspberries of the genus *[Rubus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubus)* have been cultivated since the 17th century, while smooth-skinned blueberries and cranberries of the genus *[Vaccinium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaccinium)* have been cultivated in the United States for over a century.‍[^cite_note-cambridge-9] In Japan, between the 10th and 18th centuries, the terms *ichibigo* and [*ichigo*](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/ichigo#Japanese) ([kanji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanji): [苺](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E8%8B%BA#Japanese); [katakana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katakana): [イチゴ](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E3%82%A4%E3%83%81%E3%82%B4#Japanese)) referred to many berry crops. The most widely cultivated berry of modern times is the strawberry, which is produced globally at twice the amount of all other berry crops combined.‍[^cite_note-10]

The strawberry was mentioned by ancient Romans, who thought it had medicinal properties,‍[^cite_note-11] but it was then not a staple of agriculture.‍[^cite_note-wild_crop-12] [Woodland strawberries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodland_strawberries) began to be grown in French gardens in the 14th century. The [musk strawberry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musk_strawberry) (*F. moschata*), also known as the hautbois strawberry, began to be grown in European gardens in the late 16th century. Later, the [Virginia strawberry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia_strawberry) was grown in Europe and the United States.‍[^cite_note-13] The most commonly consumed strawberry, the [garden strawberry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_strawberry) (*F. ananassa*), is an accidental hybrid of the Virginia strawberry and a Chilean variety *[Fragaria chiloensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fragaria_chiloensis)*. It was first noted by a French gardener around the mid 18th century that, when *F. moschata* and *F. virginiana* were planted in between rows of *F. chiloensis*, the Chilean strawberry would bear abundant and unusually large fruits. Soon after, [Antoine Nicolas Duchesne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_Nicolas_Duchesne) began to study the [breeding of strawberries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breeding_of_strawberries) and made several discoveries crucial to the science of plant breeding, such as the sexual reproduction of strawberry.‍[^cite_note-duchesne-14] Later, in the early 1800s, English breeders of strawberry made varieties of *F. ananassa* which were important in strawberry breeding in Europe,‍[^cite_note-15] and hundreds of [cultivars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultivars) have since been produced through the breeding of strawberries.‍[^cite_note-wild_crop-12]

### Etymology

The [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English) word *[berie](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/berie#Old_English)* ('berry, grape') comes from [Proto-Germanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Germanic_language), variously [reconstructed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconstruction_\(linguistics\)) as *[\*basją](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Reconstruction\:Proto-Germanic/basj%C4%85#Proto-Germanic)*, *[\*bazją](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Reconstruction\:Proto-Germanic/bazj%C4%85#Proto-Germanic)*, \**basjom* (source also of [Old Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse) *[ber](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/ber#Old_Norse)*, [Middle Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Dutch) *[bere](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/bere#Middle_Dutch)*, [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language) *[Beere](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Beere#German)*, 'berry', [Old Saxon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Saxon) *[winberi](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/winberi#Old_Saxon)*, [Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_language) *[weinabasi](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%F0%90%8D%85%F0%90%8C%B4%F0%90%8C%B9%F0%90%8C%BD%F0%90%8C%B0%F0%90%8C%B1%F0%90%8C%B0%F0%90%8D%83%F0%90%8C%B9#Gothic)*, 'grape'), which is of unknown origin. This and "[apple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple)" are the only fruit names in modern English which are descended from "native" [Germanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_languages) words.‍[^cite_note-16]

In botanical terminology, a berry is a simple fruit with seeds and pulp produced from the [ovary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ovary_\(plants\)) of a single flower. It is fleshy throughout, except for the seeds. It does not have a special "line of weakness" along which it splits to release the seeds when ripe (i.e. it is [indehiscent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dehiscence_\(botany\))).‍[^cite_note-17] A berry may develop from an ovary with one or more [carpels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpels) (the female reproductive structures of a flower). The seeds are usually embedded in the fleshy interior of the ovary, but there are some non-fleshy examples such as [peppers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capsicum), with air rather than pulp around their seeds. The differences between the everyday and botanical uses of "berry" result in three categories: those fruits that are berries under both definitions; those fruits that are botanical berries but not commonly known as berries; and those parts of plants commonly known as berries that are not botanical berries, and may not even be fruits.

Berries under both definitions include blueberries, cranberries, lingonberries, and the fruits of many other members of the [heather family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heather_family), as well as gooseberries, goji berries, and elderberries. The fruits of some "currants" (*[Ribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ribes)* species), such as blackcurrants, red currants and white currants, are botanical berries, and are treated as horticultural berries (or as soft fruit in the UK), even though their most commonly used names do not include the word "berry".

Botanical berries not commonly known as berries include bananas,‍[^cite_note-18] tomatoes,‍[^cite_note-Encyclopædia_Britannica-1] grapes, eggplants (aubergines), [persimmons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persimmon), watermelons, and [pumpkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pumpkin).

There are several different kinds of fruits which are commonly called berries, but are not botanical berries. Blackberries, raspberries, and strawberries are kinds of aggregate fruit;‍[^cite_note-Encyclopædia_Britannica-1] they contain seeds from different ovaries of a single flower. In aggregate fruits like blackberries, the individual "fruitlets" making up the fruit can be clearly seen. The fruits of [blackthorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackthorn) may be called "sloe berries",‍[^cite_note-19] but botanically are small stone fruits or drupes, like [plums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plum) or [apricots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apricot).

[Junipers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juniper) and [yews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxus) are commonly said to have berries, but these plants do not produce botanical fruits at all: they are [gymnosperms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnosperms), specifically [conifers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conifer), not [angiosperms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angiosperms) (flowering plants). Their "berries" are highly-modified seed-bearing [cones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conifer_cone). In [juniper berries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juniper_berries), used to flavour [gin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gin), the cone scales, which are hard and woody in most conifers, are instead soft and fleshy when ripe. The bright red berries of yews consist of a fleshy outgrowth ([aril](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aril)) almost enclosing the poisonous seed. The resemblance of these plant structures to botanical berries provides a striking example of [convergent evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_evolution) in different plant [clades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clade).

### Agricultural methods

Like most other food crops, berries are commercially grown, with both conventional pest management and [integrated pest management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_pest_management) (IPM) practices.‍<sup>\:5</sup>

Many soft fruit berries require a period of temperatures between 0 and 10 °C (32 and 50 °F) for breaking dormancy. In general, strawberries require 200–300 hours, blueberries 650–850 hours, blackberries 700 hours, raspberries 800–1,700 hours, currants and gooseberries 800–1,500 hours, and cranberries 2,000 hours.‍[^cite_note-SiteBerry-24] However, too low a temperature will kill the crops: blueberries do not tolerate temperatures below −29 °C (−20 °F), raspberries, depending on variety, may tolerate as low as −31 °C (−24 °F), and blackberries are injured below −20 °C (−4 °F).‍[^cite_note-SiteBerry-24] Spring frosts are, however, much more damaging to berry crops than low winter temperatures. Sites with moderate slopes (3%–5%), facing north or east in the [Northern Hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Hemisphere), near large bodies of water, which regulate spring temperature, are considered ideal in preventing spring frost injury to the new leaves and flowers.‍[^cite_note-SiteBerry-24] All berry crops have shallow [root systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_systems).‍[^cite_note-SiteBerry-24] Many US [land-grant university](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land-grant_university) extension offices suggest that strawberries should not be planted more than five years on the same site, due to the danger of [black root rot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_root_rot) (though many other illnesses go by the same name), which in the past has been controlled in major commercial production by annual [methyl bromide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methyl_bromide) fumigation[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-OrganicBerry-26] but which is largely prohibited now.‍ Besides the number of years in production, [soil compaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_compaction), the frequency of fumigation, and herbicide usages increase the appearance of black root rot in strawberries.‍[^cite_note-OrganicBerry-26] Raspberries, blackberries, strawberries, and many other berries are susceptible to [verticillium wilt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verticillium_wilt). Blueberries and cranberries grow poorly if the clay or silt content of the soil is more than 20%, while most other berries tolerate a wide range of [soil types](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_type).‍[^cite_note-SiteBerry-24] For most berry crops, the ideal soil is well drained sandy [loam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loam), with [a pH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_pH) of 6.2–6.8 and a moderate to high organic content; however, blueberries have an ideal pH of 4.2–4.8 and can be grown on muck soils, while blueberries and cranberries prefer poorer soils with lower [cation exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cation-exchange_capacity), lower calcium, and lower levels of phosphorus.‍[^cite_note-SiteBerry-24]

Growing most berries organically requires the use of proper [crop rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop_rotation), the right mix of [cover crops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cover_crop), and the cultivation of the correct [beneficial microorganisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_microbes) in the soil.‍[^cite_note-OrganicBerry-26] As blueberries and cranberries thrive in soils that are not hospitable to most other plants, and conventional fertilizers are toxic to them, the primary concern when growing them organically is bird management.‍[^cite_note-OrganicBerry-26]

Postharvest small fruit berries are generally stored at 90%–95% [relative humidity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_humidity) and 0 °C (32 °F).‍[^cite_note-StoreBerry-27] Cranberries, however, are frost sensitive, and should be stored at 3 °C (37 °F).‍[^cite_note-StoreBerry-27] Blueberries are the only berries that respond to [ethylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethylene), but flavor does not improve after harvest, so they require the same treatment as other berries. Removal of ethylene may reduce disease and spoilage in all berries.‍[^cite_note-StoreBerry-27] Precooling within one to two hours post-harvest to storage temperature, generally 0 °C (32 °F), via forced air cooling increases the storage life of berries by about a third.‍[^cite_note-StoreBerry-27] Under optimum storage conditions, raspberries and blackberries last for two to five days, strawberries 7–10 days, blueberries two to four weeks, and cranberries two to four months.‍[^cite_note-StoreBerry-27] Berries can be shipped under high carbon dioxide or [modified atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modified_atmosphere) of 10%–15% carbon dioxide for high carbon dioxide or 15%–20% carbon dioxide and 5%–10% oxygen for a modified atmosphere container to increase shelf life and prevent grey mold rot.‍[^cite_note-StoreBerry-27]

### Breeding

Several discoveries in the science of breeding berries were made in the 18th century by Antoine Nicolas Duchesne in his work on strawberries.‍[^cite_note-duchesne-14] In the traditional technique of [plant breeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_breeding), berries with specific desirable characteristics were chosen and allowed to sexually reproduce with other berries, and offspring with improved traits could then be selected and used for further crossing. Plants may be [hybridized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_hybrid) with different species within the same [genus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genus); hybridization between different genus may also be possible, but more difficult. Breeding may seek to increase the size and yield of the fruit, improve the flavor and quality of its nutrient content, such as antioxidants, expand the harvest season, and produce cultivars with resistance to diseases, tolerance of hot or cold conditions, and other desirable traits.‍[^cite_note-folta-28] Advancements in [molecular biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_biology) and [genetic engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_engineering) allow for a more efficient and better targeted approach in the selection for a desirable [genotype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genotype), via [marker-assisted selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marker-assisted_selection), for example.‍[^cite_note-29] [Genetic modification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetically_modified_organism) techniques can also be used for breeding berries.‍[^cite_note-folta-28]

In certain regions, berrypicking can be a large part of the economy, and it is becoming increasingly common for [western European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_European) countries such as Sweden and Finland to [import cheap labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Migrant_labor) from Thailand or Bulgaria to do berry picking.‍[^cite_note-Economist-32][^cite_note-33] This practice has come under scrutiny in the past years because of the low wages and poor living standard for the "berry-pickers", as well as the lack of worker safety.‍[^cite_note-Economist-32]

## Phytochemicals and color

Once [ripened](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ripen), berries have a contrasting color to their background (often of green leaves), making them visible and attractive to [frugivorous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frugivorous) animals and birds.‍[^cite_note-lee-35] This assists the wide [dispersal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_dispersal) of the plants' seeds.‍[^cite_note-lee-35]

Berry colors are due to natural [phytochemicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phytochemical), including [plant pigments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_pigment), such as [anthocyanins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthocyanin), together with other [flavonoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flavonoid) localized mainly in berry [skins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peel_\(fruit\)), seeds and leaves.‍[^cite_note-lee-35][^cite_note-36][^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38] Although berry pigments have [antioxidant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antioxidant) properties *[in vitro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_vitro)*,‍[^cite_note-39] there is no [physiological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physiological) evidence established to date that berry pigments have actual antioxidant or any other functions within the human body.‍[^cite_note-40] Consequently, it is not permitted to claim that foods containing polyphenols have antioxidant health value on product labels in the US or Europe.‍[^cite_note-41][^cite_note-efsa-42]

### Use in baked goods

A slice of blueberry pie

Blueberry

Dark red jam on bread

[Elderberry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elderberry)

Berries are commonly used in pies or [tarts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tart), such as [blueberry pie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blueberry_pie), [blackberry pie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackberry_pie), and [strawberry pie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strawberry_pie).

Berries are often used in baking, such as blueberry muffins, blackberry muffins, [berry cobblers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berry_cobbler), berry [crisps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crisp_\(dessert\)), berry cakes, berry [buckles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckle_\(food\)), berry [crumb cakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crumb_cakes), berry [teacakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teacake), and berry cookies.‍[^cite_note-DesBerry-43] Berries are commonly incorporated whole into the batter for baking, and care is often taken so as to not burst the berries. Frozen or dried berries may be preferable for some baked berry products.‍[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46] Fresh berries are also often incorporated into baked berry desserts, sometimes with [cream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cream), either as a filling to the dessert or as a topping.‍[^cite_note-DesBerry-43]

### Beverages

Berries are often added to water and/or [juiced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fruit_juice), as in [cranberry juice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cranberry_juice), which accounts for 95% of cranberry crop usage,‍[^cite_note-47] blueberry juice, [raspberry juice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raspberry_juice), [goji berry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goji_berry) juice, [acai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acai) juice, [aronia berry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aronia_berry) juice, and strawberry juice.‍[^cite_note-48][^cite_note-49] [Wine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wine) is the principal [alcoholic beverage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcoholic_beverage) made from berries (grapes). [Fruit wines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fruit_wine) are commonly made out of other berries. In most cases, sugars must be added to the berry juices in the process of [chaptalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaptalization) to increase the alcohol content of the wine.‍ [Examples of fruit wines made from berries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fruit_wine#List_of_fruits_and_plants_used_to_make_fruit_wine) include: elderberry wine, strawberry wine, blueberry wine, blackberry wine, redcurrant wine, huckleberry wine, goji wine and cranberry wine.‍[^cite_note-50][^cite_note-51][^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53] Berries are used in some [styles of beer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Styles_of_beer), particularly [framboise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Framboise) (made with raspberry) and other fruit [lambics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambic).

### Dried



Various [dried berries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dried_fruit)

[Currants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zante_currant), [raisins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raisin) and [sultanas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultana_\(grape\)) are examples of dried grape berries, and many other commercially important berries are available in dried form.

### Fruit preserves

Main article: [Fruit preserves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fruit_preserves)

Berries are perishable fruits with a short shelf life, and are often preserved by [drying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dried_fruit), [freezing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frozen_food), [pickling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pickling) or making fruit preserves. Berries such as blackberry, blueberry, [boysenberry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boysenberry), lingonberry, loganberry,‍[^cite_note-Taylor_&_Francis_2014_p._121-54] raspberry, and strawberry are used in jams and jellies. In the United States, [Native Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_Americans_in_the_United_States) were "the first to make preserves from blueberries".‍[^cite_note-Grotto_2007_p._53-55]

### Other usages

Chefs have created quick pickled soft fruit, such as blackberries,‍[^cite_note-56] strawberries,‍[^cite_note-57] and blueberries.‍[^cite_note-58] Strawberries can be battered and quickly fried in a [deep fryer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_fryer).‍[^cite_note-59][^cite_note-60] Sauces made from berries, such as cranberry sauce, can be frozen until hard, battered, and deep fried.‍[^cite_note-61] Cranberry sauce is a traditional food item for [Thanksgiving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thanksgiving), and similar sauces can be made from many other berries such as blueberries, raspberries, blackberries, and huckleberries.‍[^cite_note-62][^cite_note-63][^cite_note-64][^cite_note-65][^cite_note-66]

## In culture

### Dye

Berries have been used in some cultures for [dyeing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyeing). Many berries contain juices that can easily stain, affording use as a natural dye. For example, blackberries are useful for making dyes, especially when ripe berries can easily release juice to produce a [colorfast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorfast) effect.‍[^cite_note-brambleberriesintherain-67][^cite_note-68][^cite_note-FSDyeBerry-69] *Rubus* berries, such as blackberry, raspberry, [black raspberry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_raspberry), [dewberry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dewberry), loganberry, and [thimbleberry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thimbleberry) all produce dye colors. These were once used by Native Americans.‍[^cite_note-FSDyeBerry-69][^cite_note-Mahady_Fong_Farnsworth_2001_p._47-70] In Hawaii, the native [Hawaiian raspberry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaiian_raspberry) called ʻĀkala was used to dye [tapa cloth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tapa_cloth) with lavender and pink hues, whereas berries from the [dianella lily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dianella_lily) were used for blue coloration, and berries from the [black nightshade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_nightshade) were used to produce green coloration.‍[^cite_note-Krohn-Ching_1980_p._13-71]

## Research

Berry consumption is under [basic research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_research) for the potential to improve nutrition and affect chronic diseases.‍[^cite_note-72] A 2016 [meta-analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meta-analysis) found that berry consumption may be able to lower [body mass index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_mass_index), [low density lipoprotein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_density_lipoprotein) and [systolic blood pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systolic_blood_pressure).‍[^cite_note-Huang-73]
