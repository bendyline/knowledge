**Murder** is the [unlawful killing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unlawful_killing) of another [human](682482.md) without [justification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justification_\(jurisprudence\)) or valid [excuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excuse_\(legal\)) and committed with the necessary [intent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intention_\(criminal_law\)) as defined by the law in a specific [jurisdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisdiction_\(area\)).[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-Murder_1-2][^cite_note-Murder_2-3] This state of mind may, depending upon the jurisdiction, distinguish murder from other forms of unlawful [homicide](123451.md), such as [manslaughter](7397035.md). Manslaughter is killing committed in the absence of [*malice*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malice_\(law\)),[^cite_note-4] such as in the case of [voluntary manslaughter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voluntary_manslaughter) brought about by reasonable [provocation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provocation_\(legal\)), or [diminished capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diminished_capacity). [*Involuntary* manslaughter](7397035.md), where it is recognized, is a killing that lacks all but the most attenuated [guilty intent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mens_rea), [recklessness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recklessness_\(law\)).

Most societies consider murder to be an extremely serious crime, and thus believe that a person convicted of murder should receive harsh punishments for the purposes of [retribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retributive_justice), [deterrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deterrence_\(legal\)), [rehabilitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rehabilitation_\(penology\)), or [incapacitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime_prevention). In most countries, a person convicted of murder generally receives a long-term prison sentence, a [life sentence](148681.md), or [capital punishment](5902.md).[^cite_note-5] Some countries, states, and territories, including the [United Kingdom](31717.md) and other countries with English-derived [common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law), mandate [life imprisonment](148681.md) for murder, whether it is subdivided into [first-degree murder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-degree_murder_\(United_States_law\)) or otherwise.[^cite_note-6]

## Etymology

The modern English word "murder" descends from the [Proto-Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-European_language) *\*mŕ̥-trom* which meant "killing", a noun derived from *\*mer-* "to die".[^cite_note-7]

[Proto-Germanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Germanic), in fact, had two nouns derived from this word, later merging into the modern English noun: *\*murþrą* "death, killing, murder" (directly from Proto-Indo-Europea&#x6E;*\*mŕ̥-trom*), whence [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English) *morðor* "secret or unlawful killing of a person, murder; mortal sin, crime; punishment, torment, misery";[^cite_note-8] and *\*murþrijô* "murderer; homicide" (from the verb *\*murþrijaną* "to murder"), giving Old English *myrþra* "homicide, murder; murderer". There was a third word for "murder" in Proto-Germanic, continuing Proto-Indo-European *\*mr̥tós* "dead" (compare [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) *mors*), giving Proto-Germanic *\*murþą* "death, killing, murder" and Old English *morþ* "death, crime, murder" (compare German *Mord*).

The *-d-* first attested in [Middle English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_English) *mordre, mourdre, murder, murdre* could have been influenced by [Old French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_French) *murdre*, itself derived from the Germanic noun via [Frankish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankish_language) *\*murþra* (compare [Old High German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_High_German) *murdreo, murdiro*), though the same sound development can be seen with *burden* (from *burthen*). The alternative *murther* (attested up to the 19th century) springs directly from the Old English forms. Middle English *mordre* is a verb from Anglo-Saxon *myrðrian* from Proto-Germanic *\*murþrijaną*, or, according to the *[Oxford English Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_English_Dictionary)*, from the noun.[^cite_note-9]

## Definition

The eighteenth-century English jurist [William Blackstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Blackstone) (citing [Edward Coke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Coke)), in his *[Commentaries on the Laws of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commentaries_on_the_Laws_of_England)* set out the [common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law) definition of murder, which by this definition occurs

> when a person, of sound memory and discretion, unlawfully kills any reasonable creature in being and under the king's peace, with malice aforethought, either express or implied.[^cite_note-10]

&#x20;At common law, murder was normally punishable by death.[^cite_note-baker-11]

The elements of common law murder are:[^cite_note-Joshua_Dressler_2001-12]

- unlawful: This distinguishes murder from killings that are done within the boundaries of law, such as capital punishment, justified [self-defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-defense), or the killing of enemy [combatants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combatant) by lawful combatants as well as causing [collateral damage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collateral_damage) to [non-combatants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-combatant) during a war.

  [^cite_note-13]

- killing: At common law life ended with [cardiopulmonary arrest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiopulmonary_arrest)

  [^cite_note-Joshua_Dressler_2001-12]

  &#x20;– the total and irreversible cessation of blood circulation and respiration.

  [^cite_note-Joshua_Dressler_2001-12]

  &#x20;With advances in medical technology courts have adopted irreversible [cessation of all brain function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brain_death) as marking the end of life.

  [^cite_note-Joshua_Dressler_2001-12]

- through criminal act or omission: Killing can be committed by an act or an [omission](3577810.md).

  [^cite_note-14]

- of a human: This element presents the issue of when life begins. At common law, a fetus was not a human being.

  [^cite_note-15]

  &#x20;Life began when the fetus passed through the vagina and took its first breath.

  [^cite_note-Joshua_Dressler_2001-12]

- by another human: In early common law, [suicide](16831059.md) was considered murder.

  [^cite_note-Joshua_Dressler_2001-12]

  &#x20;The requirement that the person killed be someone other than the perpetrator excluded suicide from the definition of murder.

- with malice aforethought: Originally *[malice aforethought](2348828.md)* carried its everyday meaning – a deliberate and premeditated (prior intent) killing of another motivated by ill will. Murder necessarily required that an appreciable time pass between the formation and execution of the intent to kill. The courts broadened the scope of murder by eliminating the requirement of actual premeditation and deliberation as well as true malice. All that was required for malice aforethought to exist is that the perpetrator act with one of the four states of mind that constitutes "malice".

In contrast with [manslaughter](7397035.md), murder requires the mental element known as malice aforethought. Mitigating factors that weigh against a finding of intent to kill, such as "loss of control" or "diminished responsibility", may result in the reduction of a murder charge to voluntary manslaughter.[^cite_note-baker-11]

The four states of mind recognized as constituting "malice" are:[^cite_note-16]

1. [Intent to kill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intention_\(criminal_law\)),

2. Intent to inflict [grievous bodily harm](1239484.md) short of death,

3. Reckless indifference to an unjustifiably high risk to human life (sometimes described as an "abandoned and malignant heart"), or

4. Intent to commit a dangerous [felony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felony) (the "[felony murder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felony_murder)" doctrine).

Under state of mind (i), intent to kill, the *deadly weapon rule* applies. Thus, if the defendant intentionally uses a [deadly weapon](1182076.md) or instrument against the victim, such use authorizes a permissive inference of intent to kill. Examples of deadly weapons and instruments include but are not limited to guns, knives, deadly toxins or chemicals or gases and even vehicles when intentionally used to harm one or more victims.

Under state of mind (iii), an "abandoned and malignant heart", the killing must result from the defendant's conduct involving a reckless indifference to human life and a conscious disregard of an unreasonable risk of death or serious bodily injury. In Australian jurisdictions, the unreasonable risk must amount to a foreseen probability of death (or grievous bodily harm in most states), as opposed to possibility.[^cite_note-R_v_Crabbe-17]

Under state of mind (iv), the felony-murder doctrine, the felony committed must be an inherently dangerous felony, such as burglary, arson, rape, robbery or kidnapping. Importantly, the underlying felony *cannot* be a [lesser included offense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesser_included_offense) such as assault, otherwise all criminal homicides would be murder as all are felonies.

In Spanish criminal law,[^cite_note-18] *asesinato* (literally 'assassination'): takes place when any of these requirements concur: Treachery (the use of means to avoid risk for the aggressor or to ensure that the crime goes unpunished), price or reward (financial gain) or viciousness (deliberately increasing the pain of the victim). After the last reform of the [Spanish Criminal Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Criminal_Code), in force since July 1, 2015, another circumstance that turns homicide (*homicidio*) into assassination is the desire to facilitate the commission of another crime or to prevent it from being discovered.[^cite_note-19]

As with most legal terms, the precise definition of murder varies between jurisdictions and is usually codified in some form of legislation. Even when the legal distinction between murder and manslaughter is clear, it is not unknown for a jury to find a murder defendant guilty of the lesser offense. The jury might sympathize with the defendant (e.g. in a [crime of passion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime_of_passion), or in the case of a bullied victim who kills their tormentor), and the jury may wish to protect the defendant from a sentence of life imprisonment or execution.

### Degrees&#x20;

"Murder in the second degree" and "Premeditated murder" redirect here. For the album, see [Murder in the Second Degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murder_in_the_Second_Degree). For the film, see [Premeditated Murder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premeditated_Murder).

Some jurisdictions divide murder by degrees. The distinction between first- and second-degree murder exists, for example, in [Canadian murder law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murder_\(Canadian_law\)) and [U.S. murder law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murder_\(United_States_law\)#Degrees). Some US states maintain the offense of [capital murder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_murder).

The most common division is between first- and second-degree murder. Generally, second-degree murder is common law murder, and first-degree is an aggravated form. The aggravating factors of first-degree murder depend on the jurisdiction, but may include a specific intent to kill, premeditation, or deliberation. In some, murders committed by acts such as [strangulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strangulation), [poisoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisoning), or [lying in wait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lying_in_wait) are also treated as first-degree murder.[^cite_note-20] A few states in the U.S. further distinguish [third-degree murder](26346214.md#Degrees), but they differ significantly in which kinds of murders they classify as second-degree vis-à-vis third-degree. For example, Minnesota and Pennsylvania define third-degree murder as [depraved-heart murder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depraved-heart_murder) (which in most U.S. jurisdictions is called second-degree), whereas Florida defines third-degree murder as [felony murder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felony_murder) (except when the underlying felony is specifically listed in the definition of first-degree murder).[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-22][^cite_note-23]

Some jurisdictions also distinguish premeditated murder. This is the [crime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime) of wrongfully and intentionally causing the death of another human being (also known as murder) after rationally considering the timing or method of doing so, in order to either increase the likelihood of success, or to evade detection or apprehension.[^cite_note-24] State laws in the United States vary as to definitions of "premeditation". In some states, premeditation may be construed as taking place mere seconds before the murder. Premeditated murder is one of the most serious forms of homicide, and is punished more severely than manslaughter or other types of homicide, often with a [life sentence](148681.md) without the possibility of [parole](342305.md), or in some countries, the [death penalty](5902.md). In the U.S., [federal law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_federal_law) ([18 U.S.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title_18_of_the_United_States_Code) [§ 1111(a)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1111#a)) criminalizes premeditated murder, felony murder and second-degree murder committed under situations where federal jurisdiction applies.[^cite_note-25] In Canada, the [criminal code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_Code_\(Canada\)) classifies murder as either first- or second-degree. The former type of murder is often called premeditated murder, although premeditation is not the only way murder can be classified as first-degree. In the [Netherlands](21148.md), the traditional strict distinction between premeditated intentional killing (classed as murder, *moord*) and non-premeditated intentional killing (manslaughter, *doodslag*) is maintained; when differentiating between murder and manslaughter, the only relevant factor is the existence or not of premeditation (rather than the existence or not of mitigating or aggravated factors). Manslaughter (non-premeditated intentional killing) with aggravating factors is punished more severely, but it is not classified as murder, because murder is an offense which always requires premeditation.[^cite_note-26]

### Common law

According to Blackstone, English [common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law) identified murder as a *public wrong*.[^cite_note-27] According to common law, murder is considered to be *[malum in se](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malum_in_se)*, that is, an act which is evil within itself. An act such as murder is wrong or evil by its very nature, and it is the very nature of the act which does not require any specific detailing or definition in the law to consider murder a crime.[^cite_note-28]

Some jurisdictions still take a common law view of murder. In such jurisdictions, what is considered to be murder is defined by [precedent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precedent) case law or previous decisions of the courts of law. However, although the common law is by nature flexible and adaptable, in the interests both of certainty and of securing convictions, most common law jurisdictions have [codified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codification_\(law\)) their criminal law and now have statutory definitions of murder.

### Exclusions

#### General

Although laws vary by country, there are circumstances of exclusion that are common in many legal systems.

- The killing of enemy combatants who have not surrendered, when committed by lawful combatants in accordance with lawful orders in war, is generally not considered murder. Illicit killings within a war may constitute murder or homicidal [war crimes](48626.md); see [Laws of war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laws_of_war).

- [Self-defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_of_self-defense): acting in self-defense or in defense of another person is generally accepted as legal justification for killing a person in situations that would otherwise have been murder. However, a self-defense killing might be considered manslaughter if the killer established control of the situation before the killing took place, such as [imperfect self-defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperfect_self-defense). In the case of self-defense, it is called a "justifiable homicide".

  [^cite_note-crim_liability-29]

- Unlawful killings without malice or intent are considered manslaughter.

- In many common law countries, [provocation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provocation_\(legal\)) is a partial defense to a charge of murder which acts by converting what would otherwise have been murder into manslaughter (this is voluntary manslaughter, which is more severe than involuntary manslaughter).

- Accidental killings are considered homicides. Depending on the circumstances, these may or may not be considered criminal offenses; they are often considered manslaughter.

- Suicide does not constitute murder in most societies. Assisting a suicide, however, may be considered murder in some circumstances.

#### Specific to certain countries

- [Capital punishment](5902.md): some countries practice the death penalty. Capital punishment may be ordered by a legitimate court of law as the result of a conviction in a [criminal trial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_trial) with [due process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Due_process) for a serious crime. All member states of the [Council of Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Europe) are prohibited from using the death penalty.

  [^cite_note-30]

- [Euthanasia](9587.md), doctor-assisted suicide: the administration of lethal drugs by a doctor to a [terminally ill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminally_ill) patient, if the intention is solely to alleviate pain, in many jurisdictions it is seen as a special case (see the doctrine of [double effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_effect) and the case of [Dr John Bodkin Adams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Bodkin_Adams)).

  [^cite_note-google1-31]

- Killing to prevent the theft of one's property may be legal under certain circumstances, depending on the jurisdiction.

  [^cite_note-32]

  [^cite_note-33]

  &#x20;In 2013, a jury in south Texas acquitted a man who killed a [sex worker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sex_worker) who attempted to run away with his money.

  [^cite_note-34]

- Killing an intruder who is found by an owner to be in the owner's home (having entered unlawfully): legal in most US states (see [Castle doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castle_doctrine)).

  [^cite_note-njsd-35]

- Killing to prevent specific forms of aggravated rape or [sexual assault](218407.md) – killing of attacker by the potential victim or by witnesses to the scene; legal in parts of the US and in various other countries.

  [^cite_note-36]

- In some countries, the killing for what are considered reasons connected to [family honor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_honor), usually involving killing due to sexual, religious or [caste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caste) reasons (known as [honor killing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honor_killing)), committed frequently by a husband, father or male relative of the victim, is not considered murder; it may not be considered a criminal act or it may be considered a criminal offense other than murder.

  [^cite_note-37]

  [^cite_note-38]

  &#x20;International law, including the [Istanbul Convention](31752276.md) (the first legally binding convention against [domestic violence](18978563.md) and [violence against women](3634424.md)) prohibits these types of killings (see Article 42 – Unacceptable justifications for crimes, including crimes committed in the name of so-called honor).

  [^cite_note-39]

- In the United States, in most states and in federal jurisdiction, a killing by a police officer is excluded from prosecution if the officer reasonably believes they are being threatened with deadly force by the victim. This may include such actions by the victim as reaching into a glove compartment or pocket for license and registration, if the officer reasonably believes that the victim might be reaching for a gun.

  [^cite_note-NYT72816-40]

### Victim

All jurisdictions require that the victim be a natural person; that is, a human being who was still alive before being murdered. In other words, under the law one cannot murder a [corpse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cadaver), a corporation, a non-human animal, or any other non-human organism such as a plant or bacterium.

[California](5407.md)'s murder statute, [penal code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Penal_Code) [section 187](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murder_\(California\)), expressly mentioned a fetus as being capable of being killed, and was interpreted by the [Supreme Court of California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_California) in 1994 as not requiring any proof of the viability of the fetus as a prerequisite to a murder conviction.[^cite_note-davis-41] This holding has two implications. Firstly, a defendant in California can be convicted of murder for killing a fetus which the mother herself could have terminated without committing a crime.[^cite_note-davis-41] And secondly, as stated by Justice [Stanley Mosk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Mosk) in his dissent, because women carrying nonviable fetuses may not be visibly pregnant, it may be possible for a defendant to be convicted of intentionally murdering a person they did not know existed.[^cite_note-davis-41]

### Mitigating circumstances

Some countries allow conditions that "affect the balance of the mind" to be regarded as [mitigating circumstances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitigating_factor). This means that a person may be found guilty of "manslaughter" on the basis of "diminished responsibility" rather than being found guilty of murder, if it can be proved that the killer was suffering from a condition that affected their judgment at the time. [Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinical_depression), [post-traumatic stress disorder](82974.md) and medication [side-effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adverse_effect_\(medicine\)) are examples of conditions that may be taken into account when assessing responsibility.

#### Insanity

Main articles: [Insanity defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insanity_defense) and [M'Naghten rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M'Naghten_rules)

[Mental disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_Disorder_\(Insanity\)_Defense) may apply to a wide range of disorders including [psychosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychosis) caused by [schizophrenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schizophrenia) and [dementia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dementia), and excuse the person from the need to undergo the stress of a trial as to liability. Usually, [sociopathy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antisocial_personality_disorder) and other [personality disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personality_disorders) are not legally considered insanity. In some jurisdictions, following the pre-trial hearing to determine the extent of the disorder, the defense of "not guilty by reason of insanity" may be used to get a not guilty verdict.[^cite_note-42] This defense has two elements:

- That the [defendant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defendant) had a serious mental illness, disease, or defect

- That the defendant's mental condition, at the time of the killing, rendered the perpetrator unable to determine right from wrong, or that what they were doing was wrong

Under [New York](8210131.md) law, for example:

> § 40.15 Mental disease or defect. In any prosecution for an offense, it is an affirmative defence that when the defendant engaged in the proscribed conduct, he lacked criminal responsibility by reason of mental disease or defect. Such lack of criminal responsibility means that at the time of such conduct, as a result of mental disease or defect, he lacked substantial capacity to know or appreciate either: 1. The nature and consequences of such conduct; or 2. That such conduct was wrong.

— [N.Y. Penal Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N.Y._Penal_Law), § 40.15[^cite_note-43]

Under the [French Penal Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Penal_Code_of_1791):

> Article 122-1
>
> - A person is not criminally liable who, when the act was committed, was suffering from a psychological or neuropsychological disorder which destroyed his discernment or his ability to control his actions.
>
> - A person who, at the time he acted, was suffering from a psychological or neuropsychological disorder which reduced his discernment or impeded his ability to control his actions, remains punishable; however, the court shall take this into account when it decides the penalty and determines its regime.

— Penal Code §122-1 found at [Legifrance web site](http://195.83.177.9/code/liste.phtml?lang=uk\&c=33\&r=3627)

Those who successfully argue a defense based on a mental disorder are usually referred to mandatory clinical treatment until they are certified safe to be released back into the community, rather than prison.[^cite_note-french_civil_law_on_mental_health-44]

#### Postpartum depression

[Postpartum depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postpartum_depression) (also known as post-natal depression) is recognized in some countries as a mitigating factor in cases of [infanticide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infanticide). According to Susan Friedman, "Two dozen nations have infanticide laws that decrease the penalty for mothers who kill their children of up to one year of age. The United States does not have such a law, but mentally ill mothers may plead not guilty by reason of insanity."[^cite_note-45] In the [law of the Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_the_Republic_of_Ireland), infanticide was made a separate crime from murder in 1949, applicable for the mother of a baby under one year old where "the balance of her mind was disturbed by reason of her not having fully recovered from the effect of giving birth to the child or by reason of the effect of lactation consequent upon the birth of the child".[^cite_note-46] Since independence, death sentences for murder in such cases had always been [commuted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutation_of_sentence);[^cite_note-Rattigan2008-47] the new act was intended "to eliminate all the terrible ritual of the [black cap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_cap) and the solemn words of the judge pronouncing sentence of death in those cases ... where it is clear to the Court and to everybody, except perhaps the unfortunate accused, that the sentence will never be carried out."[^cite_note-48] In Russia, murder of a newborn child by the mother has been a separate crime since 1996.[^cite_note-49]

#### Unintentional

For a killing to be considered murder in nine out of fifty states in the US, there normally needs to be an element of intent. A defendant may argue that they took precautions not to kill, that the death could not have been anticipated, or was unavoidable. As a general rule, [manslaughter](7397035.md)[^cite_note-manslaughter_in_France-50] constitutes [reckless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recklessness_\(law\)) killing, but manslaughter also includes criminally negligent (i.e. grossly negligent) homicide.[^cite_note-invol_offences-51] Unintentional killing that results from an involuntary action generally cannot constitute murder.[^cite_note-52] After examining the evidence, a judge or jury (depending on the jurisdiction) would determine whether the killing was intentional or unintentional.

#### Diminished capacity

In jurisdictions using the Uniform Penal Code, such as California, [diminished capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diminished_capacity) may be a defense. For example, [Dan White](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_White) used this defense[^cite_note-53] to obtain a manslaughter conviction, instead of murder, in the [assassination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscone%E2%80%93Milk_assassinations) of Mayor [George Moscone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Moscone) and Supervisor [Harvey Milk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvey_Milk). Afterward, California amended its penal code to provide "As a matter of public policy there shall be no defense of diminished capacity, diminished responsibility, or irresistible impulse in a criminal action...."[^cite_note-54]

In the United States and Canada, these murders are referred to as [first-degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_degree_murder) or [aggravated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aggravation_\(law\)) murders.[^cite_note-murdercanada-67] Under [English criminal law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_criminal_law), [murder](3768697.md) always carries a mandatory [life sentence](3275619.md), but is not classified into degrees. Penalties for murder committed under aggravating circumstances are often higher under English law than the 15-year minimum non-parole period that otherwise serves as a starting point for a murder committed by an adult.

### Felony murder rule

Main article: [Felony murder rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felony_murder_rule)

A legal [doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctrine) in some common law jurisdictions broadens the crime of murder: when an offender kills in the commission of a dangerous crime, (regardless of intent), he or she is guilty of murder. The felony murder rule is often justified by its supporters as a means of preventing dangerous felonies,[^cite_note-68] but the case of [Ryan Holle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ryan_Holle)[^cite_note-69] shows it can be used very widely.

The felony-murder reflects the versari in re illicita: the offender is objectively responsible for the event of the unintentional crime;[^cite_note-70] in fact the figure of the civil law systems corresponding to felony murder is the [preterintentional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preterintention) homicide (art. 222-7 French penal code,[^cite_note-71][^cite_note-72][^cite_note-73] art. 584 Italian penal code,[^cite_note-74] art. 227 German penal code[^cite_note-75] etc.). Felony murder contrasts with the principle of guilt, for which in England it was, at least formally, abolished in 1957, in Canada it was quashed by the Supreme Court, while in the USA it continues to survive.[^cite_note-76][^cite_note-77][^cite_note-78]

### Year-and-a-day rule

Main article: [Year and a day rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Year_and_a_day_rule)

In some common law jurisdictions, a [defendant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defendant) accused of murder is not guilty if the victim survives for longer than [one year and one day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Year_and_a_day_rule) after the attack.[^cite_note-79] This reflects the likelihood that if the victim dies, other factors will have contributed to the cause of death, breaking the chain of [causation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causation_\(law\)); and also means that the responsible person does not have a charge of murder "hanging over their head indefinitely".[^cite_note-80] Subject to any [statute of limitations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute_of_limitations), the accused could still be charged with an offense reflecting the seriousness of the initial assault.

With advances in modern medicine, most countries have abandoned a fixed time period and test causation on the facts of the case. This is known as "delayed death" and cases where this was applied or was attempted to be applied go back to at least 1966.[^cite_note-81]

In England and Wales, the "year-and-a-day rule" was abolished by the [Law Reform (Year and a Day Rule) Act 1996](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_Reform_\(Year_and_a_Day_Rule\)_Act_1996). However, if death occurs three years or more after the original attack then prosecution can take place only with the [attorney-general](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attorney-general)'s approval.

In the United States, many jurisdictions have abolished the rule as well.[^cite_note-google-82][^cite_note-google2-83] Abolition of the rule has been accomplished by enactment of statutory criminal codes, which displaced the common-law definitions of crimes and corresponding defenses. In 2001 the [Supreme Court of the United States](31737.md) held that retroactive application of a state supreme court decision abolishing the year-and-a-day rule did not violate the [Ex Post Facto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ex_post_facto_law) Clause of [Article I of the United States Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_One_of_the_United_States_Constitution).[^cite_note-84]

The potential effect of fully abolishing the rule can be seen in the case of 74-year-old William Barnes, charged with the murder of a Philadelphia police officer Walter T. Barclay Jr., who he had shot nearly 41 years previously. Barnes had served 16 years in prison for attempting to murder Barkley, but when the policeman died on 19 August 2007, this was alleged to be from complications of the wounds suffered from the shooting – and Barnes was charged with his murder. He was acquitted on May 24, 2010.[^cite_note-85]

## Contributing factors

Certain [personality disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personality_disorder) are associated with an increased homicide rate, most notably narcissistic, anti-social, and histrionic personality disorders and those associated with psychopathology.[^cite_note-86]

Several studies have shown that there is a correlation between murder rates and [poverty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poverty).[^cite_note-87][^cite_note-88][^cite_note-89][^cite_note-Barata-2000-90] A 2000 study showed that regions of the state of [São Paulo](229422.md) in Brazil with lower income also had higher rates of murder.[^cite_note-Barata-2000-90]

## Historical attitudes



A group of [Thugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thugee) strangling a traveller on a highway in the early 19th century

In the past, certain types of homicide were lawful and justified. Georg Oesterdiekhoff wrote:

> [Evans-Pritchard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._E._Evans-Pritchard) says about the [Nuer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuer_people) from Sudan: "Homicide is not forbidden, and Nuer do not think it wrong to kill a man in fair fight. On the contrary, a man who slays another in combat is admired for his courage and skill." (Evans-Pritchard 1956: 195) This statement is true for most African tribes, for pre-modern Europeans, for Indigenous Australians, and for Native Americans, according to ethnographic reports from all over the world. ... Homicides rise to incredible numbers among [headhunter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Headhunting) cultures such as the [Papua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Guinea). When a boy is born, the father has to kill a man. He needs a name for his child and can receive it only by a man, he himself has murdered. When a man wants to marry, he must kill a man. When a man dies, his family again has to kill a man.[^cite_note-91]

In many such societies the redress was not via a legal system, but by [blood revenge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_feud), although there might also be a form of payment that could be made instead—such as the [weregild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weregild) which in early [Germanic society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_society) could be paid to the victim's family [in lieu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_lieu) of their right of revenge.

One of the oldest-known prohibitions against murder appears in the Sumerian [Code of Ur-Nammu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_of_Ur-Nammu) written sometime between 2100 and 2050 [BC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Before_Christ). The code states, "If a man commits a murder, that man must be killed." In the [Abrahamic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrahamic_religion), the first ever murder was committed by [Cain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cain) against his brother Abel out of [jealousy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jealousy).[^cite_note-92] The [prohibition against murder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murder_in_the_Bible) is one of the [Ten Commandments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_Commandments) given by God to [Moses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses) in [Exodus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Exodus) and [Deuteronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Deuteronomy), which are part of the scripture for both Jews and Christians. In [Islam](6037917.md) according to the [Qur'an](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qur'an), one of the [greatest sins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_views_on_sin) is to kill a human being who has committed no fault.[^cite_note-93] The term *assassin* derives from [Hashshashin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hashshashin),[^cite_note-94] a militant [Ismaili](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ismaili) Shi'ite sect, active from the 8th to 14th centuries. This mystic [secret society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secret_society) killed members of the [Abbasid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid), [Fatimid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatimid), [Seljuq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seljuq_dynasty) and [Crusader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusade) elite for political and religious reasons.[^cite_note-95] The [Thuggee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thuggee) cult that plagued India was devoted to [Kali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kali), the goddess of death and destruction.[^cite_note-96][^cite_note-97] According to some estimates the Thugs murdered one million people between 1740 and 1840.[^cite_note-98] The [Aztecs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aztec) believed that without regular offerings of blood the sun god [Huitzilopochtli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huitzilopochtli) would withdraw his support for them and destroy the world as they knew it.[^cite_note-99] According to [Ross Hassig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ross_Hassig), author of *Aztec Warfare*, "between 10,000 and 80,400 persons" were [sacrificed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_sacrifice) in the 1487 re-consecration of the [Great Pyramid of Tenochtitlan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Pyramid_of_Tenochtitlan).[^cite_note-100][^cite_note-101] Japanese [samurai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samurai) had the [right to strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiri-sute_gomen) with their sword at anyone of a [lower class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caste) who compromised their honour.[^cite_note-102]

### Slavery

Southern slave codes did make willful killing of a [slave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_the_United_States) illegal in most cases.[^cite_note-103] For example, the 1860 [Mississippi](16949861.md) case of *Oliver v. State* charged the defendant with murdering his own slave.[^cite_note-104] In 1811, the wealthy white planter [Arthur Hodge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_William_Hodge) was hanged for murdering several of his slaves on his plantation in the [Virgin Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Virgin_Islands).[^cite_note-105]

### Honor killings in Corsica

In [Corsica](5714828.md), [vendetta](292136.md) was a social code that required Corsicans to kill anyone who wronged their family honor. Between 1821 and 1852, no fewer than 4,300 murders were perpetrated in Corsica.[^cite_note-106]

### Abortion

Opponents of [abortion](765.md) consider abortion a form of murder, treating the [foetus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foetus) as a person.[^cite_note-107][^cite_note-108] In some countries, a foetus is a [legal person](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_person) who can be murdered,[^cite_note-110] and killing someone who is [pregnant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pregnant) is considered a double homicide.[^cite_note-111]

### Historical variation

According to scholar Pieter Spierenburg [homicide rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homicide_rates) per 100,000 in Europe have fallen over the centuries, from 35 per 100,000 in medieval times, to 20 in 1500 AD, five in 1700, to below two per 100,000 in 1900.[^cite_note-Spierenburg-118]

In the United States, murder rates have been higher and have fluctuated. They fell below 2 per 100,000 by 1900, rose during the first half of the century, dropped in the years following World War II, and bottomed out at 4.0 in 1957 before rising again.[^cite_note-119] The rate stayed in 9 to 10 range most of the period from 1972 to 1994, before falling to 5 in present times.[^cite_note-Spierenburg-118] The increase since 1957 would have been even greater if not for the significant improvements in medical techniques and emergency response times, which mean that more and more attempted homicide victims survive. According to one estimate, if the lethality levels of criminal assaults of 1964 still applied in 1993, the country would have seen the murder rate of around 26 per 100,000, almost triple the actually observed rate of 9.5 per 100,000.[^cite_note-harristhomas-120]



The historical homicide rate in [Stockholm](26741.md) since 1400 AD. The murder rate was very high in the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages). The rate has declined greatly: from 45/100,000 to a low of 0.6 in the 1950s. The last decades have seen the homicide rate rise slowly.

A similar, but less pronounced pattern has been seen in major European countries as well. The murder rate in the United Kingdom fell to 1 per 100,000 by the beginning of the 20th century and as low as 0.62 per 100,000 in 1960, and was at 1.28 per 100,000 as of 2009. The murder rate in France (excluding Corsica) bottomed out after World War II at less than 0.4 per 100,000, quadrupling to 1.6 per 100,000 since then.[^cite_note-121]

The specific factors driving these dynamics in murder rates are complex and not universally agreed upon. Much of the raise in the U.S. murder rate during the first half of the 20th century is generally thought to be attributed to gang violence associated with [Prohibition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prohibition_in_the_United_States). Since most murders are committed by young males, the near simultaneous low in the murder rates of major developed countries circa 1960 can be attributed to low birth rates during the Great Depression and World War II. Causes of further moves are more controversial. Some of the more exotic factors claimed to affect murder rates include the availability of abortion[^cite_note-122] and the likelihood of chronic exposure to [lead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_poisoning#Nervous_system) during childhood (due to the use of leaded paint in houses and [tetraethyllead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetraethyllead) as a gasoline additive in internal combustion engines).[^cite_note-123]

### Rates by country

[Murder rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_murder_rate) vary greatly among countries and societies around the world. In the [Western world](21208200.md), murder rates in most countries have declined significantly during the 20th century and are now between 1 and 4 cases per 100,000 people per year. [Latin America and the Caribbean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_America_and_the_Caribbean), the region with the highest murder rate in the world,[^cite_note-124] experienced more than 2.5 million murders between 2000 and 2017.[^cite_note-125]



UNODC: Per 100,000 population (2011)

Murder rates in jurisdictions such as Japan, [Singapore](27318.md), [Hong Kong](13404.md), Iceland, Switzerland, Italy, Spain and Germany are among the lowest in the world, around 0.3–1 cases per 100,000 people per year; the rate of the United States is among the highest of [developed countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developed_countries), around 4.5 in 2014,[^cite_note-126] with rates in larger cities sometimes over 40 per 100,000.[^cite_note-127] The top ten highest murder rates are in Honduras (91.6 per 100,000), El Salvador, Ivory Coast, Venezuela, Belize, Jamaica, [U.S. Virgin Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Virgin_Islands), Guatemala, Saint Kitts and Nevis and Zambia. #### United States



Since 1900, US homicide rates have varied by a factor of more than 2.

[^cite_note-CCJ_202601-141]

In the United States, 666,160 people were killed between 1960 and 1996.[^cite_note-142] Approximately 90% of murders in the US are committed by males.[^cite_note-143] Between 1976 and 2005, 23.5% of all murder victims and 64.8% of victims murdered by intimate partners were female.[^cite_note-144] For women in the US, homicide is the leading cause of death in the workplace.[^cite_note-ojp.usdoj.gov-145]

In the US, murder is the leading cause of death for [African American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_American) males aged 15 to 34. Between 1976 and 2008, African Americans were victims of 329,825 homicides.[^cite_note-146][^cite_note-147] In 2006, the [Federal Bureau of Investigation](11127.md)'s Supplementary Homicide Report indicated that nearly half of the 14,990 murder victims that year were black (7421).[^cite_note-148] In 2007, there were 3,221 black victims and 3,587 white victims of non-negligent homicides. While 2,905 of the black victims were killed by a black offender, 2,918 of the white victims were killed by white offenders. There were 566 white victims of black offenders and 245 black victims of white offenders.[^cite_note-149] The "white" category in the [Uniform Crime Reports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_Crime_Reports) (UCR) includes non-black [Hispanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispanic_and_Latino_Americans).[^cite_note-150] Murder demographics are affected by the improvement of [trauma care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trauma_care), which has resulted in reduced lethality of violent assaults – thus the murder rate may not necessarily indicate the overall level of social violence.[^cite_note-harristhomas-120]

Workplace homicide, which tripled during the 1980s, is the fastest growing category of murder in America.[^cite_note-ojp.usdoj.gov-145][^cite_note-151][^cite_note-152]

Development of murder rates over time in different countries is often used by both supporters and opponents of capital punishment and [gun control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gun_control). Using properly filtered data, it is possible to make the case for or against either of these issues. For example, one could look at murder rates in the United States from 1950 to 2000,[^cite_note-153] and notice that those rates went up sharply shortly after a [moratorium on death sentences](412425.md) was effectively imposed in the late 1960s. This fact has been used to argue that capital punishment serves as a deterrent and, as such, it is morally justified. Capital punishment opponents frequently counter that the United States has much higher murder rates than Canada and most [European Union](9317.md) countries, although all those countries have abolished the death penalty. Overall, the global pattern is [too complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaos_theory), and on average, the influence of both these factors may not be significant and could be more social, economic, and cultural.

Despite the immense improvements in [forensics](45710.md) in the past few decades, the fraction of murders solved has decreased in the United States, from 90% in 1960 to 61% in 2007.[^cite_note-mccune-154] Solved murder rates in major U.S. cities varied in 2007 from 36% in [Boston](24437894.md), Massachusetts, to 76% in [San Jose, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Jose,_California).[^cite_note-monitor-155] Major factors affecting the arrest rate include witness cooperation[^cite_note-mccune-154] and the number of people assigned to investigate the case.[^cite_note-monitor-155]

## Investigation and clearance rates



The U.S. has a low clearance rate—cases solved or arrests made. Murder clearance rates of Australia, Britain and Germany are in the 70s and 90s percent range, compared to the U.S. in the 50s.

[^cite_note-NYTimes_20250706-156]

The success rate of [criminal investigations](5236980.md) into murders (the [clearance rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime_clearance_rate)) tends to be relatively high for murder compared to other crimes, due to its seriousness. In the United States the 1962 clearance rate was 93%, however this had fallen to 64% by 1994. The clearance rate then stayed relatively stable, albeit with some variation, but by 2020 the clearance rate had fallen to 54%.[^cite_note-157]
