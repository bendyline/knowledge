**Khuzestan province** ([\[xuːzest̪ʰɒːn\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Persian) [//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/b/b4/Khuzestan-fa.ogg/Khuzestan-fa.ogg.mp3](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/b/b4/Khuzestan-fa.ogg/Khuzestan-fa.ogg.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup>; [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language): استان خوزستان)[^cite_note-4] is one of the 31 [Provinces of Iran](448160.md). An extension of the [Mesopotamian Plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia), it is located in the southwest of the country, bordering [Iraq](7515928.md) and the [Persian Gulf](24761.md), and covering an area of 64,055 square kilometres (24,732 mi<sup>2</sup>). Its capital is the city of [Ahvaz](599079.md).[^cite_note-Khuzestan_province_Structure-5] The provincial capital was moved from [Shushtar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shushtar) to Ahvaz in 1924.[^cite_note-ahvaz.ir-6] Since 2014, the province has been part of Iran's [Region 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regions_of_Iran).[^cite_note-7]

Khuzestan comprises much of what historians refer to as ancient [Elam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elam), whose capital was at [Susa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susa). It was once one of the most important regions in the [Ancient Near East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Near_East). In modern Iran, there has been episodic conflict between the province's [Arab population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Arabs) and the central government. Iraq [invaded Khuzestan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Iraq_War) in 1980, and the invasion was reversed at the [Battle of Khorramshahr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Khorramshahr_\(1982\)) in 1982. Containing Iran's largest oil field, it is a key strategic province.

## Etymology

In Greek and Roman sources, from the [Achaemenid period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire) until the early centuries AD, the land now called Khuzestan was known as *Susiana*, a name derived from the city of Susa (modern [Shush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susa)), which served for a time as a capital of ancient [Elam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elam) and, under the Achaemenids, as one of the imperial capitals. According to the [Achaemenid royal inscriptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_royal_inscriptions), however, the Achaemenids themselves (c. 520–330 BC) called this satrapy *Ūja* or *Uvja*, a name that also appears in historical texts as *Hūjiya*. With the rise of later dynasties such as the [Parthians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthian_Empire) and [Sasanians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_Empire), the name *Susiana* gradually fell out of use, and in the Middle Persian period it was replaced by names derived from the root *hūz*. Arab authors of later periods recorded this as *Khūzī*.[^cite_note-VanBladel-8][^cite_note-Bosworth-9][^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11]

The word *Hūjī* occurs twenty-four times in the inscriptions of [Darius the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_the_Great) and once in an inscription of [Xerxes I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerxes_I). This same word *Ūja* was rendered by the Greeks as *Uza* and *Uzi*, and was later written *Uxi* and pronounced "Uxi".[^cite_note-Kent-12] [Strabo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strabo) and [Pliny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliny_the_Elder) refer to the inhabitants of the region as the *Uxii*.[^cite_note-13]

[The *Hūzī*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huzi_people) or *Hūjī* were a non-Semitic people who lived in southwestern [Iran](14653.md) from well before the Christian era, with their capital at Susa. The word **Khuzestan**, which until the [Safavid period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_Iran) took the form *Hūzestān*, consists of the elements *hūz* and *-stān* and means "land of the Hūz". The suffix *-stān* means "land", and older maps and documents indicate that the form *Khūzī* is an altered form of *Hūzī*.[^cite_note-Minorsky-14][^cite_note-Kent-12]

According to [Richard N. Frye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_N._Frye), following the large-scale migration of Arabs into [Iran](14653.md) from the west, in particular the migration of the [Banu Ka'b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banu_Ka'b) and *Bani Lam* into southwestern Khuzestan, the province's name changed semi-officially from *Hūzestān* to *Arabistan* in the [Safavid period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_Iran) onwards, that is from the 16th to the 19th century, until under [Reza Shah](338883.md) it was officially named [Khuzestan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khuzistan_\(Sasanian_province\)).[^cite_note-FryePeoples2-15]

*Uzistan* or *Huzistan*, meaning the land of the *Uz* or [*Huz* people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huzi_people), appears in [Middle Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Persian) ([Sasanian Pahlavi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pahlavi_scripts)) as well as in [Parthian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthian_language) as *hūjestān* or *hūžestān*.[^cite_note-Durkin-16] In [Syriac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_language) the region was called *Beth Huzaye*.[^cite_note-17] A passage in a Parthian text on the Manichaean manuscript M 5569,[^cite_note-18] which describes the time and place of [Mani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mani_\(prophet\))'s death and was written in the third century AD, reads:[^cite_note-19][^cite_note-Bosworth-9]

> pad čafār saxt šahrewar māh, šahrewar rōž, dōšambat, ud ēwandas žamān, andar **awestām ī hūžistān**, ud šahrestān če bēlābād, kaδ ahrāmād hō pidar rōšn

English translation:

> Four days into the month of Shahrewar, on the day of Shahrewar, on Monday, at the eleventh hour, in the **province of Huzistan** and in the city of Beth Lapat \[that is, [Gundeshapur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gundeshapur)], the Father of Light \[that is, Mani] ascended…[^cite_note-Vameqi-20]

The [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic)-speaking people who settled in this land after the [Muslim conquest of Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_conquest_of_Persia) preserved the pronunciation of the people's name as *Hūzī*, and referred to the Hūzīs using the Arabic [broken plural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broken_plural) on the pattern *afʿāl*, that is, *Ahvāz*. They knew the city of [Ahvaz](599079.md) as the "market of the Hūzīs" (*Sūq al-Ahvāz*).[^cite_note-Bosworth-9]

Medieval Muslim geographers treated the name as covering a wide territory. [Ibn Khordadbeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Khordadbeh) applied the name *Ahvāz* to the whole of what is now Khuzestan, describing it as an extensive land made up of seven *[kuras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kura_\(administrative_division\))* or districts, which he called *Akwār al-Ahwāz*.[^cite_note-IbnKhordadbeh-21] [Richard N. Frye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_N._Frye) likewise describes the province as a wealthy lowland region divided into at least seven *kuras*, the largest of them [Hormizd-Ardashir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hormizd-Ardashir), which the Arabs called Sūq al-Ahvāz.[^cite_note-22] [Al-Istakhri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Istakhri) placed Khuzestan between [Fars](448049.md), [Isfahan](223149.md), the [Karkheh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karkheh_River) and [Lorestan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorestan), and [Khwarizmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Khwarizmi) mentioned both the province and the [*Hūzī* language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khuzi_language) in his *[Mafatih al-Ulum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mafatih_al-Ulum)* in the second half of the tenth century.

According to Frye, following substantial Arab migration into Iran from the west, in particular the settlement of the [Banu Ka'b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banu_Ka'b) and Bani Lam in southwestern Khuzestan — the province came to be known semi-officially as *Arabistan* rather than *Hūzestān* from the beginning of the [Safavid period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_Iran) onward, that is from the 16th to the 19th century. The name *Khuzestan* was restored in official usage under [Reza Shah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reza_Shah).[^cite_note-FryePeoples-23] The older form of the name also appears in [Ferdowsi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdowsi)'s *[Shahnameh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shahnameh)*, where the inhabitants of the region are called *Khuziyān*.[^cite_note-24]

## History

Main article: [History of Khuzestan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Khuzestan)

### Antiquity



The [ziggurat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ziggurat) of [Choqa Zanbil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choqa_Zanbil) in Khuzestan was a magnificent structure of the [Elamite Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elamite_Empire). Khuzestan's Elamites formed the first empire within the territory of present-day Iran, which was later supplanted by Semitic, Greek, and Persian empires.

The province of Khuzestan is one of the centres of ancient civilization, and one of the most important regions of the [Ancient Near East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Near_East), based around [Susa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susa). Human settlement in the region goes back to at least the late fourth millennium BC, and the [Elamite civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elam) took shape here in the third millennium BC.[^cite_note-iranicaonline.org-25]

Archaeological remains show the province to have been home to the [Elamite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elamite_Empire) civilization, a kingdom speaking a language that was neither Semitic nor Indo-European, and the earliest civilization within the borders of what would later be called Persia.[^cite_note-\:5-26]

In antiquity, and particularly under the Achaemenids, the region was divided into two parts: the northern and northeastern districts, known as [Anshan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anshan_\(Persia\)), which were mountainous and heavily wooded, and the southern lowlands, with a hot and humid climate and fertile alluvial plains. The southern part, known as Elam, was strongly influenced by the culture of [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia).

In the words of [Elton L. Daniel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elton_L._Daniel), the Elamites were "the founders of the first 'Iranian' empire in the geographic sense."[^cite_note-27] Hence the central geopolitical significance of Khuzestan, the seat of Iran's first empire.[^cite_note-\:5-26]

In 640 BC, the Elamites were defeated by [Ashurbanipal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashurbanipal), coming under the rule of the [Assyrian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_Empire) who brought destruction upon Susa and Chogha Zanbil. After the death of Ashurbanipal, the Assyrian Empire began to experience internal conflict, which was quietly taken advantage of by the [Medes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medes) who had hitherto been subjects of Assyria. During the reign of the Median [Cyaxares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyaxares) (625–585 BC), Elam was incorporated into the [Median Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Median_Empire), but in 538 BC, the Persian [Cyrus the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_the_Great) who established the [Achaemenid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire), was able to conquer the Elamite lands from the Medes. The Persians regarded Elam highly and the city of Susa was proclaimed as one of the Achaemenid capitals. [Darius I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_I_of_Persia) then erected a grand palace known as *Apadana* there in 521 BC.

The geographer [Strabo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strabo) described the plain of Khuzestan as among the most fertile in the world, irrigated by numerous rivers that made agriculture and settlement possible across the region.

In 331 BC, following Achaemenid defeat at the [Battle of the Uxian Defile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Uxian_Defile), Susa was conquered by [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great), and a year later in 330 BC after suffering defeat at the [Battle of the Persian Gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Persian_Gate), Persia (the Achaemenid Empire) was conquered by the [Macedonian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonian_Empire). The [Susa weddings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susa_weddings) were arranged by Alexander in 324 BC in [Susa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susa), where mass weddings took place between the [Persians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_people) and the [Macedonians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedon).[^cite_note-28] After Alexander, the [Seleucid dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucid_dynasty) came to rule the area.

As the Seleucid dynasty weakened, [Mehrdad I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mithridates_I_of_Parthia) the [Parthian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthia) (171–137 BC) gained ascendency over the region.

Over centuries, Elamite culture and peoples were eventually absorbed into these later Indo-European-speaking populations. Speakers of Elamite may have persisted in isolated regions of Khuzestan into the 10th century, on the basis of interpretations of a *Xūzī* language reported in Arab sources. Elamite is now [extinct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extinct_language).[^cite_note-VanBladel-8]

### Sassanid period

In 224, after the Parthians suffered defeat at the [Battle of Hormozdgan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Hormozdgan), fought in what is now Khuzestan, the [Sassanids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sassanids) established control over the area, bringing the 471-year rule of the [Arsacids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthian_Empire) to an end.[^cite_note-iranicaonline.org-25] The Sassanids were responsible for the many constructions that were erected in Ahvaz, [Shushtar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shushtar), and the north of [Andimeshk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andimeshk). During the early years of the reign of the Sassanian [Shapur II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shapur_II) (AD 309 or 310–379), Arabs crossed the [Persian Gulf](24761.md) from [Bahrain](18933277.md) to "Ardashir-Khora" of [Fars](448049.md) and raided the city. In retaliation, Shapur II led an expedition through Bahrain, defeated the combined forces of the Arab tribes of Taghleb, Bakr bin Wael, and Abd Al-Qays and advanced temporarily into Yamama in central [Najd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Najd). The Sassanids nonetheless resettled the aforementioned tribes in [Kerman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerman) and [Ahvaz](599079.md). Arabs named Shapur II "Shabur Dhul-aktāf" after this battle.[^cite_note-29]

The existence of prominent scientific and cultural centers such as the [Academy of Gundishapur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Gundishapur), which gathered distinguished medical scientists from [Egypt](8087628.md), [Greece](12108.md), [India](14533.md) and Rome, shows the importance and prosperity of this region during this era. The medical school at Gundishapur was founded by the order of [Shapur I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shapur_I_of_Persia). It was repaired and restored by Shapur II (also known as *Zu'l-Aktaf*, "the Possessor of Shoulder Blades") and was completed and expanded during the reign of [Anushirvan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khosrow_I).

### Muslim conquest of Khuzestan

Main article: [Muslim conquest of Khuzestan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_conquest_of_Khuzestan)

#### Rashidun period



*Masjed Jame' Dezful*. In spite of devastating damage caused by Iraqi shelling in the Iran–Iraq War, Khuzestan still possesses a rich heritage of architecture from Islamic, Sassanid, and earlier times.

The governor of [Basra](57580.md) first sent [Mughira ibn Shu'ba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughira_ibn_Shu'ba) to take the towns of Khuzestan, and the Iranian landholders of [Ahvaz](599079.md) submitted and accepted the payment of [jizya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jizya). Mughira was subsequently removed from office and replaced by [Abu Musa al-Ash'ari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Musa_al-Ash'ari).[^cite_note-ReferenceB-30]

In 639, the Muslim [Rashidun Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashidun_Caliphate) forces under the command of Abu Musa al-Ash'ari defeated the Sassanids in Ahvaz and drove the Persian satrap [Hormuzan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hormuzan) out of the city. [Susa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susa) later fell, so Hormuzan fled to [Shushtar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shushtar). The Muslims led by Abu Musa thereafter imposed a [siege on the Sassanid forces in Shushtar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Shushtar) that lasted for 18 months. Shushtar finally fell in 642 and the Muslims subsequently established full control over the area. The [Khuzistan Chronicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khuzistan_Chronicle) records that an unknown Arab, living in the city, befriended a man in the army, and dug tunnels through the wall in return for a third of the spoil. The Arabs purged the Nestorians—the Exegete of the city and the Bishop of Hormizd, and all their students—but kept Hormuzan alive.[^cite_note-31] Hormuzan was sent to [Medina](36636.md), where he was later killed after being accused of complicity in the murder of the caliph [Umar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umar).[^cite_note-ReferenceB-30]

There followed the conquests of [Gundeshapur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gundeshapur) and of many other districts along the Tigris. The [Battle of Nahāvand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Nah%C4%81vand) finally secured Khuzestan for the Muslim armies.[^cite_note-32]

During the Muslim conquest the [Sassanids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sassanids) were allied with non-Muslim Arab tribes, which implies that those wars were religious, rather than national. For instance in 633–634, [Khaled ibn Walid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khaled_ibn_Walid) leader of the Muslim Army, defeated a force of the Sassanids' Arab auxiliaries from the tribes of Bakr, 'Ejl, Taghleb and Namer at 'Ayn Al-Tamr.[^cite_note-33]

The Muslim settlements by military garrisons in southern Iran was soon followed by other types of expansion. Some families, for example, took the opportunity to gain control of private estates.[^cite_note-34] Like the rest of Iran, the Muslim conquest thus brought Khuzestan under the rule of the Arabs of the [Umayyad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad) and [Abbasid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid) Caliphates, until [Ya'qub bin Laith as-Saffar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ya'qub_bin_Laith_as-Saffar), from southeastern Iran, raised the flag of independence once more, and ultimately regained control over Khuzestan, among other parts of Iran, founding the short-lived [Saffarid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saffarid) dynasty. From that point on, Iranian [dynasties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_Kings) would continue to rule the region in succession as an important part of Iran.

#### Umayyad and Abbasid periods

In the [Umayyad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad) period, large groups of nomads from the *Hanifa*, *[Banu Tamim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banu_Tamim)*, and *[Abd al-Qays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_al-Qays)* tribes crossed the Persian Gulf and occupied some of the richest [Basran](57580.md) territories around [Ahvaz](599079.md) and in [Fars](448049.md) during the [Second Fitna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Fitna) in 661–665 / 680–684 AD.[^cite_note-ArabTribes-35]

During the [Abbasid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid) period, in the second half of the 10th century, the *Assad* tribe, taking advantage of quarrels under the [Buwayhids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buwayhids), penetrated into Khuzestan, where a group of *Tamim* had been living since pre-Islamic times. However, following the fall of the Abbasid dynasty, the flow of Arab immigrants into [Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persia) gradually diminished, but it nonetheless continued. In the latter part of the 16th century, the *[Bani Kaab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bani_Kaab)* (pronounced Chaub in the local Gulf dialect), from [Kuwait](7515890.md), settled in Khuzestan.[^cite_note-36] And during the succeeding centuries, more Arab tribes moved from southern Iraq to Khuzestan.[^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38]

### Qajar period

[Daniel's Tomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomb_of_Daniel), located in Khuzestan, has such a shape. The shrine pictured here, belongs to Imamzadeh Hamzeh, located between Mahshahr and Hendijan.

According to [C.E. Bosworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C.E._Bosworth) in *[Encyclopædia Iranica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Iranica)*, under the [Qajar dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qajar_dynasty) the province was known, as in [Safavid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid) times, as Arabistan, and during the Qajar period was administratively a governor-generalate.[^cite_note-Bosworth-9] The northern, more populous parts of the province, with their centre at [Shushtar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shushtar), retained the older name, and were at times attached to the district of "Greater Lur" on account of the large [Bakhtiari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bakhtiari_people) population there.

In 1856, in the course of the [Anglo-Persian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Persian_War) over the city of [Herat](14128.md), British naval forces sailed up the [Karun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karun) river as far as [Ahvaz](599079.md). In the settlement that followed, however, they evacuated the province. Some tribal forces, such as those led by [Sheikh Jabir al-Kaabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheikh_Jabir_al-Kaabi), the Sheikh of [Mohammerah](464970.md), fared better in opposing the invading British forces than those dispatched by the central government, which was quite feeble. The purpose of the invasion of the province and other coastal regions of southern Iran was to force the evacuation of Herat by the Persians rather than the permanent occupation of these regions.

### Pahlavi period

Further information: [Khuzestan conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khuzestan_conflict)

#### Arab rebellion

In the two decades before 1925, although nominally part of Persian territory, the western part of Khuzestan known as the [Emirate of Muhammara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emirate_of_Muhammara) functioned for many years effectively as an autonomous emirate known as "Arabistan". The eastern part of Khuzestan was governed by [Bakhtiari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bakhtiari_people) khans. Following [Sheikh Khazʽal Ibn Jabir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khaz%CA%BDal_Ibn_Jabir)'s [rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheikh_Khazal_rebellion), the western part of Khuzestan's emirate was dissolved by the [Reza Shah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reza_Shah) government in 1925, along with other autonomous regions of [Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persia), in a bid to centralize the state. In response Sheikh Khaz'al of Mohammerah initiated a rebellion, which was suppressed by the Pahlavi government in late 1924 and early 1925. A low-level [conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khuzestan_conflict) between the central Iranian government and Arab nationalists in the province has continued since.

The provincial administration was transferred from Shushtar to the new city of Ahvaz in 1924, and the name Arabistan was progressively removed from official orders and government correspondence in favour of Khuzestan.[^cite_note-Afshar-39] The name Khuzestan came to be applied to the entire territory by 1936.[^cite_note-JMES-40] Over the following decades of Pahlavi rule, the province remained relatively quiet, while holding an increasingly important economic and strategic position.

#### Oil

Oil has been produced commercially in the province since 1908, when the [Masjed Soleyman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masjed_Soleyman) oil field was discovered some 50 miles northeast of the provincial capital, [Ahvaz](599079.md); it was the first oil discovery in the Middle East.[^cite_note-41] This initiated a period of intense foreign exploration and political involvement, culminating in the discovery in 1953 of a further, much larger, oil field in the region by the [Anglo-Persian Oil Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Persian_Oil_Company). The new [Ahvaz field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahvaz_Field) entered into production a year later, after the parliamentary regime of Prime Minister [Mohammad Mosaddegh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohammad_Mosaddegh), which had sought to nationalise the oil industry, had been overthrown in a British and United States backed [coup d'état in Tehran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1953_Iranian_coup_d'%C3%A9tat).[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43]

In the aftermath of the coup, [Mohammad Reza Pahlavi](20065598.md) consolidated power and negotiated the [Consortium Agreement of 1954](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consortium_Agreement_of_1954).[^cite_note-1953coup-44] Under the agreement, a consortium of European and American companies, the [Iranian Oil Participants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo_Persian_Oil_Company#Consortium) (IOP), formally acknowledged ownership of the oil and facilities by the [National Iranian Oil Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Iranian_Oil_Company) (NIOC), and agreed as the operator to share profits with Iran on a 50–50 basis. The IOP did so, however, on the understanding that the western consortium would not open its books to Iranian auditors or allow Iranians onto its board of directors.[^cite_note-45] The IOP continued to operate until the regime of [Ayatollah Khomeini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayatollah_Khomeini), brought to power in the [Iranian Revolution](347268.md) of 1979, confiscated their assets.

Despite declining production, the Ahvaz field, owned by NIOC and operated by [National Iranian South Oil Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Iranian_South_Oil_Company) (NISOC), remains the largest oil field and producer in Iran.[^cite_note-46]

Oil production, and related investment, paved the way for the arrival in the province of other Iranian peoples, Persian and non-Persian. In the course of the 20th century, the new immigrants reduced the province's Arab majority to a plurality among its ethnic groups.[^cite_note-\:3-47]

### Islamic Republic

#### After the revolution

With the Iranian Revolution taking place in early 1979, minority ethnic rebellions swept the country. In April 1979, there was an [uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1979_Khuzestan_uprising) in Khuzestan led by the Arab separatist group Arab Political and Cultural Organisation (APCO) which protested the introduction of theocratic rule from Tehran and sought local autonomy or independence.[^cite_note-48]

In the wake of the uprising's suppression, in 1980 a separatist group called the *Arab Popular Movement in Arabistan* (see [Arab separatism in Khuzestan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_separatism_in_Khuzestan)) [seized the Iranian Embassy in London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Embassy_siege), taking 26 hostages. The standoff over their demand for the release of 91 of their comrades held in Iranian jails ended in the storming of the building by the British [SAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Air_Service), with only one hostage-taker and one embassy staffer surviving.[^cite_note-49] Decrying "the racist rule of Khomeini", the group threatened further international action, but its standing as an internal opposition movement was compromised by its links to [Ba'athist Iraq](26215470.md).[^cite_note-autogenerated2000-50]

#### Iran–Iraq war



Iraqi soldiers surrender during the [Liberation of Khorramshahr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Khorramshahr_\(1982\)), 1982

During the [Iran–Iraq War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Iraq_War), Khuzestan was invaded by Iraq. As a result, Khuzestan suffered the heaviest damage and population displacement of all Iranian provinces during the war.[^cite_note-\:1-51][^cite_note-correspondent-52][^cite_note-53] From September 1980 until the last days of the war, the province was the scene of repeated Iranian operations to recover occupied territory, and cities including [Abadan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abadan,_Iran), [Susangerd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susangerd), [Bostan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bostan,_Iran), [Dezful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dezful), [Andimeshk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andimeshk), [Khorramshahr](464970.md) and Ahvaz came under sustained missile and artillery attack. What used to be Iran's largest refinery in Abadan was destroyed, never to fully recover. Many of the famous *nakhlestans* (palm groves) were annihilated, cities were destroyed, and historical sites were demolished.[^cite_note-\:4-54]

Despite leading a Sunni-minority regime, Iraqi President [Saddam Hussein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saddam_Hussein) had been confident that the predominantly [Shia](26961.md) Arab population of Khuzestan would react enthusiastically to the prospect of union with Iraq and would undermine the Iranian resistance. However, the Iranians successfully stalled the Iraqi military advance, and eventually opened the window for a [counter-offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Undeniable_Victory). While there were [Ahwazi Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khuzestani_Arabs) groups ready to cooperate with the invaders, the war "forced Iran to help integrate the Arabs of Khuzestan by publishing Arabic texts" and by placing the defense of Khuzestan at the forefront of national propaganda.[^cite_note-\:4-54]

The [Battle for Khorramshahr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Khorramshahr_\(1982\)) in May 1982 proved to be a turning point in the war and is officially commemorated every year in Iran. The city, which prior to the war had been Iran's most important port, was almost completely destroyed as a result of the [scorched earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scorched_earth) policy ordered by Saddam Hussein. Iranian forces were able to prevent the Iraqis from attempting to spread the execution of this directive to other major urban centres.[^cite_note-55][^cite_note-56]

Postwar recovery and development of the province was slow. Despite benefiting from abundant natural resources, including oil, Khuzestan has compounding economic, environmental, social, and construction problems. High unemployment, water scarcity, degraded air quality, and lack of civil infrastructure are among the factors that fed popular dissatisfaction.[^cite_note-57][^cite_note-58][^cite_note-59] The frustrations manifested in protests and rallies.[^cite_note-60][^cite_note-\:1-51][^cite_note-correspondent-52][^cite_note-61][^cite_note-CrisisGroup-62][^cite_note-63]

#### Arab unrest

In 2005, Ahvaz witnessed a number of bombing incidents which followed upon the violent [riots in Ahvaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_Ahvaz_unrest). Another [wave of Arab protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Khuzestan_protests) occurred in Ahvaz and surrounding districts in 2011. The unrest, which marked the anniversary of the 2005 riots and was interpreted by some as a response to the regional [Arab Spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Spring), lasted four days and resulted in the deaths of more than a dozen protesters.[^cite_note-ganji-64][^cite_note-rasmus-65]

In 2018, [Amnesty International](18947898.md) reported that the authorities were engaged in a sweeping crackdown against Arab activists. This was in the wake of an armed attack on a military parade in Ahvaz in September, during which at least 24 people, including spectators, were killed.[^cite_note-66] Tehran, meanwhile, has accused Britain, Israel, and Saudi Arabia of being behind the violence.[^cite_note-\:3-47]

Before the [Iran–Iraq War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Iraq_War) of the 1980s, the Arabs of Khuzestan mostly resided in the rural regions along the Karkheh and Karun rivers in the southwest of the province. But after the end of the war, many displaced Arabs were relocated by the government to urban centres and smaller towns. The previously rural population had to make difficult adjustments with few resources. When in May 2022 a ten-story residential and commercial [building collapsed in Abadan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abadan_building_collapse), killing at least 41 people,[^cite_note-France24_6_June_2022-67][^cite_note-BBC_4_June_2022-68] frustrations came to the surface and there were extensive demonstrations.[^cite_note-69][^cite_note-70]

Khuzestan Arabs have complained of discrimination and the lack of linguistic and cultural rights, of poverty despite inhabiting one of the most economically productive parts of the country, of related problems of widespread drug addiction, and of the oil industry's environmental costs; Ahvaz is reputedly one of the most polluted urban areas in the world.[^cite_note-71]

### 2026 U.S.–Israeli airstrikes

Main article: [2026 Iran war](82533582.md)

On 28 February 2026, [Israel](9282173.md) and the [United States](3434750.md) launched [surprise airstrikes](82533582.md) on multiple sites and cities across Iran, [killing](82537558.md) Supreme Leader [Ali Khamenei](385653.md) and numerous other Iranian officials. On 12 and 13 March, the [Israel Defense Forces](39237.md) struck multiple internal security sites in Khuzestan Province. Among their reported targets were the provincial headquarters of the [Law Enforcement Command](5187924.md) and of the [Islamic Revolutionary Guards Corps](1285472.md) in Ahvaz, which had been hit the week before in an attack claimed by the Arab insurgent group the [Ahwaz Falcons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahwaz_Falcons),[^cite_note-72] and the base of the [Artesh](48259216.md) 292nd Armored Brigade in [Dezful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dezful).[^cite_note-73] On 11 March, Iranian authorities announced they had dismantled an armed cell in Khuzestan, seizing weapons, ammunition and explosives said to be intended for sabotage operations.[^cite_note-74] On 24 March, there were further air strikes on energy sites in Khorramshahr.[^cite_note-75]

## Demographics

### Languages

[Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language) has long been the language of wider communication in the cities of Khuzestan, and there are several urban Khuzestani Persian-speaking communities in the south of the province too.[^cite_note-IranAtlas-76] From the early 20th century onward, rural families also began teaching standard Persian to their children as the official language of the country.[^cite_note-IranAtlas-76]

Apart from Persian, other languages and dialects are also spoken in Khuzestan. A substantial portion of the province's population speaks [Khuzestani Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khuzestani_Arabic).[^cite_note-77] Another part of the population speaks the [Bakhtiari dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bakhtiari_dialect) of the [Luri language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luri_language).[^cite_note-78] The [Shushtari and Dezfuli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shushtari_dialect) dialects are spoken both in [Shushtar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shushtar) and [Dezful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dezful) and in the provincial capital.[^cite_note-IranicaDezfuli-79][^cite_note-KHUZESTANviii-80] Southern Luri varieties, including Bahmai, Tayyebi and Kohgiluyei, are spoken in [Seydun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seydun), [Ramhormoz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramhormoz), [Omidiyeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omidiyeh), [Aghajari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aghajari), [Behbahan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behbahan), [Hendijan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendijan), [Mahshahr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandar_Mahshahr) and Ahvaz.[^cite_note-81] [Qashqai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qashqai_people) Turkic speakers form part of the population of [Haftkel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haftkel_County), and [Southern Kurdish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Kurdish) is spoken as a minority language in the northern towns of the province and in [Bandar Imam Khomeini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandar_Imam_Khomeini).[^cite_note-IranAtlasHeritage-82] [Neo-Mandaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Mandaic) is spoken by no more than a few dozen elderly [Mandaeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandaeans); most Mandaeans today speak Persian or another of the province's languages.[^cite_note-Haberl_2009-83]

Until the Iran–Iraq War, the Arabic-speaking population was concentrated mainly in [Abadan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abadan_County), [Khorramshahr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khorramshahr_County), [Dasht-e Azadegan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dasht-e_Azadegan_County), [Shadegan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadegan_County), [Shush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shush_County) and [Hoveyzeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoveyzeh_County). ### Ethnic groups

Khuzestan is known for its ethnic diversity. Its population consists of [Persians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persians) (including [Dezfulis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dezful) and [Shushtaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shushtar)), [Arabs](2185.md), [Qashqai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qashqai_people) from the [Afshar tribe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afshar_tribe), [Bakhtiaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bakhtiari_people), [Northern Lurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lurs), [Mandaeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandaeans), [Kurds](17068.md) and [Armenians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Armenians).[^cite_note-Welcome_to_Encyclopaedia_Iranica-85][^cite_note-khuz-86][^cite_note-87] The province is also a winter grazing ground for several nomadic [Bakhtiari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bakhtiari_people) tribes.[^cite_note-IRNA-Tirazheh-84] As part of research commissioned by the General Culture Council in 2010, the following ethnic groups in Khuzestan were sampled: [Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Arabs), Persians, Lurs, Qashqais, Kurds and others. A field survey was conducted as well as a statistical community among residents of 288 cities and about 1400 villages across the country.[^cite_note-GeneralCulture-88] Khuzestan's population is predominantly [Shia Muslim](26961.md), with small [Christian](5211.md), [Jewish](25955086.md), [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni) and [Mandaean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandaeans) minorities.[^cite_note-khuz-86]

According to a survey conducted by the Ministry of Culture in 2010, 33.6% of the population of Khuzestan (about 1.6 million people) are Iranian Arabs.[^cite_note-BahraniKhuzestaniArabic-89] According to Bettina Leitner (2022), although no official numbers exist, an estimated 2 to 3 million inhabitants of Khuzestan are Arabs, though it is hard to determine what proportion of that population uses Arabic actively.[^cite_note-90] The Atlas of the Languages of Iran estimates around 1.6 million Arabic speakers in Khuzestan, noting that its data do not account for ethnicity or multilingualism.[^cite_note-IranAtlas-76]

### Population

According to the 1996 census, the province had an estimated population of 3.7 million people, of which approximately 62.5% were in the urban centres, 36.5% were rural dwellers and the remaining 1% were non-residents. At the time of the 2006 National Census, the province's population was 4,192,598 in 862,491 households.[^cite_note-2006_Khuzestan_Province-91] The following census in 2011 counted 4,531,720 people in 1,112,664 households.[^cite_note-2011_Khuzestan_Province-92] The 2016 census measured the population of the province as 4,710,509 in 1,280,645 households, of whom about 75.5% lived in urban areas.[^cite_note-2016_Khuzestan_Province-93]

## Geography

The province of Khuzestan can be basically divided into two regions: the rolling hills and mountainous regions north of the Ahvaz Ridge, and the plains and marshlands to its south. Roughly two-fifths of its area is mountainous and three-fifths is lowland plain.[^cite_note-SCI-Yearbook-114] The [Zagros Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zagros_Mountains) enclose the province to the north and east, giving way to foothills further inland; among the province's mountains are Kuh-e Chow, [Zard-Kuh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zard-Kuh), Shavish, Mamazad, Kuh-e Siah and Kuh-e Chal. The plain has a gentle gradient, and [salt domes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_dome) in several places contribute to the salinity of its soils and waters.[^cite_note-SCI-Yearbook-114]

The northern section maintains a [Bakhtiari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bakhtiari_people) minority, while the southern section has long had diverse minority groups known as Khuzis. Since the 1940s, an influx of job seekers from across Iran to the oil and commerce centres on the Persian Gulf coast has made the region more Persian-speaking.

Khuzestan has great potential for [agricultural expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_expansion), which is almost unrivaled by the country's other provinces. Large and permanent rivers flow over the entire territory contributing to the fertility of the land. The agricultural potential of most of these rivers, however, and particularly in their lower reaches, is hampered by the fact that their waters carry [salt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salinity), the amount of which increases as the rivers flow away from the source mountains and hills. In the case of the Karun, a single tributary, [Rud-i Shur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rud-i_Shur?action=edit\&redlink=1) ("Salty River"), which flows into the Karun above [Shushtar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shushtar), contributes most of the salt that the river carries. The freshness of the Karun's waters could therefore be greatly enhanced if the Rud-i Shur were diverted away from it. The same applies to the [Jarahi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jarahi_River) and [Karkheh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karkheh) in their lower reaches. Only the [Marun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marun_River) is exempt from this.

### Rivers

Khuzestan is the most water-rich province of Iran, and five major rivers rising in the Zagros irrigate the plain before reaching the Persian Gulf directly or indirectly. The [Karun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karun), the largest river in Iran, rises in the Zagros, is joined by the [Dez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dez_River) south of Shushtar, and flows into the [Arvand Rud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shatt_al-Arab) near the head of the Gulf. The [Karkheh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karkheh_River), the third longest river in Iran after the Karun and the [Sefid-Rud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sefid-Rud), runs some 755 kilometres southwest into the [Hawizeh Marshes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawizeh_Marshes) on the Iran–Iraq border; its name derives from a town called Karkha in the district of Shush.[^cite_note-115] The Dez rises in the central Zagros northeast of [Borujerd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borujerd), passes [Andimeshk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andimeshk) and the Dez dam and then the city of [Dezful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dezful), forms part of the boundary between Shushtar and Shush counties, and joins the Karun at Band-e Qir south of Shushtar; it has historically been the lifeline of intensive agriculture in the area.[^cite_note-116] The [Marun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marun_River) rises northwest of [Yasuj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yasuj) and, after joining the A'la, continues under the name [Jarahi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jarahi_River), irrigating the plains of Ramhormoz and Ramshir before reaching [Khowr-e Musa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khowr-e_Musa?action=edit\&redlink=1) and the Persian Gulf after some 438 kilometres. The [Zohreh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zohreh_River), also called the Hendijan, rises in [Kohgiluyeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kohgiluyeh) and enters the Gulf past the town of [Hendijan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendijan).

### Islands

The province has several islands, among them [Minoo Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minoo_Island), [Abadan Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abadan_Island), Bone, Dara and Qabr-e Nakhoda.[^cite_note-117]

### Climate

South of Khuzestan is one of the hottest places on earth, with maximum temperatures in summer sometimes exceeding 50 °C (122 °F), and remaining as high as 36 °C or 96.8 °F in the highest settlements. Reliable measurements in lowland cities range from −5 to 54.0 °C (23.0 to 129.2 °F). Khuzestan has desert conditions and experiences many sandstorms. The higher slopes may have either a [hot-summer Mediterranean climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hot-summer_Mediterranean_climate) ([Köppen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification) *Csa*) or a [continental Mediterranean climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_Mediterranean_climate) (*Dsa*) at the very highest elevations.

In the lowland areas annual rainfall ranges from 150 to 250 millimetres or 6 to 10 inches, whilst in the foothills near [Dezful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dezful) it reaches around 375 millimetres or 15 inches. The highest areas of [Izeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Izeh_County) and [Dezpart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dezpart_County) counties receive between 500 and 1,000 millimetres (20 and 40 in) of precipitation, some of it as snow.

## Water

[Iran](14653.md) ranks among the most [water stressed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_scarcity_in_Iran) countries in the world.[^cite_note-118] Despite its rivers, Khuzestan province suffers from major water problems that have been aggravated by corruption in Iran's [water supply](684928.md) sector, lack of transparency, neglect of marginalized communities, and [political favoritism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In-group_favoritism). The [Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps](1285472.md) and other politically connected entities control water resources, prioritizing projects for political and economic gain rather than public need. They divert supplies to favored regions, causing shortages in vulnerable provinces like Khuzestan and Sistan-Baluchestan. For example, water diversion projects in [Isfahan](448061.md) and [Yazd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yazd_province) provinces receive priority despite critical shortages in Khuzestan and Sistan-Baluchestan. Reports also indicate that certain agricultural and industrial enterprises with ties to the Revolutionary Guard Corps have received significant amounts of water, while small farmers and rural communities struggle with severe shortages.[^cite_note-119][^cite_note-CrisisGroup-62]

Iran's central government prioritizes water allocation for industrial and urban centers, often at the expense of rural and minority populations. These groups face severe water shortages, ecological degradation, and a loss of livelihoods. This pattern of unequal development not only exacerbates regional disparities but also fuels social unrest and environmental crises. Iran's water policy is also characterized by an overreliance on dam construction and large-scale diversion projects, primarily benefiting politically connected enterprises and urban elites. This has led to the drying of rivers, wetlands, and other vital ecosystems, intensifying dust storms and land subsidence in regions like Khuzestan and [Sistan-Baluchestan](448105.md). Such environmental degradation, combined with insufficient governmental oversight and transparency, worsens living conditions for marginalized communities, reinforcing cycles of poverty and socio-political marginalization.[^cite_note-120]

In June 2019, residents of [Ghizaniyeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghizaniyeh?action=edit\&redlink=1), some 45 kilometres from Ahvaz, [protested](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghizaniyeh_protests?action=edit\&redlink=1) against the lack of drinking water and were met by security forces. In a parliamentary session in September 2020, members representing the province reported that 700 villages faced water stress, that unemployment in the Mahshahr constituency stood at about 25 per cent, and that a major agricultural development scheme covering 550,000 hectares, approved in 1996, remained unfinished.[^cite_note-RadioFarda-121]

### Migration from Khuzestan province

Historically, Khuzestan, one of Iran's most water-rich provinces, has been hit hard by the mismanagement of water resources. The drying of rivers, including the [Karkheh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karkheh_River) and [Karun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karun), has made agriculture unsustainable in many parts of the province. In recent years, Khuzestan has witnessed a significant exodus of its rural population as people move to cities for work and better living conditions. The situation has been exacerbated by periodic dust storms, which further degrade the environment and make life untenable in affected regions. Khuzestan has the highest rate of out-migration of any Iranian province, with poor water quality, dust, and extreme summer heat among the reasons commonly cited; in 2018 a member of parliament reported that some 400,000 people had left the province for northern cities in recent years.[^cite_note-IRNA-Migration-122] This environmental migration has led to social tensions in the region, as local communities face declining living standards and heightened unemployment.[^cite_note-CrisisGroup-62][^cite_note-123]

## Politics

Main article: [Politics of Khuzestan Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics_of_Khuzestan_Province)

Khuzestan is ethnically diverse, home to many different ethnic groups.[^cite_note-khuz-86] This has a bearing on Khuzestan's electoral politics, with ethnic minority rights playing a significant role in the province's political culture. The province's geographical location bordering Iraq and its oil resources also make it a politically sensitive region, particularly given its history of foreign intervention, notably the Iraqi invasion of 1980. The province returns 18 members to the [Islamic Consultative Assembly](383276.md).

Some ethnic groups complain over the distribution of the revenue generated by oil resources, with claims that the central government is failing to invest profits from the oil industry in employment generation, post-war reconstruction and welfare projects. Low human development indicators among local Khuzestanis are contrasted with the wealth generation of the local oil industry. Minority rights are frequently identified with strategic concerns, with ethnic unrest perceived by the Iranian government as being generated by foreign governments to undermine the country's oil industry and its internal stability. The politics of Khuzestan therefore have international significance and go beyond the realm of electoral politics.

According to [Jane's Information Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane's_Information_Group), "Most Iranian Arabs seek their constitutionally guaranteed rights and do not have a separatist agenda. ... While it may be true that some Arab activists are separatists, most see themselves as Iranians first and declare their commitment to the state's territorial integrity."[^cite_note-124]

### Traditions and religion

The people of Khuzestan are predominantly [Shia Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shia_Muslims), with small [Sunni Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Muslim), [Jewish](15624.md), [Christian](18337522.md), and [Mandaean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandaeanism) minorities. ### Cuisine

See also: [Iranian cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_cuisine)

[Seafood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seafood) is the most important part of Khuzestani [cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuisine), but many other dishes are also featured. The most popular Khuzestani dish is *Ghalyeh Mahi*, a fish dish that is prepared with heavy spices, onions and cilantro. The fish used in the dish is locally known as *mahi soboor* ([shad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shad) fish), a species of [fish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish) found in the Persian Gulf. Other provincial specialties include *Ghalyeh Meygu* ("shrimp casserole"), *ashe-mohshala* (a [Khorramshahri](464970.md) breakfast stew), *sær shir* (a [Dezfuli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dezful) breakfast of heavy cream), *hælim* (a [Shushtari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shushtar) breakfast of wheatmeal with shredded lamb), and *kohbbeh* (a deep-fried rice cake with ground beef filling and other spices of Arabic origin, a variant on [Levantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant) [kibbeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kibbeh)).[^cite_note-127]

### Historical figures

Many scientists, philosophers, and poets have come from Khuzestan, including [Abu Nuwas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Nuwas), [Abdollah ibn-Meymun Ahvazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdollah_ibn-Meymun_Ahvazi?action=edit\&redlink=1), the astronomer [Nowbækht-e Ahvazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naubakht) and his sons, as well as [Jorjis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jorjis?action=edit\&redlink=1), the son of [Bakhtshua Gondishapuri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bukhtishu), [Ibn Sakit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Sakit?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Da'bal-e Khazai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Da'bal-e_Khazai?action=edit\&redlink=1) and [Sheikh Mortedha Ansari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morteza_Ansari), a prominent Shi'a scholar from [Dezful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dezful).

### Attractions of Khuzestan

The [Iran National Heritage Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran_National_Heritage_Organization) lists 140 sites of historical and cultural significance in Khuzestan, reflecting the fact that the province was once the seat of Iran's most ancient empire.

Some of the more popular sites of attraction include:



The *[Parthian Prince](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shami_statue)*, found in Khuzestan c. AD 100, is kept at The [National Museum of Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Iran), [Tehran](57654.md).

- [Choqa Zanbil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choqa_Zanbil): the seat of the [Elamite Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elamite_Empire), this [ziggurat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ziggurat) is a five-story temple that is one of the greatest ancient monuments in the Middle East today, and is inscribed on the [UNESCO](21786641.md) [World Heritage List](44940.md). The monolith, with its labyrinthine walls made of thousands of large bricks with Elamite inscriptions, manifests the sheer antiquity of the shrine. The temple was built in honour of [Inshushinak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inshushinak), the protector deity of the city of [Susa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susa).

- [Shushtar Historical Hydraulic System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shushtar_Historical_Hydraulic_System): a complex of waterfalls, watermills, bridges and ancient dams, also a UNESCO World Heritage Site. In and around Shushtar there are many displays of ancient hydraulic engineering, including the Band-e Mizan and the Band-e Qeysar, dams on the Karun some 2,000 years old, and the Shadervan Bridge. The Friday Mosque of Shushtar was built by the [Abbasids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid) and has 54 pillars.

- *Shush-Daniel*: burial site of the Jewish prophet [Daniel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_\(biblical_figure\)). He is said to have died in [Susa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susa) on his way to [Jerusalem](16043.md) upon the order of Darius. The grave of [Ya'qub bin Laith as-Saffar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ya'qub_bin_Laith_as-Saffar) is also located nearby, as are the [Apadana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apadana) palace and [Shush Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shush_Castle).

- [Dezful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dezful) (*Dezh-pol*), whose name is taken from a bridge (*pol*) over the [Dez river](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dez_River) having 12 spans built by the order of [Shapur I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shapur_I_of_Persia). This is the same bridge that was called "Andamesh Bridge" by historians such as [Istakhri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istakhri), who says the city of [Andimeshk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andimeshk) takes its name from it. [Muqaddasi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muqaddasi) called it "the City of the Bridge". The old town also preserves the Tiznow and Suzangar houses and the Shah Rokn al-Din and Karnasian baths.

- [Izeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Izeh), or *Izaj*, was one of the main targets of the invading Islamic army in their conquest of Persia. Ibn Battuta, who visited the city in the 14th century, refers to many monasteries, [caravanserais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caravanserai), [aqueducts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqueduct_\(watercourse\)), schools, and fortresses in the town. The rock reliefs of [Kul-e Farah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kul-e_Farah) and [Eshkaft-e Salman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eshkaft-e_Salman) lie nearby, and the bronze statue of *The Parthian Man*, kept at the [National Museum of Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Iran), is from here.

- [Masjed Soleyman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masjed_Soleyman), another ancient town, has ancient fire altars and temples such as *Sar-Masjed* and *Bard-e Neshandeh*. It is also the winter resting area of the [Bakhtiari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bakhtiari_people) tribe, and where [William Knox D'Arcy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Knox_D'Arcy) dug Iran's first oil well.

- [Abadan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abadan,_Iran) is said to be where the tomb of [Elijah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elijah) is. The city also has an Armenian church, the Rangooni Mosque, the Abadan Museum and the former Taj cinema.

- *[Iwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iwan) of [Hermes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermes)*, and *Iwan of Karkheh*, two enigmatic ruins north of [Susa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susa).

## Economy



The massive Karun-3 dam was inaugurated as part of a drive to meet Iran's growing energy demands.

Khuzestan is the major oil-producing region of [Iran](14653.md), and as such is one of the wealthiest provinces in Iran. Khuzestan ranks third among Iran's provinces in GDP.[^cite_note-128]

In 2005, Iran's government announced it was planning the country's second nuclear reactor to be built in Khuzestan province.[^cite_note-129] The 360 MW reactor was to be a [light water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_water_reactor) [pressurized water reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressurized_water_reactor).[^cite_note-130]

Khuzestan is also home to the [Arvand Free Trade Zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arvand_Free_Trade_Zone),[^cite_note-131] one of six free trade zones in Iran, and to the Petrochemical Special Economic Zone (PETZONE) at [Mahshahr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandar_Mahshahr).

Because of high temperatures and the concentration of heavy industry, Khuzestan is the largest consumer of energy in Iran after [Tehran province](202698.md).[^cite_note-132] The province's thermal and gas-fired power stations include Ramin, Zargan, Abadan, Khorramshahr and Fajr.[^cite_note-133] The long hours of sunshine in the province have been identified as a significant potential resource for [solar power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_power_in_Iran).

The electricity industry in the province dates to the oil era: a 600 kW steam plant was built at Tembi in [Masjed Soleyman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masjed_Soleyman) to power the pumping station carrying crude oil to the [Abadan Refinery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abadan_Refinery), and it also lit the installations and parts of the town.[^cite_note-KEPDC-134] Ahvaz received its first generating plant around 1929, built by the merchant Mirza Hossein Khan Moqarrar on the eastern bank of the Karun; at first electricity was billed by the number of lamps rather than by meter.[^cite_note-KEPDC-134]

### Shipping

The [Karun River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karun_River) is the only navigable river in Iran. The British, up until recent decades, after the discovery by [Austen Henry Layard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austen_Henry_Layard), transported their merchandise via the Karun's waterways, passing through Ahvaz all the way up to [Langar near Shushtar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langar_near_Shushtar?action=edit\&redlink=1), and then by road to [Masjed Soleyman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masjed_Soleyman), the site of their first oil wells in the Naftoon oil field. Commercial navigation as far as Ahvaz has since ceased for lack of maintenance.

The ports of [Abadan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abadan,_Iran) and [Khorramshahr](464970.md) (reached from the [Bahmanshir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahmanshir_River?action=edit\&redlink=1)), [Mahshahr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandar_Mahshahr) and [Bandar Imam Khomeini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandar_Imam_Khomeini) were commercially important before the Iran–Iraq War and, together with the Khorramshahr–Tehran railway, formed a major transport corridor; they were severely damaged during the war and have recovered only slowly.



Sketch of the Abadan island showing rivers and date palm plantations

### Agriculture

The abundance of water and the fertility of the soil have made this region a rich and well-endowed land. The variety of agricultural products such as [wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheat), [barley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barley), [oilseeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oilseeds), [rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice), [eucalyptus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eucalyptus) and medicinal herbs; the existence of many palm and [citrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citrus) farms; the proximity of mountains suitable for raising [olives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olive); and of course [sugar cane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_cane) all show the great potential of this fertile region. In 2005, 51,000 hectares of land were planted with sugar cane, producing 350,000 tons of [sugar](27712.md). The province is also Iran's leading producer of [dates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Date_palm), with a harvest of about 560,000 tonnes in 2020. The abundance of water supplies, rivers, and dams also supports fishery industries.

The Abadan island is an important area for the production of dates, but it suffered from the Iraqi invasion during the [Iran–Iraq War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Iraq_War). The palm groves are irrigated by [tidal irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrigation_in_Iran#Tidal_irrigation_at_Abadan_island,_Iran).[^cite_note-135] At high [tide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tide), the [water level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_level) in the rivers rises and the river flow enters the irrigation canals dug from the river towards the inland plantations. At low tide, the canals drain the unused part of the water back to the river.

### Industry



Shahid Abbaspour Dam

There are several [cane sugar mills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cane_sugar_mill) in Khuzestan province, among them *[Haft Tepe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haft_Tepe)* and *Karun Agro Industry* near [Shushtar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shushtar). The Haft Tepe complex has long produced paper from sugar-cane residue.

Steel, rolling and pipe-making plants are among the industries with the greatest effect on the provincial economy; they include [Khuzestan Steel Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khuzestan_Steel_Company?action=edit\&redlink=1), the [National Iranian Steel Industrial Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Iranian_Steel_Industrial_Group?action=edit\&redlink=1), Kavian rolling mill and the Ahvaz Pipe and Rolling Mills Company.[^cite_note-\:1-arpco-136]

The [Karun 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karun-3_dam) and 4 dams, and the Karkheh Dam, as well as the petroleum reserves, provide Iran with national sources of revenue and energy. The petrochemical and steel industries, pipe making, the power stations that feed the national electricity grid, the chemical plants, and the large refineries are some of Iran's major industrial facilities.

### Oil

The province is also home to [Yadavaran Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yadavaran_Field), which is a major oil field in itself and part of the disputed [Al-Fakkah Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Fakkah_Field). Khuzestan holds 80% of Iran's onshore oil reserves, and thus 57% of Iran's total oil reserves, making it indispensable to the Iranian economy.[^cite_note-137]
