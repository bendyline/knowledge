# Cruise missile

For a missile that follows a ballistic trajectory, see [Ballistic missile](38025.md).



A BGM-109 [Tomahawk](31238.md) flying in November 2002

A **cruise missile** is an unmanned self-propelled guided [missile](19594.md) that sustains flight through [aerodynamics lift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerodynamics) for most of its flight path. Cruise missiles are designed to deliver a large [warhead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warhead)[^cite_note-1] or [payload](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Payload)[^cite_note-2] over long distances with high precision. Modern cruise missiles are capable of traveling at high [subsonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerodynamics#Incompressible_aerodynamics), [supersonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supersonic), or [hypersonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypersonic) speeds, are self-navigating, and are able to fly on a non-[ballistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballistics), extremely low-altitude trajectory.[^cite_note-3]&#x20;

## History



The [V-1 flying bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V-1_flying_bomb), the first operational cruise missile

The idea of an "aerial torpedo" was shown in the 1909 British film *[The Airship Destroyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Airship_Destroyer)*, in which flying torpedoes controlled wirelessly are used to bring down airships bombing London.[^cite_note-4]&#x20;

In 1916, the American aviator [Lawrence Sperry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Sperry) built and patented an "aerial torpedo", the [Hewitt-Sperry Automatic Airplane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hewitt-Sperry_Automatic_Airplane), a small [biplane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biplane) carrying a [TNT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TNT) charge, a Sperry [autopilot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autopilot) and barometric altitude control. Inspired by the experiments, the [United States Army](32087.md) developed a similar flying bomb called the [Kettering Bug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kettering_Bug). Germany had also flown trials with remote-controlled aerial gliders *([Torpedogleiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siemens_torpedo_glider))* built by [Siemens-Schuckert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siemens-Schuckert) beginning in 1916.[^cite_note-5]&#x20;

In the interwar period, Britain's [Royal Aircraft Establishment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Aircraft_Establishment) developed the [Larynx (Long Range Gun with Lynx Engine)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAE_Larynx), which underwent a few flight tests in the 1920s.[^cite_note-6]&#x20;

In the Soviet Union, [Sergei Korolev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergei_Korolev) headed the [GIRD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GIRD)-06 cruise missile project from 1932 to 1939, which used a rocket-powered boost-[glide bomb](233100.md) design. The 06/III (RP-216) and 06/IV (RP-212) contained [gyroscopic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyroscopic) guidance systems.[^cite_note-7] The vehicle was designed to boost to 28 km (17 mi) altitude and glide a distance of 280 km (170 mi), but test flights in 1934 and 1936 only reached an altitude of 500 metres (1,600 ft).&#x20;

In 1944, during World War II, Germany deployed the first operational cruise missiles. The [V-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V-1_flying_bomb), often called a [flying bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flying_bomb), contained a gyroscope guidance system and was propelled by a simple [pulsejet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulsejet) engine, the sound of which gave it the nickname of ["buzz bomb" or "doodlebug"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buzz_bomb).[^cite_note-8] Accuracy was sufficient only for use against very large targets (the general area of a city), while the range of 250 km (160 mi) was significantly lower than that of a bomber carrying the same payload. The main advantages were speed (although not sufficient to outperform contemporary propeller-driven interceptors) and expendability. The production cost of a V-1 was only a small fraction of that of a [V-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V-2) [supersonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supersonic) [ballistic missile](38025.md) with a similar-sized warhead.[^cite_note-9] Unlike the V-2, the initial deployments of the V-1 required stationary launch ramps which were susceptible to bombardment.[^cite_note-10] Nazi Germany, in 1943, also developed the [Mistel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mistel) [composite aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composite_aircraft) program, which can be seen as a rudimentary [air-launched cruise missile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air-launched_cruise_missile), where a piloted fighter-type aircraft was mounted atop an unpiloted bomber-sized aircraft that was packed with explosives to be released while approaching the target. Bomber-launched variants of the V-1 saw limited operational service near the end of the war, with the pioneering V-1's design reverse-engineered by the Americans as the [Republic-Ford JB-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic-Ford_JB-2) cruise missile.[^cite_note-11]&#x20;

Immediately after World War II, the [United States Air Force](32090.md) had 21 different guided missile projects, including proposed cruise missiles. By 1948, all but four of these projects had been canceled: the [Air Materiel Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Materiel_Command) Banshee, the [SM-62 Snark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SM-62_Snark), the [SM-64 Navaho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SM-64_Navaho), and the MGM-1 Matador. The Banshee design was similar to [Operation Aphrodite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Aphrodite); like Aphrodite, it failed, and was canceled in April 1949.[^cite_note-12] Concurrently, the US Navy's [Operation Bumblebee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Bumblebee), was conducted at [Topsail Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topsail_Island), [North Carolina](21650.md), from c. 1 June 1946, to 28 July 1948. Bumblebee produced proof-of-concept technologies that influenced the US military's other missile projects.&#x20;

During the [Cold War](325329.md), both the United States and the Soviet Union experimented further with the concept, of deploying early cruise missiles from land, submarines, and aircraft. The main outcome of the United States Navy submarine missile project was the [SSM-N-8 Regulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SSM-N-8_Regulus) missile, based upon the V-1 but powered by an [Allison J33](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allison_J33) jet engine. The Regulus entered service but was phased out with the advent of [submarine launched ballistic missiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_launched_ballistic_missile) that did not require the submarine to surface in order to launch the missile and guide it to its target.&#x20;

The United States Air Force's first operational surface-to-surface missile was the winged, mobile, nuclear-capable [MGM-1 Matador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MGM-1_Matador), also similar in concept to the V-1. Deployment overseas began in 1954, first to [West Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Germany) and later to the [Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_China) and South Korea. On 7 November 1956, the U.S. Air Force deployed Matador units in West Germany, whose missiles were capable of striking targets in the [Warsaw Pact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warsaw_Pact), from their fixed day-to-day sites to unannounced dispersed launch locations. This alert was in response to the crisis posed by the Soviet attack on Hungary which suppressed the [Hungarian Revolution of 1956](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_Revolution_of_1956).&#x20;

Between 1957 and 1961 the United States followed an ambitious and well-funded program to develop a nuclear-powered cruise missile, [Supersonic Low Altitude Missile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supersonic_Low_Altitude_Missile) (SLAM). It was designed to fly below the enemy's radar at speeds above [Mach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mach) 3 and carry [hydrogen bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_bombs) that it would drop along its path over enemy territory. Although the concept was proven sound and the 500-megawatt (670,000 hp) engine finished a successful test run in 1961, no airworthy device was ever completed. The project was finally abandoned in favor of [ICBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ICBM) development.&#x20;

While [ballistic missiles](38025.md) were the preferred weapons for land targets, heavy nuclear and [conventional weapon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conventional_weapon) tipped cruise missiles were seen by the USSR as a primary weapon to destroy United States naval [carrier battle groups](7772.md). Large submarines (for example, [Echo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Echo-class_submarine) and [Oscar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar-class_submarine) classes) were developed to carry these weapons and shadow United States battle groups at sea, and large bombers (for example, [Backfire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tupolev_Tu-22M), [Bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tupolev_Tu-95), and [Blackjack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tupolev_Tu-160) models) were equipped with the weapons in their air-launched cruise missile (ALCM) configuration.&#x20;

## List of cruise missiles by type

See also: [List of cruise missiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cruise_missiles)

Cruise missiles can be categorized by payload/warhead size, speed, range, and launch platform. Often variants of the same missile are produced for different launch platforms (for instance, air- and submarine-launched versions). [Guidance systems](319341.md) vary across missiles. Cruise missiles can be fitted with any of a variety of [navigation systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigation_system) ([inertial navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_navigation), [TERCOM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TERCOM), or [satellite navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_navigation)). Larger cruise missiles can carry either a conventional or a [Nuclear warhead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_warhead), while smaller ones carry only conventional warheads.&#x20;

### Long-range subsonic



India's Nirbhay missiles mounted on a truck-based launcher

China, France, India, Iran, Israel, Japan, North Korea, Russia, South Korea, Ukraine, and the United States have developed several long-range subsonic cruise missiles. These missiles have a range of over 1,000 kilometres (620 mi) and fly at about 800 kilometres per hour (500 mph).[^cite_note-52] They typically have a launch weight of about 1,500 kilograms (3,300 lb)[^cite_note-53] and can carry either a conventional or a nuclear warhead. Earlier versions of these missiles used [inertial navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_navigation); later versions use much more accurate [TERCOM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TERCOM) and [DSMAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TERCOM#DSMAC) systems. Most recent versions can use [satellite navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_navigation).&#x20;

## Deployment



[AGM-129 ACM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-129_ACM) being secured on a [B-52H bomber](18933037.md)

The most common mission for cruise missiles is to attack high-value targets such as ships, command bunkers, bridges and dams. Modern guidance systems permit accurate attacks.&#x20;

As of 2001, the BGM-109 [Tomahawk missile](31238.md) model has become a significant part of the United States naval arsenal. It gives ships and submarines a somewhat accurate, long-range, conventional land attack weapon. Each costs about US\$1.99 million.[^cite_note-85] Both the Tomahawk and the AGM-86 were used extensively during [Operation Desert Storm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Desert_Storm). On 7 April 2017, during the [Syrian Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_the_Syrian_Civil_War_\(January%E2%80%93April_2017\)#April_2017), U.S. warships fired more than 50 cruise missiles into a Syrian airbase in retaliation for a Syrian chemical weapons attack against a rebel stronghold.[^cite_note-86]&#x20;

The [United States Air Force](32090.md) (USAF) deploys an [air-launched cruise missile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air-launched_cruise_missile), the [AGM-86 ALCM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-86_ALCM). The [Boeing B-52 Stratofortress](18933037.md) is the exclusive delivery vehicle for the AGM-86 and [AGM-129 ACM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-129_ACM). Both missile types are configurable for either conventional or nuclear warheads.&#x20;

The USAF adopted the AGM-86 for its bomber fleet while AGM-109 was adapted to launch from trucks and ships and adopted by the USAF and Navy. The truck-launched versions, and also the Pershing II and SS-20 Intermediate Range Ballistic Missiles, were later destroyed under the bilateral INF (Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces) treaty with the USSR.&#x20;

The British [Royal Navy](26061.md) (RN) also operates cruise missiles, specifically the U.S.-made Tomahawk, used by the RN's nuclear submarine fleet. UK conventional warhead versions were first fired in combat by the RN in 1999, during the [Kosovo War](16760.md) (the United States fired cruise missiles in 1991). The [Royal Air Force](25679.md) uses the [Storm Shadow](306908.md) cruise missile on its [Typhoon](167667.md) and previously its [Tornado GR4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tornado_GR4) aircraft. It is also used by France, where it is known as SCALP EG, and carried by the [Armée de l'Air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arm%C3%A9e_de_l%27Air)'s [Mirage 2000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirage_2000) and [Rafale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rafale) aircraft.&#x20;



The [Indian Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Army)'s BrahMos supersonic cruise missiles mounted on Mobile Autonomous Launchers (MAL)

India and Russia have jointly developed the supersonic cruise missile [BrahMos](1814876.md). There are three versions of the Brahmos: ship/land-launched, air-launched, and sub-launched. The ship/land-launched version was operational as of late 2007. The Brahmos have the capability to attack targets on land. Russia also continues to operate other cruise missiles: the [SS-N-12 Sandbox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS-N-12_Sandbox), [SS-N-19 Shipwreck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS-N-19_Shipwreck), [SS-N-22 Sunburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS-N-22_Sunburn) and [SS-N-25 Switchblade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS-N-25_Switchblade). Germany and Spain operate the [Taurus missile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KEPD_350) while Pakistan has made the [Babur missile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babur_missile)[^cite_note-CSIS_Missile_Threat3-87] Both the [People's Republic of China](5405.md) and the [Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_China) ([Taiwan](25734.md)) have designed several cruise missile variants, such as the well-known [C-802](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C-802), some of which are capable of carrying biological, chemical, nuclear, and conventional warheads.&#x20;

#### France

See also: [France and weapons of mass destruction](589091.md)

The French *[Force de frappe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_de_frappe)* nuclear forces include both land and sea-based bombers with *[Air-Sol Moyenne Portée](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air-Sol_Moyenne_Port%C3%A9e)* (ASMP) high-speed medium-range nuclear cruise missiles. India in 2017 successfully flight-tested its indigenous [Nirbhay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nirbhay) ('Fearless') land-attack cruise missile, which can deliver nuclear warheads to a strike range of 1,000 km.[^cite_note-Nirbhay,_ToI,_2017-92] Nirbhay had been flight-tested successfully.[^cite_note-Nirbhay,_ToI,_2017-92][^cite_note-93]&#x20;

#### Pakistan

See also: [Pakistan and weapons of mass destruction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan_and_weapons_of_mass_destruction)

Pakistan currently has five cruise missile systems: the [air-launched](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air-launched_cruise_missile) [Ra'ad-I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatf-VIII_\(Ra%27ad\)) and its enhanced version [Ra'ad-II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ra%27ad-II) as well as [Taimoor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taimoor); the ground and [submarine launched](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine-launched_cruise_missile) [Babur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babur_\(cruise_missile\));[^cite_note-CSIS_Missile_Threat3-87][^cite_note-95][^cite_note-96] ship-launched [Harbah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harbah) missile[^cite_note-97] and surface launched [Zarb missile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/YJ-62).[^cite_note-98] Both, [Ra'ad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatf-VIII_\(Ra%27ad\)) and [Babur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babur_\(cruise_missile\)), can carry nuclear warheads between 10 and 25 kt, and deliver them to targets at a range of up to 300 km (190 mi) and 450 km (280 mi) respectively.[^cite_note-99] Babur has been in service with the [Pakistan Army](1965925.md) since 2010, and [Pakistan Navy](1183789.md) since 2018.[^cite_note-100]&#x20;

#### Russia

See also: [Russia and weapons of mass destruction](588941.md)



Export variant of the [Kalibr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3M-54_Kalibr) missile

Russia has [Kh-55SM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raduga_Kh-55) cruise missiles, with a range similar to the United States' [AGM-129](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-129) range of 3000 km, but are able to carry a more powerful warhead of 200 kt. They are equipped with a [TERCOM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TERCOM) system which allows them to cruise at an altitude lower than 110 meters at subsonic speeds while obtaining a [CEP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_error_probable) accuracy of 15 meters with an [inertial navigation system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_navigation_system). They are air-launched from either [Tupolev Tu-95s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tupolev_Tu-95), [Tupolev Tu-22Ms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tupolev_Tu-22M), or [Tupolev Tu-160s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tupolev_Tu-160), each able to carry 16 for the Tu-95, 12 for the Tu-160, and 4 for the Tu-22M. A [stealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stealth_technology) version of the missile, the Kh-101 is in development. It has similar qualities as the Kh-55, except that its range has been extended to 5,000 km, is equipped with a 1,000 kg conventional warhead, and has stealth features which reduce its probability of intercept.[^cite_note-101]&#x20;

After the collapse of the Soviet Union, the most recent cruise missile developed was the [Kalibr missile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalibr_missile) which entered production in the early 1990s and was officially inducted into the Russian arsenal in 1994. However, it only saw its combat debut on 7 October 2015, in [Syria](7515849.md) as a part of the [Russian military campaign in Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_military_intervention_in_the_Syrian_Civil_War). The missile has been used 14 more times in combat operations in Syria since its debut.&#x20;

In the late 1950s and early 1960s, the Soviet Union was attempting to develop cruise missiles. In this short time frame, the Soviet Union was working on nearly ten different types of cruise missiles. However, due to resources, most of the initial types of cruise missiles developed by the Soviet Union were Sea-Launched Cruise Missiles or Submarine-Launched Cruise Missiles ([SLCMs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine-launched_cruise_missile)). The [SS-N-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS-N-1) cruise missile was developed to have different configurations to be fired from a submarine or a ship. However, as time progressed, the Soviet Union began to work on air-launched cruise missiles as well ([ALCM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ALCM)). These ACLM missiles were typically delivered via bombers designated as "Blinders" or "Backfire". The missiles in this configuration were called the AS-1, and AS-2 with eventual new variants with more development time. The main purpose of Soviet-based cruise missiles was to have defense and offensive mechanisms against enemy ships; in other words, most of the Soviet cruise missiles were anti-ship missiles. In the 1980s the Soviet Union had developed an arsenal of cruise missiles nearing 600 platforms which consisted of land, sea, and air delivery systems.[^cite_note-102]&#x20;
