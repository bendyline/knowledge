**Falcon Heavy** is a [super heavy-lift launch vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_heavy-lift_launch_vehicle)[^cite_note-9] with [partial reusability](336014.md) that can carry cargo into Earth orbit and beyond. It is designed, manufactured and launched by American [aerospace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerospace_manufacturer) company [SpaceX](832774.md).

The rocket consists of a center core on which two [Falcon 9](2647515.md) boosters are attached, and a second stage on top of the center core.[^cite_note-sxf9o20100508-10] Falcon Heavy has the second highest payload capacity of any currently operational launch vehicle behind [NASA](18426568.md)'s [Space Launch System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Launch_System) (SLS), and the fourth-highest capacity of any rocket to reach orbit, trailing behind the SLS, [Energia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energia_\(rocket\)) and the [Saturn V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_V).

SpaceX conducted [Falcon Heavy's maiden launch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Heavy_test_flight) on February 6, 2018, at 20\:45 [UTC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinated_Universal_Time).[^cite_note-cbs-harwood-11] As a [dummy payload](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boilerplate_\(spaceflight\)), the rocket carried [a Tesla Roadster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elon_Musk's_Tesla_Roadster) belonging to SpaceX founder [Elon Musk](909036.md), with a mannequin dubbed "Starman" in the driver's seat.[^cite_note-bbc-12] The second Falcon Heavy launch occurred on April 11, 2019, and all three booster rockets successfully returned to [Earth](9228.md).[^cite_note-13] The third Falcon Heavy launch successfully occurred on June 25, 2019. Since then, Falcon Heavy has been certified for the [National Security Space Launch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Security_Space_Launch) (NSSL) program.[^cite_note-14]

Falcon Heavy was designed to be able to carry humans into space beyond [low Earth orbit](47568.md) (LEO), although as of February 2018, SpaceX does not intend to transport people on Falcon Heavy, nor pursue the [human-rating certification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human-rating_certification) process to transport NASA [astronauts](664.md).[^cite_note-wsj-15] Both Falcon Heavy and Falcon 9 are expected to eventually be superseded by the [Starship](59118602.md) super-heavy lift launch vehicle, currently being developed.[^cite_note-sn20170929-16]

## History

Concepts for a Falcon Heavy launch vehicle using three [Falcon 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_1) core boosters, with an approximate payload-to-LEO capacity of two tons,[^cite_note-17] were initially discussed as early as 2003.[^cite_note-18] The concept for three core booster stages of the company's then as-yet-unflown [Falcon 9](2647515.md) was referred to in 2005 as the *Falcon 9 Heavy*.[^cite_note-nsf20051010-19]

SpaceX unveiled the plan for the Falcon Heavy to the public at a [Washington, D.C.](108956.md), news conference in April 2011, with an initial test flight expected in 2013.[^cite_note-sfn20110405-20]

A number of factors delayed the planned maiden flight to 2018, including two anomalies with Falcon 9 launch vehicles, which required all engineering resources to be dedicated to failure analysis, halting flight operations for many months. The integration and structural challenges of combining three Falcon 9 cores were more difficult than expected.[^cite_note-sdc20170720-21]

In July 2017, Elon Musk said, "It actually ended up being way harder to do Falcon Heavy than we thought. ... We were pretty naive about that".[^cite_note-issR\&Dconf20170719-36\:00-22]

The initial test flight for the first Falcon Heavy lifted off on February 6, 2018, at 20\:45 UTC, carrying its dummy payload, [Elon Musk's personal Tesla Roadster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elon_Musk's_Tesla_Roadster), beyond Mars orbit.[^cite_note-cbs-harwood-11]

### Conception and funding

Musk first mentioned Falcon Heavy in a September 2005 news update, referring to a customer request from 18 months prior.[^cite_note-23] Various solutions using the planned [Falcon 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_5) (which was never flown) had been explored, but the only cost-effective, reliable iteration was one that used a 9-engine first stage—the Falcon 9. The Falcon Heavy was developed using [private](752732.md) [capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_capital) with Musk stating that the cost was more than US\$500 million. No government financing was provided for its development.[^cite_note-tsr20140310-24]

### Design and development



From left to right, [Falcon 9 v1.0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_v1.0), three versions of [Falcon 9 v1.1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_v1.1), three versions of [Falcon 9 v1.2 (Full Thrust)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_Full_Thrust), three versions of [Falcon 9 Block 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_Block_5), Falcon Heavy and Falcon Heavy Block 5

The Falcon Heavy design is based on [Falcon 9](2647515.md#Development_history)'s [fuselage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuselage) and engines. By 2008, SpaceX had been aiming for the first launch of Falcon 9 in 2009, while "Falcon 9 Heavy would be in a couple of years". Speaking at the 2008 [Mars Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_Society) Conference, Musk also indicated that he expected a hydrogen-fueled upper stage would follow two to three years later (which would have been around 2013).[^cite_note-25]

By April 2011, the capabilities and performance of the Falcon 9 vehicle were better understood, SpaceX having completed two successful demonstration missions to [low Earth orbit](47568.md) (LEO), one of which included [reignition of the second-stage engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_COTS_Demo_Flight_1#Second_stage). At a press conference at the [National Press Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Press_Club_\(United_States\)) in Washington, D.C., on April 5, 2011, Musk stated that Falcon Heavy would "carry more payload to orbit or [escape velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escape_velocity) than any vehicle in history, apart from the [Saturn V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_V) Moon rocket ... and Soviet [Energia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energia_\(rocket\)) rocket".[^cite_note-PR-20110815-26] In the same year, with the expected increase in demand for both variants, SpaceX announced plans to expand manufacturing capacity "as we build towards the capability of producing a Falcon 9 first stage or Falcon Heavy side booster every week and an upper stage every two weeks".[^cite_note-PR-20110815-26]

In 2015, SpaceX announced a number of changes to the Falcon Heavy rocket, worked in parallel to the [upgrade of the Falcon 9 v1.1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_Full_Thrust) launch vehicle.[^cite_note-sn20150320-27] In December 2016, SpaceX released a photo showing the Falcon Heavy interstage at the company headquarters in [Hawthorne, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawthorne,_California).[^cite_note-28]

### Testing

By May 2013, a new, partly underground test stand was being built at the [SpaceX Rocket Development and Test Facility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_launch_facilities) in [McGregor, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McGregor,_Texas), specifically to test the triple cores and twenty-seven rocket engines of the Falcon Heavy.[^cite_note-29] By May 2017, SpaceX conducted the first static fire test of flight-design Falcon Heavy center core at the McGregor facility.[^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31]

In July 2017, Musk discussed publicly the challenges of testing a complex launch vehicle like the three-core Falcon Heavy, indicating that a large extent of the new design "is really impossible to test on the ground" and could not be effectively tested independent of actual [flight tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_test).[^cite_note-issR\&Dconf20170719-36\:00-22]

By September 2017, all three first stage cores had completed their static fire tests on the ground test stand.[^cite_note-32] The first Falcon Heavy static fire test was conducted on January 24, 2018.[^cite_note-staticfireverge-33]

### Maiden flight

Main article: [Falcon Heavy test flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Heavy_test_flight)

In April 2011, Musk was planning for a first launch of Falcon Heavy from [Vandenberg Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vandenberg_Air_Force_Base), California on the United States [west coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Coast_of_the_United_States) in 2013.[^cite_note-PR-20110815-26][^cite_note-reut20110405-34] SpaceX refurbished [Launch Complex 4E](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vandenberg_Space_Launch_Complex_4) at Vandenberg AFB to accommodate Falcon 9 and Heavy. The first launch from the [Cape Canaveral](306778.md), Florida [east coast](89126.md) launch complex was planned for late 2013 or 2014.[^cite_note-PR-20110405-35]

Due partly to the failure of [SpaceX CRS-7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_CRS-7) in June 2015, SpaceX rescheduled the maiden Falcon Heavy flight in September 2015 to occur no earlier than April 2016.[^cite_note-sn20150902-36] The flight was to be launched from the refurbished [Kennedy Space Center Launch Complex 39A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kennedy_Space_Center_Launch_Complex_39A).[^cite_note-Falcon_Heavy_2015_Launch-37][^cite_note-38] The flight was postponed again to late 2016, early 2017,[^cite_note-nsf-20160809-39] summer 2017,[^cite_note-red_dragon_2020-40] late 2017[^cite_note-41] and finally to February 2018.[^cite_note-sfn_fh_january-42]

At a July 2017 meeting of the [International Space Station](15043.md) Research and Development meeting in [Washington, D.C.](108956.md), Musk downplayed expectations for the success of the maiden flight:

> There's a real good chance the vehicle won't make it to orbit ... I hope it makes it far enough away from the pad that it does not cause pad damage. I would consider even that a win, to be honest.[^cite_note-issR\&Dconf20170719-36\:00-22]

In December 2017, Musk tweeted that the dummy payload on the maiden Falcon Heavy launch would be [his personal Tesla Roadster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elon_Musk's_Tesla_Roadster) playing [David Bowie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bowie)'s "[Space Oddity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Oddity_\(song\))" (though the song actually used for the launch was "[Life on Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_on_Mars_\(song\))"), and that it would be launched into an [orbit around the Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliocentric_orbit) that will reach the orbit of [Mars](14640471.md).[^cite_note-P_Plait-43][^cite_note-sn20171202-44] He released pictures in the following days.[^cite_note-45] The car had three cameras attached to provide "epic views".[^cite_note-bbc-12]

On December 28, 2017, the Falcon Heavy was moved to the launch pad in preparation of a static fire test of all 27 engines, which was expected on January 19, 2018.[^cite_note-46] However, due to the [U.S. government partial shutdown that began on January 20, 2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/January_2018_United_States_federal_government_shutdown), the testing and launch were further delayed.[^cite_note-47] The static fire test was conducted on January 24, 2018.[^cite_note-staticfireverge-33][^cite_note-48] Musk confirmed via [Twitter](9988187.md) that the test "was good" and later announced the rocket would be launched on February 6, 2018.[^cite_note-elon_tweet_27jan-49]



[Maiden launch of the Falcon Heavy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Heavy_test_flight)

On February 6, 2018, after a delay of over two hours due to high winds,[^cite_note-spax_tween_06feb-50] Falcon Heavy lifted off at 20\:45 UTC.[^cite_note-cbs-harwood-11] Its side boosters landed safely on [Landing Zones 1 and 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landing_Zones_1_and_2) a few minutes later.[^cite_note-elon_tweet_06feb-51] However, only one of the three engines on the center booster that were intended to restart ignited during descent, causing the booster to be destroyed upon impacting the ocean at a speed of over 480 km/h (300 mph).[^cite_note-52][^cite_note-\:0-53]

Initially, Elon Musk tweeted that the Roadster had overshot its planned [heliocentric orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliocentric_orbit), and would reach the [asteroid belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid_belt). Later, observations by telescopes showed that the Roadster would only slightly exceed the orbit of Mars at [aphelion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aphelion).[^cite_note-54]

### Later flights

Main article: [List of Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy launches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Falcon_9_and_Falcon_Heavy_launches)



Falcon Heavy built to [Falcon 9 Block 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_Block_5) specifications on the launch pad in June 2019

A year after the successful demo flight, SpaceX had signed five commercial contracts worth US\$500–750 million, meaning that it had managed to cover the development cost of the rocket.[^cite_note-55] The second flight, and first commercial one, occurred on April 11, 2019,[^cite_note-Cooper-56] launching [Arabsat-6A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabsat-6A), with all three boosters landing successfully for the first time.

The third flight occurred on June 25, 2019, launching the [STP-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Test_Program) (U.S. Department of Defense Space Test Program) payload.[^cite_note-Cooper-56] The payload was composed of 25 small spacecraft.[^cite_note-STP2_payload-57] Operational [Geostationary transfer orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geostationary_transfer_orbit) (GTO) missions for [Intelsat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelsat) and [Inmarsat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inmarsat), which were planned for late 2017, were moved to the [Falcon 9 Full Thrust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_Full_Thrust) rocket version as it had become powerful enough to lift those heavy payloads in its expendable configuration.[^cite_note-sfn-20160830-58][^cite_note-sn-20161103-59] In June 2022, the [U.S. Space Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Space_Force) certified Falcon Heavy for launching its top secret satellites, with the first such launch being USSF-44 which happened at November 1, 2022;[^cite_note-60] and the second of which being USSF-67,[^cite_note-61] which was launched 11 weeks after USSF-44. [ViaSat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ViaSat) selected the Falcon Heavy in late 2018 for the launch of its [ViaSat-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ViaSat-3) satellite which was scheduled to launch in the 2020–2022 timeframe;[^cite_note-62] however it would not launch until May 1, 2023.[^cite_note-63] On October 13, 2023, Falcon Heavy embarked on its 8th flight carrying NASA's [Psyche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psyche_\(spacecraft\)) probe to the asteroid [16 Psyche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/16_Psyche). This mission only had the side boosters return to Earth with the center core expended, a decision made to create more tolerable margins for the mission.

Following the announcement of NASA's [Artemis program](60758751.md) of returning humans to the Moon, the Falcon Heavy rocket has been mentioned several times as an alternative to the expensive [Space Launch System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Launch_System) (SLS) program, but NASA decided to exclusively use SLS to launch the [Orion capsule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orion_\(spacecraft\)).[^cite_note-64][^cite_note-Commercial_LVs-65] Falcon Heavy was contracted to transport the [Dragon XL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_XL) spacecraft to the [Lunar Gateway](53648310.md). It was also selected to launch the first two elements of the Lunar Gateway, the [Power and Propulsion Element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_and_Propulsion_Element) (PPE), and the [Habitation and Logistics Outpost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habitation_and_Logistics_Outpost) (HALO).[^cite_note-nasa-20210709-66] However, in March 2026, NASA announced it would pause development of Gateway and reallocate hardware toward a [lunar surface base](8420417.md).[^cite_note-67] Falcon Heavy will launch [Astrobotic Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrobotic_Technology)'s Griffin lander as part of the Artemis Program's [Commercial Lunar Payload Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Lunar_Payload_Services) (CLPS) initiative. On October 14, 2024, Falcon Heavy transported NASA's [Europa Clipper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europa_Clipper) into space to explore Jupiter's moon [Europa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europa_\(moon\)).[^cite_note-68]

## Design



Falcon Heavy on NASA launch pad [LC-39A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kennedy_Space_Center_Launch_Complex_39A)

Falcon Heavy consists of a structurally strengthened Falcon 9 as the "core" component, with two additional [Falcon 9](2647515.md) first stages with aerodynamic nose-cones mounted outboard serving as [strap-on boosters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_rocket),[^cite_note-sxf9o20100508-10] conceptually similar to the [Delta IV Heavy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_IV_Heavy) launcher and proposals for the [Atlas V Heavy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_V#Atlas_V_Heavy) and Russian [Angara A5V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angara_\(rocket_family\)#Angara_A5). This triple first stage carries a standard Falcon 9 second stage, which in turn carries the payload in a fairing. Falcon Heavy has the second highest lift capability of any operational rocket, with a payload of 63,800 kg (140,700 lb) to low Earth orbit, 26,700 kg (58,900 lb) to [Geostationary Transfer Orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geostationary_Transfer_Orbit), and 16,800 kg (37,000 lb) to [trans-Mars injection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliocentric_orbit#Trans-Mars_injection).[^cite_note-Capabilities_&_Services-69] The rocket was designed to meet or exceed all current requirements of human rating. The structural safety margins are 40% above flight loads, higher than the 25% margins of other rockets.[^cite_note-70] Falcon Heavy was designed from the outset to carry humans into space and it would restore the possibility of flying crewed missions to the Moon or Mars.[^cite_note-sxfh-4]



The Merlin 1D engine

The first stage is powered by three Falcon 9 derived cores, each equipped with nine [Merlin 1D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merlin_1D) engines. The Falcon Heavy has a total sea-level thrust at liftoff of 22.82 MN (5,130,000 lbf), from the 27 Merlin 1D engines, while thrust rises to 24.68 MN (5,550,000 lbf) as the craft climbs out of the atmosphere.[^cite_note-sxfh-4] The upper stage is powered by a single Merlin 1D engine modified for vacuum operation, with a thrust of 934 kN (210,000 lbf), an expansion ratio of 117\:1 and a nominal burn time of 397 seconds. At launch, the center core throttles to full power for a few seconds for additional thrust, then throttles down. This allows a longer burn time. After the side boosters separate, the center core throttles back up to maximum thrust. For added reliability of restart, the engine has dual redundant [pyrophoric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrophoric) igniters ([Triethylaluminium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triethylaluminium)-[Triethylborane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triethylborane)) (TEA-TEB).[^cite_note-sxf9o20100508-10] The interstage, which connects the upper and lower stage for Falcon 9, is a carbon fiber [aluminum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium) core composite structure. Stage separation occurs via reusable separation [collets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collet) and a pneumatic pusher system. The Falcon 9 tank walls and domes are made from [Aluminum–lithium alloy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium%E2%80%93lithium_alloy). SpaceX uses an all-[friction stir welded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friction_stir_welding) tank. The second stage tank of Falcon 9 is simply a shorter version of the first stage tank and uses most of the same tooling, material, and manufacturing techniques. This approach reduces manufacturing costs during vehicle production.[^cite_note-sxf9o20100508-10]



A Falcon Heavy with a second stage mission-extension kit, boosters set up for recovery, and an expendable center core.

All three cores of the Falcon Heavy arrange the engines in a structural form SpaceX calls *Octaweb*, aimed at streamlining the manufacturing process,[^cite_note-sxn20130802octaweb-71] and each core includes four extensible landing legs.[^cite_note-sxn20130802fh_legs-72] To control the descent of the boosters and center core through the atmosphere, SpaceX uses four retractable [grid fins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grid_fins) at the top of each of the three Falcon 9 boosters, which extend after separation.[^cite_note-73] Immediately after the side boosters separate, three engines in each continue to burn for a few seconds in order to control the booster's trajectory safely away from the rocket.[^cite_note-sxn20130802fh_legs-72][^cite_note-faa201304v1-74] The grid fins then deploy as the boosters turn back to [Earth](9228.md), followed by the extending of the landing legs. The side boosters land softly on the ground in fully/partially reusable launch configuration. The center core continues to fire until stage separation. In fully reusable launches, its grid fins and legs deploy and the center core touches down on a drone ship. If boosters are to be expended, then the landing legs and grid fins are omitted from the vehicle. The landing legs are made of carbon fiber with an aluminum [honeycomb structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honeycomb_structure). The four legs stow along the sides of each core during liftoff and extend outward and down just before landing.[^cite_note-pm20120207-75]

Most Falcon Heavy launches have used a second stage equipped with a medium or long mission‑extension kit, which enable a longer coast phase between second‑stage ignitions. The kit includes strengthened [COPV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composite_overwrapped_pressure_vessel) tanks for pressurization control, additional [TEA-TEB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TEA-TEB) ignition fluid, and most visibly, a gray band painted on the metal skin over the [RP-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RP-1) tank, which absorbs sunlight and helps prevent the propellant from freezing during the long coast phase.[^cite_note-76]

The Falcon Heavy uses a 4.5 m (15 ft) [interstage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstage) attached to the first stage core.[^cite_note-EspaceEtExplorationNo51-77] It is a [composite structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composite_material) consisting of an [aluminum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium) [honeycomb core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honeycomb_structure) surrounded by [carbon fiber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_fiber) face sheet plies. Unlike for Falcon 9, the black thermal protection layer on the interstage of Block 5 center core boosters is later painted white, as seen in the Falcon Heavy flights so far, probably due to aesthetics of the Falcon Heavy Logo, providing it a greyish color.[^cite_note-78] The overall length of the vehicle at launch is 70 m (230 ft), and the total fueled mass is 1,420 t (3,130,000 lb). Without recovery of any stage, the Falcon Heavy can theoretically inject a 63.8 t (141,000 lb) payload into a low Earth orbit, or 16.8 t (37,000 lb) to [Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus) or [Mars](14640471.md).[^cite_note-EspaceEtExplorationNo51-77] However, because of the structural limitations, the maximum weight Falcon Heavy can lift is reduced.[^cite_note-gao202206-79]

The Falcon Heavy includes first-stage [recovery systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_reusable_launch_system_development_program#Technologies), to allow SpaceX to [return the first stage boosters to the launch site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Return_to_launch_site) as well as recover the first stage core following landing at an [Autonomous Spaceport Drone Ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_Spaceport_Drone_Ship) barge after completion of primary mission requirements. These systems include four deployable [landing legs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Launch_vehicle_landing_gear), which are locked against each first-stage tank core during ascent and deploy just prior to touchdown. Excess propellant reserved for Falcon Heavy first-stage recovery operations will be diverted for use on the primary mission objective, if required, ensuring sufficient performance margins for successful missions. The nominal payload capacity to a [geostationary transfer orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geostationary_transfer_orbit) (GTO) is 8 t (18,000 lb) with recovery of all three first-stage cores (the price per launch is US\$97 million), versus 26.7 t (59,000 lb) in fully expendable mode. The Falcon Heavy can also inject a 16 t (35,000 lb) payload into GTO if only the two side boosters are recovered.[^cite_note-EspaceEtExplorationNo51-77]

## Capabilities



Twenty-seven Merlin engines firing during launch of [Arabsat-6A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabsat-6A) in 2019

The partially reusable Falcon Heavy falls into the [heavy-lift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy-lift_launch_vehicle) range of launch systems, capable of lifting 20–50 t (44,000–110,000 lb) into low Earth orbit (LEO), under the classification system used by a NASA human spaceflight review panel.[^cite_note-hsf200910-80] A fully expendable Falcon Heavy is in the [super heavy-lift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_heavy-lift_launch_vehicle) category with a maximum payload of 64 t (141,000 lb) to LEO.

The initial concept (Falcon 9-S9 2005) envisioned payloads of 24.75 t (54,600 lb) to LEO, but by April 2011 this was projected to be up to 53 t (117,000 lb)[^cite_note-sxfh-20110404-81] with [geostationary transfer orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geostationary_transfer_orbit) (GTO) payloads up to 12 t (26,000 lb).[^cite_note-sx20111203-82] Later reports in 2011 projected higher payloads beyond LEO, including 19 t (42,000 lb) to GTO,[^cite_note-83] 16 t (35,000 lb) to [translunar trajectory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-lunar_injection), and 14 t (31,000 lb) on a trans-Martian orbit to [Mars](14640471.md).[^cite_note-spx110405-84][^cite_note-8m-85]

By late 2013, SpaceX raised the projected GTO payload for Falcon Heavy to 21.2 t (47,000 lb).[^cite_note-86]



Long exposure of a night launch, June 25, 2019

In April 2017, the projected LEO payload for Falcon Heavy was raised from 54.4 to 63.8 t (120,000 to 141,000 lb). The maximum payload is achieved when the rocket flies a fully [expendable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expendable_launch_system) launch profile, not recovering any of the three first-stage boosters.[^cite_note-spacex-capabilities-2] With just the core booster expended, and two side-boosters recovered, Musk estimates the payload penalty to be around 10%, which would still yield over 57 t (126,000 lb) of lift capability to LEO.[^cite_note-87] Returning all three boosters to the launch site rather than landing them on drone ships would yield about 30 t of payload to LEO.[^cite_note-musk20170929payload-88]

### Reusability

Main article: [SpaceX reusable launch system development program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_reusable_launch_system_development_program)



Falcon Heavy [reusable side boosters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reusable_launch_system) land in unison at [Cape Canaveral](306778.md) [Landing Zones 1 and 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landing_Zones_1_and_2) following a [test flight on February 6, 2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Heavy_test_flight)

From 2013 to 2016, SpaceX conducted parallel development of a reusable rocket architecture for [Falcon 9](2647515.md), that applies to parts of Falcon Heavy as well. Early on, SpaceX had expressed hopes that all rocket stages would eventually be [reusable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reusable_launch_system).[^cite_note-nsf20090112-89] SpaceX has since demonstrated routine land and sea recovery of the [Falcon 9 first stage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Falcon_9_first-stage_boosters), and has successfully recovered multiple [payload fairings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Payload_fairing).[^cite_note-90][^cite_note-sfn20170331-91] In the case of Falcon Heavy, the two outer cores separate from the rocket earlier in the flight, and are thus moving at a lower velocity than in a Falcon 9 launch profile.[^cite_note-pm20120207-75] For the first flight of Falcon Heavy, SpaceX had considered attempting to recover the second stage,[^cite_note-92] but did not execute this plan.

Falcon Heavy payload performance to [geosynchronous transfer orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geosynchronous_transfer_orbit) (GTO) is reduced by the reusable technology, but at a much lower price. When recovering all three booster cores, GTO payload is 8 t (18,000 lb).[^cite_note-spacex-capabilities-2] If only the two outside cores are recovered while the center core is expended, GTO payload would be approximately 16 t (35,000 lb).[^cite_note-EspaceEtExplorationNo51-77] As a comparison, the next-heaviest contemporary rocket until April 2024, the fully expendable Delta IV Heavy, could deliver 14.2 t (31,000 lb) to GTO.[^cite_note-93]

### Propellant crossfeed

Falcon Heavy was originally designed with a "propellant crossfeed" capability, whereby the center core engines would be supplied with fuel and oxidizer from the two side cores until their [separation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separation_event).[^cite_note-nss201111-94] This approach had previously been proposed by [Vladimir Chelomei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Chelomei) for the [UR-700](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Rocket#UR-700) launch system. Operating all engines at full thrust from launch, with fuel supplied mainly from the side boosters, would deplete the side boosters sooner, allowing their earlier separation to reduce the mass being accelerated. This would leave most of the center core propellant available after booster separation.[^cite_note-prop_cross-95]

Musk stated in 2016 that crossfeed would not be implemented.[^cite_note-96] Instead, the center booster throttles down shortly after liftoff to conserve fuel, and resumes full thrust after the side boosters have separated.[^cite_note-sxfh-4]

## Launch prices



A Falcon Heavy launching an [ViaSat-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ViaSat-3) from [LC-39A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LC-39A) in 2026.

At an appearance in May 2004 before the [United States Senate Committee on Commerce, Science, and Transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Senate_Committee_on_Commerce,_Science,_and_Transportation), Musk testified, "Long term plans call for development of a heavy lift product and even a super-heavy, if there is customer demand. We expect that each size increase would result in a meaningful decrease in cost per pound to orbit. ... Ultimately, I believe US\$500 per pound or less is very achievable".[^cite_note-USsenate200405-97] This \$1,100/kg (\$500/lb) goal stated by Musk in 2011 is 35% of the cost of the lowest-cost-per-pound [LEO](47568.md)-capable launch system in a 2001 study: the [Zenit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zenit_\(rocket_family\)), a medium-lift launch vehicle that could carry 14 t (31,000 lb) into LEO for US\$35–50 million.[^cite_note-98] In 2011, SpaceX stated that the cost of reaching [low Earth orbit](47568.md) could be as low as \$2,200/kg (\$1,000/lb) if an annual rate of four launches can be sustained, and as of 2011 planned to eventually launch as many as 10 Falcon Heavies and 10 Falcon 9s annually.[^cite_note-spx110405-84]

The published prices for Falcon Heavy launches have changed as development progressed, with announced prices for the various versions of Falcon Heavy priced at US\$80–125 million in 2011,[^cite_note-sxfh-20110404-81] US\$83–128 million in 2012,[^cite_note-sx20111203-82] US\$77–135 million in 2013,[^cite_note-sx20130909-99] US\$85 million for up to 6.4 t (14,000 lb) to [GTO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geostationary_transfer_orbit) in 2014, US\$90 million for up to 8 t (18,000 lb) to GTO in 2016.[^cite_note-100]

From 2017 to early 2022, the price has been stated at US\$150 million for 63.8 t (141,000 lb) to LEO or 26.7 t (59,000 lb) to GTO (fully expendable).[^cite_note-101] This equates to a price of US\$2,350 per kg to LEO and US\$5,620 per kg to GTO. In 2022, the published price for a reusable launch was \$97 million.[^cite_note-spacex-capabilities2-102] In 2022 NASA contracted with SpaceX to launch the [Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope](28371067.md) on a Falcon Heavy for approximately \$255 million, including launch service and other mission related costs.[^cite_note-103]

The nearest competing U.S. rocket was [United Launch Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Launch_Alliance)'s [Delta IV Heavy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_IV_Heavy) with a LEO payload capacity of 28.4 t (63,000 lb) costs US\$12,340 per kg to LEO and US\$24,630 per kg to GTO.[^cite_note-104] The Delta IV Heavy was retired in 2024.

Competitors from 2024 onwards may include [Blue Origin](887418.md)'s [New Glenn](51585988.md) (45 t to LEO), [Relativity Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativity_Space)'s [Terran R](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terran_R) (34 t to LEO), and United Launch Alliance (ULA) [Vulcan Centaur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulcan_Centaur) (27 t to LEO). From late 2025 onwards, the SpaceX [Starship](59118602.md) (100+ t to LEO) may also be included.

### First commercial contracts

In May 2012, SpaceX announced that [Intelsat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelsat) had signed the first commercial contract for a Falcon Heavy flight. It was not confirmed at the time when the first Intelsat launch would occur, but the agreement had SpaceX delivering satellites to [geosynchronous transfer orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geosynchronous_transfer_orbit) (GTO).[^cite_note-ro20120530-193][^cite_note-sx20120529-194] In August 2016, it emerged that this Intelsat contract had been reassigned to a [Falcon 9 Full Thrust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_Full_Thrust) mission to deliver [Intelsat 35e](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelsat_35e) into orbit in the third quarter of 2017.[^cite_note-sfn-20160830-58] Performance improvements of the [Falcon 9](2647515.md) vehicle family since the 2012 announcement, advertising 8.3 t (18,000 lb) to GTO for its expendable flight profile,[^cite_note-spacex-falcon9-195] enabled the launch of this 6 t satellite without upgrading to a Falcon Heavy variant.

In 2014, [Inmarsat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inmarsat) booked three launches with Falcon Heavy,[^cite_note-spacenews20140702-196] but due to delays, switched a payload to [Ariane 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_5) for 2017.[^cite_note-sn-20161208-197] Similarly to the [Intelsat 35e](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelsat_35e) case, another satellite from this contract, [Inmarsat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inmarsat) 5-F4, was switched to a Falcon 9 Full Thrust due to the increased liftoff capacity.[^cite_note-sn-20161103-59] The remaining contract covered the launch of [Inmarsat-6 F1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inmarsat-6_F1) in 2020 on a [Falcon 9](2647515.md).[^cite_note-gunter-inmarsat6-198]

### Department of Defense contracts

In December 2012, SpaceX announced its first Falcon Heavy launch contract with the [United States Department of Defense](7279897.md) (DoD). The [United States Air Force](32090.md) [Space and Missile Systems Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_and_Missile_Systems_Center) awarded SpaceX two [Evolved Expendable Launch Vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolved_Expendable_Launch_Vehicle) (EELV)-class missions, including the [Space Test Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Test_Program) 2 (STP-2) mission for Falcon Heavy, originally scheduled to be launched in March 2017,[^cite_note-Leonard2016-199][^cite_note-spacenews-20160809-200] to be placed at a near circular orbit at an altitude of 700 km (430 mi), with an inclination of 70.0°.[^cite_note-eoPortal-201]

In April 2015, SpaceX sent the U.S. Air Force an updated letter of intent outlining a certification process for its Falcon Heavy rocket to launch national security satellites. The process included three successful flights of the Falcon Heavy including two consecutive successful flights, and the letter stated that Falcon Heavy can be ready to fly national security payloads by 2017.[^cite_note-sn20150415-202] But in July 2017, SpaceX announced that the first test flight would take place in December 2017, pushing the second launch (Space Test Program 2) to June 2018.[^cite_note-STP2_payload-57] In May 2018, on the occasion of the first launch of the Falcon 9 [Block 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_Full_Thrust_Block_5) variant, a further delay to October 2018 was announced, and the launch was eventually pushed to June 25, 2019.[^cite_note-Cooper-56] The STP-2 mission used three Block 5 cores.[^cite_note-planetary20180511-203]

SpaceX was awarded 40% of the launches in Phase 2 of the [National Security Space Launch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Security_Space_Launch) (NSSL) contracts, which includes several launches, a vertical integration facility, and development of a larger fairing, from 2024 to 2027.[^cite_note-204]

#### Space Test Program 2 (STP-2) mission

The payload for the [STP-2 mission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Test_Program) of the Department of Defense included 25 small spacecraft from the U.S. military, NASA, and research institutions:[^cite_note-STP2_payload-57]

The [Green Propellant Infusion Mission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Propellant_Infusion_Mission) (GPIM) was a payload; it is a project partly developed by the U.S. Air Force to demonstrate a less-toxic propellant.[^cite_note-About-126][^cite_note-NASA_PDF_2013-205]

Another secondary payload is the miniaturized [Deep Space Atomic Clock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_Space_Atomic_Clock) that is expected to facilitate autonomous navigation.[^cite_note-206] The U.S. Air Force Research Laboratory's Demonstration and Science Experiments (DSX) has a mass of 500 kg (1,100 lb) and will measure the effects of very low frequency radio waves on space radiation.[^cite_note-STP2_payload-57] The British 'Orbital Test Bed' payload is hosting several commercial and military experiments.

Other small satellites included Prox 1, built by [Georgia Tech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgia_Tech) students to test a 3D-printed thruster and a miniaturized [gyroscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyroscope), [LightSail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LightSail_2) by [The Planetary Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Planetary_Society),[^cite_note-PSO-125] Oculus-ASR nanosatellite from [Michigan Tech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_Tech),[^cite_note-Oculus-ASR_(Nanosat_6)-133] and [CubeSats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CubeSat) from the [U.S. Air Force Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Air_Force_Academy), the [Naval Postgraduate School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_Postgraduate_School), the [United States Naval Research Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Naval_Research_Laboratory), the [University of Texas at Austin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Texas_at_Austin), [California Polytechnic State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Polytechnic_State_University), and a CubeSat assembled by students at [Merritt Island High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merritt_Island_High_School) in [Florida](18933066.md).[^cite_note-STP2_payload-57]

The Block 5-second stage allowed multiple reignitions to place its many payloads in multiple orbits. The launch was planned to include a 5 t (11,000 lb) ballast mass,[^cite_note-207] but the ballast mass was later omitted from the 3.7 t (8,200 lb) total mass for the payload stack.[^cite_note-208]

### NASA contracts

#### Solar System transport missions

In 2011, NASA [Ames Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ames_Research_Center) proposed a [Mars mission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exploration_of_Mars) called [*Red Dragon*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Red_Dragon) that would use a Falcon Heavy as the launch vehicle and trans-Martian injection vehicle, and a variant of the [Dragon capsule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_capsule) to [enter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_entry) the [Martian atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martian_atmosphere). The proposed science objectives were to detect [biosignatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biosignature) and to drill approximately 1 m (3.3 ft) underground, in an effort to sample reservoirs of [water ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_on_Mars#Evidence_of_frozen_water) known to exist under the surface. The mission cost as of 2011 was projected to be less than US\$425 million, not including the launch cost.[^cite_note-sdc20110731-209] SpaceX 2015 estimation was 2,000–4,000 kg (4,400–8,800 lb) to the surface of Mars, with a soft [retropropulsive landing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retropropulsive_landing) following a limited atmospheric deceleration using a parachute and [heat shield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_shield).[^cite_note-nsf20150511-210] Beyond the *Red Dragon* concept, SpaceX was seeing potential for Falcon Heavy and Dragon 2 to carry science payloads across much of the [Solar System](26903.md), particularly to [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter)'s moon [Europa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europa_\(moon\)).[^cite_note-nsf20150511-210] However, SpaceX announced in 2017 that propulsive landing for Dragon 2 would not be developed further, and that the capsule would not receive landing legs. Consequently, the *Red Dragon* missions to Mars were canceled in favor of [Starship](59118602.md), a larger vehicle using a different landing technology.[^cite_note-theverge-211]

#### *Psyche*, *Europa Clipper*, and *Dragonfly*



The 2024 fully-expended launch of NASA's *[Europa Clipper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europa_Clipper)*.

NASA chose Falcon Heavy as the launch vehicle for its [Psyche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psyche_\(spacecraft\)) mission to a metallic asteroid; it launched on October 13, 2023.[^cite_note-212] The contract was worth US\$117 million.[^cite_note-213][^cite_note-214][^cite_note-215]

*Europa Clipper* was initially targeted to be launched on an SLS rocket. However, due to extensive delays, in 2021 NASA awarded the launch contract to SpaceX for a fully expendable Falcon Heavy.[^cite_note-216] It launched on October 14, 2024.[^cite_note-NSF_Europa-217]

*[Dragonfly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragonfly_\(Titan_space_probe\))*, a rotorcraft mission to Saturn's moon [Titan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titan_\(moon\)), is scheduled to launch on a Falcon Heavy in July 2028.[^cite_note-218]
