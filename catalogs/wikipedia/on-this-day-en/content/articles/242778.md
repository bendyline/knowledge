[Agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture) is a major industry in the [United States](3434750.md), which is a net exporter of food.[^cite_note-3] As of the 2022 census of agriculture, there were 1.9 million farms (down 6.9% from 2017), covering an area of 880.1 million acres, an average of 463 acres per farm.[^cite_note-4]

Agricultural activity is particularly concentrated in the [Central Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Valley_\(California\)) of California and the [Great Plains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Plains). The Great Plains are a vast expanse of flat arable land in the center of the nation covering an area west of the [Great Lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Lakes) and east of the [Rocky Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocky_Mountains). The eastern wetter half is a major corn- and soybean-producing region known as the [Corn Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corn_Belt), and the western drier half is known as the [Wheat Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheat_production_in_the_United_States#Geography) because of its high rate of wheat production.[^cite_note-5] The [Central Valley of California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Valley_of_California#Agriculture) produces [fruits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fruits), [vegetables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetables), and [nuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nut_\(fruit\)). The [American South](179553.md#Late_19th_and_20th_century%E2%80%94industrialization_and_Great_Migration) has historically been a large producer of [cotton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton), [tobacco](30942.md), and [rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice), but it has declined in agricultural production over the past century. [California](5407.md) leads the nation in citrus production and is the number two producer of oranges in the world behind only [Brazil](3383.md).

The U.S. has led developments in seed improvement, such as [hybridization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterosis), and in expanding uses for crops from the work of [George Washington Carver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington_Carver) to [bioplastics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioplastic) and [biofuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biofuel). The [mechanization of farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanised_agriculture) and [intensive farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intensive_farming) have been major themes in [U.S. history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._history), including [John Deere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Deere_\(inventor\))'s steel plow, [Cyrus McCormick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_McCormick)'s [mechanical reaper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_reaper), [Eli Whitney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eli_Whitney)'s [cotton gin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton_gin), and the widespread success of the [Fordson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fordson) tractor and the [combine harvester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combine_harvester). Modern agriculture in the U.S. ranges from [hobby farms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hobby_farm) and small-scale producers to large [commercial farms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_farming) that cover thousands of acres of cropland or [rangeland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rangeland).

[Corn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize), [turkeys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkeys), [tomatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomatoes), [potatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potatoes), [peanuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peanut), and [sunflower seeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunflower_seed) constitute some of the major holdovers from the [agricultural endowment of the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_World_crops).

Colonists had more access to land in the colonial United States than they did in Europe. The organization of labor was complex, including free persons, slaves and indentured servants depending on the regions where either slaves or poor landless laborers were available to work on family farms.[^cite_note-6]

European agricultural practices greatly affected the [New England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_England) landscape. Colonists brought livestock over from Europe which caused many changes to the land. Grazing animals required a lot of land and food and the act of grazing itself destroyed native grasses, which were being replaced by European species. New species of weeds were introduced and began to thrive as they were capable of withstanding the grazing of animals, whereas native species could not.[^cite_note-Cronon,_William_2003-7]

The practices associated with keeping livestock also contributed to the deterioration of the forests and fields. Colonists would cut down the trees and then allow their cattle and livestock to graze freely in the forest and never plant more trees. The animals trampled and tore up the ground so much as to cause long-term destruction and damage.[^cite_note-Cronon,_William_2003-7]

[Soil exhaustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_retrogression_and_degradation) was a major problem in New England agriculture. While farming with oxen allowed colonists to farm more land, it increased erosion and decreased [soil fertility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_fertility). This was due to deeper plow cuts in the soil that allowed the soil more contact with oxygen causing [nutrient depletion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutrient_depletion). In grazing fields in New England, the soil was being compacted by the large number of cattle and this did not give the soil enough oxygen to sustain life.[^cite_note-Cronon,_William_2003-7]

In the United States, farms spread from the colonies westward along with the settlers. In cooler regions, [wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheat) was often the crop of choice when lands were newly settled, leading to a "wheat frontier" that moved westward over the course of years. Also very common in the antebellum [Midwest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midwest) was farming [corn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize) while raising [hogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hog_\(swine\)), complementing each other especially since it was difficult to get grain to market before the canals and railroads. After the "wheat frontier" had passed through an area, more diversified farms including [dairy cattle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dairy_cattle) generally took its place. Warmer regions saw plantings of [cotton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton) and herds of [beef cattle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beef_cattle). In the early colonial South, raising [tobacco](30942.md) and cotton was common, especially through the use of slave labor until the [Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War). With an established source for labor, and the development of the [cotton gin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton_gin) in 1793, the South was able to maintain an economy based on the production of cotton. By the late 1850s, the South produced 100% of the 374 million pounds of cotton used in the United States. The rapid growth in cotton production was possible because of the availability of slaves.[^cite_note-8] In the northeast, slaves were used in agriculture until the early 19th century.[^cite_note-9] In the Midwest, slavery was prohibited by the [Freedom Ordinance of 1787](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Ordinance).



2015 US\$ value of agricultural output of the US since 1961

The introduction and broad adoption of scientific agriculture since the mid-19th century contributed to economic growth in the United States. This development was facilitated by the [Morrill Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morrill_Act) and the [Hatch Act of 1887](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hatch_Act_of_1887) which established in each state a [land-grant university](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land-grant_university) (with a mission to teach and study agriculture) and a federally funded system of [agricultural experiment stations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_experiment_station) and [cooperative extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_extension) networks which place [extension agents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extension_agent) in each state.

[Soybeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soybean) were not widely cultivated in the United States until the early 1930s, and by 1942 it became the world's largest soybean producer, due in part to World War II and the "need for domestic sources of fats, oils, and meal". Between 1930 and 1942, the United States' share of world soybean production grew from 3% to 47%, and by 1969 it had risen to 76%. By 1973, soybeans were the United States' "number one [cash crop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash_crop), and leading export commodity, ahead of both wheat and corn".[^cite_note-10] Although soybeans developed as the top [cash crop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cash_crop), [corn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize) also remains as an important commodity. As the basis for "industrial food," corn is found in most modern day items at the grocery store. Aside from items like candy and soda, which contain [high-fructose corn syrup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-fructose_corn_syrup), corn is also found in non-edible items like the shining wax on store advertisements.[^cite_note-11]

Significant areas of farmland were abandoned during the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression) and incorporated into nascent [national forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_National_Forest). Later, "Sodbuster" and "Swampbuster" restrictions written into federal farm programs starting in the 1970s reversed a decades-long trend of [habitat destruction](1707053.md) that began in 1942 when farmers were encouraged to plant all possible land in support of the war effort. In the United States, federal programs administered through local [Soil and Water Conservation Districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_and_Water_Conservation_District) provide [technical assistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_assistance) and partial funding to farmers who wish to implement management practices to conserve soil and limit erosion and floods.[^cite_note-12]

Farmers in the early United States were open to planting new crops, raising new animals and adopting new innovations as increased agricultural productivity in turn increased the demand for shipping services, containers, credit, storage, and the like.[^cite_note-13]

Although four million farms disappeared in the United States between 1948 and 2015, the total output from the farms that remained more than doubled. The number of farms with more than 2,000 acres (810 ha) almost doubled between 1987 and 2012, while the number of farms with 200 acres (81 ha) to 999 acres (404 ha) fell over the same period by 44%.[^cite_note-time-14]

Farm productivity increased in the United States from the mid-20th century until the late-20th century when productivity began to stall.[^cite_note-15]

From 1986 to 2018 about 30 million acres of cropland were abandoned.[^cite_note-16]

- High-value crops include [fruits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fruit), [vegetables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetable), [melons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melon), [tree nuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_nut), [greenhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse), [nursery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nursery_\(horticulture\)) crops, and horticultural specialties.

- [Cattle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cattle)

- [Hogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestic_pig)

- [Dairy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dairy)

- [Poultry](23197.md) and [eggs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egg_\(food\))

One characteristic of the [agricultural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture) industry that sets it apart from others is the number of individuals who are [self-employed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-employment). Frequently, [farmers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farmer) and [ranchers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranchers-Cattlemen_Action_Legal_Fund_v._USDA) are both the principal operator, the individual responsible for successful management and day-to-day decisions, and the primary laborer for his or her operation. For agricultural workers who sustain an injury, the resultant loss of work has implications on physical health and financial stability.[^cite_note-29]

The United States has over 14,000 [certified organic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_certification) farms, covering more than 5 million acres, though this is less than 1% of total US farmland. The output of these farms has grown substantially since 2011, and exceeded US\$7.5 billion in 2016.[^cite_note-30]

## Governance



Agriculture subsidy, from a [Congressional Budget Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congressional_Budget_Office) report. Note: chart does not show sugar subsidies.

Main articles: [Agricultural policy of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_policy_of_the_United_States) and [Agricultural subsidy](171866.md)

Agriculture in the United States is primarily governed by periodically renewed [U.S. farm bills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_farm_bill). Governance is both a federal and a local responsibility, with the [United States Department of Agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Agriculture) serving as the federal department responsible. Government aid includes research into crop types and regional suitability as well as many kinds of federal government subsidies, price supports and loan programs. U.S. farmers are not subject to [production quotas](22136400.md) and some laws are different for farms compared to other workplaces.[^cite_note-31][^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33]

Labor laws prohibiting children in other workplaces provide some exemptions for children working on farms with complete exemptions for children working on their family's farm.[^cite_note-34] Children can also gain permits from vocational training schools or [4-H](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4-H) clubs which allow them to do jobs they would otherwise not be permitted to do.

A large part of the U.S. farm workforce is made up of migrant and seasonal workers, many of them recent immigrants from Latin America. Additional laws apply to these workers and their housing which is often provided by the farmer.

[Farmworkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farmworkers) in the [United States](3434750.md) have unique demographics, wages, working conditions, organizing, and environmental aspects. According to the [National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_for_Occupational_Safety_and_Health), approximately 2,112,626 full-time workers were employed in production agriculture in the U.S. in 2019 and approximately 1.4 to 2.1 million hired crop workers are employed annually on crop farms in the U.S.[^cite_note-35] In 2025, the [Department of Agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Agriculture) found the average age among farmworkers to be 39.6 years old, the average wage to be \$17.23 per hour,[^cite_note-36] and the average number of hours worked to be 40.8 per week.[^cite_note-37]

The types of farmworkers include field crop workers, nursery workers, greenhouse workers, supervisors, etc.[^cite_note-38] The [Department of Labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Labor) findings for the years 2019–2020 report that 63% of crop workers were born in [Mexico](3966054.md), 30% in the mainland United States or [Puerto Rico](23041.md), 5% in [Central America](6121.md), and 2 percent in other regions.[^cite_note-39] The amount of farm labor in the United States has changed substantially: in 1870, almost 50 percent of the U.S. population was employed in agriculture;[^cite_note-Farmworkers_in_the_United_States_\:0-40] as of 2025, less than 1% of the population was directly employed in agriculture.[^cite_note-41]

Potential health and safety issues that may be associated with farm work include vehicle rollovers, falls, musculoskeletal injuries, hazardous equipment, grain bins, pesticides, unsanitary conditions, and respiratory disease. According to the United States Department Of Labor, farmworkers are at risk of work-related lung diseases, noise-induced hearing loss, skin diseases, and certain cancers related to chemical use.[^cite_note-42] Farm workers also suffer disproportionately from heat stress, with fewer than average seeking treatment. While some progress has been made, many farmworkers continue to struggle for fair pay, proper training, and safe working conditions.[^cite_note-43]

## Occupational safety

Main article: [Agricultural safety and health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_safety_and_health)

&#x20;Agriculture ranks among the most hazardous industries due to the use of chemicals and risk of injury.[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-NIOSHblog-45] Farmers are at high risk for fatal and nonfatal injuries (general [traumatic injury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traumatic_injury) and [musculoskeletal injury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musculoskeletal_injury)), work-related lung diseases, [noise-induced hearing loss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noise-induced_hearing_loss), skin diseases, chemical-related illnesses, and certain cancers associated with chemical use and prolonged sun exposure.[^cite_note-NIOSHblog-45][^cite_note-NIOSH_Pest-46][^cite_note-\:0-47] In an average year, 516 workers die doing farm work in the U.S. (1992–2005). Every day, about 243 agricultural workers suffer lost-work-time injuries, and about 5% of these result in permanent impairment.[^cite_note-NIOSH_AgInj-48] Tractor overturns are the leading cause of agriculture-related fatal injuries, and account for over 90 deaths every year. The [National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_for_Occupational_Safety_and_Health) recommends the use of [rollover protection structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roll_over_protection_structures) on tractors to reduce the risk of overturn-related fatal injuries.[^cite_note-NIOSH_AgInj-48]

Farming is one of the few industries in which families (who often share the work and live on the premises) are also at risk for injuries, illness, and death. Agriculture is the most dangerous industry for young workers, accounting for 42% of all work-related fatalities of young workers in the U.S. between 1992 and 2000. In 2011, 108 youth, less than 20 years of age, died from farm-related injuries.[^cite_note-Youth-49] Unlike other industries, half the young victims in agriculture were under age 15.[^cite_note-50] For young agricultural workers aged 15–17, the risk of fatal injury is four times the risk for young workers in other workplaces[^cite_note-51] Agricultural work exposes young workers to safety hazards such as machinery, confined spaces, work at elevations, and work around livestock. The most common causes of fatal farm-related youth injuries involve machinery, motor vehicles, or drowning. Together these three causes comprise more than half of all fatal injuries to youth on U.S. farms.[^cite_note-NIOSH_guidelines_impact-52] Women in agriculture (including the related industries of [forestry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forestry) and [fishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fishing)) numbered 556,000 in 2011.[^cite_note-NIOSHblog-45]

### Farmer suicide

This section is an excerpt from [Farmers' suicides in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farmers'_suicides_in_the_United_States).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Farmers%27_suicides_in_the_United_States\&action=edit)]

[Farmers' suicides in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farmers'_suicides_in_the_United_States) refers to the instances of American [farmers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farmers) [taking their own lives](16831059.md), largely since the 1980s, partly due to their falling into [debt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debt), but mainly as a larger [mental-health crisis among U.S. agriculture workers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_Health_in_United_States_Agricultural_Workers). In [the Midwest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Midwest) alone, over 1,500 farmers have taken their own lives since the 1980s. It mirrors a crisis happening globally: in Australia, a farmer dies by suicide every ten days;[^cite_note-54] in the United Kingdom, one farmer a week takes their own life; and in France it is one every two days. [Almost 300,000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farmers'_suicides_in_India) farmers have died by suicide since 1995 in India.[^cite_note-Farmers'_suicides_in_the_United_States_guardian-55][^cite_note-56]

Farmers are among the most likely to die by suicide, in comparison to other occupations, according to a study published in January 2020 by the [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention](6811.md) (CDC).[^cite_note-Farmers'_suicides_in_the_United_States_z267-57] Researchers at the [University of Iowa](241119.md) found that farmers, and others in the agricultural trade, had the highest suicide rate of all occupations from 1992 to 2010, the years covered in a 2017 study.[^cite_note-Farmers'_suicides_in_the_United_States_z267-57] The rate was 3.5 times that of the general population.[^cite_note-Farmers'_suicides_in_the_United_States_z267-57] This echoed a study conducted the previous year by the CDC[^cite_note-Farmers'_suicides_in_the_United_States_nyt-58] and another undertaken by the [National Rural Health Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Rural_Health_Association) (NRHA).[^cite_note-Farmers'_suicides_in_the_United_States_Williamson_20230419-59]

## Impacts of agriculture on human health

Agriculture in the U.S. makes up approximately 75% of the [country's pesticide use](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticides_in_the_United_States). Agricultural workers are at high risk for being exposed to dangerous levels of pesticides, whether or not they are directly working with the chemicals.[^cite_note-\:0-47] For example, with issues like [pesticide drift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_drift), farmworkers are not the only ones exposed to these chemicals; nearby residents come into contact with the pesticides as well.[^cite_note-60] The frequent exposure to these pesticides can have detrimental effects on humans, resulting in adverse health reactions associated with [pesticide poisoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_poisoning).[^cite_note-61] Migrant workers, especially women, are at higher risk for health issues associated with pesticide exposure due to lack of training or appropriate safety precautions.[^cite_note-62][^cite_note-ReferenceB-63] United States agricultural workers experience 10,000 cases or more of physician-diagnosed [pesticide poisoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_poisoning) annually.[^cite_note-64]

### Drinking water contamination

The application of agricultural chemicals provides a threat to drinking water quality. [Nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrate) is a form of nitrogen found naturally in agricultural crops. Nitrate-based fertilizer salts replenish depleted nutrients in croplands throughout the United States. Drinking water contaminated with nitrate has been linked to methemoglobinemia, premature births, thyroid dysfunction, cancer, and cognitive impairment.[^cite_note-65]

### Heavy metals

[Heavy metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_metals), such as lead, zinc, and copper, are naturally found in sedimentary and igneous rocks.[^cite_note-\:4-66] One way humans introduce these metals is through agricultural practices. Fertilizers containing phosphorus contribute to the amount of heavy metals in soils. Pesticides also add heavy metals to soils, depending on their chemical formula and toxicity. Heavy metal buildup in soils near bodies of water can contaminate drinking and recreational sources. The aggregation of heavy metals via air, water, and food can be detrimental to bones, organs, and organ systems.[^cite_note-\:8-67][^cite_note-\:4-66] In children, exposure to lead, zinc, and copper can affect the nervous system.[^cite_note-\:8-67][^cite_note-\:4-66]

### Direct contact exposure

Farmworkers are exposed to [organophosphate insecticides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organophosphate) by direct contact or inhalation from aerial application. A pregnant mother can pass organophosphates to an unborn baby, potentially causing abnormal birth deformities.[^cite_note-\:2-68][^cite_note-\:3-69] Prolonged exposure can lead to neurological complications in adults and delayed brain progression in children.[^cite_note-\:2-68][^cite_note-\:3-69]

[Neonicotinoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neonicotinoid) pesticides include imidacloprid, clothianidin, and thiamethoxam, which are applied in liquid form. It is absorbed through the skin and can cause both nervous systems to deteriorate, an abnormal opening in the heart wall, and liver destruction. In farmworkers, frequent application has also been linked to oxidative DNA damage, lung dysfunction, and neurological symptoms such as tremors and memory loss.[^cite_note-70][^cite_note-71]

### Environmental justice implications

The effects of agricultural contamination on low-income, marginalized communities have raised important environmental justice concerns. Farm workers and communities of color, who often live near the farms where they work are more likely to be exposed to harmful chemicals, pesticides, and nitrate through direct contact, inhalation, or drinking water.[^cite_note-\:6-72]

Many of these communities source their drinking water from well water, that is at high risk of being contaminated with agricultural runoff, which can cause immediate and long-term health issues.[^cite_note-\:6-72] Inequities of [groundwater pollution](44413707.md) are compounded in underserved communities by economic and deeper structural factors.[^cite_note-\:7-73] Existing regulations in much of the United States, may not be adequate to control the contamination of ground water in these communities, especially those dependent on private well water, which does not require testing under the [Safe Drinking Water Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safe_Drinking_Water_Act).[^cite_note-\:7-73]

## Environmental issues

### Climate change

This section is an excerpt from [Climate change and agriculture in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_and_agriculture_in_the_United_States).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Climate_change_and_agriculture_in_the_United_States\&action=edit)]

Climate change and agriculture are complexly related processes. In the [United States](3434750.md), agriculture is the second largest emitter of [greenhouse gases](21350772.md) (GHG), behind the energy sector.[^cite_note-Climate_change_and_agriculture_in_the_United_States_\:02-74] Direct GHG emissions from the agricultural sector account for 8.4% of total U.S. emissions, but the loss of [soil organic carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_carbon) through soil erosion indirectly contributes to emissions as well.[^cite_note-Climate_change_and_agriculture_in_the_United_States_\:12-75] While agriculture plays a role in propelling climate change, it is also affected by the direct (increase in temperature, change in rainfall, flooding, drought) and secondary (weed, pest, disease pressure, infrastructure damage) consequences of climate change.[^cite_note-Climate_change_and_agriculture_in_the_United_States_\:02-74][^cite_note-Climate_change_and_agriculture_in_the_United_States_\:22-76] The United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) is a regulatory, research, and support body in [American agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_agriculture_in_the_United_States).[^cite_note-Climate_change_and_agriculture_in_the_United_States_\:32-77] They work to understanding the impact of climate change on farming and to helping American farmers adapt.[^cite_note-Climate_change_and_agriculture_in_the_United_States_\:32-77] [USDA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Agriculture) research indicates that these climatic changes will lead to a decline in yield and nutrient density in key crops, as well as decreased livestock productivity.[^cite_note-78][^cite_note-79] Climate change poses unprecedented challenges to U.S. agriculture due to the sensitivity of agricultural productivity and costs to changing climate conditions.[^cite_note-Climate_change_and_agriculture_in_the_United_States_usdatechbull193522-80] Rural communities dependent on agriculture are particularly [vulnerable to climate change](66204363.md) threats.[^cite_note-Climate_change_and_agriculture_in_the_United_States_\:22-76] Native communities are also vulnerable, and the Intertribal Agricultural Council (IAC) documents, supports, and advocates for the reintegrative practices of Native American and Alaskan agricultural producers.[^cite_note-Climate_change_and_agriculture_in_the_United_States_\:52-81] The IAC is particularly focused on the economic impact and potential of regenerative farming practices for native people.[^cite_note-Climate_change_and_agriculture_in_the_United_States_\:52-81]

The [US Global Change Research Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Global_Change_Research_Program) (2017) identified four key areas of concern in the agriculture sector: reduced productivity, degradation of resources, health challenges for people and livestock, and the adaptive capacity of agriculture communities.[^cite_note-Climate_change_and_agriculture_in_the_United_States_\:22-76]

Large-scale adaptation and mitigation of these threats relies on changes in farming policy.[^cite_note-Climate_change_and_agriculture_in_the_United_States_\:12-75][^cite_note-82]

## Demographics

1,224,726 of 3,374,044 producers in the U.S. (36%) are female, as of 2022. This is 0.2% less than 2017 and varies based on state. It was highest in Arizona and Hawaii (48%).[^cite_note-\:5-83] Inequality and respect are common issues for these workers, as many have reported that they are not being respected, listened to, or taken seriously due to traditional views of women as housewives and caretakers.[^cite_note-84]

Women may also face resistance when attempting to advance to higher positions. Other issues reported by female farm workers include receiving less pay than their male counterparts and a refusal or reluctance by their employers to offer their female workers the same additional benefits given to male workers such as housing.[^cite_note-85]

In 2022, there were 46,738 African-American farmers in the United States, 4% less than 2017. This represents 1.4% of all U.S. farmers. The vast majority of African-American farmers were in the south-eastern states. The state with the highest share of black farmers is Mississippi (12%).[^cite_note-86]

## Industry

See also: [Poultry farming in the United States § Industry overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poultry_farming_in_the_United_States#Industry_overview), [Pig farming § Industry overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pig_farming#Industry_overview), and [Meat-packing industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meat-packing_industry)

Historically, farmland has been owned by small property owners, but as of 2017 institutional investors, including foreign corporations, had been purchasing farmland.[^cite_note-newfoodeconomy.org-87] In 2013 the largest producer of pork, [Smithfield Foods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smithfield_Foods), was bought by a company from China.[^cite_note-newfoodeconomy.org-87]

As of 2017, only about 4% of farms have sales over \$1m, but these farms yield two-thirds of total output.[^cite_note-\:1-88] Some of these are large farms have grown organically from private family-owned businesses.[^cite_note-\:1-88]

In the case of the [meat-packing industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meat-packing_industry), large processing companies such as [Tyson Foods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyson_Foods), [Hormel Foods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hormel_Foods) and [Pilgrim's Pride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilgrim's_Pride) contract out to farmers to grow the chickens, then process, package, and possibly market them under their own brands.[^cite_note-Sainato-89] For example, in the 1960s, poultry processing companies experimented with owning and operating their own farms, but shifted away from it in later decades.[^cite_note-Sainato-89]

## Land ownership laws

As of 2019, six states—[Hawaii](13270.md), [Iowa](26810748.md), [Minnesota](19590.md), [Mississippi](16949861.md), [North Dakota](21651.md), and [Oklahoma](22489.md)—have laws banning foreign ownership of farmland. [Missouri](19571.md), [Ohio](22199.md), and Oklahoma are looking to introduce bills banning foreign ownership as of 2019.[^cite_note-agriculture.com-90][^cite_note-91]
