# Holiest sites in Shia Islam

See also: [Holiest sites in Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holiest_sites_in_Islam)



The [Kaaba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaaba) at [Masjid al-Haram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masjid_al-Haram), located in [Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecca), [Saudi Arabia](349303.md), is the [holiest site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holiest_sites_in_Islam) in [Shia Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam).

The three **holiest sites in** **Shia Islam**, in descending order, are [Masjid al-Haram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masjid_al-Haram) in [Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecca), the [Prophet's Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophet's_Mosque) in [Medina](36636.md), and [Al-Aqsa Mosque Compound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Aqsa) in [Jerusalem](16043.md).[^cite_note-Abu_Sway-1][^cite_note-NG-2][^cite_note-Cite_quran|33|09|e=73|s=ns-3][^cite_note-Cite_quran|9|25|e=129|s=ns-4][^cite_note-Cite_quran|48|22|e=29|s=ns-5][^cite_note-\:8-6] Beyond this shared recognition across both [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam) and [Shia](26961.md) traditions, the two branches place varying degrees of religious and devotional importance on other sites. [Twelver Shi'ism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelver_Shi'ism) is the [largest branch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_schools_and_branches) of [Shia Islam](26961.md) and a major [Islamic](6037917.md) tradition. In [Shia Islam](26961.md), sites associated with the [Imamate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imamate) hold exceptional significance. Holy cities such as [Najaf](200512.md), [Karbala](210957.md), [Mashhad](406512.md), [Kadhimiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kadhimiya), [Samarra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samarra), [Kufa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kufa), and [Qom](638742.md) are major pilgrimage centers and attract the world's largest numbers of pilgrims annually.[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9] The [Twelve Imams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_Imams) are also revered in Sunni Islam as [family members of Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahl_al-Bayt) and as highly important figures in [Islamic history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Islam), and Sunnis too visit their shrines out of veneration.[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12][^cite_note-13][^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15][^cite_note-16]

## Holiest sites accepted by all Muslims

Main article: [Holiest sites in Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holiest_sites_in_Islam)



The [Kaaba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaaba) at [Masjid al-Haram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masjid_al-Haram) in [Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecca), is the holiest site in Shia Islam.



The [Prophet's Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophet's_Mosque) in [Medina](36636.md), is the second holiest site in Shia Islam



[Al-Aqsa Mosque](65759.md) in [Jerusalem](16043.md) is the third holiest site in Shia Islam

### Masjid al-Haram

Mecca in Saudi Arabia is the holiest city, as it is the birthplace of Islam, the birthplace [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad), and home to the holiest site, Kaaba in Masjid al-Haram.[^cite_note-Trofimov2008-17] Only Muslims are allowed to enter the city.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETuckerRoberts2008673-18] The rites of hajj pilgrimage is to [circumambulate the Kaaba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tawaf) seven times. It contains the [Maqam Ibrahim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maqam_Ibrahim), the [Zamzam Well](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zamzam_Well), the hills of [Safa and Marwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safa_and_Marwa), [Hateem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hijr_Ismail), and the [Black Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Stone), which belonged to [Adam and Eve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_and_Eve) in [Paradise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jannah). After Abraham built the Kaaba, an angel brought him the Black Stone, a celestial stone that had fallen from Heaven on the [Abu Qubays Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Qubays_\(mountain\)). The Black Stone is believed to be the only remnant of the original structure made by Abraham. After placing the Black Stone in the eastern corner of the Kaaba, Abraham received a revelation in which God told the aged prophet that he should now go and proclaim the pilgrimage to mankind.

Muhammad played a central role in the Kaaba's restoration. Prior to his prophethood, Muhammad was involved in its rebuilding following flood damage in 605 CE, during which he resolved a dispute among [Quraysh clans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quraysh) by proposing that the Black Stone be placed on a cloth and lifted collectively, with Muhammad himself setting it in position. After the [Conquest of Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conquest_of_Mecca), Muhammad entered the Kaaba and removed all of its idols, dedicating the house exclusively to the worship of God. He reaffirmed the Kaaba's association with Abraham in Islamic tradition. The area of Mecca also includes [Mount Arafat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Arafat), [Mina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mina,_Saudi_Arabia), [Muzdalifah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muzdalifah) and [Jannat al-Mu'alla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jannat_al-Mu'alla). As one of the [Five Pillars of Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Pillars_of_Islam), every Muslim who is capable must perform the hajj at least once in their lifetime. Hajj is one of the largest annual gatherings in the world, second only to pilgrimages to the Shia Shrines.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlatt201527-19]

### The Prophet's Mosque

The Prophet's Mosque in Medina, Saudi Arabia, is the second holiest site. Muhammad himself was involved in the mosque's construction. The mosque's land originally belonged to two young orphans, Sahl and Suhayl, and when they learned that Muhammad wished to acquire their land to build a mosque, they went to him and offered the land as a gift. Muhammad insisted on paying a price because they were orphaned children. The price agreed upon was paid by [Abu Ayyub al-Ansari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Ayyub_al-Ansari), who thus became the donor of the mosque on behalf of Muhammad. One of the most notable parts of the mosque is its Green Dome, where Muhammad is buried under it. Originally an open-air building, the mosque served as a community center, a court of law, and a religious school. It contained a raised platform or pulpit (*[minbar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minbar)*) for the people who taught the Quran and for Muhammad to give the Friday sermon (*[khutbah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khutbah)*). Subsequent Muslim rulers greatly expanded and decorated the mosque. In 1909, under the reign of Ottoman Sultan [Abdul Hamid II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdul_Hamid_II), it became the first place in the [Arabian Peninsula](47858.md) to be provided with electrical lights. It is a tradition to visit the mosque after or before the pilgrimage to Mecca.

### The Al-Aqsa Mosque

Al-Aqsa Mosque Compound in Jerusalem is where Muhammad ascended bodily into heaven during the night journey (Isra' and Mi'raj), had a [vision of afterlife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afterlife), and returned. It is the third-holiest site, held in esteem by both Sunni and Shia Muslims due to its history as a place of worship by many prophets such as Abraham, [David](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_in_Islam), [Solomon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomon_in_Islam), [Elijah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elijah_in_Islam) and [Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_in_Islam).[^cite_note-Michigan_C_19862-20] The compound contains the [Dome of the Rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dome_of_the_Rock), the [Qibli Mosque (Al-Aqsa Mosque)](65759.md), [Al-Marwani Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomon's_Stables) and others. Verses 1–18 of [Surāh an-Najm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surah_An-Najm) of the Quran, and some verses of the [Surāh al-’Isrā’](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Isra'), mentions the Al-Aqsa Mosque and allude to the night journey.[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-Cite_quran|17|1|e=7|s=ns-22] In 610 CE, Muhammad was taken by angels [Gabriel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel) and [Michael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_\(archangel\)) from Masjid al-Haram in Mecca to visit "The Furthest Mosque" (Masjid al-Aqsa) in Jerusalem over the night, where he led the prayer among the prophets, and was then taken to the heavens from the [Foundation Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundation_Stone).[^cite_note-23]

## Shrines of the Imams

### Najaf

#### Imam Ali Shrine



[Imam Ali Shrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imam_Ali_Shrine) in [Najaf](200512.md), Iraq, is a profoundly [holy site in Shia Islam](21935937.md). It contains the burial place of Ali, who was Muhammad's cousin, his son-in-law, and the first Shia Imam. As the burial site of one of the most important figures of Islam who is likely [referenced in the Quran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_in_the_Quran), the shrine is considered by Shia Muslims as the holiest site in Islam after Mecca, Medina, and Jerusalem. Born inside the Kaaba and raised in Muhammad's household, Ali was the first man to accept Islam, with his status said to be second only behind Muhammad. Muhammad established a pact of brotherhood with him, a distinction he granted to no one else but Ali. Revered for his courage, justice, honesty, and magnanimity, Ali is considered the archetype of pre-Islamic and Islamic chivalry, with Muhammad equating Ali's status to him with Aaron's status to Moses. Sunni Muslims regard Ali as the last of the [Rashidun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashidun) ('rightly-guided'), while Shia Muslims venerate him as [Muhammad’s rightful successor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghadir_Khumm).

The Imam Ali Shrine in Najaf, Iraq, contains the tomb of Ali, Muhammad's cousin, his son-in-law, and the first Shia Imam. The shrine's significance and holiness is profound. All Muslims revere Ali, but the Shia in particular, as a paragon of justice, knowledge, and piety. As the burial site of one of Islam's most important figures, the Imam Ali Shrine is considered by Shia Muslims as the holiest site in Islam after Mecca, Medina, and Jerusalem.[^cite_note-Never_Again!-24][^cite_note-atimes.com-25][^cite_note-26][^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28][^cite_note-29] The Quran contains numerous debatable references to Ali, including in the [Verse of walaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verse_of_walaya), [Laylat al-mabit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laylat_al-mabit), and [Verse of Ikmal ad-Din](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verse_of_Ikmal_ad-Din). Numerous sayings of Muhammad highly praise Ali. [The Hadith of the Position](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadith_of_the_position), attributed to Muhammad, equates the standing of Ali to him with the standing of Aaron to Moses. Muhammad established a pact of brotherhood with him, a distinction he granted to no one else but Ali. Ali played a pivotal role in the early years of Islam, with Sunni Muslims regard Ali as the last of the Rashidun caliphs, while Shia Muslims reveres him as Muhammad’s rightful successor. Every year, millions of pilgrims visit the shrine.

The shrine is surrounded by the world's largest cemetery, [Wadi al-Salam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wadi_al-Salam). It is believed that being buried near Ali brings spiritual benefits, and many request to be interred in this sacred ground.[^cite_note-30] Ali was buried in Najaf following [his assassination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assassination_of_Ali). Before his death, Ali instructed his sons, [Hasan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasan_ibn_Ali) and Husayn, regarding the location of his burial. In accordance with his wish, Ali's grave was kept secret, and its location was known only to the Shia Imams. This was to protect his grave from desecration by the [Kharijites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kharijites). His grave remained a closely-guarded secret for over a century. It wasn't until the 8th century when the grave was revealed by the sixth Shia Imam, Sadiq. It became publicly known during the reign of the [Abbasid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Caliphate) caliph [Harun al-Rashid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harun_al-Rashid), after the decline of the Umayyad dynasty and the dispersal of the Kharijites. He built the first structure over the tomb, which included a green dome. Over the centuries, the shrine has undergone numerous expansions. The [Buyid dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buyid_dynasty) made significant contributions by building a large dome over the grave.[^cite_note-31] The [Safavid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_Iran) in the 16th century also played a crucial role in further developing the shrine, adding ornate decorations, and expanding the complex.[^cite_note-32] The shrine is an architectural masterpiece, distinguished by its golden dome and minarets. The central hall, where the tomb is located, is adorned with intricate mirror work, mosaics, and calligraphy.[^cite_note-33] Many hadiths from the Shia Imams and from Muhammad are about Ali.

Muhammad: *"Are you not content, Ali, that you have the same rank with regard to me as Aaron had with regard to Moses, except that there is no prophet after me? There is no sword but [Zulfiqar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zulfiqar) (Ali's sword), and there is no hero but Ali. Whoever visits Ali while he is alive, it is as if he has visited me; and whoever visits him after his death, it is as if he has visited me during my lifetime. I am the city of knowledge, and Ali is its gate."* [^cite_note-34][^cite_note-35][^cite_note-36]

### Karbala

#### Imam Husayn and Al-Abbas Shrines



[Imam Husayn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imam_Husayn_Shrine) and [Al-Abbas Shrines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Abbas_Shrine) in [Karbala](210957.md), Iraq, are two of the profoundly holy sites in Shia Islam. Husayn, the son of Ali, grandson of Muhammad, and the third Shia Imam, is revered by all Muslims as a [Martyr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martyr). His suffering and martyrdom at the [Battle of Karbala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Karbala) became symbols of sacrifice for justice and truth against injustice and falsehood. Husayn is a universal, borderless, interfaith and [meta-religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meta-religion) symbol. [Abbas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbas_ibn_Ali), also known as Abu al-Fadl, was Husayn's half-brother and his standard-bearer. Inheriting Ali's boldness and bravery, he is highly revered as an ultimate paragon of courage, self-sacrifice, and for his death while attempting to obtain water from the [Euphrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphrates) river for the thirsty children. His heroic death at the Battle of Karbala contributed to the development of a legendary figure revered among both Shia and Sunni Muslims. Over the centuries, the Euphrates changed course and it now flows around the Abbas Shrine. It is said that the Euphrates has come to Abbas now. Each year, millions commemorate [Ashura](488563.md) during [Muharram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muharram) and later undertake the [Arba'in pilgrimage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arba'in), the largest annual public gathering on earth.

The holy city of Karbala in Iraq contains the Imam Husayn and Al-Abbas Shrines, two of the profoundly holy sites in Shia Islam. Husayn was the son of Ali, the grandson of Muhammad, and the third Shia Imam. Husayn's shrine stands on the site of his grave, where he was martyred during the Battle of Karbala.[^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38] The martyrdom of Husayn and Abbas is commemorated annually by over 20 million Muslims inside Karbala.[^cite_note-Islam_in_the_World-39][^cite_note-Routledge-40][^cite_note-Zondervan-41][^cite_note-42] Up to 34 million pilgrims visit the city to observe Ashura, which marks the anniversary of Husayn's martyrdom. The main event is the Arba'in pilgrimage, where up to 40 million visit the city, the largest annual public gathering on earth.[^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44] Most of the pilgrims travel on foot and come from all around Iraq and nearly 60 countries.[^cite_note-45] Husayn is a universal, borderless and meta-religion symbol.[^cite_note-46][^cite_note-ibtimes.co.uk-47] The Arba'een pilgrimage, while rooted in Shia Islam, has emerged as a symbol of interfaith engagement. It increasingly attracts participants from various religious backgrounds, including Christians, Jews, and non-Abrahamic faiths such as Hindus, Yazidis, and Zoroastrians, who come to commemorate and mourn the martyrdom of Husayn.[^cite_note-auto-48][^cite_note-49][^cite_note-50][^cite_note-ibtimes.co.uk-47] In addition to Arba'in and Muharram, other significant occasions that draw large crowds to the shrine include the birthdays and martyrdom anniversaries of other Shia Imams.

Husayn, along with his companions and most of his family members, was brutally killed and beheaded by the forces of the Umayyad caliph [Yazid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yazid_I). Husayn’s stand against Yazid’s oppressive regime has since been revered as a symbol of resistance and martyrdom. Following the martyrdom, Husayn's grave became a major place of pilgrimage. The first structure over his grave was constructed by the order of al-Mukhtar in the late 7th century.[^cite_note-51] The shrine is renowned for its stunning architecture and intricate decorations, which includes a golden dome, several minarets, and expansive courtyards. The dome is adorned with gold-plated tiles, and the interior features elaborate mirror work, mosaics, and calligraphy that depict verses from the Quran and sayings of Muhammad and the Shia Imams. The shrine also includes a museum that houses objects related to Husayn and the Battle of Karbala, as well as a library. Muhammad reported the events of Karbala on several occasions; For example, he gave a small bottle of soil to [Umm Salama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umm_Salama) and told her that the soil inside the bottle would turn into blood after Husayn was killed. There are narrations that Gabriel informed Muhammad at the time of Husayn's birth that his people would kill Husayn. Several hadiths from the Shia Imams and Muhammad are about Husayn.

Muhammad: *"Hasan and Husayn are the masters of the youth of Paradise. Whoever loves them loves me and whoever hates them hates me. Husayn is from me, and I am from Husayn. God loves whoever loves Husayn. Indeed, Husayn is the Lamp of Guidance and the Ark of [Salvation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvation)."* [^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53][^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55][^cite_note-56][^cite_note-57]

The Al-Abbas Shrine stands opposite the Imam Husayn Shrine. It is the mausoleum of Abbas ibn Ali, a son of Ali, Husayn's half-brother, his standard-bearer, and a key figure in the Battle of Karbala. Abbas is revered for his loyalty and bravery, and his shrine attracts millions of pilgrims, especially during Arba'een.[^cite_note-58] Abbas, also known as Abu al-Fadl, was a respected warrior known for his unwavering support of his brother Husayn. He played a crucial role in the Battle of Karbala, where he was martyred while attempting to fetch water for the thirsty children and companions of Husayn. His sacrifice is deeply commemorated.[^cite_note-59] The construction of the shrine began in the early Islamic period, with significant contributions from various rulers. The earliest recorded construction was during the Abbasid Caliphate, with later renovations and expansions by the Safavid and Qajar dynasties.[^cite_note-Heinz_Halm_20042-60] The shrine is an architectural marvel, featuring a grand golden dome and two towering minarets. It includes several courtyards, prayer halls, and a museum. The central hall, where the tomb is located, is lavishly decorated with intricate tile work, mirror mosaics, and calligraphy depicting verses from the Quran and sayings of Muhammad and the Shia Imams.[^cite_note-61]

### Mashhad

#### Imam Reza Shrine



[Imam Reza Shrine](14030191.md) in [Mashhad](406512.md), [Iran](14653.md), receives nearly 30 million pilgrims annually and is the most visited site in Islam. As one of the holiest sites in Shia Islam, it contains the burial place of [Reza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_al-Rida), the eighth Shia Imam who is also part of the chain of [mystical authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mysticism) in [Sunni Sufism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism). Under [Ma'mun's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ma'mun) coercion, Reza became the only member of Muhammad's household who was appointed [heir apparent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wali_al-Ahd) of the Abbasid Caliphate, and the only Shia Imam to hold such a position. The Abbasid caliph also offered Reza the throne, which he refused. Known for his exceptional knowledge, Reza was issuing [fatwas](11259.md) at the Prophet's Mosque by the age of twenty, with his reputation spreading throughout the [Islamic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_world). His lineage traces through Ali and Muhammad's daughter Fatima, via the line of Husayn. The vast complex is the world's [largest mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_mosques) after Masjid al-Haram in Mecca and the Prophet's Mosque in Medina, with the city of Mashhad being named the [Cultural Capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_culture) of the Islamic world by [OIC](47725.md).

The Imam Reza Shrine is the mausoleum of Ali al-Rida, the eighth Shia Imam, located in Mashhad, the province of [Razavi Khorasan](1289401.md), Iran. As one of the holiest sites in Shia Islam, nearly 30 million Muslims making [pilgrimages](23763.md) to the shrine every year,[^cite_note-\:1-62][^cite_note-\:2-63][^cite_note-\:3-64] the most visited site in Islam.[^cite_note-\:4-65][^cite_note-\:5-66] He is also part of the chain of mystical authority and asceticism in Sunni Sufism, making him widely respected in Sunni Islam as well. [Uyoun Akhbar Al-Ridha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uyoun_Akhbar_Al-Ridha) recorded miracles which have occurred at the shrine.[^cite_note-67] The shrine covers an area of 1,200,000 square meters, the world's largest mosque after Masjid al-Haram in Mecca and the Prophet's Mosque in Medina. The [Goharshad Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goharshad_Mosque), the [Astan Quds Razavi Central Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astan_Quds_Razavi_Central_Museum), [The Central Library of Astan Quds Razavi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Library_of_Astan_Quds_Razavi), and the Razavi University of Islamic Sciences, are also contained within the complex. The complex is on the [tentative list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_World_Heritage_Sites_in_Iran#Tentative_list) of [UNESCO](21786641.md) [World Heritage Sites](44940.md). [Also buried](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_burials_at_Imam_Reza_shrine) within the shrine are the members of the [Timurid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timurid_Empire), Safavid and [Qajar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qajar_Iran) family members, alongside the Abbasid caliph Harun al-Rashid, polymath [Sheikh Bahāʾī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baha_al-Din_al-Amili), Crown Prince [Abbas Mirza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbas_Mirza), [Supreme Leader](36814344.md) [Ali Khamenei](385653.md), and many other notable political figures, scholars, and clerics. The city of Mashhad has been named the Cultural Capital of the Islamic World by the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation.[^cite_note-68][^cite_note-69][^cite_note-70][^cite_note-71][^cite_note-72]

Al-Rida was born in Medina into Muhammad's household in 765 CE. He was contemporary with the Abbasid caliphs Harun al-Rashid, [al-Amin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Amin) and [al-Ma'mun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ma'mun). Both Shia and Sunni sources describe him as exceptionally knowledgeable in youth, engaging in scholarly discussions with the greatest scholars of his time.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELewis2022-73] By the age of twenty, al-Rida was issuing fatwas at the Prophet's Mosque, with his reputation spreading throughout the Islamic world.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMadelung1985-74] His father designated him as his successor during his lifetime. After his father's death, al-Rida adopted a quiet life in Medina and focused on teaching, but his growing reputation drew the attention of the Abbasid caliph al-Maʾmun. Following the [Fourth Fitna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Fitna), the caliph faced Shia uprisings. To legitimize his rule, al-Ma'mun summoned al-Rida to [Khorasan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khorasan) and appointed him as heir apparent. Despite the reluctance, al-Rida accepted the offer on the condition that he would not interfere in governmental affairs. His journey from Medina to Khorasan is the most significant episode in his lifetime. Among the events that were recorded, the most notable is the [Hadith of the Golden Chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadith_of_the_Golden_Chain). When arrived in Khorasan, al-Mamun offered al-Rida the throne, which the Imam rejected. He enjoyed greater freedom than most of his forefathers, benefiting from the relatively open atmosphere of his time and the [Islamic Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age).

After al-Rida's appointment, the Abbasids in Baghdad opposed al-Mamun's decision. The caliph was briefly deposed in favor of [Ibrahim ibn al-Mahdi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibrahim_ibn_al-Mahdi), prompting him to march with al-Rida toward Baghdad. The Imam, however, fell ill and died mysteriously when the party reached [Tus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tus,_Iran) in 818 CE. His sudden death followed shortly after the assassination of [Fadl ibn Sahl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Fadl_ibn_Sahl), the Persian [vizier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vizier) of al-Mamun who was close to the Imam. al-Mamun is widely seen as responsible for both deaths. Imam Reza's grave became a major destination for pilgrimage, and the surrounding settlement gradually developed around his tomb, creating a new city called Mashhad, meaning “place of martyrdom.” The earliest structures around the shrine date back to the 9th century, with significant patronage and expansion carried out during various periods, including by the [Samanid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samanid_Empire), [Seljuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seljuk_Empire), [Ilkhanid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilkhanate), [Timurid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timurid_Empire), [Afsharid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afsharid_Iran), Safavid, and [Qajar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qajar_Iran) Empires. It is still expanding today.[^cite_note-75][^cite_note-76] The Imam Reza Shrine has been regarded as a very important place of pilgrimage and a major spiritual center for both Shia and Sunni Muslims.[^cite_note-77] Since the presence of Imam Reza in the eastern Islamic region had a significant impact in favor of the [Alids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alids) and against the Abbasids, it also contributed to the spread of Shia teachings in the region. After his death, the propagation of his teachings was entrusted to his narrators and students, and his burial site gradually became a spiritual center for Shias and, shortly afterward, for Sunnis as well.[^cite_note-78][^cite_note-79][^cite_note-80] Visitors ranging from common people to many Shia and Sunni kings made pilgrimages to the shrine throughout history.

The shrine is renowned for its vast scale, architectural grandeur, artistic richness, and spiritual atmosphere. The complex features an icconic golden dome, multiple minarets, numerous courtyards ([sahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahn)) and prayer halls ([riwaq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riwaq_\(arcade\))). The Imam's grave is beneath the gilded dome. The interior of the shrine is richly decorated with mirror work, tile mosaics, stucco carvings, and intricate calligraphy featuring Quranic verses and religious inscriptions. The shrine is an unmistakable Islamic landmark, with the city of Mashhad regarded as Iran's spiritual capital. The shrine also serves as a major center for religious ceremonies, gatherings, and educational activities, attracting scholars and students from across the world. There are numerous historical and modern reports of non-Muslims and even [atheists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheism) from across the world visiting the shrine and converting to Islam upon visiting. Some of them, who had initially come only as tourists or out of curiosity, were deeply affected by the shrine's spiritual atmosphere and converted.[^cite_note-81] The shrine has long been associated with reports of miraculous events ([karāmāt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karamat)) experienced by pilgrims and visitors.ʿUyūn Akhbār al-Riḍā by [Ibn Babawayh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Babawayh) has preserved accounts related to these events, and later centuries produced a large body of narratives related specifically to the shrine as a place of healing and spiritual experiences.[^cite_note-82] Many modern accounts describe pilgrims who visited the shrine seeking relief from illnesses and personal hardships and later reported unexpected recoveries or improvements.[^cite_note-83][^cite_note-84] These accounts have been collected in numerous works and are widely published.[^cite_note-85] Several hadiths from the Shia Imams and Muhammad highlight the importance of pilgrimage to the shrine.

God will surely forgive the sins of any sinful person who goes on pilgrimage to his shrine."* [^cite_note-\:6-86][^cite_note-87][^cite_note-88][^cite_note-89]

### Kadhimiya

#### The Kazimiyya Mosque



[Kazimiyya Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Kazimiyya_Mosque) in Baghdad, Iraq, is a holy site in Shia Islam. it contains the burial places of the seventh and ninth Shia Imams, respectively [Kazim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musa_al-Kazim) and his grandson [Jawad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_al-Jawad). Also known as the Jawadayn Shrine, the Kazimiyya Shrine is unique for having two golden domes. They were built to symbolize the two Imams buried there, with each golden dome representing one of the two Imams. Kazim played a key role in eradicating [extreme views and exaggerations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghulat), and is also revered for his piety in Sunni Islam. He is a link in the initiatic golden chain in Sufism, and some Sufi saints are associated with him. Jawad was the youngest Imam, becoming Imam at the age of seven. His Imamate is justified by drawing parallels with Jesus and John the Baptist, both of whom in the Quran received their prophetic missions in childhood.

The Al-Kazimiyya Mosque, also known as the Jawadayn Shrine, is located in the Kādhimiya suburb of Baghdad, Iraq. Also buried within the premises are scholars [Shaykh Mufīd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Shaykh_Al-Mufid) and [Shaykh Naṣīr ad-Dīn aṭ-Ṭūsi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasir_al-Din_al-Tusi). Directly adjacent to the mosque are two smaller shrines, belonging to the brothers [Sayyid Raḍī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Sharif_al-Radi) (who compiled [Nahjul-Balāghah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nahj_al-Balagha)) and [Sayyid Murtadā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Sharif_al-Murtada) and Qadi Abu Yusuf al-Ansari. The Kazimiyya Shrine is unique for having two golden domes. They were built to symbolize the two Imams buried there, with each golden dome representing one of the two Imams.

The mosque was built on the site of a Quraysh cemetery, which was created with the original [Round City of Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Round_City_of_Baghdad). The cemetery was founded by Abbasid Caliph [al-Mansur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mansur) so that members of his family and internment can be buried there. It is generally believed that [Zubaidah bint Ja'far](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zubaidah_bint_Ja'far) and al-Mansur himself were also buried in this location. The current building dates to the restoration carried out by the Safavid Iranian king [Ismail I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ismail_I) from 1502 to 1524. It was further ornamented by the Ottoman Sultan [Suleiman the Magnificent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suleiman_the_Magnificent) after he [conquered Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capture_of_Baghdad_\(1534\)) in 1534.

Similar to many other Islamic settlements throughout history, settlements throughout time started to develop around the mosque which came to be known as the Kadhimiyya area. In 1611, the Ottoman Sultan [Ahmed I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmed_I) granted Jamal al-Din bin Mullah Ali, a descendant from [Bani Shaiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bani_Shaiba), a [firman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firman) (royal decree), allowing him and his family to have full custodianship of the mosque and its shrine. Ancestors of Jamal became known as the al-Jamali family who were entrusted with the mosque and kept the firman. Among the most notable members of the al-Jamali family was Sheikh Abbas al-Jamali, who lived next to the mosque with visitors meeting him and was the father of Iraqi Statesman [Muhammad Fadhel al-Jamali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Fadhel_al-Jamali), who would become one of the founders of the [United Nations](31769.md).

#### Al-Askari Shrine

Al-Askari Shrine in Samarra, Iraq, containts the tomb of the tenth and eleventh Shia Imams, Hādī and his son Askarī.[^cite_note-90] It is a holy site in Shia Islam. The Iranian king [Nasir al-Din Shah Qajar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naser_al-Din_Shah_Qajar) added the shrine's golden dome in 1868.

Jannat al-Baqi Cemetery in Medina, Saudi Arabia, was founded by Muhammad and holds the graves of several of his family members and companions. Among them are the four Shia Imams: Hasan (2nd Imam), Sajjad (4th Imam), Baqir (5th Imam), and Sadiq (6th Imam). When Muhammad arrived in Medina from Mecca, al-Baqi was a land covered with boxthorn. After Muhammad’s arrival, the houses of Medina developed near al-Baqi, which was therefore considered the public tomb.

During the construction of the Prophet’s Mosque, [As'ad ibn Zurarah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/As'ad_ibn_Zurara), one of Muhammad's companions, died. Muhammad chose the spot to be a cemetery, and As'ad was the first individual to be buried in al-Baqi among the [Ansar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ansar_\(Islam\)). While Muhammad was outside Medina for the Battle of Badr, his daughter [Ruqayyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruqayya_bint_Muhammad) fell ill and died. She was buried in al-Baqi. Ruqayyah was the first person from the Ahl al-Bayt (Family of Muhammad) to be buried in this cemetery. Shortly after Muhammad arrived from Badr, [Uthman bin Maz'oon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uthman_ibn_Maz'un) died and was buried in al-Baqi. He was considered the first companion of Muhammad from the [Muhajirun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhajirun) to be buried in the cemetery.

When his youngest son Ibrahim died, Muhammad commanded that he be buried there as well. Following his instructions, two of his daughters, [Zainab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zainab_bint_Muhammad) and [Umm Kulthum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umm_Kulthum_bint_Muhammad), were also buried in the cemetery. Uthman, the third Rashidun caliph, was also buried in the cemetery. Over time, numerous domes and structures were built over various graves in al-Baqi. In 1806, an alliance of the [House of Saud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Saud) and the followers of [Wahhabism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wahhabism), known as the [Emirate of Diriyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Saudi_state), demolished the cemetery. After its reconstruction, the cemetery was demolished again under the [Sultanate of Nejd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Saudi_state) in 1926. These demolitions were condemned across the Islamic world, but the Saudi regime has rejected calls for reconstruction.[^cite_note-Mohammadi-92][^cite_note-Islamica-93]

### Qom

#### Fatima Masumeh Shrine



The holy city of Qom in Iran is the world's largest center for Shia Islamic scholarship, and home to the [Fatima Masumeh Shrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatima_Masumeh_Shrine). [Fatima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatima_bint_Musa) was a descendant of Muhammad, the granddaughter of Sadiq, the daughter of Kazim, the sister of Reza, and the aunt of Jawad, the sixth, seventh, eighth and ninth of the Twelve Imams. Regarded as a [saint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint) and revered as the embodiment of feminine virtues, she is often compared to the Prophet's daughter.

The Fatima Masumeh Shrine in Qom, Iran, is a holy city in Shia Islam along with Najaf, Karbala, Mashhad, Kadhimiya, Samarra and Kufa. The city is the world's largest center for Shia Islamic scholarship.[^cite_note-94] Fatima Masumeh was a descendant of Muhammad, the granddaughter of Ja'far al-Sadiq, the daughter of Musa al-Kazim, the sister of Ali al-Rida, and the aunt of Muhammad al-Jawad, the sixth, seventh, eighth and ninth of the Twelve Imams. The shrine is a major destination for pilgrimage. Every year, millions travel to Qom to honor Fatima Masumeh and seek her intercession with God. [Also buried within the shrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_burials_at_Fatima_Masumeh_Shrine) are three daughters of the ninth Shia Imam al-Jawad, Persian poet [Parvin Etesami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parvin_E'tesami), six members of the Safavid family, eleven members of the Qajar family, and many other notable political figures, scholars, and clerics. The shrine has attracted dozens of seminaries and religious schools.

When her brother Imam Reza was summoned to Khorasan, Fatima set out to join his brother but fell ill along the way in the then-Sunni town of [Saveh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saveh). There she asked to be taken to the nearby Shia town of Qom, where she died a few days later, after seventeen days. Before her death, a local Shia figure by the name of Musa ibn Khazraj al-Ash'ari brought Fatima to Qom and hosted her during her final days. Fatima is known by the title Ma'suma (lit. 'the immaculate, the infallible'). Fatima Masumeh is revered as the embodiment of the feminine virtues in Shia Islam, where she is recognized for piety and religious scholarship, and often compared to Fatima Zahra, daughter of Muhammad. She is revered by Shia Muslims as a saint who would [intercede](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shafa'a) on the Judgement Day and performs miracles, such as healing those with incurable diseases.

Many hadiths recorded from Shia Imams praising the veneration of Fatima Masumeh, and proclaiming that those who make a pilgrimage to her shrine will certainly be admitted to heaven. The Fatima Masumeh Shrine in Qom is crowded every day of the year with Shia men, women, and children from all around the world. Some stay for hours or days praying at the mosque and [circumambulating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumambulation) her tomb. Qom's economy is heavily dependent on pilgrimage. In turn, the city is the most conservative in Iran.[^cite_note-95][^cite_note-96] Many miracles have been recorded as taking place at this shrine, and they are documented in a special office within the shrine complex. Some are published in the shrines monthly newspaper, the Payam-e Astan. Pilgrims at the shrine follow rituals that have been passed down for centuries. Imam Reza, Fatima's brother, outlined these ritual acts as he described the way he visited her Shrine. The prayer Imam Reza dictated to his sister continues to be part of the pilgrimage. Since the Safavid era, additional rituals have been added that are now typical for many pilgrimages, including [ritual washing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ritual_purification) beforehand, dressing in perfumed clothing, and entering the site with one's right foot.

### Ismailism, Zaydism, and Alawites

Main articles: [Ismailism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ismailism), [Zaydism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaydism), and [Alawites](436616.md)



[Al-Azhar Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Azhar_Mosque) in Cairo, Egypt, was first established around 970 CE as a Shia Ismaili institution, and later became a major center of Sunni learning. Its affiliated university, [Al-Azhar University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Azhar_University), is one of the oldest continuously operating universities in the world. For centuries, Al-Azhar has been regarded as one of the foremost institution in the Islamic world.

Historically, the most important place in Ismailism was Cairo during the Fatimid period. When the Fatimids established their caliphate in the 10th century, they ruled as Ismaili leaders. Al-Azhar Mosque and University was founded specifically to teach Ismaili theology, philosophy, and law, and it functioned as the intellectual heart of the Ismailism. Al-Azhar remains the single most powerful symbol of Ismaili achievement, representing the only time Ismailism governed a major Islamic empire.

After its dedication in 972, and with the hiring by mosque authorities of 35 scholars in 989, the mosque slowly developed into what it is today. Over the course of its over a millennium-long history, the mosque has been alternately neglected and highly regarded. Because it was founded as a Shiite Ismaili institution, [Saladin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saladin) and the Sunni Ayyubid dynasty that he founded shunned al-Azhar, removing its status as a congregational mosque and denying [stipends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stipend) to students and teachers at its school.

These moves were reversed under the [Mamluk Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamluk_Sultanate_\(Cairo\)), under whose rule numerous expansions and renovations took place. Later rulers of Egypt showed differing degrees of deference to the mosque and provided widely varying levels of financial assistance, both to the school and to the upkeep of the mosque. Today, al-Azhar remains a deeply influential institution in Egyptian society that is highly revered and a symbol of Islamic Egypt.

Al-Hakim Mosque is important to Ismailism because it comes from the period when Ismaili leaders ruled openly as caliphs. It was built under the Fatimid Imamate in Cairo and functioned as a major state mosque and teaching center for Ismaili doctrine. It represents a time when Ismailism was not a minority tradition but the ideological core of a powerful empire. Teaching there was tied directly to the Fatimid daʿwa.



[Al-Hakim Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Hakim_Mosque) in Cairo, Egypt, was built around 990 CE by [Ḥākim bi-Amr Allāh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Hakim_bi-Amr_Allah), the sixth [Fatimid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatimid_Caliphate) caliph and the sixteenth Ismaili leader.

Architecturally, the mosque consists of a [hypostyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypostyle) prayer hall and a wide internal courtyard (*sahn*), accessed via a projecting entrance [portal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portal_\(architecture\)). Its most notable features are its two unusual [minarets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minaret): the original minarets of the mosque have ornate multi-tiered designs but, for reasons that remain unclear, these were encased shortly afterwards inside the massive square bastions still seen today.

Alawites are an [ethnoreligious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnoreligious_group) group, an offshoot of Shia Islam as a *[ghulat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghulat)* branch during the ninth century. Alawites venerate Ali, the first Imam, as a manifestation of the divine essence. It is the only *ghulat* sect still in existence today. The group was founded during the ninth century by [Ibn Nusayr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Nusayr), who was a disciple of the tenth Imam, Hadi, and of the eleventh Imam, Askari.

The Quran is one of their holy books, but its interpretation differs significantly from the mainstream Shia Muslims and aligns with early [Batiniyya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batiniyya). Alawite theology and rituals differ sharply from Shia Islam in several important ways. For instance, various Alawite rituals involve the drinking of [wine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wine) and the sect does not prohibit [the consumption of alcohol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcoholic_beverage) for its adherents. As a creed that teaches the symbolic/esoteric reading of [Qur'anic verses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%80yah), Alawite theology is based on the belief in [reincarnation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reincarnation) and views Ali as a divine incarnation of God. Moreover, Alawite clergy and scholarships insist that their religion is theologically distinct from Shia Islam.

Alawites have historically kept their beliefs secret from outsiders and non-initiated Alawites, so rumours about them have arisen. At the core of the Alawite creed is the belief in a divine Trinity, comprising three aspects of the one God. The aspects of the Trinity are *Mana* (meaning), *Ism* (Name) and *Bab* (Door). Alawite beliefs hold that these [emanations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emanationism) underwent [re-incarnation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reincarnation) cyclically seven times in human form throughout history. According to Alawites, the seventh incarnation of the trinity consists of Ali, Muhammad himself, and Salman the Persian. An important component of Alawism is the belief in the [transfer of souls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metempsychosis), rejected by both the Shia and Sunni, leading to the Alawites being considered heretics by theologians of Sunni and Shia Islam. Khidr holds special spiritual and mystical significance. Unlike mainstream Sunni and Shia Islam, Alawite theology incorporates esoteric interpretations of Qur’anic figures, and Khidr is viewed as a hidden, eternal guide, a spiritual intermediary, and a source of divine wisdom.

Zaydism, also referred to as Fiver Shi'ism, is a branch of Shia Islam that emerged in the eighth century following [Zayd ibn Ali's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zayd_ibn_Ali) [unsuccessful rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolts_of_Zayd_ibn_Ali) against the Umayyad Caliphate. Zaydism is typically considered the Shia branch that is closest to Sunni Islam, although the "classical" form of Zaydism (usually referred to as Hadawi) historically changed its stance on Sunni and Shia traditions multiple times, to the point where Zaydis' simply accepting Ali as a rightful successor to Muhammad was enough to consider them Shia. Zaydis regard rationalism as more important than Quranic literalism and historically were quite tolerant towards Shafi'i Sunnism, the jurisprudential school of about half of the Yemenis. Most of the world's Zaydis are located in northwestern Yemen and Najran in Saudi Arabia.

Zayd ibn ʿAlī was the son of Imam Sajjad, and great-grandson of Ali. His unsuccessful revolt against the Umayyad Caliphate, in which he died, gave rise to the Zaydiyya sect of Shia Islam, which holds him as the next Imam after his father Imam Sajjad. Zayd ibn Ali is also seen as a major religious figure by many Sunnis and was supported by the prominent Sunni jurist, Abu Hanifa, who issued a fatwa in support of Zayd against the Umayyads. To most Shias however, his elder half-brother Imam Baqir is seen as the next Imam. Nevertheless, Zayd is considered an important revolutionary figure by Shias and a martyr (shaheed) by all schools of Islam, including both Sunnis and Shias. The call for revenge for his death, and for the brutal display of his body, contributed to the [Abbasid Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_revolution).

## Historically Significant Places

### Quba Mosque



[Quba Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quba_Mosque) in Medina, Saudi Arabia, was the first mosque built by Muhammad, established on the first day of his [emigration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hijrah). He personally participated in laying its foundations.

The Quba Mosque, located on the outskirts of Medina, Saudi Arabia, holds the distinction of being the first mosque in the world.[^cite_note-109][^cite_note-110] It was founded by Muhammad upon his arrival in the vicinity of Medina during the hijra (migration from Mecca). He is reported to have personally participated in laying its foundations.[^cite_note-111]

The mosque is frequently mentioned in Islamic tradition due to its association with piety and ritual purity.[^cite_note-112] Several hadiths state that performing prayer in Quba Mosque carries special merit. While The Quba Mosque is not considered a holy site, it occupies a distinguished place in Islamic tradition.

### Masjid al-Qiblatayn



[Masjid al-Qiblatayn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masjid_al-Qiblatayn) in Medina, Saudi Arabia, is the place where Muhammad received God’s command to change the direction of prayer (qibla) from Jerusalem to Mecca.

Masjid al-Qiblatayn in Medina, Saudi Arabia, is renowned for its association with a pivotal moment in Islam. The mosque is identified as the place where Muhammad received the revelation instructing Muslims to change the direction of prayer (qibla) from Jerusalem to Mecca.[^cite_note-113][^cite_note-114] The event occurred approximately 16–17 months after the Hijrah.[^cite_note-115] The mosque symbolized the transition of Islam from its early phase toward a distinct religious community with Mecca as its spiritual center.

For this reason, Masjid al-Qiblatayn occupies a special place in Islamic memory. The mosque is one of the few mosques in the world to have contained two mihrabs (niches indicating the *qibla*) in different directions. It is among the few mosques that date to the time of Muhammad, along with the Quba Mosque and the Prophet's Mosque, considering that Masjid al-Haram and Al-Aqsa Mosque are associated with earlier prophets.

### Ibrahimi Mosque



[Hebron](38577.md) in [Palestine](241405.md) was where Abraham settled and bought the [Cave of the Patriarchs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cave_of_the_Patriarchs) as a burial place for his wife Sarah. Abraham, [Isaac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac), and [Jacob](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob), along with their wives [Sarah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah), [Rebecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebecca_\(biblical_figure\)), and [Leah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leah), are all buried in the cave. Muslims built the Ibrahimi Mosque on top of the cave in 750 AD to honor them, and believe that Muhammad visited Hebron on his night journey from Mecca to Jerusalem.

Hebron in Palestine is venerated in Judaism, Christianity and Islam. Abraham settled in Hebron and bought the Cave of the Patriarchs as a burial place for his wife Sarah. Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, along with their wives Sarah, Rebecca, and Leah, were all buried in the cave. Muslims built the Ibrahimi Mosque on top of the cave to honor Abraham, and believe that Muhammad visited Hebron on his night journey from Mecca to Jerusalem. There is also a left footprint in a small niche at the mosque, believed to be from Muhammad.[^cite_note-vitullo2003-116]

Following the [Babylonian captivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_captivity), the [Edomites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edom) settled in Hebron. During the first century BC, [Herod the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herod_the_Great) built the wall that still surrounds the Cave of the Patriarchs today, which later became a [church](6325.md), and then a [mosque](19894.md). With the exception of a [brief Crusader control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Jerusalem), successive Muslim dynasties ruled Hebron from the 7th century CE until the [Ottoman Empire's](22278.md) [dissolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Ottoman_Empire) following [World War I](4764461.md), when the city became part of [British Mandatory Palestine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandatory_Palestine). Hebron is one of the [oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_oldest_continuously_inhabited_cities).

### Mount Sinai



[Mount Sinai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Sinai) in the Asian part of [Egypt](8087628.md), is the place where according to the [Torah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torah), [Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible), and [Quran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quran), [Moses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses) received the [Ten Commandments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_Commandments) from God.

The Sinai Peninsula is associated with the prophets [Harun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aaron_in_Islam) ([Aaron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aaron)) and [Musa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses_in_Islam) ([Moses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses)). Numerous references to Mount Sinai exist in the Quran, where it is called *Ṭūr Saināʾ*,[^cite_note-Cite_quran|23|20|t=y|s=ns-117] *Ṭūr Sīnīn*,[^cite_note-Cite_quran|95|2|t=y|s=ns-118] and *aṭ-Ṭūr*[^cite_note-Cite_quran|2|63|e=93|s=ns-119] and *al-Jabal* (both meaning "the Mount").[^cite_note-Cite_quran|7|103|e=156|s=ns-120] As for the adjacent *[Wād](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wadi) Ṭuwā* ([Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valley) of Tuwa), it is considered as being *muqaddas*[^cite_note-Cite_quran|20|9|e=99|s=ns-121][^cite_note-Cite_quran|79|15|e=25|s=ns-122] (sacred),[^cite_note-IbnKathir_Al-Ahmad-123][^cite_note-Elhadary2016-124] and a part of it is called *Al-Buqʿah Al-Mubārakah* ("The Blessed Place").[^cite_note-Cite_quran|28|3|e=86|s=ns-125]

It is the location where, according to the sacred scriptures of the three major [Abrahamic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrahamic_religions) (Torah, Bible, and Quran), Moses received the Ten Commandments from God. The summit has a mosque that is still used by Muslims. At the summit also is "Moses' cave", where Moses is believed to have waited to receive the Ten Commandments from God.

### Umayyad Mosque



Umayyad Mosque in [Damascus](8914.md), [Syria](7515849.md), where Muslims and Christians alike consider it the burial place of the head of [John the Baptist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_the_Baptist), revered in Islam as [Yahya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_the_Baptist_in_Islam). [Islamic eschatology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_eschatology) holds that the [Mahdi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahdi) will emerge before the [End of Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eschatology) to restore justice, and this is the place where [Isa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_in_Islam) (Jesus) will descend from heaven at his [Second Coming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Coming) to accompany him. The mosque also holds special importance in Shia Islam, as it contains shrines commemorating [Husayn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Husayn_ibn_Ali), whose martyrdom is frequently compared to that of John the Baptist. Imam Husayn's head was kept there before being transported to Karbala, while his surviving family members were imprisoned in the mosque.

The importance of Umayyad Mosque in Damascus, Syria, stems from the eschatological reports concerning the mosque and historic events associated with it. The head of John the Baptist, revered in Islam as Yahya, is believed to be inside the mosque, which also houses one of only four original copies of the Quran. Prayers in the Umayyad mosque are considered to be equal to those offered in Al-Aqsa Mosque.[^cite_note-DumperStanley2007-Damascus-126] The Mosque is also the place where Muslims believe Isa (Jesus) will return at the end of times, atop the "Minaret of Isa" of the mosque, during the time of a [Fajr prayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fajr_\(prayer\)).

The mosque's site has been used as a house of worship since the [Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age). It enjoyed great prestige throughout its history and was regarded as a "wonder of the world" by many medieval Muslim writers. Muslims and Christians alike consider the site a holy place. The mosque also holds a special importance in Shia Islam since it contains shrines commemorating Husayn, whose martyrdom is frequently compared to that of John the Baptist.[^cite_note-127] There is a location in the mosque where Husayn's head was kept.[^cite_note-128] Furthermore, the surviving members of his family were imprisoned in the mosque for 60 days.[^cite_note-129]

### Jabal al-Nour





[Jabal al-Nour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jabal_al-Nour) in the Hejaz region of Saudi Arabia, where Muhammad received his [first revelation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad's_first_revelation) in the Hira Cave, holds tremendous significance.

Jabal al-Nour in Mecca holds tremendous significance for Muslims throughout the world, as it is here where Muhammad received his first revelation of the Quran, which consisted of the first five verses of [Surah Al-Alaq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Alaq) from the angel Gabriel. Visitors climb the mountain daily to see the Cave of Hira, the place where Muhammad also received the first revelation on the *[Laylat al-Qadr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laylat_al-Qadr)* (night of power) from Gabriel. During the *[Hajj](23487999.md)* (pilgrimage), an estimated five thousand visitors climb it daily to see the cave.

Before Muhammad's first revelation, he had [transcendental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendence_\(religion\)) dreams, in which were signs that his prophethood had begun and that the stones in Mecca would greet him with the *[salaam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/As-salamu_alaykum)*. These dreams lasted for six months. An increasing need for solitude led Muhammad to seek seclusion and meditation ([Muraqabah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muraqabah)) in the rocky hills of Mecca. He retreated to the cave for one month each year, engaging in seclusion ([Tahannuth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ta%E1%B8%A5annuth)). He took provisions and fed the poor who came to him. Before returning home to his family for more provisions, he would circumambulate the Kaaba seven times.

### Badr, Uhud and Khaybar







The sites of the three major battles, [Badr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Badr), [Uhud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Uhud), and [Khaybar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Khaybar), located in Saudi Arabia, hold great historical significance. The Battle of Badr is referred to in the Quran as the Day of Criterion. The much less equipped army of Muhammad’s small companions defeated the massive army of the Quraysh [polytheists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytheism), led by [Abu Jahl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amr_ibn_Hisham). The battle has been remembered as a decisive victory attributed to divine intervention. The Battle of Uhud is revered because many of Muhammad’s companions were martyred there, including his paternal uncle and milk-brother [Hamza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamza_ibn_Abd_al-Muttalib). The Quran discusses the events and lessons of Uhud in considerable detail. The Battle of Khaybar holds significance because of the major victory over the [Jewish strongholds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_tribes_of_Arabia), an important turning point in the consolidation of the [early Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Muslims). The site holds special significance in Shia Islam because of the prominent role of [Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali), Muhammad's cousin, his son-in-law and the first Shia Imam. He killed the Jewish Knight [Marhab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marhab), torn the fortress' gate from its hinges and used it as a bridge for the Muslim forces to cross the moat. The gate was reportedly so heavy that forty men were later needed to put it back in place. During Muhammad’s lifetime and his battles against the polytheists, Ali, Abu Bakr, Umar, and Uthman were Muhammad's closest companions, always standing by his side and shaping the Islamic history.

The Battle of Badr is referred to as The Day of the Criterion in the Quran. On 13 March 624 CE, Muhammad, commanding an small army of his companions, defeated a massive army of the Quraysh polytheists led by Abu Jahl. It was the first large-scale engagement between the Muslims and the Quraysh Meccans. Advancing from the north, the Muslims faced the polytheist Meccans. The Muslim victory strengthened Muhammad's position; The Medinese eagerly joined his future expeditions, and tribes outside Medina openly allied with Muhammad. The battle has been passed down as a decisive victory attributable to divine intervention. The site of Badr marks the location of the battle and is regarded as one of the most significant historical sites in Islam. The story of Badr has been passed down throughout the centuries, before being combined in the multiple [biographies of Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biographies_of_Muhammad) that exist today. All knowledge of the battle comes from traditional Islamic accounts.

Mount Uhud was the site of the second battle between Muhammad and the polytheists of his tribe, Quraysh. The Battle was fought on 19 March, 625 CE, between a small force of Muslims and a much larger polytheist force from Mecca. It is especially revered because many companions of Muhammad, including Hamza, were martyred there, and Muhammad is reported to have said, "Uhud is a mountain that loves us and we love it."[^cite_note-130] The grave of Hamza is located near the Mount. The Quran discusses the events and lessons of the Uhud Battle in considerable detail, especially in [Surah Al Imran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Imran), verses 121–180.

The Battle of Khaybar was fought between the early Muslims and the Jewish community of Khaybar in April 628 CE. Khaybar was home to a sizable community of Jewish tribes. As Muhammad's army began to march on Khaybar, the [Banu Ghatafan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghatafan) and other Jewish tribes did not, or could not, send the reinforcements that had been expected to arrive to defend the settlement, further endangering the Jewish army's poor fortifications. After a brief period of fighting, Khaybar fell to the Muslims and the Jewish knight Marhab was killed by Ali. Khaybar was a major victory and marked an important turning point in the consolidation of the early Muslims. The site holds special significance in Shia Islam because of the prominent role of Ali in the battle. Ali is said to have lifted the fortress' gate from their hinges, climbed into the moat and held them up to make a bridge whereby the attackers gained access to the redoubt. The gate was so heavy that forty men were later required to put it back in place.
