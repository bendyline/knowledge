**Pauline Lee Hanson** ([née](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N%C3%A9e) **Seccombe**, formerly **Zagorski**; born 27 May 1954) is an Australian politician who is the leader of [One Nation](83013.md). She has been a [senator](165705.md) for [Queensland](59051.md) since 2016, and was the [member of Parliament](61565.md) (MP) for the Queensland division of [Oxley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_of_Oxley) from 1996 to 1998.

Born in [Brisbane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brisbane), Hanson worked in small businesses and was a councillor of [Ipswich City Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ipswich_City_Council), joining the [Liberal Party](18453.md) in 1995. She was [preselected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preselection) as the Liberal candidate for the division of Oxley at the [1996 federal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_Australian_federal_election), but was disendorsed by the party shortly before the election for her controversial comments about [Aboriginal Australians](2912594.md). Hanson remained on the ballot paper as the Liberal candidate, winning the election to sit as an independent, before co-founding One Nation in 1997. She was unsuccessful in her re-election attempt at the [1998 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1998_Australian_federal_election).

Hanson unsuccessfully contested the [2001 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001_Australian_federal_election) as the leader of One Nation, but was expelled from the party in 2002. A [District Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/District_Court_of_Queensland) jury found Hanson guilty of electoral fraud in 2003, but her convictions were later overturned by the [Queensland Court of Appeal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queensland_Court_of_Appeal). She spent 11 weeks in jail prior to the appeal being heard. Following her release, Hanson ran in several state and federal elections, as the leader of her [United Australia Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauline's_United_Australia_Party) and as an independent before rejoining One Nation in 2013 and becoming leader again the following year. She was narrowly defeated at the [2015 Queensland state election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_Queensland_state_election), but was elected to the Senate at the [2016 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Australian_federal_election), along with three other members of the party. She was re-elected at the [2022 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Australian_federal_election).

Since 1996, Hanson has been widely characterised as [radical right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_right_\(Europe\)), [far-right](221220.md) and [right-wing populist](8354382.md).[^cite_note-Sengul-2023-1][^cite_note-Fenton-Smith-2020-2][^cite_note-Crosby-2019-3] She has called for massive reductions in the number of migrants allowed into Australia, and has made controversial critical comments about Indigenous Australians, Asian Australians, Islam and African migrants. In 2017 and 2025, Hanson gained media attention for [wearing a burqa into the Australian Senate](81683761.md). She was suspended from the Senate for seven days for the 2025 event.

## Early life and career

Hanson was born Pauline Lee Seccombe on 27 May 1954 in [Woolloongabba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woolloongabba), a suburb of [Brisbane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brisbane). She is the fifth of seven children and the youngest daughter of John Alfred "Jack" Seccombe and Hannorah Alousius Mary "Norah" Seccombe (née Webster).[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5] She first attended school at Buranda Girls' School, and then later [Coorparoo State High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coorparoo_State_High_School) in [Coorparoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coorparoo) which she left at age 15, shortly before her first marriage and pregnancy.[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7]

Jack and Norah Seccombe owned a [fish and chip shop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish_and_chip_shop) in [Ipswich, Queensland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ipswich,_Queensland), where Hanson and her siblings worked from a young age, preparing meals and taking orders. As she grew older, she helped keep the accounts.[^cite_note-ABC-2004-8]

In 1978, Hanson (then Pauline Zagorski) met Mark Hanson, a tradesman on Queensland's [Gold Coast](59251.md). They married in 1980 and established a construction business specialising in [roof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roofer) plumbing. Hanson ran the administrative side, while her husband handled the workforce. In 1987, the couple divorced and the company was liquidated. Hanson later bought a fish and chip shop with a new business partner, Morrie Marsden in [Silkstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silkstone,_Queensland) in Ipswich. The shop was sold upon her election to [Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Parliament) in 1996.[^cite_note-9]

### Entry into politics

Hanson's first election to political office was in 1994, when she won a seat on the [Ipswich City Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ipswich_City_Council). She ran on a platform opposing extra funding for [a new library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Ipswich#Initiatives) that would provide free public [Internet access](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_access).[^cite_note-10] Hanson held the seat for 11 months before being removed in 1995 due to administrative changes.[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12]

#### 1996 election

In August 1995,[^cite_note-The_Age-2003a-13] she joined the [Liberal Party](18453.md) and in 1996 was endorsed as the Liberal candidate for the [House of Representatives](61565.md) seat of [Oxley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_of_Oxley), based on Ipswich, for the [March 1996 federal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_Australian_federal_election). The Labor incumbent, [Les Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_Scott), held it with an almost 15% two-party majority, making it the safest Labor seat in Queensland. Hanson was given little chance of winning the seat. However, Hanson received widespread media attention when, leading up to the election, she advocated the abolition of special government assistance for [Aboriginal Australians](2912594.md), and she was subsequently disendorsed by the Liberal Party. Ballot papers had already been printed listing Hanson as the Liberal candidate, and the [Australian Electoral Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Electoral_Commission) had closed nominations for the seat.[^cite_note-14] As a result, Hanson was still listed as the Liberal candidate when votes were cast, even though Liberal leader [John Howard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Howard) had declared she would not be allowed to sit with the Liberals if elected.[^cite_note-15]

On election night, Hanson took a large lead on the first count and picked up enough preferences from [Democrat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Democrats) voters to defeat Scott on the sixth count. Her victory came amid [Labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Labor_Party)'s near-collapse in Queensland, which saw it reduced to only two seats in the state. Hanson won 54% of the [two-candidate-preferred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-candidate-preferred) vote. Had she still been running as a Liberal, the 19.3-point swing would have been the largest two-party swing of the election.[^cite_note-16] Since Hanson had been disendorsed, she entered Parliament as an [independent](2172378.md).[^cite_note-17]

## MP for Oxley (1996–1998)

Hanson gained extensive media coverage during her campaign and once she took her seat in the House. Her first speech attracted considerable attention for the views it expressed on benefits for [Indigenous Australians](12598742.md), welfare, immigration and [multiculturalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiculturalism). During her term in Parliament, Hanson spoke on social and economic issues such as the need for a fairer child support scheme and concern for the emergence of the working-class poor. She also called for more accountable and effective administration of Indigenous affairs. Hanson's supporters viewed her as an ordinary person who challenged '[political correctness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_correctness)' as a threat to Australia's identity.

The reaction of the mainstream political parties was negative, with parliament passing a resolution (supported by all members except [Graeme Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graeme_Campbell_\(politician\))) condemning her views on immigration and multiculturalism. However, the prime minister at the time, John Howard, refused to [censure](698874.md) Hanson or speak critically about her, acknowledging that her views were shared by many Australians,[^cite_note-18] commenting that he saw the expression of such views as evidence that the "pall of political correctness" had been lifted in Australia, and that Australians could now "speak a little more freely and a little more openly about what they feel".[^cite_note-Ward-1997a-19]

Hanson immediately labelled Howard a "strong leader" and said Australians were now free to discuss issues without "fear of being branded as a bigot or racist". Over the next few months, Hanson attracted populist [anti-immigration](6491626.md) sentiment and the attention of the [Citizens' Electoral Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizens'_Electoral_Council), the [Australian League of Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_League_of_Rights), and other right-wing groups. Then-Minister for Immigration [Philip Ruddock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Ruddock) announced a tougher government line on refugee applications, and cut the family reunion intake by 10,000, despite an election promise to maintain immigration levels.[^cite_note-Ward-1997a-19] Various academic experts, business leaders and several state premiers attacked the justification offered by Ruddock, who had claimed that the reduction had been forced by continuing high unemployment. Various ethnic communities complained that this attack on multiculturalism was a cynical response to polls showing Hanson's rising popularity. Hanson claimed credit for forcing the government's hand.[^cite_note-Ward-1997-20]

On 10 September 1996, Hanson gave her [maiden speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maiden_speech) to the House of Representatives, which was widely reported in the media. Opening her speech, Hanson said, "I won the seat of Oxley largely on an issue that has resulted in me being called a racist. That issue related to my comment that Aboriginals received more benefits than non-Aboriginals." Hanson then asserted that Australia was in danger of being "swamped by Asians", and that these immigrants "have their own culture and religion, form ghettos and do not assimilate". Hanson argued that "mainstream Australians" were instead subject to "a type of reverse racism ... by those who promote political correctness and those who control the various taxpayer funded 'industries' that flourish in our society servicing Aboriginals, multiculturalists and a host of other minority groups". This theme continued with the assertion that "present governments are encouraging separatism in Australia by providing opportunities, land, moneys and facilities available only to Aboriginals".

Among a series of criticisms of Aboriginal [land rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_rights), access to welfare and reconciliation, Hanson criticised the [Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aboriginal_and_Torres_Strait_Islander_Commission) (ATSIC), saying: "Anyone with a criminal record can, and does, hold a position with ATSIC". This was followed by a short series of statements on family breakdown, youth unemployment, international debt, the [Family Law Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_Law_Act_1975), [child support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_support), and the privatisation of [Qantas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qantas) and other national enterprises. The speech also included an attack on [immigration](1100670.md) and [multiculturalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiculturalism), a call for the return of high-tariff [protectionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protectionism), and criticism of [economic rationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_rationalism).[^cite_note-21] Her speech was delivered uninterrupted by her fellow parliamentarians as it was the courtesy given to MPs delivering their maiden speeches.

### Founding of One Nation

Main article: [One Nation](83013.md)

In February 1997, Hanson, [David Oldfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Oldfield_\(politician\)) and [David Ettridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Ettridge) founded the Pauline Hanson's One Nation political party.[^cite_note-22][^cite_note-23] Disenchanted rural voters attended her meetings in regional centres across Australia as she consolidated a support base for the new party. An opinion poll in May of that year indicated that the party was attracting the support of 9% of Australian voters and that its popularity was primarily at the expense of the Coalition's base.[^cite_note-Ward-1997-20]

Hanson's presence in the suburb of [Dandenong, Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dandenong), to launch her party, was met with demonstrations on 7 July 1997, with 3,000–5,000 people rallying outside. A silent vigil and multicultural concert were organised by the [Greater Dandenong City Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Greater_Dandenong) in response to Hanson's presence, while a demonstration was organised by an anti-racism body. The majority of attendees were of Asian origin, where an open platform attracted leaders of the Vietnamese, Chinese, East Timorese and Sri Lankan communities. Representatives from churches, local community groups, lesbian and gay and socialist organisations also attended and addressed the crowd.[^cite_note-24]

In its late 1990s incarnation, One Nation called for zero net immigration, an end to multiculturalism and a "revival" of Australia's Anglo-Celtic cultural tradition which it said had been diminished, the abolition of native title and the [Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aboriginal_and_Torres_Strait_Islander_Commission) (ATSIC), an end to special Aboriginal funding programs, opposition to Aboriginal reconciliation which the party says will create two nations, and a review of the [1967 constitutional referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1967_Australian_referendum_\(Aboriginals\)) which gave the Commonwealth power to legislate for Aborigines. The party's economic position was to support protectionism and trade retaliation, increased restrictions on foreign capital and the flow of capital overseas, and a general reversal of globalisation's influence on the Australian economy. Domestically, One Nation opposed privatisation, competition policy, and the GST, while proposing a government-subsidised people's bank to provide 2% loans to farmers, small businesses, and manufacturers. On foreign policy, One Nation called for a review of Australia's United Nations membership, a repudiation of Australia's UN treaties, an end to foreign aid, and a ban on foreigners from owning Australian land.[^cite_note-25]

### 1998 re-election campaign



1998 campaign poster

In 1999, *[The Australian](396952.md)* reported that support for One Nation had fallen from 22% to 5%.[^cite_note-26] One Nation Senate candidate Lenny Spencer blamed the press together with party director David Oldfield for the October 1998 election defeat,[^cite_note-27] while the media reported the redirecting of preferences away from One Nation as the primary reason, with a lack of party unity, poor policy choices, and an "inability to work with the media" also responsible.[^cite_note-28]

Ahead of the [1998 federal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1998_Australian_federal_election), an electoral [redistribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redistribution_\(Australia\)) essentially split Oxley in half. Oxley was reconfigured as a marginal Labor seat, losing most of its more rural and exurban area while picking up the heavily pro-Labor suburb of [Inala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inala,_Queensland). A new seat of [Blair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_of_Blair) was created in the rural area surrounding Ipswich.[^cite_note-Crikey-2017-29] Hanson knew her chances of holding the reconfigured Oxley were slim, especially after former Labor state premier [Wayne Goss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayne_Goss) won preselection for the seat.[^cite_note-30] After considering whether to contest a [Senate](165705.md) seat—which, by most accounts, she would have been heavily tipped to win—she opted to contest Blair.[^cite_note-Crikey-2017-29] Despite its very large notional Liberal majority (18.7%), most of her base was now located there.

Hanson launched her 1998 election campaign with a focus on jobs, rather than a focus on race/ethnicity or on "the people" against "the elites". Instead, Hanson focused on unemployment and the need to create more jobs not through government schemes but by "cheap loans to business, by more apprenticeships, and by doing something about tariffs".[^cite_note-31]

Hanson won 36% of the primary vote,[^cite_note-APH-32] slightly over 10% more than the second-place Labor candidate, Virginia Clarke. However, with all three major parties [preferencing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranked_voting_systems) each other ahead of Hanson, Liberal candidate [Cameron Thompson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cameron_Thompson_\(politician\)) was able to win the seat despite finishing in third place on the first count. Thompson overtook Clarke on National preferences and defeated Hanson on Labor preferences.[^cite_note-APH-32] It has been suggested by Thompson that Hanson's litigation against parodist [Pauline Pantsdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauline_Pantsdown) was a distraction from the election, which contributed to her loss.[^cite_note-33]

Nationally, One Nation gained 8.99% of the Senate vote[^cite_note-34] and 8.4% of the Representatives vote,[^cite_note-APH-32] but only one MP was elected – [Len Harris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Len_Harris_\(politician\)) as a Senator for Queensland. [Heather Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heather_Hill_\(politician\)) had been elected to this position, but the High Court of Australia ruled that, although she was an Australian citizen, she was ineligible for election to sit as a Senator because she had not renounced her British citizenship.[^cite_note-35] The High Court found that, at least since 1986, Britain had counted as a 'foreign power' within the meaning of [section 44(i) of the Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Section_44_of_the_Constitution_of_Australia).[^cite_note-36] Hanson alleged in her 2007 autobiography *Pauline Hanson: Untamed & Unashamed* that a number of other politicians had dual citizenship yet this did not prevent them from holding positions in Parliament. Many MPs and Senators were removed from office during the [2017–18 Australian parliamentary eligibility crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017%E2%80%9318_Australian_parliamentary_eligibility_crisis).

In 1998, [Tony Abbott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Abbott) established a trust fund called "Australians for Honest Politics Trust" to help bankroll civil court cases against the One Nation Party and Hanson herself.[^cite_note-37] [John Howard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Howard) denied any knowledge of the existence of such a fund.[^cite_note-38] Abbott was also accused of offering funds to One Nation dissident Terry Sharples to support his court battle against the party. However, Howard defended the honesty of Abbott in this matter.[^cite_note-39] Abbott conceded that the political threat One Nation posed to the Howard Government was "a very big factor" in his decision to pursue the legal attack, but he also said he was acting "in Australia's national interest". Howard also defended Abbott's actions, saying, "It's the job of the Liberal Party to politically attack other parties – there's nothing wrong with that."[^cite_note-40]

## Between terms (1998–2016)

### 2001 election campaign



Hanson at the Kurri Kurri Nostalgia Festival in 2011

At the next [federal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001_Australian_federal_election) on 10 November 2001, Hanson ran for a Queensland Senate seat but narrowly failed. She accounted for her declining popularity by claiming that the [Liberals](18453.md) under [John Howard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Howard) had stolen her policies.[^cite_note-The_Age-2003-41]

> It has been widely recognised by all, including the media, that John Howard sailed home on One Nation policies. In short, if we were not around, John Howard would not have made the decisions he did.[^cite_note-The_Age-2003-41]

Other interrelated factors that contributed to her political decline from 1998 to 2002 include her connection with a series of advisors with whom she ultimately fell out ([John Pasquarelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Pasquarelli), David Ettridge and David Oldfield); disputes amongst her supporters; and a lawsuit over the organisational structure of One Nation.

In 2003, following her release from prison, Hanson unsuccessfully contested the New South Wales state election, running for a seat in the upper house. In January 2004, Hanson announced that she did not intend to return to politics,[^cite_note-42] but then stood as an independent candidate for one of Queensland's seats in the Senate in the [2004 federal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Australian_federal_election). At the time, Hanson declared, "I don't want all the hangers-on. I don't want the advisers and everyone else. I want it to be this time, Pauline Hanson." She was unsuccessful, receiving only 31.77% of the required quota of primary votes,[^cite_note-AEC-43] and did not pick up enough additional support through preferences. However, she attracted more votes than the One Nation party (4.54% compared to 3.14%)[^cite_note-AEC-43] and, unlike her former party, recovered her deposit from the Australian Electoral Commission and secured \$150,000 of public electoral funding.[^cite_note-44] Hanson claimed to have been vilified over campaign funding.[^cite_note-45]

Hanson contested the [electoral district of Beaudesert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_district_of_Beaudesert) as an independent at the [2009 Queensland state election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_Queensland_state_election).[^cite_note-46] After an election campaign dominated by discussion over hoax photographs, she placed third behind the [Liberal National Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_National_Party_of_Queensland)'s [Aidan McLindon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aidan_McLindon) and Labor's Brett McCreadie. There were conflicting media reports as to whether she had said she would not consider running again.[^cite_note-47][^cite_note-48]

On 23 July 2010, while at an event promoting her new career as a motivational speaker, Hanson expressed interest in returning to the political stage as a [Liberal](18453.md) candidate if an invitation were to be offered by the leader [Tony Abbott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Abbott) in the [2010 federal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Australian_federal_election).[^cite_note-49] No such offer was made.

### Rattnergate scandal

In March 2011, Hanson ran as an independent candidate for the [New South Wales Legislative Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_South_Wales_Legislative_Council) at the [2011 state election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_New_South_Wales_state_election),[^cite_note-50] but was not elected, receiving 2.41% of the primary statewide vote but losing on preferences.[^cite_note-51][^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53] Following the election, Hanson alleged that "dodgy staff" employed by the [NSW Electoral Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NSW_Electoral_Commission) put 1,200 votes for her in a pile of blank ballots, and she claimed that she had a forwarded NSW Electoral Commission internal email as evidence of this.[^cite_note-54] Hanson then commenced legal proceedings to challenge the outcome of the election in the NSW Supreme Court, which sat as the Court of Disputed Returns.[^cite_note-55]

From the start of proceedings, the NSW Electoral Commissioner maintained the view that Hanson's claims lacked substance.[^cite_note-56] The man who alerted Hanson to the alleged emails, who identified himself as "Michael Rattner", failed to appear in court on 8 June 2011.[^cite_note-57] "Rattner" was revealed to be Shaun Castle, a history teacher who posed as a journalist to obtain embargoed progressive poll results.[^cite_note-58]

"Michael Rattner" was a pun on [Mickey Mouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mickey_Mouse), and reports linked the pseudonym to an "anti-voter-fraud" organisation led by Amy McGrath and [Alasdair Webster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alasdair_Webster).[^cite_note-59]

After having refused to answer questions on the grounds of self-incrimination, Castle apologised to the court and was granted protection from prosecution by Justice McClellan, before being compelled to answer questions relating to the fraudulent email.[^cite_note-60] The judge ordered that Hanson's legal costs of more than \$150,000 be paid by the State of New South Wales – a move which outraged [Greens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Greens) MP [Jeremy Buckingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Buckingham), who would have been replaced by Hanson had her challenge been successful. Questioning whether Hanson's legal action should have gone ahead at all given the nature of the evidence, Buckingham said, "This lack of judgement shows that she's unfit for public office."[^cite_note-61] Earlier, the judge, Justice McClellan, said Hanson had no other remedy but to take legal action after receiving the fraudulent email.[^cite_note-62]

### Expulsion and return to One Nation



Hanson in March 2007 at the launch of her autobiography *Untamed and Unashamed*

At the [1999 state election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1999_New_South_Wales_state_election), One Nation co-founder [David Oldfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Oldfield_\(politician\)) was elected to the [New South Wales Legislative Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_South_Wales_Legislative_Council), the state parliament's upper house. However, in 2000, Oldfield was expelled from One Nation for an alleged verbal dispute with Hanson. Within weeks, Oldfield had established the splinter group, [One Nation NSW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Nation_NSW).

One Nation won three seats in the [Western Australian Legislative Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Australian_Legislative_Council) at the [2001 state election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001_Western_Australian_state_election), but the electoral success of One Nation began to deteriorate after this point because the split-away of One Nation NSW began to spark further lack of party unity, and a series of gaffes by One Nation members and candidates, particularly in Queensland.

Hanson resigned from One Nation in January 2002, and John Fischer, the state leader from Western Australia, was elected the federal president of One Nation.[^cite_note-63]

On 24 May 2007, Hanson launched [Pauline's United Australia Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauline's_United_Australia_Party) (PUAP).[^cite_note-64] The party name invokes that of the historic [United Australia Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Australia_Party).[^cite_note-65] Speaking on her return to politics, she stated, "I have had all the major political parties attack me, been kicked out of my own party and ended up in prison, but I don't give up."[^cite_note-66] In October 2007, Hanson launched her campaign song, entitled "Australian Way of Life", which included the line, "Welcome everyone, no matter where you come from."[^cite_note-67] The election campaign received media attention due to Hanson hiring a convicted child predator, Bob Hutton, as a volunteer for PUAP. Hutton had been charged with raping two children in 2003, and remained on the national child offender registry until 2018, though he claimed innocence. Hanson defended Hutton, stating "\[i]f I thought Bob was guilty of that I wouldn't have him around me."[^cite_note-68] Under the PUAP banner, Hanson again contested one of Queensland's seats in the Senate in the [2007 federal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_Australian_federal_election), when she received over 4% of total votes.[^cite_note-69]

After an unsuccessful campaign in the [2009 Queensland state election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_Queensland_state_election), Hanson announced in 2010 that she planned to deregister Pauline's United Australia Party, sell her Queensland house and move to the United Kingdom.[^cite_note-70][^cite_note-71][^cite_note-72][^cite_note-73] The announcement was warmly welcomed by [Nick Griffin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_Griffin), leader of the far-right [British National Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_National_Party) (BNP).[^cite_note-74] While considering the move, Hanson said that she would not sell her house to Muslims.[^cite_note-75] However, following an extended holiday in Europe, Hanson said in November 2010 that she had decided not to move to Britain because it was "overrun with immigrants and refugees".[^cite_note-76] Hanson lives in [Beaudesert, Queensland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaudesert,_Queensland).[^cite_note-77]

In 2013, Hanson announced that she would stand in the [2013 federal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2013_federal_election).[^cite_note-78] She rejoined the One Nation party and was a Senate candidate in New South Wales.[^cite_note-79] She did not win a seat, attracting 1.22% of the first preference vote.[^cite_note-80]



Pauline Hanson in a [Jabiru J230](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jabiru_J230) at [Caboolture Airfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caboolture_Airfield) for the Caboolture Air Show in April 2016. The aircraft has "Fed Up" slogan decals on the side.

In November 2014, Hanson announced that she had returned as One Nation leader, prior to the party's announcement, following support from One Nation party members.[^cite_note-81] She announced that she would contest the seat of [Lockyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_district_of_Lockyer) in the 2015 Queensland state election.[^cite_note-82] One Nation held the Queensland seat of Lockyer from 1998 to 2004. In February 2015, Hanson lost the seat by a narrow margin.[^cite_note-83][^cite_note-84][^cite_note-85][^cite_note-86]

## Senator for Queensland (2016–present)

### First term (2016–2022)

In mid-2015, Hanson announced that she would contest the [Senate](165705.md) for Queensland at the [2016 federal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Australian_federal_election), and also announced the endorsement of several other candidates throughout Australia. She campaigned on a tour she called "Fed Up" in 2015 and spoke at a [Reclaim Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reclaim_Australia) rally.[^cite_note-87] Hanson won a seat in the Senate at the election,[^cite_note-88] and One Nation won 9% of the vote in Queensland.[^cite_note-89] According to the rules governing the allocation of Senate seats following a [double dissolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_dissolution), Hanson served a full six-year term.[^cite_note-90] Hanson secured a spot on the [National Broadband Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Broadband_Network) parliamentary committee.[^cite_note-91]

After being elected to the parliament, she and other One Nation senators voted with the [governing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turnbull_government) [Coalition](1051313.md) on a number of welfare cuts,[^cite_note-92] and usually supported the government.[^cite_note-93]

On 17 August 2017, Hanson received criticism after wearing a [burqa](165826.md), which she claims "oppresses women", into the Senate. Attorney-General [George Brandis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Brandis) got a standing ovation from Labor and Greens senators after he gave an "emotional" speech, telling Hanson, "To ridicule that community, to drive it into a corner, to mock its religious garments is an appalling thing to do."[^cite_note-94][^cite_note-95] Following the incident, polling found that 57% of Australians supported Hanson's call to ban the burka in public places, with 44% "strongly" supporting a ban.[^cite_note-96] In August 2017, the party's constitution was changed, for Hanson to become party President for as long as she may wish and to choose her successor, who may also continue until resignation.[^cite_note-97]

On 22 March 2018, Hanson announced that One Nation would back the [Turnbull government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turnbull_government)'s corporate tax cuts.[^cite_note-98][^cite_note-99][^cite_note-100] She subsequently reversed her position, citing the failure of the government to cut immigration levels and support coal-fired power.[^cite_note-101]

On 15 October 2018, Hanson proposed an "It's OK to be white" motion in the [Australian Senate](165705.md) intended to acknowledge the "deplorable rise of anti-white racism and attacks on Western civilisation".[^cite_note-102] It was supported by most senators from the governing [Liberal–National Coalition](1051313.md), but was defeated 31–28 by opponents who called it a racist slogan from the white supremacist movement.[^cite_note-103][^cite_note-104] The following day, the motion was "recommittal", and this time rejected unanimously by senators in attendance, with its initial supporters in the Liberal–National Coalition saying they had voted for it due to an administrative error (One Nation did not attend the recommittal vote).[^cite_note-105]

On 18 September 2019, the Liberal government announced that Hanson would co-chair the newly announced parliamentary inquiry into family law along with Kevin Andrews.[^cite_note-106] She proposed a parliament motion advocating opposition to the proposed [Great Reset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Reset) of the [World Economic Forum](172185.md), on the belief that it is cover for creating a [New World Order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_World_Order_\(conspiracy_theory\)). Her proposal was defeated by 37 votes to 2.[^cite_note-107]

In 2019, Hanson campaigned against a ban on climbing [Uluru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uluru), a sacred site for local Aboriginal people.[^cite_note-Karp-2020-108] Shortly before the ban came into effect in August, the [Nine Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nine_Network) paid for Hanson's trip to Uluru, and on their *[A Current Affair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Current_Affair_\(Australian_TV_program\))* program she was shown in a controversial episode climbing the rock.[^cite_note-Karp-2020-108]

Beginning in May 2019, Hanson was a regular contributor on the Nine Network's *[Today](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Today_\(1982_TV_program\))*.[^cite_note-109] She was removed from the role in July 2020 after describing people who lived in Melbourne public housing as drug addicts who couldn't speak English.[^cite_note-110]

Following the [2019 federal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Australian_federal_election), One Nation obtained \$2.8 million in electoral expenses from the [Australian Electoral Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Electoral_Commission). Later, the Commission required One Nation to repay \$165,442 as money that had not been spent or not spent for electoral purposes. In addition, it is reported: "Hanson has personally agreed to an enforceable undertaking. And the party must in future make sure all invoices are in Hanson's name, the party's name or the name of a party officer. And make sure that all invoices match payment receipts, credit card or bank statements."[^cite_note-111]

In June 2021, following media reports that the proposed [national curriculum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Curriculum) was "preoccupied with the oppression, discrimination and struggles of [Indigenous Australians](12598742.md)", the Senate approved a motion tabled by Hanson calling on the federal government to reject [critical race theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_race_theory), despite it not being included in the curriculum.[^cite_note-112]

### Second term (2022–present)

In July 2022, the Senate opened with the [Lord's Prayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord's_Prayer) and an [Acknowledgement of Country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acknowledgement_of_Country), as normal. The ceremony includes the words: "\[the Parliament] recognises the [Ngunnawal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ngunnawal) and [Ngambri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ngambri) peoples as the traditional custodians of the Canberra meetings, and pays respects to elders past, present and emerging." Despite having sat through the ceremony many times before, this time Hanson stormed out of the chamber, yelling "No I don't, and I never will!"[^cite_note-9News-2022-113]

Hanson later stated that her opposition was to a motion that the [Aboriginal flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Aboriginal_flag) and [Torres Strait Islander flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torres_Strait_Islander_flag), which are both official [flags of Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Australian_flags), should be raised inside the Senate chamber alongside the [Australian flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_Australia).[^cite_note-9News-2022-113]

On 24 March 2023, Hanson and fellow One Nation senator [Malcolm Roberts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malcolm_Roberts_\(politician\)) attended the "Let Women Speak" rally held by anti-transgender activist [Kellie-Jay Keen-Minshull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kellie-Jay_Keen-Minshull) and both were also photographed alongside Keen-Minshull.[^cite_note-114][^cite_note-115] On 30 March, Hanson criticised [One Nation New South Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Nation_New_South_Wales) leader [Mark Latham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Latham)'s homophobic tweet in response to openly gay fellow politician [Alex Greenwich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alex_Greenwich) and called for him to apologise.[^cite_note-116]

Hanson actively supported the "No" campaign for the [2023 Australian Indigenous Voice referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_Australian_Indigenous_Voice_referendum), and was frequently accused of stoking racial divisions.[^cite_note-Plevey-117] She compared the proposed Voice to [apartheid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apartheid), asserting it would give "a minority of people more political power than the majority based on race".[^cite_note-118] In August 2023, Hanson reshared a video on Facebook from 2021 that had been deceptively edited and clipped to depict an argument between a white woman and Indigenous woman. Hanson asserted that the video clip, which only resurfaced as the debate over the Voice had begun, was an example of racial division that would occur if the Voice referendum succeeded. After being approached by ABC Investigations regarding the video, Hanson quietly removed it from her Facebook page.[^cite_note-119]



Hanson speaking at a far-right rally in November 2025

In September 2024, Hanson proposed an amendment to the [Sex Discrimination Act 1984](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sex_Discrimination_Act_1984) to remove mentions of gender identity. Hanson described the bill as supporting "biological reality over contrived gender identities". The bill failed to pass the Senate.[^cite_note-120]

In November 2024, Hanson accused Independent Senator [Fatima Payman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatima_Payman) of being a dual citizen of Australia and Afghanistan, in violation of[section 44 of the Constitution of Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Section_44_of_the_Constitution_of_Australia). Payman, who was born in [Afghanistan](737.md), formally renounced her Afghan citizenship in October 2021 before entering parliament; Hanson alleged that Payman had not provided enough evidence of her lack of dual-citizenship. A senate motion put forward by Hanson to investigate the status of Payman's citizenship was defeated 35 votes to 3, with Hanson, [Malcolm Roberts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malcolm_Roberts_\(politician\)), and [Ralph Babet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Babet) being the only Senators to vote in favour. Payman called Hanson racist, and a "disgrace to the human race", but later withdrew those comments.[^cite_note-121]

On 24 November 2025, Hanson wore a burqa on the Senate floor in a scene similar to a stunt performed in 2017. This was after a bill to [ban the burqa](53714495.md) failed. The entire Senate chamber was suspended for 90 minutes as a result of Hanson's action.[^cite_note-Truu-2025-122] She was formally [censured](698874.md) by the Senate the next day and was suspended for seven sitting days.[^cite_note-123]

On 17 June 2026, Hanson made [her first formal address at the National Press Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Press_Club_\(Australia\)#Pauline_Hanson,_2026) since entering politics in 1996. Her appearance followed a recent surge in support for the One Nation party.[^cite_note-e891-124] In the speech, Hanson attacked multiculturalism, transgender people, industrial relations, and the [ABC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Broadcasting_Corporation) and [SBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SBS_\(Australian_TV_channel\)). Hanson's address was compared to the [far-right](221220.md) movement in the [United States](3434750.md) and [United Kingdom](31717.md).[^cite_note-Dhanji-125]

Hanson appointed Chris Taylor, former policy adviser to prime minister [Scott Morrison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scott_Morrison) as her chief of staff in August 2026, replacing [James Ashby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Ashby). Ashby moved to a new role as party director for One Nation.[^cite_note-126]

In September 2026, Hanson made an alliance with [Australia's Voice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australia's_Voice) Senator [Fatima Payman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatima_Payman) and Independent MP [Dai Le](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dai_Le) to request changes to how the Parliament allocates staff to parties. The three claimed that the system favours major parties.[^cite_note-127]

### Race, immigration and multiculturalism

In her maiden speech, Hanson proposed a drastic reduction in immigration with particular reference to immigrants from Asia.[^cite_note-130] Condemning [multiculturalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiculturalism_in_Australia), One Nation has railed against government immigration and multicultural policies.[^cite_note-131]

While Hanson stated in 2018 that she does not support reinstating the [White Australia policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Australia_policy),[^cite_note-132] in 2026 on a podcast with far-right activist [Tommy Robinson](32537879.md), she said she laments the end of the White Australia policy.[^cite_note-boscaini-133]

After Hanson was elected to Parliament in 1996, journalist [Tracey Curro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tracey_Curro) asked her on *[60 Minutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/60_Minutes_\(Australian_TV_program\))* whether she was [xenophobic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenophobic). Hanson replied, "Please explain?"[^cite_note-Curro-134] This response became a much-parodied [catchphrase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catchphrase) within Australian culture,[^cite_note-135] and was included in the title of the 2016 [SBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SBS_\(Australian_TV_channel\)) documentary film *[Pauline Hanson: Please Explain!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauline_Hanson:_Please_Explain!)*

In 2006, Hanson stated that African immigrants were bringing diseases into Australia and were of "no benefit to this country whatsoever". She also expressed her opposition to Muslim immigration.[^cite_note-136] Ten years after her maiden speech, its effects were still being discussed within a racism framework,[^cite_note-137] and were included in resources funded by the Queensland Government on "Combating racism in Queensland".[^cite_note-138] In 2007, Hanson publicly backed [Kevin Andrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevin_Andrews_\(Australian_politician\)), then [Minister for Immigration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Immigration_and_Citizenship_\(Australia\)) under John Howard, in his views about African migrants and crime.[^cite_note-139]

In 2022, Hanson claimed that Australian tourists returning from [Bali](4147.md) had brought disease, saying that they brought manure back to Australia, leading to [Murray Watt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murray_Watt) deploring Hanson's rant as shameful.[^cite_note-\:02-140] Hanson's statement later attracted more anger from [Sandiaga Uno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandiaga_Uno), who condemned her statement as an insult to Bali and ridiculed her for calling Bali a sovereign nation while telling her to "check [Google](1092923.md) first".[^cite_note-141] Governor [I Wayan Koster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Wayan_Koster) also denounced Hanson's claims as lies.[^cite_note-142]

In 2018, Hanson called for immigration numbers to be capped at 75,000 a year.[^cite_note-143] In 2025, Hanson called for immigration to be cut to 130,000 a year.[^cite_note-144][^cite_note-145]

In a June 2026 speech to the [National Press Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Press_Club_\(Australia\)), Hanson stated that Australia "cannot be a multicultural society" and "must" become [monocultural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monocultural), arguing that immigration had diluted the singular Australian identity. Hanson has stated that Australia is a multiracial society.[^cite_note-\:4-146][^cite_note-\:0-147]

### Indigenous Australians

On her election in 1996, Hanson was a critic of the [Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aboriginal_and_Torres_Strait_Islander_Commission) (ATSIC) and has long criticised recognition of Aboriginal land and programs that assist Indigenous Australians.[^cite_note-148]

In April 2023, at an anti-Voice event, Hanson falsely claimed that an increase in the First Nations population was attributed to people falsely claiming Aboriginal ancestry. She announced that she was drafting a private member's bill that would change the definition of "Aboriginality" in response to the increase of First Nations-identified people. Hanson also called herself a friend of "the true blacks – from up Darwin, up Cairns", which caused an "outcry" from Indigenous people at the event.[^cite_note-Plevey-117]

In September 2026, comments Hanson made in a podcast the previous year resurfaced, where she called Indigenous Australians "the world's most primitive race" and claimed "they've achieved nothing."[^cite_note-149] Her comments attracted criticism from across the media and political spectrum,[^cite_note-150] with Liberal Party Senator [Jacinta Nampijinpa Price](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacinta_Nampijinpa_Price) saying the comments had entered "appalling territory" and saying that "all decent Australians" should condemn Hanson's comments.[^cite_note-151] In response, Hanson stated that she did not mean to cause offence, but refused to retract the remarks.[^cite_note-staszewska-152]

### Islam

Hanson has made various remarks which have been described as reflecting anti-Muslim or Islamophobic rhetoric.[^cite_note-153][^cite_note-154][^cite_note-155][^cite_note-sandeman-156]

> I've got no time for radical Islam. Their religion concerns me because of what it says in the Koran. They hate Westerners. That's what it's all about. You say, 'Well, there's good Muslims out there.' How can you tell me there are good Muslims?

— Pauline Hanson, 16 February 2026

[^cite_note-schmidt-157]

In February 2026, Hanson was widely criticised for remarks in a Sky News interview where she suggested that there are no "good" Muslims. Hanson later apologised for the comment, while also stating "I am not backing down."[^cite_note-158][^cite_note-schmidt-157]

#### Proposed Muslim immigration ban

After the 2016 [Pulse nightclub shooting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulse_nightclub_shooting), Hanson called for a [ban on Muslim immigration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_immigration_ban) to Australia.[^cite_note-159][^cite_note-160] The same year, Hanson announced several policies, including a ban on building new mosques until a [royal commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_commission) into whether Islam is a religion or a political ideology has been held, and installing [CCTV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CCTV) cameras in all existing mosques.[^cite_note-161] She has called for a [moratorium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moratorium_\(law\)) on accepting Muslim immigrants into Australia.[^cite_note-162]

In her 2016 maiden speech in the Senate, she said that "We are in danger of being swamped by Muslims who bear a culture and ideology that is incompatible with our own" and that they should "[go back to where \[they\] came from](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Go_back_to_where_you_came_from)", and called for banning Muslim migration.[^cite_note-Norman-2016-163] The speech prompted a walkout by Senate members of the [Australian Greens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Greens).[^cite_note-Norman-2016-163]

After the [January 2017 Melbourne car attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/January_2017_Melbourne_car_attack), Hanson repeated her stance on banning Muslims from entering Australia. In a live interview after the attack, she stated, "All terrorist attacks in this country have been by Muslims", on which she was corrected by a journalist.[^cite_note-164] In response, the [Islamic Council of Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Council_of_Victoria) asked for a public apology for Hanson's statement.[^cite_note-165]

On 24 March 2017, after the [2017 Westminster attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_Westminster_attack), Hanson made an announcement in a video posted to social media, holding up a piece of paper with her own proposed hash tag "#Pray4MuslimBan". "That is how you solve the problem, put a ban on it and then let's deal with the issues here", she said.[^cite_note-166]

By 2026, Hanson's position had shifted from a total Muslim immigration ban to just banning immigrants from a number of Muslim nations.[^cite_note-167]

#### Halal

In 2015, Hanson wrongly claimed that [Halal certification in Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halal_certification_in_Australia) was funding terrorism.[^cite_note-168]

On 22 June 2017, Hanson moved a motion in the [Senate](165705.md) calling on the government to respond to the [Halal inquiry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halal_certification_in_Australia). The motion was passed.[^cite_note-169][^cite_note-170]

#### Burqa

On 17 August 2017, Hanson wore a [burqa](165826.md) onto the floor of the [Australian Senate](165705.md) in a move to rally support for a national ban of the religious attire, citing "national security" concerns.[^cite_note-Murphy-2017-171] The move quickly became widely condemned by [Labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Labor_Party), the [Greens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Greens), and the [Liberal Party](18453.md).[^cite_note-172] In response, the Attorney-General [George Brandis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Brandis), who is tasked with giving advice on national security legislation, gave an "emotional" speech calling Hanson's stunt "an appalling thing to do" and advised Hanson "to be very very careful of the offense you may do to the religious sensibilities of other Australians", to which both Labor and Greens Senators gave a standing ovation.[^cite_note-173][^cite_note-Murphy-2017-171]

### Climate change

Hanson rejects scientific consensus on climate change and wants Australia to halt its climate mitigation strategies, build more coal-fired power plants and embrace nuclear power.[^cite_note-174] One Nation's policies include abolishing the federal [Department of Climate Change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Climate_Change,_Energy,_the_Environment_and_Water),[^cite_note-175] which provides advice to government on these matters,[^cite_note-176] and banning battery energy storage.[^cite_note-177]

In her address to the National Press Club on 17 June 2026, Hanson called climate change a "hoax", called for a ban on wind farm energy, incorrectly claimed the Clean Energy Finance Corporation had received \$200 billion of taxpayers' funding and claimed that One Nation's policy for nuclear energy would be at the "world's cheapest price", a claim contrary to modelling by the CSIRO on the cost of comparative energy production.[^cite_note-auto-178]

### Abortion

On her original election to Parliament in 1996, Hanson was pro-choice, however since 2022, Hanson and One Nation policy is to restrict access to abortion services for women and “seek every opportunity to roll back brutal and extreme abortion law”.[^cite_note-179] She has appeared on podcasts with anti-abortion activist [Joanna Howe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joanna_Howe).[^cite_note-180]

Hanson has said that 20 weeks was too late for an abortion[^cite_note-181] and has been criticised for "recycling the same misinformation about late-term abortion that has been used by the pro-life movement in the US".[^cite_note-182]

### Gun control

Hanson opposed the government's proposed measures to restrict access to certain high-powered firearms, following the [2025 Bondi Beach shooting](81839099.md).[^cite_note-ABC_News-2026-183]

A 2019 report found that One Nation had received over \$6,000 in disclosed donations from pro-gun groups during the 2011–2018 period, with concerns that these donations threatened to compromise Australia's safety by undermining gun control laws.[^cite_note-184] In 2019, an Al Jazeera investigation showed One Nation officials James Ashby and Steve Dickson secretly discussing potential donations from the National Rifle Association and efforts to weaken Australia's gun laws.[^cite_note-185]

### COVID-19

Hanson has been accused of using [Facebook](7529378.md) to exploit the [COVID-19](63030231.md) crisis. She has been accused of having "sought to exploit the COVID-19 crisis by manufacturing anti-Chinese sentiment in Australia with the purpose of legitimising her party's long-standing policies on issues like immigration", such as by "almost exclusively referring to COVID-19 as the 'Chinese virus'". She is also said to have "used the crisis to call for Australia to withdraw from its free trade agreement with China, has urged Australians to boycott products made in China, called for the immediate suspension of all Chinese foreign investment into Australia and suggested that backpackers and foreign workers should be denied welfare assistance and called for Australia to cease all foreign aid". She accused the United Nations and World Health Organisation of acting as a "propaganda arm of the Chinese Government". Hanson has also been accused of having "floated the unfounded conspiracy theory that COVID-19 was developed in a Chinese laboratory and has suggested that China has 'unleashed' Coronavirus on the world". This occurred during a time of increased racist attacks against Chinese and Asian people living in Australia.[^cite_note-186]

## Legal issues

### Fraud conviction and reversal

A 1999 civil suit reached the [Queensland Court of Appeal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queensland_Court_of_Appeal) in 2000 involving disgruntled former One Nation member Terry Sharples and led to a finding of fraud when registering One Nation as a political party.[^cite_note-222]

On 20 August 2003, a jury in the [District Court of Queensland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/District_Court_of_Queensland) convicted Hanson and [David Ettridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Ettridge) of [electoral fraud](382165.md). Both Hanson and Ettridge were sentenced to three years imprisonment for falsely claiming that 500 members of the Pauline Hanson Support Movement were members of the political organisation Pauline Hanson's One Nation to register that organisation in Queensland as a political party and apply for electoral funding. Because the registration was found to be unlawful, Hanson's receipt of electoral funding worth \$498,637 resulted in two further convictions for dishonestly obtaining property, each with three-year sentences, to run concurrently with the first. The sentence was widely criticised in the media, and by some politicians, including the prime minister, [John Howard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Howard), and Liberal politician [Bronwyn Bishop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronwyn_Bishop), as being too harsh.[^cite_note-223][^cite_note-QCA-2003-224]

On 6 November 2003, delivering judgment the day after hearing the appeal, the [Queensland Court of Appeal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queensland_Court_of_Appeal) quashed all of Hanson and Ettridge's convictions. Hanson, having spent 11 weeks in jail, was immediately released along with Ettridge.[^cite_note-225] The court's unanimous decision was that the evidence did not support a conclusion [beyond reasonable doubt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beyond_reasonable_doubt) that the people on the list were not members of the Pauline Hanson's One Nation party and that Hanson and Ettridge knew this when the application to register the party was submitted. Accordingly, the convictions regarding registration were quashed. The convictions regarding funding, which depended on the same facts, were also quashed.[^cite_note-QCA-2003-224]

In 1996, Hanson began a relationship with [David Oldfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Oldfield_\(politician\)). In 2000, all of Hanson's relations with Oldfield ended when he was dismissed from Pauline Hanson's One Nation.[^cite_note-Hurst-2010-245]
