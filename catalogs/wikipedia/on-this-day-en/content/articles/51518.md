A **dam** is a structure that impounds water or restricts its flow. Dams are classified into four basic types: [gravity dams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_dam) are massive structures made of concrete or [masonry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masonry) that rely on their weight to resist the force of impounded water; [embankment dams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embankment_dam) are large [earthworks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthworks_\(engineering\)) consisting of rocks, clay, sand, soil, or gravel; [buttress dams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buttress_dam) consist of a sloped, concrete face supported on the downstream side by a series of triangular [buttresses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buttress); and [arch dams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arch_dam) use a curved concrete wall to transfer the weight of the water to the surrounding valley walls.

Dams provide for [irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrigation), [hydropower](14073.md), [water supply](684928.md), [flood management](20913655.md), [recreation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_water_sports), [inland navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inland_navigation), and [fish farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish_farming). Irrigation is a critical application of dams: about 20% of the world's arable land is irrigated using water from [reservoirs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reservoir) impounded by dams. Dams generate hydropower, providing a clean and renewable source of electricity, and also supply water for household and industrial needs.

An early dam was [Jawa Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jawa_Dam_\(Jordan\)) in modern [Jordan](7515964.md), built around 3000 BCE. The [Hittite Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hittites) built several dams in modern Turkey between the 17th and 13th centuries BCE. In the 1st century CE, the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire) began building masonry gravity dams – typically with vertical faces on both upstream and downstream sides. In [medieval Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Europe), dams impounded water that powered [water wheels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_wheel) for [milling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watermill) and [mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining). The process of designing dams began to transform in the late 18th century from an informal practice to an [engineering discipline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulic_engineering) rooted in science. In the 20th century, the widespread availability of concrete and heavy construction machinery led to the creation of many large-scale dam projects worldwide.

The most visible part of a dam is the barrier that retains water, but most dams contain additional structures that perform important functions. When a dam blocks a navigable river, [locks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lock_\(water_navigation\)) may be incorporated into the project to allow ships to pass through. [Fish ladders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish_ladders) are included in many dams to enable fish to migrate upstream. [Spillways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spillway) are often included to safely release excess reservoir water downstream and prevent catastrophic overflows. Dam outlets allow reservoirs to be partially drained to purge sediment, perform maintenance, or increase water flow downstream.

A dam may be deliberately removed for various reasons: if it poses a safety hazard, if the dam no longer fulfills its original purpose, to restore fish migration routes, or to improve the health of downstream rivers by improving sediment flow. Dams occasionally fail, resulting in flooding and loss of life. Many principles governing the design of safe dams have been developed based on [lessons learned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lessons_learned) from [dam failures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dam_failure).

## Definition and etymology

Broadly, the word "dam" refers to any structure that retains water or prevents flooding.[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-4] In the context of [civil engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_engineering), the word is typically limited to structures that impound water or raise the level of a body of water.[^cite_note-5][^cite_note-10] This article uses the latter definition; therefore, it excludes dikes and [levees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levee) on the banks of a river, and [diversion dams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diversion_dam) which divert a portion of a flowing river without raising water levels.

The English word "dam" is found in [Middle English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_English), and traces back to *dam* in the [Germanic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_languages) – [Middle Low German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Low_German), [Middle Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Dutch), and [Old Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse). The word's roots include [Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_\(language\)) *faur-dammjan* ('to stop up'), and the [Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European) base \**dhē-* ('to set, put in place').[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"Dam",_''Collins_English_Dictionary''-11]

## Types

Human-made dams are classified by their structural type: [embankment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embankment_dam), [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_dam), [buttress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buttress_dam), [arch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arch_dam), and composite. Natural dams include [rockslide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockslide_dam), [volcanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_dam), and [beaver dams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaver_dam).[^cite_note-12]

### Embankment dam

Main article: [Embankment dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embankment_dam)

Embankment dams

A diagram illustrating the cross-section of a typical embankment dam.

Many [embankment dams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embankment_dam) have a clay core and gradually sloping faces both upstream and downstream.

[^cite_note-13]

A large rocky dam, with a reservoir behind it, and green hills in the background.

[Misogawa Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misogawa_Dam) in Japan is an [embankment dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embankment_dam).

The most common type of dam is an embankment dam, constructed from compacted natural materials – such as rock, [clay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clay), sand, gravel, and soil – forming a broad, mound-like barrier.[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-16] They are classified as rockfill or earthfill, depending on the primary material used.[^cite_note-17] Among the largest embankment dams are [Tarbela Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarbela_Dam), [Nurek Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nurek_Dam), and [Chicoasén Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicoas%C3%A9n_Dam).[^cite_note-18]

Embankment dams can be built from locally available materials, which make them less expensive to build than concrete dams requiring imported rocks and costly cement. They can also be built on softer soils because their broad base spreads their weight over a greater area (as opposed to heavy gravity dams that require [bedrock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bedrock) foundations).[^cite_note-19]

The primary drawback of embankment dams is that they are inherently [permeable](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/permeable), so water can seep through or beneath the dam.[^cite_note-20] Mitigation techniques to reduce seepage include placing a drainage system beneath the dam, [injecting grout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grout_curtain) into the soil below the dam, and including a vertical layer of impervious material within the dam.[^cite_note-21] If an impervious layer is included, it may be made of clay, cement, or [asphalt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitumen).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui2009162–163-22][^cite_note-24] Failure to properly mitigate seepage can lead to dam failure caused by "piping" – water starts to flow through (or under) the dam in a small channel, which gradually enlarges until a large hole is pierced in the dam.[^cite_note-25]

Early embankment dams were often built of a single type of earth, but starting in the mid-16th century, engineers began to use several types of material layered in zones.[^cite_note-26][^cite_note-28] A typical zone pattern is a clay center (a vertical wall, extending from the riverbed to the crest of the dam), with gradually sloping banks of soil on both upstream and downstream sides, and both faces covered with large rocks.[^cite_note-29] Large rocks on the upstream face protect the structure from wave action.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui2009161,_163–164-30]  In the 1970s, the Concrete Face Rockfill Dam (CFRD) design was invented, which is a rock-filled embankment dam with concrete slabs on the upstream face, sealed with waterproof joints.[^cite_note-31][^cite_note-33]

### Gravity dam

Main article: [Gravity dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_dam)

Gravity dams

A diagram showing the cross-section of a typical gravity dam.

A typical [gravity dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_dam) has a nearly vertical upstream face, and a broad base.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter1994170–172-34]

[^cite_note-35]

A massive concrete dam with a reservoir behind it.

[Grande Dixence Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grande_Dixence_Dam) in Switzerland is one of the [world's tallest gravity dams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_dams). The people atop the dam's crest illustrate its size.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"Grande_Dixence_Fact_Sheet",_''Grande_Dixence_SA''-36]

Gravity dams rely on their weight to resist the force of upstream water. Historically, gravity dams were built of masonry – stone, brick, or rubble – held together by [mortar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mortar_\(masonry\)), but nearly all modern examples are made of concrete.[^cite_note-37][^cite_note-39] The cost of concrete is much higher than earthfill used in embankment dams, so gravity dams are generally more expensive. An approach to reduce cost is to incorporate hollow chambers inside the dam – provided the dam's stability and strength are not compromised.[^cite_note-40][^cite_note-galleries-42] Notable gravity dams include [Grande Dixence Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grande_Dixence_Dam) and [Grand Coulee Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Coulee_Dam).[^cite_note-43]

The crest (top) of a gravity dam is generally a straight line stretching between the walls of the valley it crosses. When the crest is curved (the convex side of the curve always faces upstream) it is called an [arch-gravity dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arch-gravity_dam) (discussed [below](#Composite)).[^cite_note-gravDamStraight-44][^cite_note-46][^cite_note-archThickness-47] The [cross-section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_section_\(geometry\)) of gravity dams is roughly triangular, with a flat bottom resting on the valley floor, and two inclined faces (upstream and downstream) that meet at the crest.[^cite_note-49] To ensure that the dam is stable and will not tip over, the profile must conform to the [middle-third rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle-third_rule), which states that the forces acting on the dam (gravity, water pressure, etc.) must produce a net force that is directed at the middle portion of the base (rather than directed near the downstream edge of the base).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui2009148,_151–152-50] The thickness and inclination of a gravity dam must also be carefully designed to ensure stability. For non-overflow gravity dams, the thickness of the base should be about 70 to 85% of the height.[^cite_note-51][^cite_note-thick-52] The inclination (steepness, measured as run/rise) of the downstream face is typically 0.75 to 0.8, and inclination of the upstream face should be vertical or at most 0.05.[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-inclination-55]

Because gravity dams are so heavy, they must rest on bedrock; a gravity dam built over soil would compress the soil, cause the dam to settle, and perhaps crack and fail.[^cite_note-57] If the bedrock has cracks or defects, it must be prepared by injecting grout or placing concrete plugs.[^cite_note-58] A concern that designers must address is "uplift": if water seeps under the dam structure, the water pressure can apply extreme upward force on the dam structure, which may lead to leaks or even dam failure. This risk can be mitigated with the use of [grout curtains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grout_curtain) under the dam (which prevent water from seeping under the dam) and drainage systems under the dam, which lead water away when pressure increases.[^cite_note-59]

### Buttress dam

Main article: [Buttress dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buttress_dam)

Buttress dams

A diagram showing the cross-section of a typical buttress dam.

A [buttress dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buttress_dam) has an inclined upstream face supported by multiple buttresses.

[^cite_note-Buttress-60]

A huge concrete dam with trees at its base.

About 20 buttresses are visible in this photo of [Latyan buttress dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latyan_Dam) in Iran.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScott1968353-61]

A buttress dam consists of an inclined upstream face supported on the downstream side by numerous triangular [buttresses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buttress).[^cite_note-63] Most buttress dams are made of concrete.[^cite_note-64] Unlike a gravity dam (where the upstream face is nearly vertical) the upstream face of a buttress dam is sloped, typically with an inclination between 0.2 and 1.67.[^cite_note-Buttress-60][^cite_note-inclination-55] The inclination of the downstream face typically ranges from 0.09 to 0.8.[^cite_note-Buttress-60][^cite_note-inclination-55] The triangular profile of the reservoir's water pushes downward onto the dam, forcing it into the ground and increasing its stability.[^cite_note-Buttress-60] Notable buttress dams include [Itaipu Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Itaipu_Dam), [Lucendro Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lago_di_Lucendro), and [Al Massira Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Massira_Dam).[^cite_note-65]

Buttress dams use much less concrete than comparable gravity dams, but the cost savings are offset by a more complex construction process.[^cite_note-67] Buttress dams are not as strong as gravity dams, and are suited only for lower heights. Because buttress dams have a much smaller footprint (the area of ground the dam structure rests upon) than gravity dams, the risks associated with uplift forces (from water beneath the dam) are lower in buttress dams.[^cite_note-68]

The individual buttresses may experience slight movements relative to each other. If the upstream face of the dam were a solid piece of concrete, the movements of the buttresses could introduce large stresses, resulting in cracking of the upstream dam face. To mitigate this, the upstream face is divided into multiple pieces, one per buttress, called the "buttress heads". Adjacent buttress heads are typically separated by a gap, and the gaps are filled with flexible seals.[^cite_note-GapSeal-69]

### Arch dam

Main article: [Arch dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arch_dam)

Arch dams

A diagram, in plan view, of a typical arch dam.

[Arch dams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arch_dam) transmit the weight of impounded water into the valley walls.

[^cite_note-ArchCurve-70]

[^cite_note-abut-71]

A thin, curved, concrete dam, impounding a large reservoir.

[This arch dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Dam) in Tasmania is curved in both the vertical and horizontal directions.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"Gordon_Dam",_''Engineers_Australia''-72]

An arch dam is a curved concrete structure that transfers the force of the impounded water horizontally into the valley walls. This design differs from gravity or buttress dams, which transfer the force downward into the foundation.[^cite_note-ArchCurve-70] Arch dams are relatively thin: the thickness of their base is less than half their height,[^cite_note-archThickness-47] and they are made of masonry or concrete.[^cite_note-ArchConcrete-74] The central angle subtended by an arch dam can be relatively shallow or nearly semicircular, ranging from 46 to 140 degrees.[^cite_note-75][^cite_note-77] Notable arch dams include [Hongrin Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lac_de_l'Hongrin), [Flaming Gorge Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flaming_Gorge_Dam), [Moiry Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moiry_Dam), and [Contra Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contra_Dam).[^cite_note-78]

All arch dams are curved, but there are a variety of shapes they may assume. Most older arch dams used a "constant radius" shape, which resembles a section of a vertical cylinder.[^cite_note-79][^cite_note-80] A more complex shape is the "constant angle" shape, which gradually reduces radius from the crest to the base.[^cite_note-82] Research into optimizing dam shapes for maximum strength led dam engineers to adopt the constant angle shape for many arch dams, beginning in 1914.[^cite_note-ConstAngle-81] Another shape is "double curved", which resembles a section of a dome and is defined by incorporating curvature in the vertical – as well as horizontal – direction.[^cite_note-83]

Regardless of the shape of an arch dam's curvature, the arch must transfer the weight of the reservoir water into the valley walls. Arch dams can only be built in narrow valleys with strong, steep, rock walls that can withstand tremendous forces. In some dams, concrete [abutments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abutment) must be constructed between the arch and the valley walls to safely transfer the load.[^cite_note-StrongWall-84][^cite_note-abut-71]

### Other dam structures

Composite dam structures

A large, curved, concrete dam with a reservoir behind it; the reservoir is surrounded by forested hillsides.

[Sayano-Shushenskaya Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayano-Shushenskaya_Dam) in Russia is an example of an [arch-gravity dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arch-gravity_dam).

A concrete dam consisting of about ten arch shapes, with a reservoir behind it.

[Bartlett Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartlett_Dam) in the US is a multiple-arch dam.

Some dams combine features from two of the basic dam structures. An [arch-gravity dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arch-gravity_dam) combines features from arch dams and gravity dams: the overall shape is an arch, but it is not a true arch dam because the thickness of the dam's base is more than half of its height – giving it a weight and [footprint](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/footprint) that is characteristic of gravity dams.[^cite_note-85][^cite_note-87][^cite_note-archThickness-47]

A multiple-arch dam[^cite_note-89] combines features of arch dams with buttress dams. It is similar to a buttress dam, but the upstream face is not flat – rather, the face consists of a number of small arch dams: each arch connects one pair of adjacent buttresses.[^cite_note-90][^cite_note-92]

A [barrage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barrage_\(dam\)) is a dam that has a spillway integrated into the dam structure, with multiple [gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floodgate) regulating the flow over the spillway.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"Glossary",_''Reclamation_Library''-6] [Dikes and levees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levee) – which share the same design as embankment dams – are not true dams because they generally line the banks of a river or sea, whereas dams are placed *crosswise* in a valley.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDenny2010191-9]

### Natural dams

&#x20;An aerial photo of an arid mountain range, with a lake.

&#x20;[Usoi Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usoi_Dam) is a [rockslide dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockslide_dam) formed in 1911 by an earthquake.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEvans201193,_506-93]

A small dam built of wood branches, with a pond behind it and vegetation on both sides.

&#x20;[Beavers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaver) build [beaver dams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaver_dam) by dragging tree branches into streams.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBaker2003294–298-94]

Not all dams are made by humans: natural dams are barriers created by processes such as [rockslides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockslide), [lava flows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lava_flow), or by the dam-building behavior of [beavers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaver).[^cite_note-95]

A [rockslide dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockslide_dam) is a natural dam formed by a rockslide that slides into a valley and blocks the flow of a river, forming a lake on the upstream side.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEvans2011ix-96] There are thousands of rockslide dams around the world, including one created in 2010 in Pakistan that formed [Attabad Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attabad_Lake).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEvans2011ix-96] Rockslide dams have the potential to cause catastrophic loss of life, if they fail and create an [outburst flood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outburst_flood). In 1786, [an earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1786_Kangding-Luding_earthquake) created a rockslide dam on the [Dadu River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dadu_River) in China, which failed ten days later, killing 100,000 people.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEvans2011x-97] Risks of outburst floods can be mitigated by building spillways on rockslide dams to lower the water level.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEvans2011x-97] Engineers have used rockslide dams as foundations upon which to build new dams.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEvans2011xi-98] Rarely, engineers have used [blasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drilling_and_blasting) on mountainsides to trigger a rockslide and create a crude embankment dam, called a "blast-fill" dam.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEvans2011xi,_55,_624–626-99]

[Volcanic dams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_dam) are the result of [volcanic activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_activity), which can create dams from lava flows, [lahar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lahar) deposits, [pyroclastic flows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyroclastic_flow), or other debris.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEvans2011519-100] Volcanic activity about 13,000 years ago created [The Barrier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Barrier), a lava dam in Canada, which impounded [Garibaldi Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garibaldi_Lake).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMorison2023§_2.4.2-101]

[Beaver dams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaver_dam) are structures built by beavers across a stream. Beavers build the dams at night, carrying mud, stones, and branches in their forepaws and teeth. The dam creates a pond which protects against predators and facilitates food access. Some dams contain lodges – chambers built within the dam – in which beavers can dwell. Beaver dams modify the local environment in a way that creates new [habitats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habitat) for other species, making beavers a [keystone species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keystone_species) and [ecosystem engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem_engineer).[^cite_note-102]

[\[aa\]](#cite_note-106)

The main purposes that dams serve include [irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrigation), [hydropower](14073.md), [water supply](684928.md), [flood management](20913655.md), [recreation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_water_sports), [inland navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inland_navigation), and [fish farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish_farming).[^cite_note-107] Many dams – called "multi-purpose dams" – support two or more primary functions.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter1994Forward_p._x-108]

Irrigation is a critical application of dams: about 20% of the world's [arable land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arable_land) is irrigated by water that originated in reservoirs impounded by dams (as of 2022).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchleiss202269-109] In addition to directly moving water from the reservoir to irrigation canals, dams can also support irrigation by "dry-season releases": the dam impounds water during the wet season, and releases it downstream during the [dry season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dry_season), thus ensuring water in the river year-round.[^cite_note-110]

Hydropower provides clean, [renewable energy](25784.md) in the form of hydroelectricity. As of 2024, global hydropower capacity accounted for about 14% of the world's electricity supply.[^cite_note-HydroElec-111] More than 80% of the world's total reservoir water storage capacity is used for hydropower, as of 2006.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELemperiere20061066-112] The world's 40 largest reservoirs store 40% of the world's reservoir water, of which 90% is devoted to hydropower, as of 2006.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELemperiere20061066-112]  In some climates, hydropower dams can act as an annual buffering system: the reservoir can be filled during the rainy season, then during the dry season (when it is typically hotter and electricity is needed to run [air conditioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_conditioning) systems) the water can be released to generate electricity.[^cite_note-PumpedStorage-113]

Some hydropower dams provide a [pumped-storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pumped-storage_hydroelectricity) capability: these dams consume excess electricity (for example, from [solar power](13690575.md) on a sunny day) to drive pumps that lift water from a low reservoir to a higher reservoir. When the electrical grid needs more power (for example, on a cloudy day), the water can be released to power the dam's generators to create hydroelectricity.[^cite_note-PumpedStorage-113] A pumped-storage capability can be used in a 24-hour cycle: during the night, when community use of electricity is low, conventional power sources (nuclear, oil) can power pumps to lift water into reservoirs; then – during the peak consumption hours in daytime – the water can be released through the dam's generators to generate electricity.[^cite_note-PumpedStorage-113]

Water supply – for domestic and industrial use – is the third most common purpose of large[^cite_note-large-103] dams. In 2025, 3,394 large dams were dedicated to this use.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"World_Register_of_Dams",_''ICOLD''-104] Industrial usage is about twice domestic usage, but some of the water withdrawn from reservoirs (such as water used solely for cooling purposes) is returned to the river system.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELemperiere20061066–1067-114]

Flood management is an important function of many dams.[^cite_note-FloodMgmt-115] In 2025, there were 2,510 large dams in the world devoted to flood management.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"World_Register_of_Dams",_''ICOLD''-104] These dams do not try to prevent *all* floodwaters from reaching downstream. Instead, they try to reduce the peak flood level (height) to a safe limit. Since floods are so unpredictable, these goals are typically expressed as statistical margins based on lengthy [return periods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Return_period). For example, a dam may be designed with the goal of safely regulating 1-in-100-year floods.[^cite_note-FloodMgmt-115] The flood-control benefits of dams may become increasingly important in the 21st century as flood risks are projected to increase due to [climate change](5042951.md).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoulange20214–5-116]

Many dams are built on rivers for the purpose of keeping the water level sufficiently high to support [transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inland_navigation), including [barges](4177.md) that carry freight. These dams are typically low, and are found near industries that require cargo to be transported on [waterways](174851.md).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELemperiere20061067-117] Other dams are designed with the primary goal of supporting recreation or fish farming.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELemperiere20061067-117]

A [weir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weir) in Spain

A [tailings dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tailings_dam) is a dam that impounds [tailings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tailings) – the waste generated by [mining operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining_operations).[^cite_note-119] Most tailings dams are embankment structures.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlightJewell19986,_8,_29-120] Unlike conventional water-storage dams – which are typically built in valleys – tailings dams may be built on flat ground, with an embankment that encloses the tailings on all sides.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlightJewell19989-121] Tailings dams are unique because they are often enlarged over time: as mine operations continue, the embankments are repeatedly raised.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlightJewell19989–10-122] Tailings often include toxic by-products of mining, such as [arsenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arsenic) or [lead](17747.md). Therefore, tailings dams usually incorporate special protective measures to ensure that materials from the tailings do not contaminate the water supply outside the dam.[^cite_note-123] Protective measures include foundation grouting, liners (clay or membrane), drainage blankets, and under-drains.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFell20141111,_1123–1134-124]

A [cofferdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cofferdam) is a temporary watertight structure built at a work site to create a dry area for construction until the project is completed.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui2009240–244-125] Cofferdams are commonly used when building bridge supports in lakes, rivers, and oceans.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGerwick2014148–155-126] When a dam is constructed in a river valley, cofferdams are often built upstream to divert the river through temporary tunnels or channels. The tunnels (or channels) carry the water around the construction site and discharge it downstream, keeping the work site dry.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui2009240–244-125]

A [weir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weir) is a low, flat structure built across a riverbed. Weirs do not fully block a river; instead, they regulate its flow in a controlled manner.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKitchen201613–18-127] Some weirs are designed to maintain a water level in a stretch of river,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKitchen201613–14-128] while others minimize erosion of riverbanks.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKitchen201616–17-129] Weirs also serve landscaping or recreation purposes,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKitchen201617–18-130] or function as [measuring gauges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stream_gauge) (the total water flow can be computed from the depth of the water passing over the weir).[^cite_note-131]

&#x20; If the ridge surrounding a dam's reservoir contains a [saddle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saddle_\(landform\)) (low point) below the planned level of the reservoir, a saddle dam must be built to fill in the low point. Saddle dams should be designed with the same detail and care as the primary dam.[^cite_note-132][^cite_note-134]

Underground dams are used to block the flow of [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater) and store it below the surface. They are small-scale structures constructed in arid regions where water is scarce. Some underground dams are built by digging a [trench](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trench) in the path of naturally flowing groundwater and placing a vertical, impervious barrier, then refilling the trench. Another design, used in sandy regions, is to build a low dam across a small valley so that occasional rainstorms will cause sand and water to accumulate behind the dam (the sand will inhibit evaporation of the groundwater). In both of these designs, a [well](15105978.md) or pipe is placed upstream of the barrier to withdraw the water.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEOnderYilmaz2005-135]

## History

Main article: [History of dams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_dams)

### Antiquity

Ruins of a masonry dam located in a desert.

The [Great Dam of Marib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marib_Dam) in Yemen was built around 500 BCE.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter199424–27-136]

The earliest known dam is [Jawa Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jawa_Dam_\(Jordan\)), built around 3000 BCE near [Amman](45007.md), Jordan. This embankment dam was part of an elaborate water supply system and was 28 m (92 ft) thick[^cite_note-thick-52] and 5.5 m (18 ft) high.[^cite_note-137][^cite_note-139] Around 2600 BCE, the Egyptians built the [Sadd el-Kafara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sadd_el-Kafara) embankment dam near [Cairo](6293.md), although it failed around the time its construction was completed.[^cite_note-Sadd-140]

The [Hittite Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hittites) built several dams between the 17th and 13th centuries BCE, including one near the [Eflatun Pınar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eflatun_P%C4%B1nar) temple in modern Turkey.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter199413–14-141] Beginning around 1500 BCE, the [Sabaean peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabaeans) built a series of dams across the Wadi Danah, located in modern [Yemen](350939.md). This work culminated with [the Great Dam of Marib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marib_Dam) (built around 500 BCE) which was 700 m (2,300 ft) long and 20 m (66 ft) high.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter199424–27-136] An early dam in China – built by [Sunshu Ao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunshu_Ao) around 580 BCE – impounded [Afengtang Reservoir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afengtang_Reservoir?action=edit\&redlink=1), which still exists today.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter199441–43-142] In [Sri Lanka](26750.md), several dams – including [Tissa Wewa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tissa_Wewa_\(Anuradhapura\)) – were built between c. 370 and c. 300 BCE to create reservoirs. Some of the dams were several kilometers long.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter199433–36-143]

### Roman era

The remains of a bridge and dam, in a calm lake.

The [Band-e Kaisar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Band-e_Kaisar) dam in Iran was built in the 3rd century CE.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter199476-144]

The [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire) constructed major waterworks – including [aqueducts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_aqueduct) and tunnels – starting in the 5th century BCE, but they did not begin building significant dams until the 1st century CE.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter199455–58-145] [Roman dams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Roman_engineering#Dams) were typically [masonry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masonry) gravity dams with vertical faces on both upstream and downstream sides, although some were reinforced on the downstream side with buttresses or rock embankments.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter199457-146] The Romans were the first to use [cement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cement) as a construction material, which could be mixed with small rocks to form [concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concrete), or mixed with sand to form [mortar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mortar_\(masonry\)) to join bricks or stones. Some Roman cements, particularly those containing [volcanic ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pozzolana), were waterproof.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter199456–57-147]

One of the earliest dams built by the Romans was also their tallest: [Subiaco Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subiaco_Dams) was built around 60 CE and was 40 m (130 ft) tall and 13.5 m (44 ft) thick.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter199458-148] The Romans built about 80 known dams in [Hispania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispania) (modern Spain),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter199459-149] including [Proserpina Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proserpina_Dam), which impounded 6 million m<sup>3</sup> of water and was still operational in 2026.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter199462–64-150] Roman dam technology was applied in nearby countries: after Persian king [Shapur I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shapur_I) defeated emperor [Valerian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valerian_\(emperor\)), he put Roman prisoners to work building the [Band-e Kaisar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Band-e_Kaisar) dam spanning the [Karun River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karun_River).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter199476-144] The [Iron Gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Gate_\(Antioch\)) was an early arch-gravity dam built by [Justinian I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justinian_I) around 550 CE near [Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antioch) in modern Turkey.[^cite_note-151][^cite_note-153]

### Post-classical Asia and Middle Ages

&#x20;A tall, thin masonry dam positioned between two nearly vertical rock faces.

The [Kurit Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurit_Dam) was built around 1350 CE.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter199490-154]

One of the earliest dams built in Japan was [Sayama embankment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayama_pond), built near [Osaka](52230.md) in 380 CE, which was 8 m (26 ft) high and 300 m (980 ft) long.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter1994106-155] [Kurit Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurit_Dam) – the world's first large, thin arch dam – was built in Persia (modern-day Iran) around 1350 CE. It was 26 m (85 ft) high and was later raised to 64 m (210 ft). It remained the world's tallest dam until the start of the 20th century.[^cite_note-156]

In Europe, dams were used to power water wheels for [milling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watermill) and [mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining).[^cite_note-157] An early example was [Bazacle weir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bazacle) built around 1170 CE in France.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter1994108-158] Dams to create [fish ponds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish_farming) were common in Europe, and hundreds were built in [Bohemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohemia) during the 15th and 16th centuries, creating ponds covering a total of 1,800 km<sup>2</sup>.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter1994120–122-159] Dams for irrigation included the Spanish dams [Almansa Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almansa_Dam) (1384) – an arch-gravity dam; and the still-standing[^cite_note-160] [Elche Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elche_Dam?action=edit\&redlink=1) (Spain, 1640) – the first true arch dam built in Europe since Roman times.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter1994124–127-161][^cite_note-163] Several dams were built to supply [Istanbul](3391396.md) with water, including one in 1560 that brought water from [Belgrad Forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgrad_Forest).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter1994131–132-164] Another purpose of dams was transportation: [Saint-Ferréol Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Ferr%C3%A9ol_Dam) was built in France in 1675 to provide water for the [Midi Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midi_Canal). It remained one of the highest embankment dams in the world for over a century.[^cite_note-165] Several books on the subject of dam design were published in the 1700s by European authors including [Jacob Leupold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_Leupold), [Albert Brahms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Brahms), [Johann Silberschlag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Silberschlag), and [Oliver Evans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Evans).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter1994152-166]

### Industrial Revolution

A stone dam with a reservoir behind it, surrounded by trees.

&#x20;In Australia, [Parramatta Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Parramatta) tested the limits of how thin a dam could be.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter1994151,_183-167]

In the late 18th century, the process of designing dams began to transform from an [empirical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_evidence) practice – based on experience and [trial and error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trial_and_error) – into an [engineering discipline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering) rooted in science.[^cite_note-Science-168] Important figures in this evolution included the French scientist [Charles-Augustin de Coulomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles-Augustin_de_Coulomb) who, in 1776, created [a formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohr%E2%80%93Coulomb_theory) that described how soil reacts under stress. The formula was later applied to dam engineering by [Alexandre Collin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandre_Collin?action=edit\&redlink=1).[^cite_note-Science-168] In 1847, [François Zola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_Zola) became the first engineer to design [an arch dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zola_Dam) based on an analytical consideration of stresses.[^cite_note-169] The French engineer [J. Augustine DeSazilly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Augustine_DeSazilly) established that the optimal cross-section for a gravity dam was a triangle, with a vertical face on the upstream side.[^cite_note-170]

These scientific foundations led to safer, larger dams of all types. [Glencorse Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glencorse_Reservoir) in Britain (1824) was a 21 m (69 ft) high [embankment dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embankment_dam) that contained a clay core and had gently sloping faces.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter1994157-171] In France, [Gouffre d'Enfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gouffre_d'Enfer?action=edit\&redlink=1) masonry [gravity dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_dam) (1866) was 60 m (200 ft) tall.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter1994170–172-34] The world's first large [buttress dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buttress_dam) was [Mir Alam Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mir_Alam_Tank) (1804) in India.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter1994151,_183-167] In Australia, [Parramatta Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Parramatta) (1856) tested the limits of how thin an arch dam could be.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter1994151,_183-167]

### Modern era

&#x20;A long, straight road on top of a dyke, with the ocean on one side and a lake on the other.

&#x20;The [Afsluitdijk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afsluitdijk) embankment dam was built in the Netherlands in 1932 to enclose part of the North Sea and turn it into a [freshwater lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IJsselmeer).

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEVogt2019-172]

In the first half of the 20th century, many large dams were built, particularly in [Western Europe](33800.md) and the US.[^cite_note-173] After [World War II](32927.md) (WWII), the availability of heavy construction machinery – such as [bulldozers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulldozers), [dump trucks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dump_trucks), and [scrapers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheel_tractor-scraper) – contributed to a substantial increase in dam construction worldwide.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter1994165–167-174] Notable dams of the modern era include massive concrete gravity dams like [Hoover Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoover_Dam) (US, 1936)[^cite_note-175] and Three Gorges Dam (China, 2006).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"China's_Three_Gorges_Dam",_''BBC''-176] Some modern embankment dams are even larger, including [Tarbela Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarbela_Dam) (Pakistan, 1976)[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJansen19885–6-177] and [Nurek Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nurek_Dam) (Tajikistan, 1980).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBerlow1998113,_195-178]

The invention of [grout curtain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grout_curtain) technologies enabled dams to be safely built on top of porous soils.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter1994213-179][^cite_note-180] This allowed Aswan High Dam (1970) to be built across the [Nile River](21244.md), which has a deep, sandy riverbed. Grout was pumped 208 m (682 ft) deep into the sand, creating an underground barrier spanning 57,000 m<sup>2</sup> which prevented water from seeping beneath the dam.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter1994213-179]

The modern era also saw the emergence of arguments against dam construction. After climate change became a global concern, debates emerged arguing whether the electricity produced by dams was as [clean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clean_energy) as solar power or [wind generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_generation). Although [hydroelectricity](381399.md) itself is clean, dam opponents argue that adverse environmental impacts[^cite_note-182] outweigh any benefits.[^cite_note-183]

The number of large[^cite_note-large-103] dams in the world in 2025 was 62,362.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"World_Register_of_Dams",_''ICOLD''-104] The total number of human-made dams – of any size – in the world in 2019 was estimated at 2.8 million.[^cite_note-185]

## Environmental impacts

Further information: [Human impact on the environment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_impact_on_the_environment)

A factory with three large chimneys.

Hydropower dams produce clean, renewable energy.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchleiss202269,_71-193]

&#x20;However, the production of cement required to build concrete dams generates [greenhouse gases](21350772.md) ([kilns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cement_kiln) shown).

[^cite_note-194]

Dams can have a major impact on the environment by altering rivers, animal habitats, vegetation, forests, [biodiversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity), and [microclimates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microclimate).[^cite_note-EnvImpacts-195] Dams often degrade the quality and volume of the downstream water – especially in the dry season – which can severely impact plant and animal species that depend on the water.[^cite_note-WaterQuality-196][^cite_note-197] In extreme cases, dams can lead to [deforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation) and [desertification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desertification) downstream, as has happened in [Iran](14653.md), where reservoirs behind dams lose about 2.2 billion m<sup>3</sup> of water each year to evaporation, resulting in severely limited flows downstream from some dams.[^cite_note-198] Desertification has also caused the [Aral Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aral_Sea) – formerly the fourth largest inland sea in the world – to virtually disappear; partially due to dams constructed in the former Soviet Union to support irrigation.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKayiranga2024§§_"1._Introduction",_"4.3._Water_cycle_dynamics_and_related_decision-making_context_over_the_Aral_Sea_basin",_"5._Conclusions"-199]

Rivers normally carry [sediment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sediment) that replenishes soil downstream of the dam site. Sediment flow is reduced after a dam is constructed, because sediment is trapped in the reservoir,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchleiss202272–74,_721–722-200] which may cause river deltas to shrink due to lack of replenishment.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBunbury202345–46-201] The tremendous weight of water in a reservoir can trigger [landslides](37208.md) and [induce earthquakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Induced_seismicity#Artificial_lakes),[^cite_note-202] as occurred after filling the reservoirs of the [Hoover Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoover_Dam) (US, 1936) and [Nurek Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nurek_Dam) (Tajikistan, 1972).[^cite_note-203]

A dam and its reservoir can cause species of fish and terrestrial animals to become [threatened](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threatened_species) or [extinct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extinction).[^cite_note-habitatImpacts-204] In Malaysia, the [Temenggor Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temenggor_Dam) inundated important forests and threatened about 100 species of mammals and 300 species of birds.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui200993-205][^cite_note-206] A study on the global impact of 31,780 dams on 7,369 fish species found that 53% of the species are threatened with [extirpation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extirpation) (local extinction) in parts of their range.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKeijzer2024§_3.2_"Potentially_lost_range"-207][^cite_note-208] Migratory fish are especially vulnerable: in the [Yangtze river](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yangtze_river) alone, dams have caused five species to reach the verge of extinction, despite efforts to preserve them.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHuang2024-209][^cite_note-211] Around 2010, the [Chinese paddlefish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_paddlefish) became extinct primarily due to construction of [Gezhouba Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gezhouba_Dam), and the [baiji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baiji) river dolphin became [functionally extinct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_extinction) following construction of Three Gorges Dam.[^cite_note-extinct-212][^cite_note-213]

The [Diama Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diama_Dam) (Senegal and Mauritania, 1986) blocked the flow of the [Senegal River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senegal_River), causing increased salinity downstream that led to [dieback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_dieback) of native vegetation. Together with agriculture, it contributed to the development of invasive aquatic plants in the upstream and to a strong decline in fishing catches through the blockage of fish migrations and the reduction in size and connectivity of wetlands and main stem.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETaïbi202393–94-214] Dams can impede [fish migration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish_migration) by blocking access to spawning habitat upstream of the dam.[^cite_note-215] [Mequinenza Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mequinenza_Dam) (Spain, 1964) on the [Ebro River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebro_River) blocked the migratory paths of [sturgeons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sturgeon), [shad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shad), and [lampreys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamprey); and its reservoir created a new habitat where [invasive species](67554.md) flourished, such as [zebra mussel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zebra_mussel), [Asian clam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_clam), and [wels catfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wels_catfish).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMariano2023775-216]

Air quality may be affected both while the dam is being built, and after it is completed. During construction, large amounts of airborne [particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particulates) are introduced to the atmosphere;[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchleiss202271-217] and after the dam begins operating, the reservoir's water can influence the [humidity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humidity) and may impact the microclimate near the dam site.[^cite_note-218]

Electricity produced by hydropower dams is renewable and is generally much cleaner than power produced from [fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchleiss202269,_71-193] Dams with large reservoirs in cold climates produce 30 to 60 times less [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) (CO<sub>2</sub>) and methane than [power stations](212141.md) that burn coal or petroleum products.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchleiss202271-217] Although the production of electricity is clean, the process of building a concrete dam is responsible for the release of large amounts of [greenhouse gases](21350772.md) into the atmosphere, which contribute to climate change.[^cite_note-219] To produce one cubic meter of concrete, between 100 and 300 kg of CO<sub>2</sub> is emitted.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"Concrete_CO2_Fact_Sheet",_''NRMCA''7-220][^cite_note-221] CO<sub>2</sub> is also emitted from reservoir water as [organic matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_matter) decomposes; the organic matter includes all plants and trees submerged by the reservoir, as well as plant life carried into the reservoir from upstream.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchleiss202270–71-222]

## Socio-economic impacts

A chart showing world hydropower generation from 1965 to 2025, which increases from 1,000 TWh to 4,000 TWh in that period.

Dams generated more electricity than any other renewable energy source during the period 1965 to 2025.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERitchie2025Figure_"Renewable_electricity_generation,_World"-223]

Dams can provide [significant benefits](#Dam_Benefits) to a community through their primary functions such as irrigation, water supply, and hydroelectricity. They can also contribute to economic development by supporting industrial growth and increased employment.[^cite_note-224] The reservoirs created by some dams can provide recreational opportunities such as boating, [water-skiing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water-skiing), swimming, [kayaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kayaking), canoeing, sailing, and [waterfowl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterfowl) hunting.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKakoyannis20021–6-225][^cite_note-227]

Dams can also adversely impact the quality of life in local communities. A dam and its reservoir may interfere with transportation networks, degrade water quality, alter [natural landscapes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_landscape), submerge important archaeological sites, and displace large numbers of people.[^cite_note-SocEcoImpacts-228]

Land adjacent to a reservoir may become saturated with water, increasing soil salinity and affecting agricultural production. The level of [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater) surrounding a reservoir may rise, and the [quality of water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quality_of_water) obtained from wells may degrade.[^cite_note-WaterQuality-196] Some rivers – prior to being dammed – experienced an annual flood cycle that deposited nutrient-rich soil in the valley lowlands; after damming, the flood cycle was disrupted, and farmers may be required to use fertilizer to maintain the same crop yields.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBunbury202369-229][^cite_note-230]

Some dam projects threaten to flood [natural wonders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landmark) or [cultural heritage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historic_site) sites.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGyau-Boakye200125-231]  Aswan High Dam (Egypt, 1970) would have submerged the [Philae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philae_temple_complex) and [Abu Simbel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Simbel) temples had they not been relocated by an [international rescue effort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Campaign_to_Save_the_Monuments_of_Nubia).[^cite_note-232][^cite_note-234] [Itaipu Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Itaipu_Dam) (Brazil and Paraguay, 1984) inundated the renowned [Guaíra Falls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gua%C3%ADra_Falls).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEChellaney201554-235]

A huge concrete dam with a large reservoir behind it, and topped with several large gantries.

[Three Gorges Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Gorges_Dam) in China displaced 1.4 million people.

[^cite_note-3gorgesdisplace-236]

Communities that live near a dam and its reservoir are often required to [relocate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_transfer), resulting in major [disruptions](3528567.md) to their culture and [livelihoods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Livelihood).[^cite_note-displace-237] Aswan High Dam displaced 50,000 [Nubians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nubia) and devastated the Nubian community.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECollins2002110,_186–189-238] [Danjiangkou Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danjiangkou_Reservoir) (China, 1974) required the forced relocation of 383,000 people.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter1994231–232-239] Three Gorges Dam (China, 2003) resulted in the resettlement of 1.4 million people.[^cite_note-3gorgesdisplace-236] When [Akosombo Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akosombo_Dam) was built in Ghana in 1965, it created [Lake Volta](1477772.md) – the largest reservoir in the world[^cite_note-241] – which covered 3% of Ghana's landmass.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGyau-Boakye200125-231] The reservoir displaced 80,000 people, many of whom were forced to find new occupations: farmers became fishermen; fishermen became farmers.[^cite_note-242] Many sites of ancestor worship were submerged, leading families to abandon traditional rituals and customs, which disrupted the [social order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_order) and caused [antisocial behaviors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antisocial_behavior).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGyau-Boakye200125-231]

Many dam projects require extensive modifications to local infrastructure. New housing may be built for workers, and electrical power transmission lines will be needed if the dam produces hydroelectricity.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchleiss202274–75-243] Dams may impact transportation: bridges and roads may need to be created or re-routed,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchleiss202274–75-243] and the reservoir may promote the growth of weeds that impede boat traffic.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGyau-Boakye200122-244] Dams and their reservoirs can increase the prevalence of water-related diseases such as [schistosomiasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schistosomiasis), [malaria](20423.md), and [onchocerciasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onchocerciasis).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGyau-Boakye200122–24-245]

### Water scarcity and international conflict

Further information: [Water conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_conflict)

A photograph of a large concrete dam and the reservoir it creates. The photo is taken from an aerial viewpoint, looking down from above. Large plumes of white water spray over a spillway.

Ethiopia built the [Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Ethiopian_Renaissance_Dam) on the [Blue Nile River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Nile_River) in 2020, over the objections of Egypt and Sudan.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERanjan202418–33-246]

[Water scarcity](15380061.md) is a major global issue affecting countries such as China, South Korea, India, Belgium, South Africa, Algeria, Jordan, and Rwanda.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEChellaney2015xiii,_3,_8-247] Water withdrawals from some rivers are so extensive that river flows are severely depleted before reaching their [delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_delta), including [Indus River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_River) (Pakistan), [Colorado River](180968.md) (US), and [Yellow River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_River) (China).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEChellaney20153-248]  In response to concerns about water shortages, some countries have pursued dam construction projects to enhance [water security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_security) and advance long-term strategic objectives.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEChellaney20152–3,_6,_26–27,_39-249] When a dam is built on a [river that flows into other nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transboundary_river), downstream nations are often concerned about the dam's effects on water availability and river flow, as well as the risk of dam failure.[^cite_note-250] Such dams can lead to international conflict between the upstream and downstream nations, particularly when the downstream nations are facing water scarcity problems.[^cite_note-Share-251]

Notable disputes about dams include Turkey's [Southeastern Anatolia Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeastern_Anatolia_Project), a major water project which includes many dams, one of which is [Karakaya Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karakaya_Dam). Most of these dams are on the [Euphrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphrates) and [Tigris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigris) rivers, which flow downstream into neighboring nations Syria and Iraq. These downstream nations have protested to Turkey about potential water supply issues.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEChellaney201548,_179–180,_197–202,_225-252]  The [Indus River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_River) is the primary river of Pakistan, with [headwaters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Headwater) in several countries, including India.  India built large dams on the Indus over the opposition of Pakistan, including the [Baglihar Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baglihar_Dam) and [Kishanganga Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kishanganga_Dam).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEChellaney201544–49,_185–187,_192–197,_262–263-253] The [Mekong River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mekong_River) traverses several countries.  China built many dams on the river without the consent of the downstream nations, including [Xiaowan Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xiaowan_Dam) and [Nuozhadu Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuozhadu_Dam).[^cite_note-254] The Nile River has been the source of tensions between the arid downstream nations (Egypt and Sudan) and the upstream nations where most of its water originates as rainfall.  In 2020, Ethiopia completed [Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Ethiopian_Renaissance_Dam) (GERD) on the Blue Nile, in spite of opposition from Sudan and Egypt.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERanjan202418–33-246]

## Design

### Design process

The dam design process can be executed in three stages: reconnaissance, [feasibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feasibility_study), and [project planning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_planning).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui20091–3-255] In the reconnaissance stage, designers visit the site, study it carefully, and gather geological, seismic, and topographic data. In the feasibility stage, detailed technical investigations are performed to assess the geology, hydrology, and [hydraulics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulics) of the site. Analysis of environmental and flood impacts begins, and inquiries are made into [land acquisition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_acquisition), [public utility](171136.md) availability, and [construction material](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Construction_material) sources (such as rocks and soil for fill material).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui20091–3-255] In the planning stage, detailed plans are created, a construction schedule is established, and [cost estimates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost_estimate) are prepared.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui20091–3-255]

### Technical investigations

&#x20;Two men are looking at a small model of a dam, which is about 1 meter tall and 2 meters wide.

Dam engineers often use [scale models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_model) to evaluate dam designs. This is a model of [Tellico Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tellico_Dam) in the US.

[^cite_note-256]

During the planning process for a dam, a large number of technical investigations are typically conducted. These investigations may be categorized as [topographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topographic), [geological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geological), and [hydrological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrological).[^cite_note-257] Topographic investigations [survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying) the dam site and use the data to prepare detailed [topographic maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topographic_map) of the region. Every aspect of the dam's design will rely on the maps, so they must be very precise.[^cite_note-258]

Geological investigations study the rocks and soil of the dam site. The dam  – and the water it impounds – exerts immense forces on the valley walls and on the ground beneath the reservoir and dam structure. An accurate understanding of the strength of the ground, and identifying any [faults](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fault_\(geology\)), is essential to minimizing seepage and reducing the risk of dam failure.[^cite_note-259] The geological research must also assess the likelihood and magnitude of earthquakes.[^cite_note-260]

Hydrological investigations examine all aspects of water flow in the vicinity of the dam. Data are produced that identify the size of the upstream watershed and annual precipitation. Studies are performed to determine how much water flows through the dam site in an average year, and how much it varies within a year and between years.[^cite_note-261] These data are used to predict the frequency and magnitude of floods at the dam site, and to establish the capacity of the dam's spillway.[^cite_note-FloodMgmt-115]

### Environmental impact assessment

Further information: [Environmental impact assessment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_assessment)

During the design process, developers in many countries are required to prepare an [environmental impact assessment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_assessment) (EIA) that documents the effects the dam (and its reservoir) will have on communities and the environment.[^cite_note-EIA-181] The EIA enables the developer and government to assess the desirability of a dam, to mitigate its impacts, and to compensate people adversely affected.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui200991–94-262] Mitigations include changing the dam's location, size, or design; compensating those impacted; or cancelling the project altogether.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchleiss202276–77,_235–238-263]

### Selection of location, structure, and material

Three important decisions made during the dam design process are [site selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Site_selection), the choice of structural type, and the selection of construction materials. These choices require detailed analysis of topography (the shape of the valley), geology (especially as it relates to the strength of the ground below and to the side of the dam), hydrology (flow of water in the valley), availability of construction materials, and potential pathways for [spillways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spillway).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui2009131–133-264] Designers must assess all forces that the dam structure must withstand, including [hydrostatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrostatic) pressure from stored water, ice loads, sediment pressure, temperature-induced stresses, uplift forces, flood events, earthquakes, concrete shrinkage, and the dam's own weight.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchleiss2022199–229-265]

The dam location should be chosen so the reservoir will be sufficiently large to meet project requirements. The location should also ensure that the ground is strong enough to support the forces that the dam structure and reservoir water will impose.[^cite_note-266] The site selection must also consider [seismic factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seismology): when [fault lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fault_lines) are discovered under or near the dam, designers must determine if they pose a risk.[^cite_note-267][^cite_note-269]

Selecting the structural type for a dam requires evaluation of a variety of factors.[^cite_note-ProsCons-270] If the dam is placed in a narrow valley, a gravity dam or arch dam may be most appropriate, especially if a tall dam is required. However, an arch dam can only be used if the walls of the valley are strong enough to support the large forces that the sides of the arch will impose.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui2009132-271] A gravity dam structure is only feasible if the ground under the dam is strong bedrock.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui2009131–132-272] Most gravity dams and arch dams are made of concrete, which is generally more expensive than earth or rock, and may influence the design choice.[^cite_note-273] If the dam must span a wide valley, an embankment dam is often the best choice. Rockfill embankment dams are appropriate if rock is plentiful near the site, and an earthfill embankment dam may be used when rock is not available.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui2009131–132-272] For any embankment dam, an ideal site will be near impervious materials – such as clay – which can be used as a core layer within the dam.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui2009131–132-272]

The following table lists some factors that designers consider when selecting a structural type.[^cite_note-ProsCons-270]

| Structure | Advantages | Characteristics and drawbacks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Embankment dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embankment_dam) | • Dam can be built on sand or dirt because its weight is spread over a large area  •  Little excavation required  • Low sensitivity to earthquakes  • Settlement of ground beneath dam is acceptable  •  Materials for dam may be found locally | • Appropriate for: low-to-medium height dams in a wide, low valley without bedrock  • Large amount of material required  •  Separate spillway must be built |
| [Gravity dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_dam) | • Minimal stress within concrete  • Integrated spillway possible  •  Handles temperature fluctuations well | • Appropriate for: dams of any height, in a narrow or wide valley  • Requires bedrock foundation  • Requires a large amount of expensive concrete  • Sensitive to earthquakes and ground settlement  • Refrigeration may be required while curing  •  Large amounts of excavation |
| [Buttress dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buttress_dam) | • Uses less concrete and has fewer cooling issues (vs gravity dams)  •  Minimal ground settlement issues  • Integrated spillway possible | • Appropriate for: low-to-medium height dams on bedrock  • High sensitivity to earthquakes  • Face of dam sensitive to temperature changes  •  Large amount of excavation  • More formwork and labor (vs gravity dams) |
| [Arch dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arch_dam) | • Little concrete required  • Less excavation needed  • Earthquake tolerant  • Few concerns with ground settlement | • Appropriate for: tall dams in a narrow valley that has strong, steep walls and can accommodate a separate spillway   • High stress within concrete  • Complex and risky abutment where dam meets valley walls  • Spillway placement may be difficult |

### Aesthetics

A bridge crossing a river in the middle of a city.

[Khaju weir and bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khaju_Bridge) in Iran is noted for its beauty.

[^cite_note-274]

A dam's appearance can be a factor when evaluating potential designs.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETurpin2008109,_114–116,_120–122-275] An early advocate for aesthetically pleasing dams was British architect [Charles Fowler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Fowler), who gave a speech in 1929 that identified [Roosevelt Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roosevelt_Dam) and [O'Shaughnessy Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O'Shaughnessy_Dam_\(California\)) as examples of beautiful dams.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETurpin2008112–114-276][^cite_note-277] Fowler asserted that dams with some curvature, particularly arch dams, tend to be perceived as more attractive than those designed with entirely straight lines.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETurpin2008106,_112–113-278] The Swiss [civil engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_engineer) [Nicholas Schnitter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Schnitter?action=edit\&redlink=1) observed that – although attractive dams are desirable – beauty is a matter of taste, making it difficult to determine if a particular plan will improve the pre-dam landscape.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETurpin2008106–107,_138-279] After WWII, the popularity of concrete as a building material gave dam designers more flexibility to create pleasing dam shapes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETurpin2008110–111-280]

In addition to [aesthetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetics), dams can also serve as symbolic monuments that provide a sense of civic pride and [cultural identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_identity).[^cite_note-pride-281] Notable examples include Hoover Dam (US, 1936), [Bratsk Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bratsk_Hydroelectric_Power_Station) (USSR, 1967), Three Gorges Dam (China, 2003), and Aswan High Dam (Egypt, 2020).[^cite_note-pride-281]

## Auxiliary structures

### Power plants

Main article: [Hydroelectricity](381399.md)

Hydropower dams

A diagram showing a hydropower plant integrated with a dam.

In this typical [hydroelectricity](381399.md) installation of a dam, shown in cross-section, the water flows from left to right.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBreeze2014159–162-282]

A large concrete dam with a reservoir behind it.

The [generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_generator) of [this dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shasta_Dam) are in the low, horizontal building lower-left. Five [penstocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penstocks) feed water from the reservoir into the generators.

Many dams include [power plants](212141.md) that run water through a [turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_turbine) connected to a [generator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_generator) to produce electricity.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBreeze2014153–162-283][^cite_note-285] The generator is typically located near the bottom of the dam, enclosed in a [powerhouse](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/powerhouse).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBreeze2014159–162-282] Some powerhouses are located *inside* the dam structure – often in hollow gravity dams – particularly when no land is available downstream.[^cite_note-286]

Designing a hydropower facility for a particular dam requires analysis of the amount of electricity desired, the amount of water available to feed into the turbine, and the height of the upstream water level above the generator (this height is called the "[head](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulic_head)"). These factors determine which turbine style is optimal, such as [Francis turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_turbine), [Pelton turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelton_turbine), or [Kaplan turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaplan_turbine).[^cite_note-287]

Water is guided to the generator from upstream (often from a reservoir) via a passage – called the [penstock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penstock) – that feeds the water into the generator, which uses the force of the water to rotate a [turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_turbine) and generate electricity.[^cite_note-Penstock-288][^cite_note-289]

### Spillways and gates

Main articles: [Spillway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spillway) and [Floodgate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floodgate)

A broad, gently sloping dam made of rocks impounds a lake. Water is spilling downhill next to the dam, within a concrete channel.

The [Oroville embankment dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oroville_Dam) in the US is the large rock structure in the middle of the image. Overflow water from the reservoir is cascading down the [spillway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spillway) on the left side.&#x20;

A large concrete dam holds back a reservoir.

The spillway of [Grand Coulee Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Coulee_Dam) is integrated into the main structure of the dam.

Many dam projects include spillways, which provide a controlled release of excess water from the reservoir into the river downstream, preventing the dam from overflowing and possibly failing.[^cite_note-290]

Unusually heavy rainfall upstream might cause the reservoir to overflow, which could lead to significant damage to the dam structure or even total failure. To mitigate that risk, spillways must be large enough to safely transfer the overflow downstream. Dam designers must perform a detailed analysis of the variability of the region's rainfall and flooding, and use that data to design the spillway's capacity to handle a specific maximum flood. For small dams, spillways are typically designed to safely handle the largest flood expected to occur once in 100 or 500 years. Large dams are typically required to handle the largest flood expected to occur once in 10,000 years.[^cite_note-FloodMgmt-115]

There are several ways to integrate spillways into a dam project. Concrete gravity dams may position the spillway directly on the dam structure. Some dams locate the spillway at a low point (saddle) of the ridge surrounding the reservoir. These saddle spillways convey the water via a [channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chute_\(gravity\)) or tunnel to discharge downstream of the dam. An unusual spillway design is the [bell-mouth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spillway#Bell-mouth_spillway),[^cite_note-291] which is a vertical shaft in the interior of the reservoir that leads to a tunnel discharging downstream.[^cite_note-292]

To operate effectively, a spillway must be carefully designed, usually with a [parabolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabola) profile at the top.[^cite_note-293] The bottom of the spillway requires an energy-dissipation feature to slow the water before it discharges into the river, thus minimizing damage from erosion.[^cite_note-294] Some spillways use an [ogee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogee) shape: the spillway starts horizontally at the dam top, becomes steeply inclined in the middle, then curves horizontally at the bottom (ensuring that water is directed away from the dam structure to minimize damage).[^cite_note-295]

Many dams include [gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floodgate) – usually positioned at the top of the spillway – to regulate the water level in the reservoir and control the rate at which overflow water is released downstream. Types of gates include [vertical lift gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_lift_gate?action=edit\&redlink=1), [drum gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drum_gate?action=edit\&redlink=1), and [radial gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radial_gate).[^cite_note-296][^cite_note-297]

### Outlets

Dam outlets are structures – usually placed in the lower part of the dam structure – which permit the reservoir to be partially drained. The water level in a reservoir may be lowered to perform maintenance, purge sediment from the floor of the reservoir, generate hydropower, increase the water flow downstream in the dry season, or reduce stress on the dam structure in an emergency situation.[^cite_note-298] Some dam projects create tunnels early in the dam construction process to divert river water around the construction site while the dam is being constructed. Those tunnels are sometimes converted to outlets after the dam is completed.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter1994225-299]

### Locks and fish bypasses

Main articles: [Lock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lock_\(water_navigation\)) and [Fish bypass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish_bypass)

Auxiliary structures

A low dam crosses a river. At one side: a rectangular pen holds barges.

A [tugboat](216434.md) is pushing [barges](4177.md) into a [lock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lock_\(water_navigation\)) integrated into [this dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lock_and_Dam_No._7).

Water is cascading down a series of about 60 concrete steps, built into the side of a sloped concrete wall.

This [fish ladder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish_ladder) (diagonal steps) enables [migratory fish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish_migration) to bypass the Wachino Dam in Japan.

If a planned dam would obstruct a [navigable river](174851.md), [locks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lock_\(water_navigation\)) may be incorporated into the project to enable boats to pass the dam in both directions. Locks consist of one or more rectangular chambers with large gates at both ends and [valves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valve) that permit each chamber to be filled and emptied.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"Navigation_Locks",_''USACE''1.1,_3.1–3.10,_4.1–4.4,_5.1–5.13-300]

Some rivers are important [migration routes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish_migration) for fish. Construction of a dam on such a river could severely affect fish movement and breeding. These ecological impacts can be mitigated by including a fish bypass facility in the dam project. Fish bypass designs include [fish ladders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish_ladder), [fish lifts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish_lift), and artificial streams that mimic a natural river.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPelikan199761–66-301]

## Construction

After the design process is complete and the site and structural type are selected, construction may begin. The construction process typically involves diverting the river around the site, excavating the foundation, grouting the underlying rock formations to improve stability and reduce seepage, and placing construction materials to form the dam structure.[^cite_note-302]

### Cofferdams and diversion of river

An aerial photograph of a river, with a large steel wall keeping the water out of a construction site.

The three-sided barrier projecting into the river is a [cofferdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cofferdam), protecting a [dam construction site.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olmsted_Locks_and_Dam)&#x20;

Building a large, permanent dam requires a dry construction site. For dams built across a valley, the river's flow must be temporarily diverted around the construction site.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui2009239–240-303] Diversion can be accomplished by building a tunnel or channel around or beneath the dam site, and creating a [cofferdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cofferdam) to direct the river into the diversion route.[^cite_note-304] After the dam is completed, cofferdams are usually removed, but for some embankment dams they are incorporated into the final dam.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui2009241,_244-305] Diversion channels and tunnels are typically plugged up or demolished; although some dam projects retain tunnels or channels as part of the permanent dam project, for example, as an outlet or a spillway.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui2009241,_244-305]

Another diversion technique is to build a semicircular cofferdam on one side of the valley, which forces the river to the other side. After half the dam is built within the cofferdam, the cofferdam is moved to the other half of the valley, and the river is directed to the first part of the dam (which has passages to let the water through). The second half of the dam is built within the cofferdam, and the cofferdam is removed.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui2009240–241-306]

For some embankment dam projects, temporary cofferdams and diversion channels may be avoided by channeling the river into a narrow course in the center of the valley and building the dam inward from both sides of the valley, leaving a gap in the middle for the river to flow through. In the dry season – when the flow of the river is relatively low – the central segment is rapidly closed, while the water simultaneously rises in the reservoir behind the dam.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui2009241–242-307]

### Preparation, grouting, and drainage system

Two workers are looking at a piece of heavy machinery that is inserting a large drill downward into the earth.

Workers are drilling holes in [a dam site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willow_Creek_Dam_\(Montana\)). [Grout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grout) will be injected into the holes to strengthen the ground and reduce water seepage.

An early step in the construction process is preparing the [foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundation_\(engineering\)), which is the rock upon which the dam structure will rest. Concrete dams – gravity dams, arch dams, and buttress dams – are very heavy and must rest on solid [bedrock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bedrock). Any soil, gravel, loose rock, or poor-quality rock must be removed to expose bedrock before the dam is built. Embankment dams have wide bases, and do not subject the ground to as much pressure as heavy dams; as a result, they often may be built on loose rocks or soil.[^cite_note-308]

A major risk in any dam project is water seeping under the dam or around its sides. To mitigate that risk, [grout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grout) is injected – before the dam is built – into the rocks under the dam and into the valley walls on either side.[^cite_note-309] Two types of grouting processes are used: [consolidation grouting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consolidation_grouting?action=edit\&redlink=1) locates rocks below and to the sides of the dam that may have cracks or defects, and injects grout under high pressure at those locations, filling cracks and strengthening the rocks.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui2009158–159-310] The other technique is [curtain grouting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtain_grouting), which injects grout deep into the rock through [boreholes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boreholes) drilled in a pattern arranged to create a solid wall of grout below and to the sides of the dam. The depth of the grout curtain may be 30% to 70% of the planned depth of the water behind the dam.[^cite_note-311]

Another approach to mitigate seepage is a drainage system, which aims to reduce the risk of "piping" (water inside the dam eroding the dam structure) or uplift (water under the dam pushing the dam upward). The drainage system collects water from inside or under the dam and carries it downstream. The drainage may consist of pipes, or a crushed rock "blanket" under the dam.[^cite_note-312] Concrete dams may have passages inside the dam structure to collect water and carry it away; these passages are called "galleries" (horizontal) or "shafts" (vertical).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui2009139–140-313][^cite_note-galleries-42]

### Building the dam

&#x20;A busy construction site in the mountains has a large network of conveyor belts.

&#x20;Concrete for this dam (background) is produced by a [concrete plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concrete_plant) (center of image, covered by corrugated metal roof). [Conveyor belts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conveyor_belt) move rocks and concrete around the site.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchrader1988553–554,_556,_560–566-314]

Two vehicles are resting on top of freshly poured, wet concrete. One of them is inserting cylindrical rods into the concrete.

The machine on the left is [vibrating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vibrator_\(mechanical\)) the [roller compacted concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roller_compacted_concrete) of this dam in Turkey.

Embankment dams and concrete dams use distinct construction methods. Embankment dams require vast amounts of soil and rock, which are usually excavated from "borrow areas" near the dam site. The soil and rocks are laid down in successive layers called lifts.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFetzer1988342–350-315] After laying down a layer, it is [compacted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_compaction) with [heavy machinery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compactor).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFetzer1988344–350-316] The layers must be carefully monitored to ensure that they contain the correct materials, are not overly wet, and are sufficiently compacted. Instruments are embedded within the dam as it is built, and are continually monitored so any defects can be quickly corrected.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFetzer1988349–352-317]

Concrete dams require a [concrete plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concrete_plant) to be built near the dam site. The plant combines [aggregate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Construction_aggregate) (rocks), [cement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cement), [fly ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fly_ash), and water to produce concrete.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchrader1988553–554,_556,_566-318] The concrete is delivered from the concrete plant to the dam structure by means of conveyor belts, [concrete buckets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bucket_\(machine_part\)#Concrete_bucket), dump trucks, or cranes. [Formwork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formwork) is built at the dam location to contain the concrete when it is placed.[^cite_note-concreteConstr-319] After it is placed into the dam structure, the concrete must be [vibrated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vibrator_\(mechanical\)) to eliminate any bubbles or air pockets.[^cite_note-concreteConstr-319] The dam is gradually built up by pouring individual "blocks" of concrete; each block is typically 1.5 to 3 m (5 to 10 ft) high, and 12 to 30 m (39 to 98 ft) wide and deep.[^cite_note-block-320] Concrete of such thickness – called [mass concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_concrete) – contracts and generates a large amount of heat as it [cures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concrete#Curing), which can lead to cracks.[^cite_note-321] To mitigate this issue, [expansion joints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expansion_joint) can be included within the dam to permit the concrete to shrink without cracking. After the heat dissipates, the expansion joints are filled with grout, and their upstream edges are sealed with strips of metal, rubber, or plastic.[^cite_note-322] Additionally, refrigeration systems may be employed that circulate coolant through the concrete by means of pipes.[^cite_note-323]

A recent innovation is [roller compacted concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roller_compacted_concrete) (RCC), which has several benefits over conventional concrete.[^cite_note-325] RCC uses less cement, permits the use of aggregate up to 100 mm in size, and does not generate as much heat as conventional concrete. RCC also permits [tracked bulldozers](313830.md) to drive on it immediately after placement, and it reduces construction costs because less formwork and labor are required.[^cite_note-326]

## Operation

### Management processes

A room with a few people seated at several tables. The tables contain a large number of knobs and buttons. The walls have large screens displaying numbers.

&#x20;[Control room](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_room) of [Itaipu Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Itaipu_Dam) on the border of Brazil and Paraguay

After a dam is completed and becomes operational, [management processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Management_process) are employed to ensure it continues to fulfill its purposes (irrigation, hydropower, etc.), avoids safety incidents, and achieves its intended lifespan. Management processes include performing maintenance and repairs, testing and inspecting facilities, [keeping records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Records_management), and [planning for emergencies](1455589.md).[^cite_note-327]

### Inspection and monitoring

&#x20;A long concrete hallway, containing a ladder and some large steel structures.

&#x20;The interiors of some dam structures contain [galleries](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/gallery) (long, horizontal passages) where operators can inspect for leaks and check monitoring devices.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui2009139–140-313]

An essential task of dam operators is surveilling the dam to identify potential safety issues.[^cite_note-328] These inspections monitor stresses acting on the dam, including:[^cite_note-Instrumentation-329]

- The reservoir's contents (water, ice, waves, and sediment) acting on the dam structure

- Earthquakes

- Weight of the dam and reservoir compressing the foundation or abutments

- Movements caused by settling of the ground beneath the dam

- Internal compression and tension stresses within the structure

- Expansion and contraction due to temperature fluctuations

These stresses can damage a dam by bending, lifting, expanding, shrinking, or shifting the structure.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchleiss2022666–667-330] To detect stresses, permanent instruments are placed within and around the dam. Instruments include [tiltmeters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiltmeter), [joint meters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_meter?action=edit\&redlink=1), [strain meters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strain_gauge), [deflectometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deflectometer), [thermometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermometer), [deformation meters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_monitoring), and [piezometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piezometer).[^cite_note-Instrumentation-329] Dam personnel monitor these sensors, and if irregular data are reported, they investigate the underlying cause, and implement necessary repairs or mitigations.[^cite_note-331]

### Sedimentation of reservoir

Most reservoirs gradually accumulate [sediment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sediment), reducing the amount of water that the reservoir can hold. When the storage capacity is reduced, the dam's ability to perform its intended purposes (irrigation, hydropower, water supply, flood control, etc.) is reduced accordingly.[^cite_note-333] Sediment enters a reservoir as soil suspended in river water; as the river empties into the reservoir, the water velocity decreases, and the sediment settles at the bottom of the reservoir.[^cite_note-334] Sediment can also enter from wind-blown soil, landslides, construction near the water, and erosion from irrigation or rainfall.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui2009270-335] Roughly half of the world's river sediment is trapped by dams – about 8 to 16 km<sup>3</sup> per year.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELemperiere20061068-332] To mitigate [sedimentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedimentation), dam operators implement strategies to reduce sediment inflow.[^cite_note-336] Sedimentation can be reduced by planting vegetation in the reservoir's [drainage basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage_basin), or by building [terraces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrace_\(earthworks\)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESiddiqui2009271–275-337]

### Dam removal

Main article: [Dam removal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dam_removal)

A dam may be deliberately removed for various reasons: if it poses a safety hazard, if the dam no longer fulfills its original purpose, to restore fish migration routes, or to improve the health of downstream rivers by improving sediment flow.[^cite_note-338] When a dam is removed, [fisheries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisheries) are restored, [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water) and [sediment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sediment) flows are re-established, sediment in the [reservoir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reservoir) gradually erodes and flows downstream, the river width increases and [braiding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braided_river) is more pronounced, natural water temperatures and animal [habitats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habitat) are restored.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBellmore2017-339][^cite_note-341]

Removal of [Iron Gate Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Gate_Dam_\(California\)) in the US

A rock dam is in a valley, with a reservoir behind it.

In 2009, before removal

A rocky valley with some construction equipment at the bottom.

In 2024, after removal

In the US, rivers and streams are obstructed by over 800,000 dams and barriers.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENiranjan2026-342] Over 1,200 dams have been removed as of 2016, with over 600 removed between 1996 and 2016.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBellmore2017-339] Between 2014 and 2018, two dams – [Elwha Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elwha_Dam) and [Glines Canyon Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glines_Canyon_Dam) – were [removed from the Elwha River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restoration_of_the_Elwha_River) in the US.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERitchie2018-343] The dam removal restored delivery of [sediment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sediment) and wood to the downstream river, and the river delta was re-established.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERitchie2018-343] A group of four dams – including [Iron Gate Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Gate_Dam_\(California\)) – was removed from the [Klamath River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klamath_River) in the US between 2020 and 2024. The removal was the result of a [sustained campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Un-Dam_the_Klamath) by [Native Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_Americans) and environmentalists. One of the goals was to restore one of the largest [salmon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salmon) migration routes on the [Pacific coast](751843.md) of North America.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKrol2024-344]

In 2021, there were over 1,000,000 dams and barriers in Europe, at least 150,000 of them no longer required.[^cite_note-345] Dams and the resulting fragmentation of rivers are a major cause of a 55% decline in migratory fish populations and an 80% decline in fish biodiversity.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcSweeney2023-346] In 2024, the [European Union](9317.md) passed the [Nature Restoration Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_Restoration_Law) which encourages the removal of unneeded dams.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENiranjan2026-342][^cite_note-347] The [Dam Removal Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dam_Removal_Europe?action=edit\&redlink=1) organization seeks to identify dams for removal and facilitate the process.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcSweeney2023-346][^cite_note-348] In 2025, over 600 dams were removed in Europe, restoring 3,740 km (2,320 mi) of rivers and streams.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENiranjan2026-342]

## Dam failure

Main article: [Dam failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dam_failure)

&#x20;The remains of a large dam, which has been cracked and broken by some event.

The concrete arch [Malpasset Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malpasset_Dam) failed in 1959 in France.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJames1988a17,_25–27-349]

&#x20;Water is rapidly pouring out of a reservoir through a gap in a huge earthen dam.

The earthen embankment [Teton Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teton_Dam) failed in the US in 1976 due to piping.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJames1988a34–41-350]

Many principles governing the design of safe dams have been developed based on [lessons learned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lessons_learned) from dam failures.[^cite_note-351] Dams can fail for many reasons. Arch dam failures can result from rock weakness at the abutments (where the sides of the dam press into the valley walls), erosion of the foundation under the dam, or shearing (slipping) where the dam meets rock. Gravity dams and buttress dams can fail due to overturning, sliding, cracking, or earthquakes.[^cite_note-352] A risk unique to earthen embankment dams is "piping": a small leak through or under the [permeable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permeability_of_soils) dam structure gradually erodes the soil, until a small channel is formed, which – if untreated – may lead to dam failure.[^cite_note-Piping-353][^cite_note-354]

A large number of dams failed during the 19th and early 20th centuries, particularly embankment dams.[^cite_note-355] Increased emphasis on dam safety caused failure rates to decrease dramatically throughout the 20th century.[^cite_note-357] Of the dams over 15 m (49 ft) high built after 1900, only 100 had failed by 1994.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter1994158–163,_229–231-358] Although the failure rate decreased, the potential for downstream damage rose due to increasing dam heights and reservoir capacities.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchnitter1994158–163,_229–231-358]

An early dam failure occurred around 2600 BCE, when Egypt's [Sadd el-Kafara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sadd_el-Kafara) dam collapsed near completion during a severe flood.[^cite_note-Sadd-140][^cite_note-359] A modern arch dam failure was [Malpasset Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malpasset_Dam) in France. Its failure in 1959 was not due to the concrete dam's shape or strength, but rather to the presence of an underground [slip line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fault_\(geology\)#Slip,_heave,_throw) which moved due to the combined weight of the dam and the reservoir water. The movement caused the dam to crack, and the resulting flood killed several hundred people downstream.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJames1988a17–27-360]

A dam failure caused by insufficient site investigation was [Vajont Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vajont_Dam) in Italy. This concrete arch dam was built in a valley that had steep sides which were prone to landslides. Designers failed to fully understand the geology of the valley and built the dam in 1959 – then the [tallest dam in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_dams) at 267 m (876 ft). In 1963, a huge landslide slid down the hillside above the reservoir, displacing nearly all the water in the reservoir, and causing a 125 m (410 ft) tall wave to overtop the dam. The resulting flood killed more than 2,000 people downstream. The dam structure itself suffered only minor damage.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJames1988a41–53-361]

In some cases, it is not possible to determine the cause of the failure. In 1963, [Baldwin Hills embankment dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baldwin_Hills_Dam_disaster) in the US failed, but – despite extensive investigations – a definitive cause was not found. Potential factors included: weak foundation soil that subsided; irregular settling of the dam structure; fault lines under the dam; a nearby oil field that was depleted of oil, then later re-pressurized; and the act of emptying and filling the reservoir.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJames1988a8–16-362] One of the deadliest dam failure incidents was the [Banqiao and Shimantan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1975_Banqiao_Dam_failure) multi-dam failure in China in 1975. [Typhoon Nina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhoon_Nina_\(1975\)) caused extensive flooding in [Henan province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henan_province), leading to the collapse of several dams in the same river system, including the [Banqiao Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banqiao_Dam) and [Shimantan Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shimantan_Dam). The total number of deaths is estimated between 171,000 and 230,000.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"Dam_Failure_Compilation",_''Bureau_of_Reclamation''114–119,_B.1–B.4-363]

## Society and culture

### Wartime targets

Dams have been targeted during wartime, with 20 documented attacks on dams between 1917 and 1993. The [Geneva Conventions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geneva_Conventions) were amended in 1949 to prohibit attacks on dams if they would cause "severe losses among the civilian population".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchleiss202297–98-364][^cite_note-366]

### Profession and regulation

Most countries with large dams have [statutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statutes) or regulations governing dam construction and inspection. The regulations vary widely among nations. Some countries have a government agency responsible for inspecting dams, but many do not.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"Regulation_of_Dam_Safety",_''ICOLD''10–11-367][^cite_note-369] The regulations of most nations typically do not specify particular dam design parameters, but instead require compliance with "recognized rules of technology" or "state of the art in science and technology".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"Regulation_of_Dam_Safety",_''ICOLD''14-370] Some countries regulate dams at a federal level, but others regulate at a province/state level.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"Regulation_of_Dam_Safety",_''ICOLD''12-371] For example, Germany has no federal regulations; instead, each state has its own statutes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"Regulation_of_Dam_Safety",_''ICOLD''41–44-372]

### Art and culture

A postage stamp shows a man and woman pushing a heavy object, with the image of a large dam in the background.

&#x20;[Dnieper dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dnieper_dam) is shown on this 1968 Soviet stamp.

Dams are featured in novels, movies, documentaries, songs, [postage stamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postage_stamp), [popular histories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_history), and political posters. In the mid-20th century, [Soviet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) artists such as [Isaak Brodsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaak_Brodsky) and [Gustav Klutsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Klutsis) highlighted large dams in artworks intended to glorify workers and industry. Their works portrayed [Mingachevir Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mingachevir_Dam), [Kayrakkum Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kayrakkum_Dam), [Bratsk Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bratsk_Dam), and [Dnieper Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dnieper_Hydroelectric_Station).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFowkes2025-373]

The [docudrama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Docudrama) film *[The Dam Busters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dam_Busters_\(film\))* (1955) portrayed a WWII [military operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Chastise) in which [Britain's RAF](25679.md) successfully bombed [Möhne Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%B6hne_Reservoir) in Germany, causing the deaths of about 1,600 civilians.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"Bombs_Away",_''The_Guardian''-374] The [Patagonia company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patagonia_\(company\)) produced the documentary *[DamNation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DamNation)* (2014) which advocates for dam removal to restore ecosystems and fish populations.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHorn2014-375]

Books about dams include *[The Johnstown Flood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Johnstown_Flood_\(book\))* (1968) by [David McCullough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_McCullough) – a popular history of [an 1889 dam failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnstown_Flood) and subsequent flood in the US.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESutor2018-376] [Edward Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Abbey) wrote the novel *[The Monkey Wrench Gang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Monkey_Wrench_Gang)* (1975) about environmental activists who sought to destroy [Glen Canyon Dam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glen_Canyon_Dam).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELichtenstein1976-377]
