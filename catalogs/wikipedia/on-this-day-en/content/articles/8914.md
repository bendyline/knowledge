**Damascus**[^cite_note-5] is the capital and largest city of [Syria](7515849.md).[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7] It is the oldest capital city in the world. Known colloquially in Syria as *aš-Šām*[^cite_note-8] and dubbed, poetically, the "City of [Jasmine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jasmine)" (مَدِيْنَةُ الْيَاسْمِينِ, *Madīnat al-Yāsmīn*),[^cite_note-worldatlas-9] Damascus is a major cultural center of the [Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant) and the [Arab world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_world).

Situated in southwestern Syria, Damascus is the center of a large metropolitan area. Nestled among the eastern foothills of the [Anti-Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Lebanon) mountain range 80 kilometres (50 mi) inland from the eastern shore of the Mediterranean on a plateau 680 metres (2,230 ft) [above sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Above_sea_level), Damascus experiences an arid climate because of the [rain shadow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rain_shadow) effect. The [Barada River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barada) flows through Damascus.

Damascus is one of the [oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_oldest_continuously_inhabited_cities).[^cite_note-10] First settled in the 3rd millennium BC, it was chosen as the capital of the [Umayyad Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Caliphate) from 661 to 750. After the victory of the [Abbasid dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_dynasty), the seat of Islamic power was moved to [Baghdad](4492.md). According to some, Damascus is the fourth-[holiest city in Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holiest_sites_in_Islam).[^cite_note-DumperStanley2007-Damascus-11][^cite_note-Birke2013-12][^cite_note-Totah2009-13] The city saw its importance decline throughout the Abbasid era, only to regain significant importance in the [Ayyubid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayyubid_dynasty), [Mamluk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamluk_sultanate) and [Ottoman](22278.md) periods.

Today, it is the seat of the central [government of Syria](20933383.md). During the [Syrian civil war](30741795.md), Damascus was named the least livable city out of 140 global cities in the [Global Liveability Ranking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Liveability_Ranking).[^cite_note-14] As of June 2023, it was the least livable out of 173 global cities in the same Global Liveability Ranking. In 2017, two development projects were launched in Damascus to build new residential districts, [Marota City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marota_City) and Basillia City to symbolize post-war reconstruction.[^cite_note-15]

## Names and etymology

See also: [Names of Damascus in different languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_of_Damascus_in_different_languages)

### Late Bronze Age

The name of Damascus first appeared in the geographical list of [Thutmose III](155730.md) as *tjmsqw* (𓍘𓄟𓈎𓅱𓈉 and 𓍘𓄟𓊃𓐰𓈎𓅱[^cite_note-archive.org-16]) in the 15th century BC.[^cite_note-17] The [etymology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etymology) of the ancient name *tjmsqw* (T-ms-q.w) is uncertain.

A number of Akkadian spellings are found in the [Amarna letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amarna_letters), from the 14th century BC: *Dimašqa* (𒁲𒈦𒋡, 𒁲𒈦𒀸𒋡), and *Dimašqì* (𒁲𒈦𒀸𒄀).

### Iron Age

It is attested as *Ša-imerišu* (𒃻𒆳𒀲𒋙) in Neo-Assyrian [Akkadian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkadian_language).

Later [Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaic_language) *Damašq* (𐡃𐡌𐡔𐡒‎) in [Old Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Aramaic), spellings of the name often include an intrusive *[resh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resh)* (letter *r*), perhaps influenced by the root *dr*, meaning "dwelling". The [Qumranic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qumran) *Darmeśeq* (דרמשק), and *Darmsûq* (ܕܪܡܣܘܩ) in [Syriac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_language),[^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19] meaning "a well-watered land".[^cite_note-20]

In Greek mythology, there are three explanations for the city's name Damaskós (Δαμασκός). According to one, it was named for the giant [Ascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascus_\(mythology\)) (Ἄσκος). Another says it was named after Damaskos, the son of [Hermes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermes) and the nymph Alimede, who traveled from [Arcadia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcadia_\(region\)) to Syria and founded a city bearing his name. A third version holds that Damaskos was a man who, after [Dionysus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dionysus) made Syria fertile with vineyards, cut them down with an axe. Enraged, Dionysus pursued and flayed him. His original name was Darmaskos, which later evolved into Damaskos.[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-22] Thus, the English and [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) name of the city is *Damascus*, which was imported from [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) Δαμασκός.

In [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language), the city is called Dimashq (دمشق *Dimašq*).[^cite_note-23] The city is also known as *aš-Šām* by the citizens of Damascus, of Syria and other Arab neighbors and Turkey (*Şam*). *Aš-Šām* is an Arabic term for "[Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant)" and for "Syria"; the latter, and particularly the historical [region of Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria_\(region\)), is called *Bilād aš-Šām* (بلاد الشام, lit. 'land of the Levant').[^cite_note-28] The latter term etymologically means "land of the left-hand side" or "the north", as someone in the [Hijaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hijaz) facing east, oriented to the sunrise, will find the north to the left. This is contrasted with the name of [Yemen](350939.md) (اَلْيَمَن *al-Yaman*), correspondingly meaning "the right-hand side" or "the south". The variation ش ء م (*š-ʾ-m*'), of the more typical ش م ل (*š-m-l*), is also attested in [Old South Arabian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_South_Arabian), 𐩦𐩱𐩣 (*šʾm*), with the same semantic development.[^cite_note-Volume_9_1997_page_261-29][^cite_note-30] The patterned Byzantine and Chinese silks available through Damascus, one of the Western termini of the [Silk Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silk_Road), gave the English language "damask".[^cite_note-31]

## Geography

Damascus was built in a strategic site on a plateau 680 m (2,230 ft) [above sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Above_sea_level) and about 80 km (50 mi) inland from the Mediterranean, sheltered by the [Anti-Lebanon Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Lebanon_Mountains), supplied with water by the [Barada River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barada), and at a crossroads between trade routes: the north–south route connecting Egypt with [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_Minor), and the east–west cross-desert route connecting [Lebanon](17771.md) with the [Euphrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphrates) river valley. The Anti-Lebanon Mountains mark the border between Syria and Lebanon. The range has peaks of over 10,000 ft (3,000 m) and blocks precipitation from the Mediterranean Sea, so the region of Damascus is sometimes subject to droughts. However, in ancient times, the Barada River, which originates from mountain streams fed by melting snow, mitigated this. Damascus is surrounded by the [Ghouta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghouta), irrigated farmland where vegetables, cereals, and fruits have been farmed since ancient times. Maps of Roman Syria indicate that the Barada River emptied into a lake of some size east of Damascus. Today it is called Bahira Atayba, the hesitant lake, because in years of severe drought, it dries up.[^cite_note-romeartlover-32]

The modern city has an area of 105 km<sup>2</sup> (41 sq mi), out of which 77 km<sup>2</sup> (30 sq mi) is urban, while [Jabal Qasioun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jabal_Qasioun) occupies the rest.[^cite_note-33]

The old city of Damascus, enclosed by the city walls, lies on the south bank of the river [Barada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barada) which is almost dry (3 cm (1 in) left). To the southeast, north, and northeast it is surrounded by suburban areas whose history stretches back to the Middle Ages: [Midan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midan) in the southwest, [Sarouja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarouja) and Imara in the north and north-west. These neighborhoods originally arose on roads leading out of the city, near the tombs of religious figures. In the 19th century outlying villages developed on the slopes of [Jabal Qasioun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jabal_Qasioun), overlooking the city, already the site of the al-Salihiyah neighborhood centered on the important shrine of medieval [Andalusian](2736.md) Sheikh and philosopher [Ibn Arabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Arabi). These neighborhoods were initially settled by Kurdish soldiers and Muslim refugees from the [Europe](9239.md) regions of the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md) which had fallen under Christian rule. Thus they were known as *al-Akrad* *(the Kurds)* and *al-Muhajirin* *(the migrants)*. They lay 2–3 km (1–2 mi) north of the old city.

From the late 19th century on, a modern administrative and commercial center began to spring up to the west of the old city, around the Barada, centered on the area known as [al-Marjeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marjeh_Square) or "the meadow". Al-Marjeh soon became the name of what was initially the central square of modern Damascus, with the city hall in it. The courts of justice, post office, and railway station stood on higher ground slightly to the south. A Europeanized residential quarter soon began to be built on the road leading between al-Marjeh and [al-Salihiyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Salihiyah,_Damascus). The commercial and administrative center gradually shifted northwards towards this area.

In the 20th century, suburbs developed north of the Barada, and to some extent to the south. In 1956–1957, the [Yarmouk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yarmouk_\(camp\)) became home to many [Palestinian](74681.md) refugees.[^cite_note-34] City planners preferred to preserve the Ghouta as far as possible, and in the later 20th century some of the main areas of development were to the north, in the western [Mezzeh](21089456.md) neighborhood and most recently along the Barada valley in [Dummar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dummar) in the northwest and on the slopes of the mountains at [Barzeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barzeh,_Syria) in the north-east. Poorer areas, often built without official approval, have developed south of the main city.

Damascus used to be surrounded by an [oasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oasis), the Ghouta region ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language): الغوطة, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Arabic): *_al-ġūṭä_*), watered by the Barada river. The [Fijeh spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ain_al-Fijah), west along the Barada valley, provided the city with drinking water, and various sources to the west were tapped by water contractors. The flow of the Barada dropped with the rapid expansion of housing and industry, leaving it almost dry. The lower aquifers are polluted by the city's runoff from heavily used roads, industry, and sewage.

### Climate

Damascus has a [cool arid climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desert_climate#Cold_desert_climates) (*BWk*) in the [Köppen-Geiger system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen-Geiger_climate_classification_system),[^cite_note-35] due to the [rain shadow effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rain_shadow_effect) of the Anti-Lebanon Mountains[^cite_note-36] and the prevailing ocean currents. Summers are prolonged, dry, and hot with less humidity. Winters are cool and somewhat rainy; snowfall is infrequent. Autumn is brief and mild, but has the most drastic temperature change, unlike spring where the transition to summer is more gradual and steady. Annual rainfall is around 130 mm (5 in), occurring from October to May.

## History

For a chronological guide, see [Timeline of Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_Damascus).

### Early settlement

[Carbon-14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon-14) dating at [Tell Ramad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tell_Ramad), on Damascus's outskirts, suggests that the site may have been occupied since the second half of the seventh millennium BC, possibly around 6300 BC.[^cite_note-39] But evidence of settlement in the wider Barada basin dating to 9000 BC exists, although no large-scale settlement was present within Damascus's walls until the second millennium BC.[^cite_note-Burns2-40]

### Late Bronze Age

#### Late Bronze I

In the early [18th dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/18th_dynasty), during the reign of Thutmose III of Egypt he mentions Damascus. The name of Damascus appeared in his geographical listas *tjmsqw* (𓍘𓄟𓈎𓅱𓈉 and 𓍘𓄟𓊃𓐰𓈎𓅱[^cite_note-archive.org-16]) in the 15th century BC.[^cite_note-41]

#### Late Bronze II

During the late [18th dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/18th_dynasty), the [Amarna letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amarna_letters) (c. 1350 BC) mentions Damascus (*Dimasqu*) as ruled by [Biryawaza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biryawaza).

During the [19th dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/19th_dynasty) (c. 1260 BC), the Damascus region and the rest of Syria became a battleground between the [Hittites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hittites) from the north and the [Egyptians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egyptians) from the south,[^cite_note-Burns5-42] ending with a signed treaty between [Hattusili III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hattusili_III) and [Ramesses II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramesses_II). The former handed over control of the Damascus area to the latter in 1259 BC.[^cite_note-Burns5-42]

The [20th dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/20th_dynasty) lost control over the Southern Levant. The arrival of the [Sea Peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_Peoples) around 1200 BC marked the end of the [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age) in the region and brought about new development of warfare.[^cite_note-Burns7-43] Damascus was only a peripheral part of this picture, which mostly affected the larger population centers of ancient Syria. But these events contributed to Damascus's emergence as an influential center during the transition from the Bronze Age to the [Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age).[^cite_note-Burns7-43]

Damascus is mentioned in [Genesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Genesis) 14\:15 as existing at the time of the [War of the Kings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chedorlaomer).[^cite_note-44] According to the 1st-century Jewish historian [Flavius Josephus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flavius_Josephus)'s *[Antiquities of the Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiquities_of_the_Jews)*, Damascus (along with [Trachonitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trachonitis)) was founded by [Uz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uz_\(son_of_Aram\)), the son of [Aram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aram,_son_of_Shem).[^cite_note-45] In Antiquities i. 7,[^cite_note-46] Josephus reports:

> [Nicholas of Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaus_of_Damascus), in the fourth book of his History, says thus: "[Abraham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham) reigned at Damascus, being a foreigner, who came with an army out of the land above [Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylon), called the land of the Chaldeans: but, after a long time, he got him up, and removed from that country also, with his people, and went into the land then called the land of [Canaan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canaan), but now the land of Judea, and this when his posterity became a multitude; as to which posterity of his, we relate their history in another work. Now the name of Abraham is even still famous in the country of Damascus; and there is a village named after him, The Habitation of Abraham.

### Iron Age

#### Aram-Damascus

[^cite_note-47]

Damascus is first documented as an important city during the arrival of the [Aramaeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaeans), a [Semitic people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitic_people), in the 11th century BC. By the start of the first millennium BC, several Aramaic kingdoms formed, as Aramaeans abandoned their nomadic lifestyle and formed federated tribal states. One of these was [Aram-Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aram-Damascus), centered on its capital, Damascus.[^cite_note-Burns9-48] The Aramaeans who entered the city without battle adopted the name "Dimashqu" for their new home. Noticing the agricultural potential of the still undeveloped and sparsely populated area,[^cite_note-Burns10-49] they established a water distribution system by constructing canals and tunnels that maximized the efficiency of the river Barada. The Romans and the Umayyads later improved the network, which still forms the basis of the water system of the old part of the city.[^cite_note-Burns13-50] The Aramaeans initially turned Damascus into an outpost of a loose federation of Aramaean tribes, known as [Aram-Zobah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zobah), based in the [Beqaa Valley](2162385.md).[^cite_note-Burns10-49]

The city gained preeminence in southern Syria when [Ezron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rezon_the_Syrian), the claimant to Aram-Zobah's throne who was denied kingship of the federation, fled Beqaa and captured Damascus by force in 965 BC. He overthrew the city's tribal governor and founded the independent entity of Aram-Damascus. As this state expanded south, it prevented the [Kingdom of Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Israel_\(united_monarchy\)) from spreading north and the two kingdoms soon clashed as they both sought to dominate trading hegemony in the east.[^cite_note-Burns10-49] Under Ezron's grandson, [Ben-Hadad I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben-Hadad_I) (880–841 BC), and his successor [Hazael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazael), Damascus annexed [Bashan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bashan) (modern-day [Hauran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hauran) region), and went on the offensive with Israel. This conflict continued until the early 8th century BC, when [Ben-Hadad II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben-Hadad_II) was captured by Israel after unsuccessfully besieging [Samaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sebastia,_Nablus). As a result, he granted Israel trading rights in Damascus.[^cite_note-Burns11-51]

Another possible reason for the treaty between Aram-Damascus and Israel was the common threat of the [Neo-Assyrian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Assyrian_Empire), which was trying to expand into the Mediterranean coast. In 853 BC, King [Hadadezer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadadezer) of Damascus led a [Levantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant) coalition that included forces from the northern Aram-Hamath kingdom and troops supplied by [King Ahab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Ahab) of Israel in the [Battle of Qarqar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Qarqar) against the Neo-Assyrian army. Aram-Damascus was victorious, temporarily preventing the Assyrians from encroaching into Syria. But after Hadadzezer was killed by his successor, Hazael, the Levantine alliance collapsed. Aram-Damascus tried to invade Israel but was interrupted by the renewed Assyrian invasion. Hazael ordered a retreat to the walled part of Damascus while the Assyrians plundered the remainder of the kingdom. Unable to enter the city, they declared their supremacy in the Hauran and Beqa'a valleys.[^cite_note-Burns11-51]

By the 8th century BC, Damascus was practically engulfed by the Assyrians and entered a Dark Age. Nonetheless, it remained the economic and cultural center of the Near East as well as the Arameaen resistance. In 727, a revolt took place in the city but was put down by Assyrian forces. After Assyria led by [Tiglath-Pileser III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiglath-Pileser_III) went on a wide-scale campaign of quelling revolts throughout Syria, Damascus became subjugated by their rule. One effect of this was stability for the city and benefits from the spice and incense trade with [Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabia). In 694 BC, the town was called *Šaʾimerišu* (Akkadian: 𒐼𒄿𒈨𒊑𒋙𒌋) and its governor was named *Ilu-issīya*.[^cite_note-52] Assyrian authority dwindled by 609–605 BC, and Syria-Palestine fell into the orbit of Pharaoh [Necho II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necho_II)'s Egypt. By 572 BC, [Nebuchadnezzar II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebuchadnezzar_II) of the [Neo-Babylonians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Babylonian) had conquered all of Syria, but Damascus's status under [Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylon) is relatively unknown.[^cite_note-Burns2123-53]

### Hellenistic period

Damascus was conquered by [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great). After his death in 323 BC, it became the site of a struggle between the [Seleucid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucid_Empire) and [Ptolemaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_dynasty) empires. Control of the city passed frequently from one empire to another. [Seleucus I Nicator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucus_I_Nicator), one of Alexander's generals, made [Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antioch) the capital of his empire, which led to the decline of Damascus's importance compared with new Seleucid cities such as [Syrian Laodicea](195594.md) in the north. Later, [Demetrius III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demetrius_III_Eucaerus) Philopator rebuilt the city according to the Greek [hippodamian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippodamian) system and renamed it "Demetrias".[^cite_note-54]

### Roman period



Ruins of the [Jupiter Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Jupiter,_Damascus) at the entrance of [Al-Hamidiyah Souq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Hamidiyah_Souq)

In 64 BC, the [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome) general [Pompey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompey) annexed the western part of Syria. The Romans occupied Damascus and incorporated it into the league of ten cities known as the [Decapolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decapolis)[^cite_note-55] which itself was incorporated into the province of Syria and granted autonomy.[^cite_note-56]

Damascus was entirely redesigned by the Romans after [Pompey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompey) conquered the region. The Old City of Damascus retains the rectangular shape of the Roman city, with its two main axes: the Decumanus Maximus (east-west; known today as the *Via Recta*) and the Cardo (north-south), the Decumanus being about twice as long. The Romans built a monumental gate at the eastern end of Decumanus Maximus. The gate originally had three arches, with the central arch for chariots and others for pedestrians.[^cite_note-romeartlover-32]



Remnants of ancient Damascus

In 23 BC, [Caesar Augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus) gave [Herod the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herod_the_Great) lands controlled by [Zenodorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zenodorus_son_of_Lysanias),[^cite_note-57] and some scholars believe Herod was also granted control of Damascus.[^cite_note-58] Either control of Damascus reverted to Syria upon Herod the Great's death or it was part of the lands given to Herod Philip that were given to Syria with his death in 33/34 AD.

It is speculated that control of Damascus was gained by [Aretas IV Philopatris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aretas_IV_Philopatris) of [Nabatea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabataean_kingdom) between the death of [Herod Philip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herod_Philip_II.) in 33/34 AD and the death of Aretas in 40 AD, but there is substantial evidence that Aretas did not control the city before 37 AD and there are many reasons it could not have been a gift from [Caligula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caligula) between 37 and 40 AD.[^cite_note-59][^cite_note-60] In fact, all these theories stem not from any actual evidence outside the New Testament but rather "a certain understanding of 2 Corinthians 11\:32" and in reality "neither from archaeological evidence, secular-historical sources, nor New Testament texts can Nabatean sovereignty over Damascus in the first century AD be proven."[^cite_note-61] Roman emperor [Trajan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajan), who annexed the Nabataean Kingdom, creating the province of [Arabia Petraea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabia_Petraea), had previously been in Damascus, as his father [Marcus Ulpius Traianus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Ulpius_Traianus_\(father_of_Trajan\)) was governor of Syria from 73 to 74 AD, where he met the Damascene architect and engineer [Apollodorus of Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollodorus_of_Damascus), who joined him in [Rome](25458.md) when he was a [consul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consul) in 91 AD and built several monuments during the 2nd century.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAbdulkarim200335–37-62]



The Biblical [Street called Straight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_called_Straight) of Damascus, leading to [Bab Sharqi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_Sharqi) Gate&#x20;

Damascus became a metropolis by the beginning of the 2nd century and in 222 it was upgraded to a *colonia* by Emperor [Septimius Severus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Septimius_Severus). During the *[Pax Romana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pax_Romana)*, Damascus and the entire Roman province of Syria began to prosper. Damascus's importance as a [caravan city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caravan_city) was evident, with the trade routes from southern [Arabia](47858.md), [Palmyra](46709309.md), [Petra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petra), and [the Silk Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Silk_Road) all converging on it. The city satisfied the Roman demands for eastern luxuries. Circa 125 AD the Roman emperor [Hadrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadrian) promoted the city of Damascus to "Metropolis of [Coele-Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coele-Syria)".[^cite_note-63][^cite_note-64]

Little remains of the architecture of the Romans, but the town planning of the old city had a lasting effect. Roman architects brought together the city's Greek and Aramaean foundations and fused them into a new layout measuring about 1,500 by 750 m (4,920 by 2,460 ft), surrounded by a wall. The wall had seven gates, but only the eastern gate, [Bab Sharqi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_Sharqi), remains. Roman Damascus lies mostly at depths of up to five meters (16 feet) below the modern city.

The old borough of [Bab Tuma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_Tuma) was developed at the end of the Roman/Byzantine era by the local [Eastern Orthodox](10186.md) community. According to the [Acts of the Apostles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_of_the_Apostles), Saint Paul and [Saint Thomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_the_Apostle) both lived in that neighborhood. Roman Catholic historians also consider Bab Tuma the birthplace of several [Popes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popes), such as [John V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_John_V) and [Gregory III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Gregory_III). Accordingly, a community of [Jewish Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_Christians) converted to Christianity with the advent of Saint Paul's proselytization. During the [Byzantine–Sasanian War of 602–628](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Sasanian_War_of_602%E2%80%93628), the city was besieged and captured by [Shahrbaraz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shahrbaraz) in 613 along with a large number of Byzantine troops as prisoners.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKaegi200375–77-65] It was in Sasanian hands until near the end of the war.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECrawford201342–43-66]

### Rashidun period

The [Prophet Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad)'s first indirect interaction with the people of Damascus was when he sent a letter, through his companion Shiya ibn Wahab, to [Harith ibn Abi Shamir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harith_ibn_Abi_Shamir), the king of Damascus. The letter stated: "Peace be upon him who follows true guidance. Be informed that my religion shall prevail everywhere. You should accept Islam, and whatever under your command shall remain yours."[^cite_note-67][^cite_note-history-68]

After most of the Syrian countryside was conquered by the [Rashidun Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashidun_Caliphate) during the reign of Caliph [Umar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umar) (r. 634–644), Damascus itself was [conquered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Damascus_\(634\)) by the [Arab Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Muslims) general [Khalid ibn al-Walid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khalid_ibn_al-Walid) in August–September 634 CE. His [army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashidun_army) had previously attempted to capture the city in April 634 but without success.[^cite_note-Burns99-69] With Damascus now in Muslim-Arab hands, the Byzantines, alarmed at the loss of their most prestigious city in the Near East, had decided to wrest back control of it. Under Emperor [Heraclius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraclius), the Byzantines fielded an army superior to that of the Rashidun in manpower. They advanced into southern Syria during the spring of 636 and consequently Khalid ibn al-Walid's forces withdrew from Damascus to prepare for renewed confrontation.[^cite_note-Burns100-70] In August, the two sides met along the [Yarmouk River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yarmouk_River) where they fought a [major battle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Yarmouk) which ended in a decisive Muslim victory, solidifying Muslim rule in Syria and Palestine.[^cite_note-Burns104-71] While the Muslims administered the city, the population of Damascus remained mostly Christian—[Eastern Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox) and [Monophysite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monophysite)—with a growing community of [Muslims](19541.md) from [Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecca), [Medina](36636.md), and the [Syrian Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Desert).[^cite_note-Burns105-72] The governor assigned to the city which had been chosen as the capital of [Islamic Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilad_al-Sham) was [Mu'awiya I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu'awiya_I) who ruled the province from 639 to 661 before establishing the Umayyad Empire.&#x20;

### Umayyad period



View of Damascus with the [Umayyad Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Mosque) in center

Following the fourth Rashidun caliph [Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali)'s death in 661, Mu'awiya was chosen as the caliph of the expanding Islamic empire. Because of the vast amounts of assets his clan, the [Umayyads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad), owned in the city and because of its traditional economic and social links with the [Hijaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hijaz) as well as the [Christian Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Arab) tribes of the region, Mu'awiya established Damascus as the capital of the entire [Umayyad Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Caliphate),[^cite_note-Burns107-73] an empire spanning an immense territory, from the Iberian Peninsula in the west to Central Asia and the Indus River in the east. With the ascension of Caliph [Abd al-Malik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_al-Malik_ibn_Marwan) in 685, a distinctive [Islamic coinage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_coinage) system was introduced, and all the surplus revenue of the Caliphate's provinces was forwarded to the treasury of Damascus. [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language) was also established as the official language (previously Greek),[^cite_note-74] it emphasized Islam's position in the empire and gave the city's Umayyad administration an advantage over the Greek-speaking Christians who previously presided over administrative affairs.[^cite_note-Burns110-75]



Damascus was the capital of the Umayyad Caliphate, which extended from Western Europe to Southern Asia

[Abd al-Malik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_al-Malik_ibn_Marwan)'s successor al-Walid initiated the construction of the [Grand Mosque of Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Mosque_of_Damascus) (known as the Umayyad Mosque) in 706. The site originally had been the Byzantine church of St. John and the Muslims maintained the building's dedication to [John the Baptist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_the_Baptist),[^cite_note-Burns113-76] and before being converted to a Byzantine church, the site was an Aramean temple, then a Hellenistic temple to Zeus and Roman temple to Jupiter.[^cite_note-77] The church in the courtyard was leveled and by 715, the mosque was complete. Al-Walid died that same year and was succeeded at first by [Suleiman ibn Abd al-Malik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suleiman_ibn_Abd_al-Malik) and then by [Umar II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umar_II), who each ruled for brief periods before the reign of [Hisham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hisham) in 724. With these successions, the status of Damascus was gradually weakening, as Suleiman chose [Ramla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramla) as his residence and Hisham chose [Resafa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resafa). Following the murder of the latter in 743, the Caliphate of the Umayyads—which by then stretched from Spain to India—was crumbling as a result of widespread revolts. During the reign of [Marwan II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marwan_II) in 744, the capital of the empire was relocated to [Harran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harran) in the [Jazira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Jazira,_Mesopotamia) region.[^cite_note-Burns122-78]

A number of institutes that were built during the Umayyad period remained well-into the 12th century including hammams, sabils, public fountains that were described by the historian [Ibn Asakir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Asakir). Khans (caravanserais), and [bimaristans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bimaristan) were also built in the city. The royal palace of Qasr Al-Khadra (the Green Palace) which was built by Mu'awiya and had a green dome next to the Umayyad Mosque, was leveled during the later Abbasid period and turned into a police barricades and a [mint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mint_\(facility\)) as described the traveler Al-Hasan [Al-Muhallabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhallabids) after visiting the city in the 10th century. During this period the expansion of the city reached beyond the original city wall limits and reached the borders of [Mount Qasioun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Qasioun).[^cite_note-\:0-79]

### Abbasid and Fatimid periods



The [Umayyad Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Mosque) was built in 715 (Umayyad period), while the [Qubbat al-Khazna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qubbat_al-Khazna) structure (on the right) was built during the latter Abbasid period

On 25 August 750, the [Abbasids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid), having already beaten the Umayyads in the [Battle of the Zab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Zab) in Iraq, conquered Damascus after facing little resistance. With the heralding of the Abbasid Caliphate, Damascus became eclipsed and subordinated by [Baghdad](4492.md), the new Islamic capital. Within the first six months of Abbasid rule, revolts began erupting in the city, albeit too isolated and unfocused to present a viable threat. Nonetheless, the last of the prominent Umayyads were executed, the traditional officials of Damascus were ostracised, and army generals from the city were dismissed. Afterwards, the Umayyad family cemetery was desecrated and the city walls were torn down, reducing Damascus to a provincial town of little importance. It mostly disappeared from written records for the next century, and the only significant improvement of the city was the Abbasid-built treasury dome in the Umayyad Mosque in 789. In 811, distant remnants of the Umayyad dynasty staged a strong uprising in Damascus that was eventually put down.[^cite_note-Burns132-80] On 24 November 847, a [multiple earthquake struck and destroyed Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/847_Damascus_earthquake), killing about 70,000 people.

[Ahmad ibn Tulun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmad_ibn_Tulun), a dissenting Turkic wali appointed by the Abbasids, conquered Syria, including Damascus, from his overlords in 878–79. Out of respect for the Umayyad rulers, he erected a shrine on the site of Mu'awiya's grave. [Tulunid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulunid) rule of Damascus was brief, lasting only until 906 before being replaced by the [Qarmatians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qarmatians), who adhered to [Shia Islam](26961.md). Due to their inability to control the vast land they occupied, the Qarmatians withdrew from Damascus and a new dynasty, the [Ikhshidids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ikhshidid), took control of it. They maintained the independence of Damascus from the Arab [Hamdanid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamdanid) dynasty of [Aleppo](159244.md) 967. A period of instability in the city followed, with a Qarmatian raid in 968, a Byzantine raid in 970, and increasing pressures from the [Fatimids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatimid) in the south and the Hamdanids in the north.[^cite_note-Burns135-81]



Entrance to Al-Malik Al-Zahir [Hammam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammam) built in 985 (Fatimid period)

The Shia Fatimids gained control in 970, inflaming hostilities between them and the city's Sunni Arabs, who frequently revolted. The Turk [Alptakin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alptakin) drove out the Fatimids five years later, and through diplomacy, prevented the Byzantines during the [Syrian campaigns of John Tzimiskes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_campaigns_of_John_Tzimiskes) from attempting to annex the city. But by 977, the Fatimids under Caliph [al-Aziz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Aziz) wrested control of the city back and tamed Sunni dissidents. The Arab geographer [al-Muqaddasi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Muqaddasi) visited Damascus in 985, remarking that the architecture and infrastructure of the city were "magnificent" but living conditions were awful. Under al-Aziz, the city saw a brief period of stability that ended with the reign of [al-Hakim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Hakim_bi-Amr_Allah) (996–1021). In 998, he executed hundreds of Damascus citizens for incitement. Three years after his mysterious disappearance, a rebellion began in southern Syria against the Fatimids but was stifled by the Fatimid Turkish governor of Syria and Palestine, [Anushtakin al-Duzbari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anushtakin_al-Dizbari), in 1029. This victory gave him mastery over Syria, displeasing his Fatimid overlords but gaining the admiration of Damascus's citizens. He was exiled by Fatimid authorities to [Aleppo](159244.md), where he died in 1041.[^cite_note-Burns138-82] From that date to 1063, there are no known records of the city's history. By then, Damascus lacked a city administration and had an enfeebled economy and a greatly reduced population.[^cite_note-Burns139-83]

### Seljuq and Ayyubid periods

With the arrival of the nomadic [Seljuq Turks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seljuq_Turks) from [Central Asia](6742.md) in the late 11th century, Damascus again became the capital of independent states. It was ruled by [Abu Sa'id Taj ad-Dawla Tutush I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tutush_I) starting in 1079 and he was succeeded by his son [Abu Nasr Duqaq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duqaq_\(Seljuk_ruler_of_Damascus\)) in 1095. The Seljuks established a court in Damascus and a systematic reversal of Shia inroads in the city. The city also saw an expansion of religious life through private endowments financing religious institutions (*[madrasas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madrasa)*) and hospitals (*maristans*). Damascus soon became one of the most important centers of propagating [Islamic thought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_thought) in the Muslim world. After Duqaq died in 1104, his mentor (*[atabeg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atabeg)*), [Toghtekin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toghtekin), took control of Damascus and the [Burid line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burid_Dynasty) of the Seljuq dynasty. Under Duqaq and Toghtekin, Damascus experienced stability, elevated status, and a revived role in commerce. In addition, the city's Sunni majority enjoyed being a part of the larger Sunni framework effectively governed by various Turkic dynasties who in turn were under the [moral authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_authority) of the Baghdad-based Abbasids.[^cite_note-Burns142-84]



The twin [muqarnas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muqarnas) domes of the [Nur al-Din Madrasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nur_al-Din_Madrasa).

[^cite_note-archnet-nuriya-86]

While the rulers of Damascus were preoccupied in conflict with their fellow Seljuqs in Aleppo and [Diyarbakir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diyarbakir), the Crusaders, who arrived in the [Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant) in 1097, conquered [Jerusalem](16043.md), [Mount Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Lebanon) and Palestine. Duqaq seemed to have been content with Crusader's rule as a buffer between his dominion and the Fatimid Caliphate of Egypt. Toghtekin, however, saw the Western invaders as a viable threat to Damascus, which, at the time, nominally included [Homs](77533.md), the Beqaa Valley, Hauran, and the Golan Heights as part of its territories. With military support from Sharaf al-Din [Mawdud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mawdud) of [Mosul](200475.md), Toghtekin managed to halt Crusader raids in the Golan and Hauran. Mawdud was assassinated in the Umayyad Mosque in 1109, depriving Damascus of northern Muslim backing and forcing Toghtekin to agree to a truce with the Crusaders in 1110.[^cite_note-Burns147-87] In 1126, the Crusader army led by [Baldwin II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baldwin_II_of_Jerusalem) fought Burid forces led by Toghtekin at [Marj al-Saffar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Marj_al-Saffar_\(1126\)) near Damascus; however, despite their tactical victory, the Crusaders failed in their objective to capture Damascus.

Following Toghtekin's death in 1128, his son, [Taj al-Muluk Buri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taj_al-Muluk_Buri), became the nominal ruler of Damascus. Coincidentally, the Seljuq prince of Mosul, [Imad al-Din Zengi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imad_ad-Din_Zengi), took power in Aleppo and gained a mandate from the Abbasids to extend his authority to Damascus. In 1129, around 6,000 [Isma'ili Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isma'ili) were killed in the city along with their leaders. The Sunnis were provoked by rumors alleging there was a plot by the Isma'ilis, who controlled the strategic fort at [Banias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banias), to aid the Crusaders in capturing Damascus in return for control of [Tyre](30796.md). Soon after the massacre, the Crusaders aimed to take advantage of the unstable situation and launch an [assault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusade_of_1129) against Damascus with nearly 2,000 knights and 10,000 infantry. However, Buri allied with Zengi and managed to prevent their army from reaching the city.[^cite_note-Burns148-9-88] Buri was assassinated by Isma'ili agents in 1132; he was succeeded by his son, [Shams al-Mulk Isma'il](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shams_al-Mulk_Isma'il) who ruled tyrannically until he was murdered in 1135 on secret orders from his mother, [Safwat al-Mulk Zumurrud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zumurrud_Khatun); Isma'il's brother, Shihab al-Din Mahmud, replaced him. Meanwhile, Zengi, intent on putting Damascus under his control, married Safwat al-Mulk in 1138. Mahmud's reign then ended in 1139 after he was killed for relatively unknown reasons by members of his family. [Mu'in al-Din Unur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu'in_al-Din_Unur), his *[mamluk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamluk)* ("slave soldier") took effective power of the city, prompting Zengi—with Safwat al-Mulk's backing—to lay siege against Damascus the same year. In response, Damascus allied with the Crusader [Kingdom of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Jerusalem) to resist Zengi's forces. Consequently, Zengi withdrew his army and focused on campaigns against northern Syria.[^cite_note-Burns151-89]



The Ayyubid-era [Citadel of Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citadel_of_Damascus)

In 1144, Zengi [conquered Edessa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Edessa_\(1144\)), a crusader stronghold, which led to [a new crusade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Crusade) from Europe in 1148. In the meantime, Zengi was assassinated and his territory was divided among his sons, one of whom, [Nur ad-Din](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nur_ad-Din_Zangi), emir of Aleppo, made an alliance with Damascus. When the European crusaders arrived, they and the nobles of Jerusalem agreed to attack Damascus. [Their siege](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Damascus_\(1148\)), however, was a complete failure. When the city seemed to be on the verge of collapse, the crusader army suddenly moved against another section of the walls and was driven back. By 1154, Damascus was firmly under Nur ad-Din's control.[^cite_note-90]

In 1164, King [Amalric of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amalric_of_Jerusalem) [invaded Fatimid Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusader_invasions_of_Egypt), requested help from Nur ad-Din. The Nur ad-Din sent his general [Shirkuh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shirkuh), and in 1166 Amalric was defeated at the [Battle of al-Babein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_al-Babein). When Shirkuh died in 1169, he was succeeded by his nephew Yusuf, better known as [Saladin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saladin), who defeated a joint crusader-Byzantine siege of [Damietta](379680.md).[^cite_note-91] Saladin eventually overthrew the Fatimid caliphs and established himself as Sultan of Egypt. He also began to assert his independence from Nur ad-Din, and with the death of both Amalric and Nur ad-Din in 1174, he was well-placed to begin exerting control over Damascus and Nur ad-Din's other Syrian possessions.[^cite_note-92] In 1177 Saladin was defeated by the crusaders at the [Battle of Montgisard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Montgisard), despite his numerical superiority.[^cite_note-93] Saladin also [besieged Kerak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Kerak_\(1183\)) in 1183, but was forced to withdraw. He finally launched a full invasion of Jerusalem in 1187 and annihilated the crusader army at the [Battle of Hattin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Hattin) in July. [Acre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acre,_Israel) fell to Saladin soon after, and Jerusalem itself [was captured](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Jerusalem_\(1187\)) in October. These events shocked Europe, resulting in the [Third Crusade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Crusade) in 1189, led by [Richard I of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_I_of_England), [Philip II of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_II_of_France) and [Frederick I, Holy Roman Emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_I,_Holy_Roman_Emperor), though the last drowned en route.[^cite_note-94]



[Al-Adiliyah Madrasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Adiliyah_Madrasa) founded by the Ayyubid Sultan [Al-Adil I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Adil_I) in 1215 (brother of [Saladin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saladin))

The surviving crusaders, joined by new arrivals from Europe, put Acre to a [lengthy siege](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Acre_\(1189%E2%80%931191\)) which lasted until 1191. After re-capturing Acre, Richard defeated Saladin at the [Battle of Arsuf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Arsuf) in 1191 and the [Battle of Jaffa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Jaffa_\(1192\)) in 1192, recovering most of the coast for the Christians, but could not recover Jerusalem or any of the inland territory of the kingdom. The crusade came to an end peacefully, with the [Treaty of Jaffa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Jaffa_\(1192\)) in 1192. Saladin allowed pilgrimages to be made to Jerusalem, allowing the Crusaders to fulfill their vows, after which they all returned home. Local crusader barons set about rebuilding their kingdom from Acre and the other coastal cities.[^cite_note-95]

Saladin died in 1193, and there were frequent conflicts between different [Ayyubid sultans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayyubid_dynasty) ruling in Damascus and Cairo. Damascus was the capital of independent Ayyubid rulers between 1193 and 1201, from 1218 to 1238, from 1239 to 1245, and from 1250 to 1260. At other times it was ruled by the Ayyubid rulers of Egypt.[^cite_note-96] During the internecine wars fought by the Ayyubid rulers, Damascus was besieged repeatedly, as, e.g., [in 1229](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Damascus_\(1229\)).[^cite_note-97]

The [Al-Salihiyah district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Salihiyah,_Damascus), situated on the slopes of [Mount Qasioun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Qasioun), was established during the mid-12th century. Its earliest inhabitants were refugees from Jerusalem who settled in Damascus after the city's capture by the Crusaders. The new inhabitants founded the Al-Umariyya Madrasa and the Hanabila Deir, forming the nucleus of the new suburb.[^cite_note-\:0-79]



The Ayyubid Al-Murshidiyya (center) and Al-Atabikiyya (background) madrasas

The Ayyubid period saw extensive urban expansion in Damascus, with the construction of numerous madrasas, mosques, and other public institutions both within and outside the Old City walls, reflecting a major phase of urban expansion. Al-Salihiyah continued to grow southward until it reached the city's original fortifications. Prominent educational institutions established during this era included the Al-Murshidiyya, Al-Jaharkasiyya, [Al-Sahiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Sahiba_Madrasa), Al-Shibliyya, [Al-Rukniyya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Rukniyah_Madrasa), and [Al-Shamiyya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Shamiyah_al-Kubra_Madrasa) madrasas.[^cite_note-\:0-79]

The Ayyubid period also witnessed the construction of the first [congregational mosques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congregational_mosque) to be built since the construction of the Umayyad Mosque approximately five centuries earlier. Unlike the Umayyad Mosque, these mosques were all located outside the Old City walls and included the [Hanabila Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanabila_Mosque) in Al-Salihiyah, Al-Mardaniyya Mosque and Madrasa (present-day [Al-Jisr al-Abyad Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Jisr_Al-Abyad_Mosque)), [Jarrah Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jarrah_Mosque) in [Al-Shaghour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Shaghur,_Damascus), [Al-Tawba Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Tawba_Mosque_\(Damascus\)) in Uqayba, and [Bab Musalla Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_Musalla_Mosque) in [Al-Midan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Midan). The period also saw the establishment of the Bimaristan al-Qaymari in Al-Salihiyah, one of the city's notable hospitals. During the same period, the Citadel of Damascus was rebuilt following the destruction of the earlier Seljuk-era citadel.[^cite_note-\:0-79][^cite_note-98] The Ayyubid-era madrasas and mausoleums are distinguished by their characteristic red domes, a defining feature of the period's architectural vocabulary. Notable examples include the red dome of Saladin's Mausoleum.

### Mamluk period



Mausoleum chamber of [sultan Baybars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baybars) in [Al-Zahiriyah Madrasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Zahiriyah_Library)

Ayyubid rule (and independence) came to an end with the Mongol invasion of Syria in 1260, in which the Mongols led by [Kitbuqa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitbuqa) entered the city on 1 March 1260, along with the King of Armenia, [Hethum I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hethum_I,_King_of_Armenia), and the Prince of Antioch, [Bohemond VI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohemond_VI_of_Antioch); hence, the citizens of Damascus saw for the first time for six centuries three Christian potentates ride in triumph through their streets.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERunciman1987307-99] However, following the Mongol defeat at [Ain Jalut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Ain_Jalut) which was led by [Qutuz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qutuz), [Baybars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baybars) and [Al-Mansur II Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mansur_II_Muhammad) on 3 September 1260, Damascus was captured five days later and became the provincial capital of the [Mamluk Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamluk_Sultanate_\(Cairo\)), ruled from Egypt, following the Mongol withdrawal. Following their victory at the [Battle of Wadi al-Khaznadar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Wadi_al-Khaznadar), the Mongols led by [Ghazan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghazan) besieged the city for ten days, which surrendered between 30 December 1299 and 6 January 1300, though its Citadel resisted.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERunciman1987439-100] Ghazan then retreated with most of his forces in February, probably because the Mongol horses needed fodder, and left behind about 10,000 horsemen under the Mongol general [Mulay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mulay).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDemurger2007146-101] Around March 1300, Mulay returned with his horsemen to Damascus,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAmitai1987247-102] then followed Ghazan back across the [Euphrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphrates). In May 1300, the Egyptian Mamluks returned from Egypt and reclaimed the entire area[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchein1979810-103] without a battle.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAmitai1987248-104] In April 1303, the Mamluks managed to defeat the Mongol army led by [Kutlushah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kutlushah) and Mulay along with their Armenian allies at the [Battle of Marj al-Saffar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Marj_al-Saffar_\(1303\)), to put an end to [Mongol invasions of the Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_invasions_of_the_Levant).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENicolle200180-105] Later on, the [Black Death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death) of 1348–1349 killed as much as half of the city's population.[^cite_note-106]



Venetian delegation in Damascus, painting of 1511. [Louvre-Lens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louvre-Lens).

In 1400, [Timur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timur), the [Turco-Mongol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turco-Mongol) conqueror, [besieged Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Damascus_\(1400\)). The Mamluk sultan dispatched a deputation from Cairo, including [Ibn Khaldun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Khaldun), who negotiated with him, but after their withdrawal, Timur sacked the city on 17 March 1401.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEIbn_Khaldun195297-107] The [Umayyad Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Mosque) was burnt and men and women were taken into slavery. A huge number of the city's artisans were taken to Timur's capital at [Samarkand](51491.md). These were the luckier citizens: many were slaughtered and their heads piled up in a field outside the north-east corner of the walls, where a city square still bears the name *Burj al-Ru'us* (between modern-day [Al-Qassaa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Qassaa) and [Bab Tuma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_Tuma)), originally "the tower of heads". Rebuilt, Damascus continued to serve as a Mamluk provincial capital until 1516.

### Ottoman period

See also: [Damascus Eyalet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damascus_Eyalet) and [Syria Vilayet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria_Vilayet)



[Al-Azm Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Azm_Palace) built in the traditional Damascene courtyard style&#x20;

In early 1516, the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md), wary of the danger of an alliance between the Mamluks and the Persian [Safavids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavids), started a campaign of conquest against the Mamluk sultanate. On 21 September, the Mamluk governor of Damascus fled the city, and on 2 October the [khutba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khutba) in the Umayyad mosque was pronounced in the name of [Selim I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selim_I). The day after, the victorious sultan entered the city, staying for three months. On 15 December, he left Damascus by Bab al-Jabiya, intent on the conquest of Egypt. Little appeared to have changed in the city: one army had simply replaced another. However, on his return in October 1517, the sultan ordered the construction of a mosque, [tekkiye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khanqah) and mausoleum at the shrine of Shaikh [Muhi al-Din ibn Arabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Arabi) in [al-Salihiyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Salihiyah,_Damascus). This was to be the first of Damascus's great Ottoman monuments. During this time, according to an Ottoman census, Damascus had 10,423 households.[^cite_note-108] The Ottomans remained for the next 400 years, except for a brief occupation by [Ibrahim Pasha of Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibrahim_Pasha_of_Egypt) from 1832 to 1840. Because of its importance as the point of departure for one of the two great [Hajj](23487999.md) caravans to [Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecca), Damascus was treated with more attention by the [Porte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Porte) than its size might have warranted—for most of this period, [Aleppo](159244.md) was more populous and commercially more important. In 1559 the western building of [Sulaymaniyya Takiyya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulaymaniyya_Takiyya), comprising a mosque and [khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caravanserai) for pilgrims on the road to Mecca, was completed to a design by the famous Ottoman architect [Mimar Sinan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mimar_Sinan), and soon afterward the [Salimiyya Madrasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salimiyya_Madrasa) was built adjoining it.[^cite_note-109] During the Ottoman period, it used to be named Sacred Damascus or Holy Damascus ([Ottoman Turkish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Turkish_language): شام شريف, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Ottoman_Turkish): *şam-ı şerif*), a title that was also used for the city of Jerusalem.[^cite_note-110][^cite_note-111]



[Khan As'ad Pasha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khan_As'ad_Pasha)

Early in the nineteenth century, Damascus was noted for its shady cafes along the banks of the Barada. A depiction of these by [William Henry Bartlett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Henry_Bartlett) was published in 1836, along with a poetical illustration by [Letitia Elizabeth Landon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letitia_Elizabeth_Landon), see  [Cafes in Damascus](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Letitia%20Elizabeth%20Landon%20\(L.%20E.%20L.\)%20in%20Fisher's%20Drawing%20Room%20Scrap%20Book,%201837/Cafes%20in%20Damascus). Under [Ottoman rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_rule), Christians and [Jews](25955086.md) were considered [dhimmis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhimmi), who were allowed to practice their religions under certain restrictions. During the [Damascus affair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damascus_affair) of 1840 the false accusation of [ritual murder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ritual_murder) was brought against members of the Jewish community of Damascus. The [massacre of Christians in 1860](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1860_Druze-Christian_conflict_in_Lebanon) was also one of the most notorious incidents of these centuries when fighting between [Druze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Druze) and [Maronites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maronites) in [Mount Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Lebanon) spilled over into the city. Several thousand Christians were killed in June 1860, with many more being saved through the intervention of the Algerian exile [Abd al-Qadir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_al-Qadir_al-Jaza'iri) and his soldiers (three days after the massacre started), who brought them to safety in Abd al-Qadir's residence and the [Citadel of Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citadel_of_Damascus). The Christian quarter of the old city (mostly inhabited by Catholics), including several churches, was burnt down. The Christian inhabitants of the notoriously poor and refractory [Midan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midan) district outside the walls (mostly Orthodox) were, however, protected by their Muslim neighbors.

&#x20;American Missionary E.C. Miller records that in 1867 the population of the city was 'about' 140,000, of whom 30,000 were Christians, 10,000 Jews, and 100,000 'Mohammedans' with fewer than 100 Protestant Christians.[^cite_note-112] In the meantime, American writer [Mark Twain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Twain) visited Damascus, then wrote about his travel in *[The Innocents Abroad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Innocents_Abroad)*, in which he mentioned: "Though old as history itself, thou art fresh as the breath of spring, blooming as thine own rose-bud, and fragrant as thine own orange flower, O Damascus, pearl of the East!".[^cite_note-113] In November 1898, German emperor [Wilhelm II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_II,_German_Emperor) toured Damascus, during his trip to the Ottoman Empire.[^cite_note-114]

### Modern period

#### 20th century

See also: [Mandate for Syria and the Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandate_for_Syria_and_the_Lebanon)

In the early years of the 20th century, nationalist sentiment in Damascus, initially cultural in its interest, began to take a political coloring, largely in reaction to the [turkicisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkicisation) program of the [Committee of Union and Progress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_of_Union_and_Progress) government established in Istanbul in 1908. The hanging of a number of patriotic intellectuals by [Jamal Pasha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamal_Pasha), governor of Damascus, in Beirut and Damascus in 1915 and 1916 further stoked nationalist feeling, and in 1918, as the forces of the [Arab Revolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Revolt) and the British Imperial forces approached, residents fired on the retreating Turkish troops.



[King Faisal of Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faisal_I_of_Iraq) and [T. E. Lawrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._E._Lawrence) on [HMS Orion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Orion) during [World War I](4764461.md), 1918.

On 1 October 1918, [T. E. Lawrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._E._Lawrence) [entered Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capture_of_Damascus_\(1918\)), the third arrival of the day, the first being the Australian [3rd Light Horse Brigade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3rd_Light_Horse_Brigade), led by Major [A.C.N. 'Harry' Olden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Charles_Olden?action=edit\&redlink=1).[^cite_note-116] Two days later, 3 October 1918, [the forces of the Arab revolt led by](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharifian_Army) [Prince Faisal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faisal_I_of_Iraq) also entered Damascus.[^cite_note-117] A military government under [Shukri Pasha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shukri_Pasha_Al-Ayyubi?action=edit\&redlink=1) was named and Faisal ibn Hussein was proclaimed [king of Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Kingdom_of_Syria). Political tension arose in November 1917, when the new [Bolshevik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolshevik) government in Russia revealed the [Sykes-Picot Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sykes-Picot_Agreement) whereby Britain and France had arranged to partition the Arab East between them. A new Franco-British proclamation on 17 November promised the "complete and definitive freeing of the peoples so long oppressed by the Turks." The [Syrian National Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_National_Congress) in March adopted a democratic constitution. However, the [Versailles Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Versailles_Conference) had granted France a [mandate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Nations_mandate) over Syria, and in 1920 a French army commanded by the General [Mariano Goybet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariano_Goybet) crossed the Anti-Lebanon Mountains, defeated a small Syrian defensive expedition at the [Battle of Maysalun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Maysalun) and [entered Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capture_of_Damascus_\(1920\)). The French made Damascus the capital of their [League of Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Nations) [Mandate for Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Mandate_of_Syria_and_the_Lebanon).



Damascus in 1933

When in 1925 the [Great Syrian Revolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Syrian_Revolt) in the [Hauran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hauran) spread to Damascus, the French suppressed it with heavy weaponry, bombing and shelling the city on 9 May 1926.[^cite_note-118] As a result, the area of the old city between [Al-Hamidiyah Souq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Hamidiyah_Souq) and [Medhat Pasha Souq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medhat_Pasha_Souq) was burned to the ground, with many deaths, and has since then been known as *[al-Hariqa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Hariqa)* ("the fire"). The old city was surrounded with barbed wire to prevent rebels from infiltrating the [Ghouta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghouta), and a new road was built outside the northern ramparts to facilitate the movement of armored cars. Reporter [George Seldes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Seldes) viewed 308 bodies, and suggested there might be more dead under the rubble—and that a maximum might be one thousand. "When the Muslims, who had rebelled, threatened to kill all Christians, General Maurice Sarrail gave the civilian population time to evacuate, then ordered Fort Gouraud to fire some warning shots, then shell the rebel sector." [^cite_note-119]

On 21 June 1941, 3 weeks into the Allied [Syria-Lebanon campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria-Lebanon_campaign), [Damascus was captured](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Damascus_\(1941\)) from the [Vichy French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vichy_French) forces by a mixed British Indian and Free French force. The French agreed to withdraw in 1946, following the British intervention during the [Levant Crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant_Crisis), thus leading to the full independence of Syria. Damascus remained the capital and has been unchanged even following the start of [Ba'athist rule](57134800.md) in 1963.

**Culture**

Damascus positioned itself globally in the 20th century, as demonstrated by the [Arab Academy of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Academy_for_Science,_Technology_and_Maritime_Transport) established in 1919. By 1928 the academy had grown to 109 members, many of which were Western orientalists. Damascus was a centre of learning and continued to matter in different areas partly because of the capital effect. This effect resulted in the establishment of institutions and organizations in the city and the centralization of learning the capital.[^cite_note-120] In 1979, the [Old City of Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_City_of_Damascus), with its collection of archaeological and historical religious sites, was listed as a [World Heritage Site](44940.md) by [UNESCO](21786641.md).

#### Civil war

Main article: [Syrian civil war](30741795.md)



The [Damascus Opera House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damascus_Opera_House) (opened in 2004) stands behind the Damascene Sword Monument, a landmark erected in 1960 in the [Umayyad Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Square)&#x20;

By January 2012, [clashes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rif_Dimashq_Governorate_campaign) between the regular army and rebels reached the outskirts of Damascus, reportedly preventing people from leaving or reaching their houses, especially when security operations there intensified from the end of January into February.[^cite_note-121]

By June 2012, bullets and shrapnel shells smashed into homes in Damascus overnight as troops battled the Free Syrian Army in the streets. At least three tank shells slammed into residential areas in the central Damascus neighborhood of Qaboun, according to activists. Intense exchanges of assault rifle fire marked the clash, according to residents and amateur video posted online.[^cite_note-122]

&#x20;The Damascus suburb of [Ghouta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghouta) suffered heavy bombing in December 2017 and a further wave of bombing started in February 2018, also known as [Rif Dimashq Offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rif_Dimashq_offensive_\(February%E2%80%93April_2018\)).



Israeli-occupied [Golan Heights](7515865.md) and [Israeli invasion of Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Israeli_invasion_of_Syria) in December 2024

On 20 May 2018, Damascus and the entire Rif Dimashq Governorate came fully under government control for the first time in 7 years after the evacuation of [IS](9087364.md) from [Yarmouk Camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yarmouk_Camp).[^cite_note-123] In September 2019, Damascus entered the *[Guinness World Records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinness_World_Records)* as the least liveable city, scoring 30.7 points on the [Economist](15303116.md)'s [Global Liveability Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Liveability_Ranking) in 2019, based on factors such as: stability, healthcare, culture and environment, education, and infrastructure.[^cite_note-124] However, the trend of being the least liveable city on Earth started in 2017,[^cite_note-125] and continued as of 2024.[^cite_note-126]

Syrian rebels, led by the [HTS](53015755.md) rebel group [entered Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Damascus_\(2024\)) on 8 December 2024 after a [series of offensives](78460726.md), capturing [Sednaya Prison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sednaya_Prison) and later resulting in [the collapse](78548066.md) of [Ba'athist Syria](57134800.md).[^cite_note-127]

On 23 February 2025, Israeli Prime Minister [Benjamin Netanyahu](56472.md) demanded the complete demilitarization of southern Syria in the provinces of [Quneitra](7445647.md), [Daraa](5317030.md) and [Suweyda](3096933.md),[^cite_note-128] and the withdrawal of Syrian forces from Syrian territory south of Damascus.[^cite_note-129] Syria's new government under President [Ahmed al-Sharaa](44007699.md) rejected Netanyahu's demands.[^cite_note-130][^cite_note-131] Hours later, Israel conducted a wave of airstrikes in Damascus and southern Syria.[^cite_note-132]

## Economy



Bank Al-Sharq and the [Blue Tower Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Tower_Hotel), a 4-star hotel in Hamra Street.

The historical role that Damascus played as an important trade center has changed in recent years due to political development in the region as well as the development of modern trade.[^cite_note-Damascus-133] Most goods produced in Damascus, as well as in Syria, are distributed to countries of the Arabian peninsula.[^cite_note-Damascus-133] Damascus has also held an annual [international trade exposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damascus_International_Fair) every fall, since 1954.[^cite_note-134]

The tourism industry in Damascus has a lot of potential; however, the civil war has hampered these prospects. The abundance of cultural wealth in Damascus has been modestly employed since the late 1980s, with the development of many accommodation and transportation establishments and other related investments.[^cite_note-Damascus-133] Since the early 2000s, numerous boutique hotels and bustling cafes have opened in the old city, which attracts plenty of European tourists and Damascenes alike.[^cite_note-The_Wall_Street_Journal-135]



[Municipalities of Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Municipalities_of_Damascus)

In 2009, new office space was built and became available on the real estate market.[^cite_note-136] [Marota City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marota_City) and Basilia City are two new development projects in Damascus.[^cite_note-137] These two projects are viewed as post-war reconstruction efforts. The [Damascus stock exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damascus_Securities_Exchange) formally opened for trade in March 2009, and the exchange is the only stock exchange in Syria.[^cite_note-138] It is located in the Barzeh district, within Syria's financial markets and securities commission. Its final home is to be located in the upmarket business district of [*Yaafur*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaafour).[^cite_note-AP-139]

Damascus is home to a wide range of industrial activities, such as textile, [food processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_processing), cement, and various chemical industries.[^cite_note-Damascus-133] The majority of factories are run by the state, limited [privatization](24661.md) in addition to economic activities led by the [private sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_sector), which were permitted starting in the early 2000s with the liberalization of trade that took place.[^cite_note-Damascus-133] Traditional handcrafts and artisan copper engravings are still produced in the old city.[^cite_note-Damascus-133]

## Demographics

### Population



Three Damascene women, 1873: [peasant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peasant) (left), [Druze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Druze) in *[tantour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tantour)* headdress, and urban lady wearing *qabqab* (i.e. kabkab or [platform shoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platform_shoes))



Scene in a [bazaar](556904.md) in Damascus, in 1838, by [Charles G. Addison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_G._Addison). [British Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Library).

In the 2004 Census of Population conducted by [Central Bureau of Statistics (Syria)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Bureau_of_Statistics_\(Syria\)), Damascus had a population of 1,552,161 living in 309,317 of its 345,097 total dwellings.[^cite_note-CBS-140]

Damascus is the center of a crowded metropolitan area with an estimated population of 5 million. The metropolitan area of Damascus includes the cities of [Douma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douma,_Syria), [Harasta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harasta), [Darayya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darayya), [Al-Tall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Tall_\(Syria\)) and [Jaramana](5146490.md).

The city's growth rate is higher than in Syria as a whole, primarily due to [rural-urban migration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urbanization) and the influx of young Syrian migrants drawn by employment and educational opportunities.[^cite_note-Britannica-141] The migration of Syrian youths to Damascus has resulted in an average age within the city that is below the national average.[^cite_note-Britannica-141] Nonetheless, the population of Damascus is thought to have decreased in recent years as a result of the ongoing [Syrian civil war](30741795.md).

### Ethnicity

The vast majority of Damascenes are Syrian [Arabs](2185.md). The [Kurds](3821855.md) are the second largest ethnic minority, with a population of approximately 300,000.[^cite_note-142] They reside primarily in the neighborhoods of Wadi al-Mashari ("Zorava" or "Zore Afa" in Kurdish) and [Rukn al-Din](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rukneddine).[^cite_note-143][^cite_note-144] Other minorities include [Palestinians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestinians_in_Syria), [Armenians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenians_in_Syria), [Assyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrians_in_Syria), [Turkmens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Turkmen), [Circassians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circassians_in_Syria) and a small [Greek community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greeks_in_Syria).

[Islam](6037917.md) is the largest religion. The majority of Muslims are [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni) while [Alawites](436616.md) and [Twelver Shi'a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelver_Shi'a) comprise sizeable minorities. Alawites live primarily in the neighbourhoods of Mezzeh 86 and Sumariyah part of the [Mezzeh](21089456.md) district, in addition to the neighbourhood of Eish al-Warwar in the district of [Barzeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barzeh,_Syria). Twelvers primarily live near the Shia holy sites of [Sayyidah Ruqayya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayyidah_Ruqayya_Mosque) and [Sayyidah Zaynab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayyidah_Zainab_Mosque,_Damascus). It is believed that there are more than 200 mosques in Damascus, the most well-known being the [Umayyad Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Mosque).[^cite_note-145]

Christians represent about 10%–15% of the population.[^cite_note-146] Several [Eastern Christian rites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Christianity_in_Syria) have their headquarters in Damascus, including the [Syriac Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Orthodox_Church), the [Syriac Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Catholic_Church), the [Melkite Greek Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melkite_Greek_Catholic_Church), and the [Greek Orthodox Church of Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Orthodox_Church_of_Antioch). The Christian districts in the city are [Bab Tuma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_Tuma), [Qassaa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qassaa) and Ghassani. Each have many churches, most notably the ancient [Chapel of Saint Paul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapel_of_Saint_Paul), [St. Paul Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Catholic_Cathedral_of_Saint_Paul), [Our Lady of the Dormition Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_of_Our_Lady_of_the_Dormition), and [St Georges Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_of_Saint_George,_Damascus) in Bab Tuma. At the suburb of [Soufanieh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Our_Lady_of_Soufanieh) a series of [apparitions of the Virgin Mary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apparitions_of_the_Virgin_Mary) have reportedly been observed between 1982 and 2004.[^cite_note-147] The Patriarchal See of the Syriac Orthodox is based in Damascus, Bab Touma.

A smaller [Druze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Druze) minority inhabits the city, notably in the mixed Christian-Druze suburbs of [Tadamon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tadamon,_Syria),[^cite_note-148] [Jaramana](5146490.md),[^cite_note-149] and [Sahnaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahnaya).

There was a small [Jewish community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Jews) namely in what is called *[Harat al-Yahud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_Quarter_of_Damascus)* ("the Jewish quarter"). They are the remnants of an ancient and much larger Jewish presence in Syria, dating back at least to Roman times, if not before to the time of King David.[^cite_note-150] There was once a significant Jewish community in Damascus, but as of 2023, no Jews remain.[^cite_note-151]

#### Sufism

[Sufism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism) throughout the second half of the 20th century has been an influential current in the Sunni religious practises, particularly in Damascus. The largest women-only and girls-only Muslim movement in the world happens to be Sufi-oriented and is based in Damascus, led by [Munira al-Qubaysi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munira_al-Qubaysi). Syrian Sufism has its stronghold in urban regions such as Damascus, where it also established political movements such as Zayd, with the help of a series of [mosques](19894.md), and clergy such as [Abd al-Ghani al-Nabulsi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_al-Ghani_al-Nabulsi), [Sa'id Hawwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sa'id_Hawwa), [Abd al-Rahman al-Shaghouri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_al-Rahman_al-Shaghouri) and [Muhammad al-Yaqoubi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_al-Yaqoubi).[^cite_note-152]

## Historical sites

Main article: [Old city of Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_city_of_Damascus)



Typical historic Damascene street



A street adjacent to the Citadel of Damascus

The [Ancient City of Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_City_of_Damascus) was inscribed on the [World Heritage List](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Heritage_List) in 1979. Beginning with the [Aramaic kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaic_Kingdom_of_Damascus) (11th-7th centuries BC), Damascus grew into the political and urban hub for the region. Today, the ancient city encompasses over 125 cultural heritage sites that represent Damascus's long history, including archaeological and architectural remains from the [Hellenistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period), Roman, Byzantine, Islamic, and Ottoman periods.[^cite_note-153] Tragically, ongoing conflict and war continues to threaten the archaeological heritage and prevent archaeological discovery of Damascus.[^cite_note-154]



Interior of a house in Damascus (1890s)

The [Citadel of Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citadel_of_Damascus) is in the northwest corner of the Old City. The *[Damascus Straight Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damascus_Straight_Street)* (referred to in the account of the [conversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversion_of_Saint_Paul) of [St. Paul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_of_Tarsus) in [Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_of_the_Apostles) 9\:11), also known as the *[Via Recta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Via_Recta)*, was the [decumanus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decumanus_Maximus) (east–west main street) of Roman Damascus, and extended for over 1,500 m (4,900 ft). Today, it consists of the street of Bab Sharqi and the Souk Medhat Pasha, a covered market. The [Bab Sharqi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_Sharqi) street is filled with small shops and leads to the old Christian quarter of [Bab Tuma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_Tuma) (St. Thomas's Gate), where [St George's Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_of_Saint_George,_Damascus), the seat of the [Syriac Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Orthodox_Church), is notably located. [Medhat Pasha Souq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medhat_Pasha_Souq) is also a main market in Damascus and was named after [Midhat Pasha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midhat_Pasha), the Ottoman governor of Syria who renovated the Souk. At the end of Bab Sharqi Street, one reaches the [House of Ananias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Saint_Ananias), an underground chapel that was the cellar of [Ananias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ananias_of_Damascus)'s house. The [Umayyad Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Mosque), also known as the Grand Mosque of Damascus, is one of the largest mosques in the world and also one of the oldest sites of continuous prayer since the rise of Islam. A shrine in the mosque is said to contain the body of [St. John the Baptist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._John_the_Baptist). The [mausoleum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mausoleum_of_Saladin) where [Saladin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saladin) was buried is located in the gardens just outside the mosque. [Sayyidah Ruqayya Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayyidah_Ruqayya_Mosque), the shrine of the youngest daughter of [Husayn ibn Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Husayn_ibn_Ali), can also be found near the Umayyad Mosque. The ancient district of [Amara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amara_District) is also within walking distance from these sites. Another heavily visited site is [Sayyidah Zaynab Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayyidah_Zainab_Mosque,_Damascus), where the tomb of [Zaynab bint Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaynab_bint_Ali) is located.&#x20;



[Maktab Anbar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maktab_Anbar) built in the traditional Damascene style with a central fountain

Shias, Fatemids, and Dawoodi Bohras believe that after the battle of Karbala (680 AD) in Iraq, the Umayyad Caliph Yezid brought Imam Husain's head to Damascus, where it was first kept in the courtyard of Yezid Mahal, now part of the Umayyad Mosque complex. All other remaining members of Imam Husain's family (left alive after Karbala), along with the heads of all other companions who were killed at Karbala, were also brought to Damascus. These members were kept as prisoners on the outskirts of the city (near [Bab al-Saghir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_al-Saghir)), where the other heads were kept at the same location, now called Ru'ûs ash-Shuhadâ-e-Karbala or ganj-e-sarha-e-shuhada-e-Karbala.[^cite_note-155] There is a qibla (place of worship) marked at the place, where devotees say [Imam Ali-Zain-ul-Abedin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_ibn_Husayn) used to pray while in captivity.

The [Harat Al Yehud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_Quarter_of_Damascus)[^cite_note-156] or Jewish Quarter is a recently restored historical tourist destination popular among Europeans before the outbreak of civil war.[^cite_note-157] Vacationers can enjoy the neighborhood and scenic ancient homes abandoned by the completely departed [Syrian Jewish community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Jews).[^cite_note-158]

### Walls and gates of Damascus



[Bab Tuma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_Tuma) gate

The Old City of Damascus with an approximate area of 86.12 hectares[^cite_note-159] is surrounded by ramparts on the northern and eastern sides and part of the southern side. There are seven extant city gates, the oldest of which dates back to the Roman period. These are, clockwise from the north of the citadel:

- [Bab al-Faradis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_al-Faradis) ("the gate of the orchards", or "of the paradise")

- [Bab al-Salam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_al-Salam) ("the gate of peace"), all on the north boundary of the Old City

- [Bab Tuma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_Tuma) ("Touma" or "Thomas's Gate") in the north-east corner, leading into the Christian quarter of the same name,

- [Bab Sharqi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_Sharqi) ("eastern gate") in the east wall, the only one to retain its Roman plan

- [Bab Kisan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_Kisan) in the south-east, from which tradition holds that Saint Paul made his escape from Damascus, lowered from the ramparts in a basket; this gate has been closed and turned into [Chapel of Saint Paul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapel_of_Saint_Paul) marking this event,

- [Bab al-Saghir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_al-Saghir) (The Small Gate), leading to [Bab al-Saghir Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_al-Saghir_Cemetery).

- [Bab al-Jabiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_al-Jabiya) at the entrance to Souk Midhat Pasha, in the southwest.

Other areas outside the walled city also bear the name "gate": [Bab al-Faraj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_al-Faraj_\(Damascus\)), [Bab Mousalla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_Mousalla?action=edit\&redlink=1) and [Bab Sreija](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_Sreija?action=edit\&redlink=1), both to the south-west of the walled city.

- [Bayt Farhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayt_Farhi?action=edit\&redlink=1), a large late-18th-century courtyard Jewish mansion built for the prominent Farhi family.

  [^cite_note-160]

- [Beit al-Mamlouka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beit_al-Mamlouka_Hotel), a 17th-century Damascene house, serving as a luxury [boutique hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boutique_hotel) within the old city since 2005.

### Threats to the future of the old City

Due to the rapid decline of the population of Old Damascus (between 1995 and 2009 about 30,000 people moved out of the old city for more modern accommodation),[^cite_note-Hendawi-161] a growing number of buildings are being abandoned or are falling into disrepair. In March 2007, the local government announced that it would be demolishing Old City buildings along a 1,400 m (4,600 ft) stretch of rampart walls as part of a redevelopment scheme. These factors resulted in the Old City being placed by the [World Monuments Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Monuments_Fund) on its 2008 Watch List of the 100 Most Endangered Sites in the world.[^cite_note-press_release-162][^cite_note-163] It is hoped that its inclusion on the list will draw more public awareness to these significant threats to the future of the historic Old City of Damascus.

#### State of old Damascus

In spite of the recommendations of the [UNESCO](21786641.md) World Heritage Center:[^cite_note-164]

- Souq al-Atiq, a protected buffer zone, was destroyed in three days in November 2006;

- King Faisal Street, a traditional hand-craft region in a protected buffer zone near the walls of Old Damascus between the Citadel and *Bab Touma*, is threatened by a proposed motorway.

- In 2007, the [Old City of Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_City_of_Damascus) and notably the district of [Bab Tuma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_Tuma) were recognized by The World Monument Fund as one of the most endangered sites in the world.

  [^cite_note-165]

In October 2010, [Global Heritage Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Heritage_Fund) named Damascus one of 12 [cultural heritage sites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_heritage) most "on the verge" of irreparable loss and destruction.[^cite_note-166]

## Transportation

Main articles: [Transport in Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_in_Syria) and [Rail transport in Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_transport_in_Syria)



[King Long](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Long) KLQ 6118GQ bus used for public transport in Damascus

Damascus is linked with other major cities in Syria via a [motorway network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_in_Syria). The [M5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M5_Motorway_\(Syria\)) connects Damascus with [Homs](77533.md), [Hama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hama), [Aleppo](159244.md) and [Turkey](11125639.md) in the north and [Jordan](7515964.md) in the south. The M1 is going from Homs onto [Latakia](195594.md) and [Tartus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tartus).

The [M4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M4_Motorway_\(Syria\)) links the city with [Al-Hasakah](7573335.md) and [Iraq](7515928.md). The [M1 highway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M1_motorway_\(Syria\)) connects the city to western Syria and [Beirut](37428.md).

The main airport is [Damascus International Airport](4202280.md), approximately 20 km (12 mi) away from the city, with connections to a few Middle Eastern cities. Before the beginning of the [Syrian civil war](30741795.md), the airport had connectivity to many Asian, European, African, and, South American cities.

Streets in Damascus are often narrow, especially in the older parts of the city, and speed bumps are widely used to limit the speed of vehicles. Many taxi companies operate in Damascus. Fares are regulated by law and taxi drivers are obliged to use a [taximeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taximeter).



[Al-Hejaz Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hejaz_Railway_Station)

[Public transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_transport) in Damascus depends extensively on [buses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buses) and [minibuses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minibuses). There are about one hundred lines that operate inside the city and some of them extend from the city center to nearby suburbs. There is no schedule for the lines, and due to the limited number of official bus stops, buses will usually stop wherever a passenger needs to get on or off. The number of buses serving the same line is relatively high, which minimizes the waiting time. Lines are not numbered, rather they are given captions mostly indicating the two endpoints and possibly an important station along the line. Between 2019 and 2022, more than 100 modern buses were delivered from [China](5405.md) as part of the international agreement. These deliveries strengthened and modernized the public transport of Damascus.[^cite_note-170][^cite_note-171]

Served by [Syrian Railways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Railways), the former main railway station of Damascus was [al-Hejaz railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hejaz_Railway), about 1 km (5⁄8 mi) west of the old city. The station is now defunct and the tracks have been removed, but there still is a ticket counter and a shuttle to Damascus [Qadam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qadam) station in the south of the city, which now functions as the main railway station.

In 2008, the government announced a plan to construct a Damascus Metro.[^cite_note-172] The green line will be an essential west–east axis for the future public transportation network, serving Moadamiyeh, Sumariyeh, Mezzeh, Damascus University, Hijaz, the Old City, Abbassiyeen and Qaboun Pullman bus station. A four-line metro network is expected to be in operation by 2050.

## Culture



[National Museum of Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Damascus)

Damascus was chosen as the [2008 Arab Capital of Culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Arab_Capital_of_Culture).[^cite_note-173] The preparation for the festivity began in February 2007 with the establishing of the Administrative Committee for "Damascus Arab Capital of Culture" by a presidential decree.[^cite_note-174] Museums in Damascus includes [National Museum of Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Damascus), [Azem Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azem_Palace), Military Museum, [October War Panorama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/October_War_Panorama) Museum, Museum of Arabic Calligraphy and [Nur al-Din Bimaristan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nur_al-Din_Bimaristan)

### Sports and leisure



[Al-Fayhaa Sports Complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Fayhaa_Sports_Complex)

Popular sports include [football](10568.md), [basketball](3921.md), swimming, tennis, table tennis, [equestrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equestrianism) and [chess](5134.md). Damascus is home to many football clubs that participate in the [Syrian Premier League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Premier_League) including [al-Jaish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Jaish_SC_\(Syria\)), [al-Shorta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Shorta_SC_\(Syria\)), [Al-Wahda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Wahda_SC_\(Syria\)) and [Al-Majd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Majd_SC). Many Other sports clubs are located in several districts of the city: [Barada SC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barada_SC), [Al-Nidal SC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Nidal_SC?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Al-Muhafaza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Muhafaza_SC), Qasioun SC, al-Thawra SC, Maysalun SC, al-Fayhaa SC, Dummar SC, al-Majd SC and al-Arin SC.

The [fifth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1976_Pan_Arab_Games) and the [seventh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1992_Pan_Arab_Games) [Pan Arab Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_Arab_Games) were held in Damascus in 1976 and 1992 respectively.

The now modernized [Al-Fayhaa Sports City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Fayhaa_Sports_Complex) features a basketball court and a hall that can accommodate up to 8,000 people. In late November 2021, [Syria's national basketball team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria's_national_basketball_team) played there against [Kazakhstan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazakhstan_men's_national_basketball_team), making Damascus host of Syria's first international basketball tournament in almost two decades.[^cite_note-175]

&#x20;The city also has a modern golf course located near the Ebla Cham Palace Hotel on the southeastern outskirts of Damascus.



[Booza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booza) being sold in the [Bakdash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bakdash_\(ice_cream_parlor\)) ice cream shop in the Damascus market

Damascus has a busy nightlife. [Coffeehouses](370815.md) offer [Arabic coffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_coffee), [tea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_tea) and [nargileh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nargileh) (water pipes). [Card games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Card_game), [tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tables_\(board_game\)) ([backgammon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backgammon) variants), and [chess](5134.md) are activities frequented in cafés.[^cite_note-176] These coffeehouses had an international reputation in the past, as indicated by [Letitia Elizabeth Landon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letitia_Elizabeth_Landon)'s poetical illustration, *Cafes in Damascus*, to a picture by [William Henry Bartlett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Henry_Bartlett) in Fisher's Drawing Room Scrap Book, 1837.[^cite_note-177] Current movies can be seen at Cinema City which was previously known as [Cinema Dimashq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinema_Dimashq).
