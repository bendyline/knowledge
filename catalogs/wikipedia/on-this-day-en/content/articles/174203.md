**Epsom** is a town in the borough of [Epsom and Ewell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_and_Ewell) in [Surrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrey), England, about 14 miles (22 kilometres) south of [central London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_London). The town is first recorded as *Ebesham* in the 10th century and its name probably derives from that of a [Saxon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_settlement_of_Britain) landowner. The earliest evidence of human activity is from the mid-[Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age_Britain), but the modern settlement probably grew up in the area surrounding St Martin's Church in the 6th or 7th centuries and the street pattern is thought to have become established in the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/England_in_the_Middle_Ages). Today the [High Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Street) is dominated by the clock tower, which was erected in 1847–8.

Like other nearby settlements, Epsom is located on the [spring line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_line_settlement) where the permeable [chalk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalk) of the [North Downs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Downs) meets the impermeable [London Clay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Clay). Several tributaries of the [Hogsmill River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hogsmill_River) rise in the town and in the 17th and early 18th centuries, the spring on [Epsom Common](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Common) was believed to have healing qualities. The mineral waters were found to be rich in *Epsom salts*, which were later identified as [magnesium sulphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium_sulphate). [Charles II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_II_of_England,_Scotland_and_Ireland) was among those who regularly [took the waters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Took_the_waters) and several prominent writers, including [John Aubrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Aubrey), [Samuel Pepys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Pepys) and [Celia Fiennes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celia_Fiennes) recorded their visits. The popularity of the [spa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spa) declined rapidly in the 1720s as a result of competition from other towns, including [Bath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bath,_Somerset) and [Tunbridge Wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Tunbridge_Wells).

Organised horse racing on [Epsom Downs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Downs) is believed to have taken place since the early 17th century. The popularity of Epsom grew as [The Oaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Oaks) and [The Derby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Derby) were established in 1779 and 1780 respectively. The first [grandstand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grandstand) at the [racecourse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Downs_Racecourse) was constructed in 1829 and more than 127,000 people attended Derby Day in 1843. During the [1913 Derby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1913_Epsom_Derby) the [suffragette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suffragette) [Emily Davison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emily_Davison) sustained fatal injuries after being hit by King [George V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_V)'s horse.

The opening of the [first railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Town_railway_station) in Epsom in 1847, coupled with the breakup of the Epsom Court estate, stimulated the development of the town. Today [Epsom station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_railway_station) is an important railway junction, where lines to [London Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Victoria_station) and [London Waterloo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Waterloo_station) diverge. Since 1946, the town has been surrounded on three sides by the [Metropolitan Green Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Green_Belt), which severely limits the potential for expansion. Two [local nature reserves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_nature_reserve), Epsom Common and [Horton Country Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horton_Country_Park), are to the west of the centre and Epsom Downs, to the south, is a [Site of Nature Conservation Importance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Site_of_Nature_Conservation_Importance).

## Toponymy

The first written record of a settlement at Epsom dates from the 10th century, when its name is given as *Ebesham*.[^cite_note-Gover_1934_p74-2] It appears in the [Domesday Book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domesday_Book) of 1086 as *Evesham*[^cite_note-Surrey_Domesday-3] and in subsequent surviving documents as *Ebbesham* and *Ebesam* (12th century), *Epsam* (15th century), *Ebbisham* and *Epsham* (16th century) and *Epsome* (17th century). The first known use of the modern spelling *Epsom* is from 1718.[^cite_note-Gover_1934_p74-2]

The name "Epsom" is thought to derive from that of a [Saxon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saxons) landowner, either as *Ebba's ham* or *Ebbi's ham* (where *ham* means home or settlement).[^cite_note-Gover_1934_p74-2][^cite_note-EEHE_Brief_History-4][^cite_note-6] Alternatively the name may come from *ebbe*, the [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English) word for "flow", which may reference an [intermittent stream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourne_\(stream\)) or [spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_\(hydrology\)) in the area.[^cite_note-Swete_1860_p8-7]

The first surviving record of land at [Horton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horton,_Surrey) is from a charter of 1178, in which the modern spelling is used. It is also recorded as *Hortune* (1263) and *Hortone* (1325). The name is thought to derive from the Old English words *horh* and *tun* and is generally agreed to mean a "muddy farm".[^cite_note-Gover_1934_p74-2]

The town centre crossroads, where Ashley and Waterloo Roads meet the High Street

Epsom is a town in north [Surrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrey), approximately 14 miles (22 km) south of central London. It lies on the southern edge of the [London Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Basin) and the highest point in the parish at Epsom Downs is 155 m (509 ft) above [ordnance datum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordnance_datum).[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_p1-8] The High Street defines the main west–east axis of the town and runs roughly parallel to the [Leatherhead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leatherhead_railway_station)-[Sutton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sutton_\(Surrey\)_railway_station) railway line.

To the west of the town centre are two [local nature reserves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_nature_reserve), [Epsom Common](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Common) and [Horton Country Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horton_Country_Park), both of which are owned and managed by the borough council.[^cite_note-EEBC_Common-9][^cite_note-HCP-10] [Epsom Downs Racecourse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Downs_Racecourse) is on Epsom Downs, an area of chalk [downland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downland) to the south of the town.[^cite_note-Epsom_Downs_BC-11]

Within the ancient parish boundaries are two settlements which were once part of Epsom. Horton, to the west of the centre, is the former location of five psychiatric hospitals, which were redeveloped to provide housing in the early 2000s.[^cite_note-EEBC_Horton-12] [Langley Vale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langley_Vale), a small village to the south of the racecourse, was predominantly developed in the first half of the 20th century.[^cite_note-13]

### Geology

Like many of the towns and villages between [Croydon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croydon) and [Guildford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guildford), Epsom is a [strip parish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strip_parish) and [spring line settlement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_line_settlement). The areas to the south and east of the town lie on the [chalk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalk_Group) of the [North Downs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Downs) and the centre, north and west are on the [London Clay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Clay). Separating the two, and running in parallel bands along a north–south axis, are narrow [outcrops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outcrop) of [Thanet Sands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thanet_Formation) and the [Lambeth Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambeth_Group).[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_p1-8][^cite_note-Geology_map-14][^cite_note-Lee_1849-15] The chalk is a natural [aquifer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquifer) and [tributaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tributary) of the [Hogsmill River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hogsmill_River) rise at several points along the boundary between the [permeable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permeability_\(Earth_sciences\)) and impermeable rock types. During the late 17th and early 18th century, the spring on Epsom Common was thought to have healing properties and the waters were later found to be rich in [magnesium sulphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium_sulfate), which became known as "[Epsom salts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsomite)".[^cite_note-Lee_1849-15][^cite_note-16]

## History

### Early history

The earliest evidence of human activity in Epsom is from the mid-[Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age_Britain). Remains of pits, ditches and post holes in Long Grove Road indicate that the area north of the town centre was used for agriculture in prehistoric times, which may suggest the presence of an established settlement nearby.[^cite_note-17] Pottery [sherds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherd) and worked [flints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flint), found on the site of the former [Manor Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manor_Hospital,_Epsom) site in Horton, show that human activity continued into the early [Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Iron_Age)[^cite_note-18] and two [staters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stater#Non-Greek_staters) (coins) from this period have been found in the area.[^cite_note-EUS_2003-19]

Although the route of [Stane Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stane_Street_\(Chichester\)), the road between *[Londinium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Londinium)* and [Chichester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noviomagus_Reginorum), passes immediately to the east of St Martin's Church, there is not thought to have been a [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Britain) settlement there.[^cite_note-EUS_2003-19][^cite_note-Hall_2008-20] Archaeological evidence of Roman activity has been found to the north of the present town centre: A [tile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tile) [kiln](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiln), which may have been connected to the 1st- and 2nd-century [brickworks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brickworks) on [Ashtead Common](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashtead_Common), was discovered during the construction of the [West Park Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Park_Hospital,_Epsom) in the 1920s.[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_pp2-4-21][^cite_note-22] There may also have been a [villa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_villa) at Epsom Court.[^cite_note-EEHE_Brief_History-4]

The [Anglo-Saxon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Saxon) settlement at Epsom was most likely established in the late 6th or early 7th century.[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_pp2-4-21][^cite_note-White_Harte_1992_intro-23] It would have been administered as part of [Copthorne Hundred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copthorne_Hundred) and was probably located close to the site of the present St Martin's Church, which is built on a [knoll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hillock) of chalk at the base of the North Downs.[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_pp2-4-21][^cite_note-White_Harte_1992_intro-23][^cite_note-Nail_1965-24][^cite_note-25] Saxon burials have been excavated at two sites to the east of the modern town centre, close to the church.[^cite_note-26][^cite_note-27] A [garnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garnet) [pendant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendant), depicting a bearded male wearing a [Phrygian cap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phrygian_cap), was discovered in the same area in the 1960s. The pendant is thought to have originated from the Mediterranean in the 7th century and its discovery suggests that high-status individuals were living in or travelling though Epsom in the Anglo-Saxon period.[^cite_note-White_Harte_1992_intro-23][^cite_note-28]

The origins of the modern town centre are obscure, but the principal road pattern may have developed during the Middle Ages. In the 12th century, a [nucleated village](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleated_village) is thought to have been founded at the west end of the present High Street.[^cite_note-EUS_2003-19] The 1840 [tithe map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tithe_map) shows a series of narrow, regular [plots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_lot) along the north side of the High Street, which would be consistent with a planned, medieval settlement.[^cite_note-EUS_2003-19]

### Governance

Epsom was held by [Chertsey Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chertsey_Abbey) from Anglo-Saxon times until the first half of the 16th century. The earliest surviving charter confirming the ownership of the town is from 967, during the reign of [King Edgar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_the_Peaceful), although the settlement is thought to have been granted to the abbey in 727.[^cite_note-Malden_1911_pp271-278-29][^cite_note-30] In [Domesday Book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domesday_Book), Epsom appears as *Evesham* and is listed as including two [mills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mill_\(grinding\)) (valued at 10[s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_shilling_coin)), two churches, 24 acres (10 hectares) of [meadow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meadow) and sufficient [woodland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodland) for 20 [hogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hog_\(swine\)). It rendered £17 per year in 1086. The residents included 34 villagers, 4 smallholders and 6 [villeins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villein).[^cite_note-Surrey_Domesday-3][^cite_note-Epsom_Domesday-31]

The manor of Horton was also held by Chertsey Abbey during the medieval period. Although there are few surviving early documents relating to the settlement, an early 15th century charter indicates that it included 168 acres (68 hectares) of land, of which 60 acres (24 ha) was pasture. The Durdans estate may also have belonged to Horton.[^cite_note-Malden_1911_pp271-278-29][^cite_note-32] There are references in historical documents to a manor of Brettgrave, also held by the abbey, which appears to have been considered part of Horton by the early 16th century.[^cite_note-Malden_1911_pp271-278-29]

Following the [dissolution of the monasteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_monasteries), [Henry VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_VIII) granted Epsom to [Nicholas Carew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Carew_\(Henry_VIII_courtier\)) in 1537, but two years later his property was forfeit when he was executed for his alleged involvement in the [Exeter Conspiracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exeter_Conspiracy). The manor was part of the [Honour of Hampton Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honour_of_Hampton_Court) from 1597 until 1554, when it was granted to [Francis Carew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Carew_\(MP_for_Castle_Rising\)) by [Mary I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_I).[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_pp5-7-33] After 1611, Epsom was briefly held by the Darcy family and was then sold to Anne Mynne, the widow of George Mynne, who had bought the manor of Horton. Both manors were inherited by her daughter, Elizabeth, who in 1648 married Richard Evelyn, the brother of the diarist, [John Evelyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Evelyn). Richard Evelyn died in 1670, but Elizabeth ran the estate until her death in 1692, when the land was split up.[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_pp5-7-33] For much of the 18th century, Epsom was held by the Parkhurst family and thereafter the lordship passed through a succession of owners before it was purchased by the borough council in 1955.[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_pp5-7-33]

[Reforms during the Tudor period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tudor_period#Local_government) reduced the importance of [manorial courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manorial_court) and the day-to-day administration of towns became the responsibility of the [vestry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vestry) of the parish church.[^cite_note-Kümin_1996_pp250-255-34] The Epsom Vestry appears to have operated as a [select vestry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vestry#Select_vestry), to which members of the gentry were appointed or [co-opted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Co-option).[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_p19-35] There was little change in local government structure over the subsequent three centuries, until the [Poor Law Amendment Act 1834](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poor_Law_Amendment_Act_1834) transferred responsibility for [poor relief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poor_relief) to the [Poor Law Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poor_Law_Commission), whose local powers were delegated to the newly formed [poor law union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poor_law_union) in 1836. The Epsom Union was made responsible for workhouses in around a dozen parishes in the area, all of which were consolidated into a workhouse on the Dorking Road, now the site of Epsom Hospital.[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_p24-36][^cite_note-Abdy_2001_p26-37] A Local Board of Health, with responsibility for sanitation, sewerage and drinking water supply, was formed in 1850.[^cite_note-Home_1901_p32-38]

The [Local Government Act 1888](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_Government_Act_1888) transferred many administrative responsibilities to the newly formed [Surrey County Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrey_County_Council) and was followed by an [1894 Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_Government_Act_1894) that created the Epsom Urban District Council (UDC). The UDC area was expanded to include [Ewell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ewell) in 1933[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_pp71-73-39] and the enlarged authority was awarded borough status in 1937.[^cite_note-40]

In 1951 the [civil parish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_parish) had a population of 30,860.[^cite_note-41] On 1 April 1974 the parish was abolished.[^cite_note-42]

### Epsom spa



The wellhead marking the site of the original Epsom Well was constructed in 1989.

[^cite_note-43]

By tradition, the discovery of spa water is attributed to Henry Wicker, a farmer who, in 1618, noticed that his cows refused to drink from a slow spring on Epsom Common.[^cite_note-Home_1901_pp43-45-44] However, the distinct [chemical properties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_property) of the local water had already been recognised in the preceding decades: In 1603, local physicians had noted that the local water contained "bitter purging salt"[^cite_note-Osbourne_Weaver_1996_pp27-28-45] and, in late-[Elizabethan times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabethan_era), it was thought that bathing in a pond to the west of the town centre could cure [ulcers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skin_ulcer) and other disorders.[^cite_note-Home_1901_pp43-45-44]

The first facilities for visitors were provided in 1621, when a wall was built around the spring and a shed erected for invalids.[^cite_note-Osbourne_Weaver_1996_pp27-28-45] The first authentic account of the spa dates from 1629, when Abram Booth, of the [Dutch East India Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_East_India_Company), visited Epsom and described how "\[p]eople coming there took a few glasses of the mentioned water – which has a taste different from ordinary water – after which walking up and down, these had in our opinion very good effect".[^cite_note-Osbourne_Weaver_1996_pp27-28-45][^cite_note-Abdy_2001_pp11-12-46] During the mid-17th century, several prominent individuals travelled to the spring, including [John Aubrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Aubrey), who after his visit in 1654, boiled some of the water and noted that a "flakey" [sediment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sediment), "the colour of bay-salt", was left behind.[^cite_note-Osbourne_Weaver_1996_pp27-28-45][^cite_note-47] [Samuel Pepys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Pepys) visited in both 1663 and 1667[^cite_note-Home_1901_p48-48][^cite_note-50][^cite_note-52] and the theologian, [John Owen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Owen_\(theologian\)), took the waters in 1668.[^cite_note-Home_1901_pp43-45-44]

Following the [Restoration of the monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_Restoration), [Charles II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_II_of_England,_Scotland_and_Ireland) was a regular visitor[^cite_note-Home_1901_p50-53] and it was at Epsom that he met the actress, [Nell Gwyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nell_Gwyn), who became his [mistress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mistress_\(lover\)).[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_pp11-12-46] Other royal patrons included [Prince George](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_George_of_Denmark), the [prince consort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_consort) of [Queen Anne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne,_Queen_of_Great_Britain).[^cite_note-Home_1901_pp60-61-54]



The Assembly Rooms, High Street

Despite the popularity of the spring on the Common, several early visitors were critical of their experience at Epsom. The writer, [Dorothy Osborne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorothy_Osborne), who visited in 1653, complained that the water had to stand overnight to allow the sediment to settle before drinking.[^cite_note-Osbourne_Weaver_1996_pp34-36-55] Similarly, [Celia Fiennes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celia_Fiennes) noted that Epsom was "not a quick spring", that it was often "drank drye" and to make up the deficiency, "the people do often carry water from the Common wells to fill this in a morning; this they have found out in which makes the water weake and of little operation - unless you can have it first from the well before they can have put in any other".[^cite_note-Osbourne_Weaver_1996_pp34-36-55][^cite_note-Fiennes_1888_289-56]

The popularity of Epsom continued to increase in the final decades of the 17th century and a regular coach service from London was established in 1684.[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_pp16-17-57] In the 1690s, John Parkhurst, Lord of the Manor of Epsom, began to develop the town into a spa resort.[^cite_note-Home_1901_pp43-45-44] The Assembly Rooms in the High Street were constructed in 1692 and a new well was sunk to the west of South Street. By 1707, a local businessman and [apothecary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apothecary), John Livingstone, had opened a bowling green, [gaming rooms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gambling) and a [ballroom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballroom).[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_pp16-17-57]

In the mid-1720s, the popularity of Epsom experienced a rapid decline, driven partly by the economic consequences of the [bursting of the South Sea Bubble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Sea_Company).[^cite_note-Home_1901_pp60-61-54] There was also competition from other spa towns, including Bath and Tunbridge Wells,[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_p18-58] and, by the 1750s, synthetic Epsom salts were being manufactured commercially.[^cite_note-Osbourne_Weaver_1996_p48-59][^cite_note-60] Attempts were made in the 1760s to revive the spa, but these efforts were unsuccessful.[^cite_note-Home_1901_pp60-61-54]

### Horse racing



*[The 1821 Derby at Epsom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_1821_Derby_at_Epsom)* (1821) by [Théodore Géricault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Th%C3%A9odore_G%C3%A9ricault)

Main article: [Epsom Downs Racecourse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Downs_Racecourse)

The earliest horse races on Epsom Downs are thought to have been held in the early 17th century, during the reign of [James I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_VI_and_I). By the time of the [Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Civil_War), the sport was sufficiently popular and well known that, in May 1648, [royalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavalier) forces were able to assemble on the Downs under the pretence of organising a race, before marching together to [Reigate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reigate). Following the Restoration of the monarchy, there was a further increase in the popularity of racing as Epsom became established as a spa town.[^cite_note-Home_1901_pp108-109-61]

The Irish philosopher, [John Toland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Toland), noted the suitability of the Downs for sport, writing in 1711 that the land was "covered with grass finer than Persian carpets… for sheep-walks, riding, hunting, racing, shooting, with games of most sorts for exercise of the body or recreation of the mind… they are no where else to be paralleled".[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_p90-62] The earliest formal races were run on an uphill course from [Carshalton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carshalton) to Epsom and were primarily a test of stamina rather than speed.[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_p90-62] By the mid-18th century a four-mile (six-kilometre) straight course had been established, starting at Banstead and finishing at the top of the Downs at Epsom. Heats were run in the mornings, before a break for lunch, which was followed by the final run offs in the late afternoon.[^cite_note-Hunn_1973_p40-63]



*[Diomed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diomed)* by [John Nost Sartorius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Nost_Sartorius) (1755–1828). Diomed was the winner of [the first Derby in 1780](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1780_Epsom_Derby).

[The Oaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Oaks) was established in 1779 and was named after the residence of the [12th Earl of Derby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Smith-Stanley,_12th_Earl_of_Derby) at Banstead.[^cite_note-Home_1901_pp111-112-64] The race, for three-year-old [fillies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filly), was shorter than those that had taken place in previous years and was run over a 1+1⁄2-mile (2.4 km) course. [The Derby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Derby) was first run the following year, as a shorter race of 1 mi (1.6 km) for three-year-old [colts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colt_\(horse\)),[^cite_note-Hunn_1973_p45-65] but was increased to 1+1⁄2 mi (2.4 km) in 1784.[^cite_note-Brannan_2004_p6-66] As early as 1793, the crowds associated with Derby Day were causing congestion on local roads and, in 1843, more than 127,000 people attended the race. Formal royal patronage began in 1840 with the visit of [Queen Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Victoria) and horses owned by her son, [Edward VII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_VII), won the event in 1896, 1900 and 1909.[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_p95-67] The world's first [live](4789487.md) [outside broadcast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outside_broadcasting) was filmed at the 1931 Derby by the [Baird Television Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Logie_Baird).[^cite_note-Hunn_1973_p141-68][^cite_note-69]

During the [1913 Derby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1913_Epsom_Derby), the [suffragette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suffragette), [Emily Davison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emily_Davison), [sustained fatal injuries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emily_Davison#Fatal_injury_at_the_Derby) after being hit by King [George V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_V)'s horse, Anmer.[^cite_note-Tanner_2013_pp214-215-70] The incident occurred at Tattenham Corner, the final curve before the finishing straight, after she ducked under the [guard rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guard_rail) and ran onto the course.[^cite_note-Tanner_2013_pp214-215-70] Davison was knocked unconscious and was taken to Epsom Cottage Hospital, where she died four days later on 8 June 1913.[^cite_note-Morley_1988_p103-71][^cite_note-Tanner_2013_pp284-285-72] The inquest into her death took place at Epsom on 10 June.[^cite_note-73][^cite_note-74] A road close to Tattenham Corner is named "Emily Davison Drive" in her honour[^cite_note-75][^cite_note-76] and a statue of the suffragette was installed in Epsom High Street in June 2021.[^cite_note-Orlando_2021-77]

The first permanent building on Epsom Downs is thought to have been a rubbing house, where horses could be brushed down and washed after racing. Parts of the Rubbing House pub, which now occupies the site, date from 1801.[^cite_note-Wakefield_1997_p8-78][^cite_note-79] Until the early 19th century, facilities for race attendees were limited to temporary wood and canvas structures and construction of the first [grandstand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grandstand) did not begin until 1829.[^cite_note-Wakefield_1997_p8-78][^cite_note-Abdy_2001_p103-80] The Queen's Stand, which incorporates a conference centre, was completed in 1992[^cite_note-Wakefield_1997_p8-78][^cite_note-Abdy_2001_p103-80] and the current grandstand was opened in 2009.[^cite_note-81][^cite_note-82]

### Transport and communications

&#x20;



Extract from *Mogg's Twenty Four Miles Round London, 1820* showing the turnpike road through Epsom

Following the [end of Roman rule in Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End_of_Roman_rule_in_Britain), there appears to have been no systematic planning of transport infrastructure in the local area for over a [millennium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennium). The section of Stane Street to the west of the modern town centre remained in use through the Anglo-Saxon period and is referred to as the Portway in the reign of [Henry VII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_VII_of_England). The section of the Roman road to the south of Epsom is thought to have been blocked by the enclosure of [Woodcote Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodcote_Park) in the mid-12th century.[^cite_note-Nail_1965-24] Visitors to the spa were able to reach Epsom by carriage during the 17th century, although the [turnpikes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turnpike_trust) to London and [Horsham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horsham) were not constructed until 1755.[^cite_note-83][^cite_note-84]



[Epsom Town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Town_railway_station) station building (LBSCR), photographed c. 1900

The first railway line to reach Epsom was the [London, Brighton and South Coast Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London,_Brighton_and_South_Coast_Railway) (LBSCR), which opened [a railway station in the Upper High Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Town_railway_station) in 1847. Services initially ran to Croydon, where there was a junction with the [Brighton Main Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brighton_Main_Line).[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_pp29-31-85] The line between [Epsom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_railway_station) and [Leatherhead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leatherhead_railway_station) was opened by the independent Epsom and Leatherhead Railway Company on 1 February 1859. It was initially constructed as a single-track line and services were run by the [London and South Western Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_and_South_Western_Railway) (LSWR). The LSWR's own line via [Worcester Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worcester_Park_railway_station) towards [Wimbledon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wimbledon_railway_station) was completed two months later, allowing trains from Epsom to reach [London Waterloo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Waterloo_railway_station).[^cite_note-Vardey_1988_pp168-169-86][^cite_note-Stuttard_1995_pp85-90-87] The LBSCR extended its line westwards to meet that of the LSWR in August of the same year, allowing it to run services to Leatherhead.[^cite_note-Cockman_Marshall_1988_p128-88] Two branch lines serving the race course opened to [Epsom Downs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Downs_railway_station) and [Tattenham Corner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tattenham_Corner_railway_station) in 1865 and 1901 respectively.[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_pp29-31-85]

Although both the LSWR and LBSCR ran services along the line to Leatherhead, the two companies maintained separate stations in Epsom for 70 years. Following the formation of the [Southern Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Railway_\(UK\)) in 1923, a decision was taken to combine the two on a single site. The LBSCR station was closed in 1929 and the LSWR station was reconstructed to increase the number of platforms to four, so that all trains passing through the town could serve it.[^cite_note-Cockman_Marshall_1988_p128-88][^cite_note-91] The changes coincided with the [electrification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railway_electrification_in_Great_Britain) of the lines to London Waterloo and London Victoria in 1925 and 1929 respectively.[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_p68-92] These improvements resulted in a dramatic increase in the number of [season tickets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Season_ticket) sold and stimulated the growth of the town from a population of 18,804 in 1921 to 27,089 in 1931.[^cite_note-Cockman_Marshall_1988_p138-93]

A regular postal service between Epsom and London was established in 1678. Initially the service ran three times per week,[^cite_note-94] but ran daily from 1683.[^cite_note-95] The current Post Office in the High Street opened in 1897 and a sorting office in East Street opened in 1956.[^cite_note-96] The first automatic [telephone exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephone_exchange) in the UK was opened in the town in May 1912, replacing a manual exchange dating from 1905. The system used [switching equipment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strowger_switch) designed by the American inventor, [Almon Brown Strowger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almon_Brown_Strowger), and had provision for a maximum of 500 [lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephone_line).[^cite_note-97]

### Residential development

Epsom is unusual among Surrey towns, in that it underwent considerable expansion during the [early modern period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_modern_period).[^cite_note-EUS_2003-19] As the spa developed in the 1670s, merchants from London started to construct their own mansions on the outskirts of the town. These [baroque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baroque_architecture) buildings were generally made from bricks produced at the [brickfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brickfield) on the Common. The demand for labourers brought incomers with new skills, who began to boost the local economy. The construction of a small row of shops at the junction of the High Street and South Street led to the establishment of a market in 1680 and, in 1692, two London [goldsmiths](323246.md) developed the site further by building their own houses.[^cite_note-White_Harte_1992_intro-23]

&#x20;



Epsom in 1816

By 1800, Epsom had over 400 houses and this number increased to 600 by the middle of the century.[^cite_note-EUS_2003-19] In the 1860s, plans were put forward to improve the drainage on the Common and to build houses on it, but were dropped due to local objections. Following the enclosure of the common fields to the south of the town centre in 1869, housebuilding commenced along Burgh Heath, College and Worple Roads. At around the same time, houses for the working classes were constructed in the area between the two railway lines to Sutton and Wimbledon.[^cite_note-White_Harte_1992_intro-23]

In the final decade of the 19th century, Epsom Court, to the north of the town centre, was divided and sold for development and, over the next twenty years, [terraced houses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terraced_house) were built on the land.[^cite_note-White_Harte_1992_intro-23] Shops on Waterloo Road and houses in Chase Road were built from 1928 and the Copse Edge Avenue estate was begun in the same year.[^cite_note-Essen_1992_p7-98] The award of borough status to the council in 1937, allowed it to take over running of 1500 council houses that had been built between 1920 and 1939.[^cite_note-Essen_1992_p8-99]

The 1944 [Greater London Plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_London_Plan) designated land on three sides of the town centre as part of the protected [Metropolitan Green Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Green_Belt), which severely limited the scope for urban expansion to the east, west and south.[^cite_note-100] Since limited development of [Langley Vale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langley_Vale) and the Epsom Wells estate had taken place in the early 1930s, these areas were not placed into the Green Belt and housebuilding continued into the 1950s.[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_p79-101] In the early 2000s, the [Epsom Cluster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Cluster) of psychiatric hospitals in Horton was redeveloped to provide around 1850 new homes.[^cite_note-EEBC_Horton-12]

### Commerce and industry

From much of its early history, Epsom was primarily an [agricultural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture_in_the_United_Kingdom) settlement. The two common fields attached to the medieval manor, Woodcote (350 acres (140 ha)) and Smith Hatch (500 acres (200 ha)), were divided into strips, which were assigned to residents of the town.[^cite_note-White_1928_pp54-55-102] In the 15th century, sheep farming became increasingly important and a wool fair was held in June each year until the 1870s.[^cite_note-White_1928_pp54-55-102] There was also an annual pleasure fair which took place in July.[^cite_note-White_1928_pp54-55-102] The right to hold a market at Epsom was granted to Elizabeth Evelyn by [Charles II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_II_of_England) and the charter was renewed by [James II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_II_of_England) in 1685.[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_pp5-6-103] [Enclosure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enclosure) of the common fields was completed in 1869, bringing to an end the [open-field system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-field_system) in the town.[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_p27-104][^cite_note-106]



The High Street in the early 1900s

Brick and tile manufacturing took place on Epsom Common until the end of the 18th century[^cite_note-Harte_2017_pp75-76-107] and between 1830 and 1938 at a site on Kiln Lane.[^cite_note-Wakefield_1997_pp26-27-108] In the 19th century, chalk was quarried and converted to lime at a site in College Road.[^cite_note-Wakefield_1997_pp26-27-108] Mineral water and fruit juices were bottled in the town by the Randalls company from 1884, which traded from premises in South Street until the 1980s.[^cite_note-Wakefield_1997_pp29-30-109] In the 19th century, there were two breweries in Epsom - WG Bradley and Son in South Street and Pagden's in Church Street.[^cite_note-Wakefield_1997_pp29-30-109] Dorling's Printworks was established in the town in the 1820s and for many years printed [racecards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racecard) and [brochures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brochure) for the Derby, before its closure in 1979.[^cite_note-Wakefield_1997_pp29-30-109] Today, one of the largest private employers in the town is the engineering consultancy firm [Atkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atkins_\(company\)), which moved to Epsom in 1962.[^cite_note-110]

### Epsom in the world wars



New recruits in Epsom High Street, 19 September 1914

In September 1914, Epsom became a [garrison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garrison) town. Two battalions of the University and Public Schools Brigade of the [Royal Fusiliers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Fusiliers) were billeted in the town and underwent training in Rosebery Park and on Epsom Downs.[^cite_note-Essen_1991_pp9-10-111] Some of the racecourse buildings were converted to become a war hospital and, in January 1915, 20,000 soldiers were assembled on the Downs for an inspection by [Lord Kitchener](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Kitchener,_1st_Earl_Kitchener).[^cite_note-112][^cite_note-113]



Soldiers from the Royal Fusiliers at Woodcote Park in 1915

Woodcote Park Camp was built to house the soldiers of the Royal Fusiliers in late 1914 and early 1915.[^cite_note-Essen_1991_pp9-10-111] The 350-acre (140 ha) site was considered suitable as it was close to London.[^cite_note-Essen_1991_p7-114] The first soldiers took up residence in February 1915, but the camp was vacated by the Brigade when it was dispatched to the [Western Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Front_\(World_War_I\)) three months later.[^cite_note-Essen_1991_pp9-10-111] Between May 1915 and August 1916 the camp became a convalescent hospital for Commonwealth soldiers.[^cite_note-Essen_1991_pp13-14-115] It was handed over to Canadian Forces in July 1916 and remained under their control until the end of the war.[^cite_note-Essen_1991_pp17-18-116] Following the end of the [First World War](4764461.md), the Canadian soldiers were slow to be repatriated.[^cite_note-Shortland_1998_p5-117] On 17 June 1919, a group of 400 [attacked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_riot) Epsom Police station, injuring [Station Sergeant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_ranks_of_the_United_Kingdom) Thomas Green, who died the following day.[^cite_note-118] At a trial in July 1919, five men were found guilty of riotous assembly[^cite_note-119] and were imprisoned until November of the same year.[^cite_note-Shortland_1998_p5-117]

By the outbreak of the [Second World War](32927.md) in September 1939, public [air raid shelters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_raid_shelter) had been constructed at Rosebery Park and Clay Hill Green.[^cite_note-120] Later in the war, large shelters were provided at Ashley Road, to the south of the town centre, and at Epsom Downs.[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_pp75-78-121][^cite_note-123] Over the course of the war around 440 [high-explosive bombs](47628.md) were dropped on the borough, 33 people were killed and nearly 200 houses were destroyed. Towards the end of the conflict, 30 [V-1 flying bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V-1_flying_bomb) landed in the area, including one, in July 1944, which destroyed the Ashley Road Police Station.[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_pp75-78-121]

During the Second World War, the defence of the town was largely in the hands of the 56th Surrey battalion of the [Home Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_Guard_\(United_Kingdom\)),[^cite_note-Crook_2000_p25-124] who were trained by a company of [Welsh Guards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Guards) at Epsom Racecourse.[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_p77-125][^cite_note-Crook_2000_p30-126] Throughout the borough there were 55 [Air Raid Precautions (ARP)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Raid_Precautions_in_the_United_Kingdom) posts, staffed by up to 140 paid air raid wardens and 560 volunteers.[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_p75-127] In mid-1940, the Outer London Defence Line A was constructed through Epsom, running along Christchurch Road, the High Street and Alexandra Road.[^cite_note-Wakefield_1997_p35-128][^cite_note-129]

## National and local government

### UK parliament

The town is in the parliamentary constituency of [Epsom and Ewell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_and_Ewell_\(UK_Parliament_constituency\)) and has been represented [at Westminster](13964.md) since July 2024 by [Liberal Democrat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Democrats_\(UK\)) [Helen Maguire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_Maguire).

### County council

Councillors are elected to [Surrey County Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrey_County_Council) every four years. The town is divided between two [wards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wards_and_electoral_divisions_of_the_United_Kingdom): "Epsom Town & Downs" and "Epsom West".[^cite_note-130]

### Borough council



[Epsom Town Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Town_Hall)

Epsom is divided between five wards, each of which elects three councillors to [Epsom and Ewell Borough Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_and_Ewell). The five wards are: "Stamford", "Court", "Town", "College" and "Woodcote".[^cite_note-131][^cite_note-132]

Since 1995, the Borough of Epsom and Ewell has been [twinned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sister_cities) with [Chantilly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chantilly,_Oise) in northern France.[^cite_note-133][^cite_note-134]

## Public services

### Utilities

The public [gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_gas) supply to Epsom began in 1839,[^cite_note-Tarplee_Gas_Electricity-135] when the Epsom and Ewell Gas Company purchased land on East Street for the town [gasworks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gasworks). Initially [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal) was transported by road from [Battersea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battersea), but was delivered by train following the opening of the railway in 1847.[^cite_note-Bouchard_gas-136] Gas-powered street lighting was installed in the town centre by 1840.[^cite_note-EEHE_water-137] The Epsom and Ewell Gas Company was amalgamated with the Wandsworth and Putney Gas Light & Coke Company and the Mitcham and Wimbledon District Gas Light Company in 1912.[^cite_note-Bouchard_gas-136]

The Epsom [waterworks](684928.md) were established on East Road in 1853. By 1870, there were at least two wells on the site, which supplied water to the town.[^cite_note-138] Until the mid-19th century, [sewage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sewage) was disposed of in [cesspits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cesspit), however the high [water table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_table) in the town often lead to drinking water becoming contaminated.[^cite_note-EEHE_water-137] The pond in the centre of High Street, which had become polluted with waste, was filled in 1854 under the direction of the newly formed [local board of health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_board_of_health).[^cite_note-139] The first [sewerage system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sewerage) was created the same year, which included the construction of a [sewage farm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sewage_farm) on Hook Road (then known as Kingston Lane). A comprehensive drainage plan was produced in 1895, after the formation of Epsom UDC, and arrangements were made to share the Leatherhead sewage [outfall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outfall). The site of the sewage farm was redeveloped and is now the Longmead Industrial Estate.[^cite_note-EEHE_water-137] Under the Water Act 1973, control of the water supply passed to the [Thames Water Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thames_Water_Authority), which was privatised as [Thames Water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thames_Water) in 1989.[^cite_note-Wakefield_1997_p22-140][^cite_note-141]

An [electricity generating station](212141.md) was opened in 1902 in Depot Road. Initially it was capable of generating 220 kW of power, but by the time of its closure in 1939, its [installed capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nameplate_capacity) was 2 MW.[^cite_note-Tarplee_Gas_Electricity-135][^cite_note-Crocker_1999_p113-142] Under the [Electricity (Supply) Act 1926](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electricity_\(Supply\)_Act_1926), Epsom was connected to the [National Grid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Grid_\(Great_Britain\)), initially to a 33 kV [supply ring](38824.md), which linked the town to [Croydon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croydon), Leatherhead, Dorking and Reigate. In 1939, the ring was connected to the [Wimbledon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wimbledon,_London)-[Woking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woking) main via a 132 kV [substation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_substation) at Leatherhead.[^cite_note-Tarplee_Gas_Electricity-135][^cite_note-Crocker_1999_p118-143]

### Emergency services and justice

Following the [County Courts Act 1848](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Courts_Act), a courthouse was built in Epsom.[^cite_note-Abdy_1996-144] The [County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Court) and [Magistrates' Courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magistrates'_Court) closed in 2010.[^cite_note-145] Epsom Police Station was opened in Church Street in July 1963. The Epsom force had been part of the [Metropolitan Police](192450.md) since 1829, but was transferred to [Surrey Police](3006917.md) in April 2000.[^cite_note-Abdy_1996-144][^cite_note-146]



Epsom Fire Station, Church Street

Epsom is thought to have acquired a "manual" [fire engine](146363.md) in around the 1760s. It was operated by volunteers and was kept at the clock tower in the High Street. The first full-time [brigade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_department) was established in 1870 and, by the end of the century, was based on Waterloo Road. The current fire station in Church Street was opened in 1937.[^cite_note-147] In 2021, the [fire authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_authority) for Epsom is Surrey County Council and the [statutory fire service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_services_in_the_United_Kingdom) is [Surrey Fire and Rescue Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrey_Fire_and_Rescue_Service).[^cite_note-148] Epsom Ambulance Station is run by the [South East Coast Ambulance Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_East_Coast_Ambulance_Service).[^cite_note-149]

### Healthcare

[Epsom Cottage Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Cottage_Hospital), the first hospital in Epsom, was opened in Pikes Hill in 1873. It moved to Hawthorne Place in 1877 and, in 1889, a new building was constructed in Alexandra Road to celebrate [Queen Victoria's Diamond Jubilee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Victoria#Diamond_Jubilee).[^cite_note-Epsom_NHS_our_story-150] The [suffragette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suffragette), [Emily Davison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emily_Davison), died at the hospital after being hit by King [George V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_V)'s horse at the [1913 Derby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1913_Epsom_Derby).[^cite_note-Morley_1988_p103-71][^cite_note-Tanner_2013_pp284-285-72] Epsom Cottage Hospital officially closed in 1988, however the premises are used today as a community hospital offering physiotherapy and rehabilitation services.[^cite_note-151]

[Epsom Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Hospital) was built by the Poor Law Guardians adjacent to the [workhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Workhouse) on Dorking Road in 1890.[^cite_note-Epsom_NHS_our_story-150][^cite_note-Abdy_2001_p44-152] It was made the responsibility of Surrey County Council in 1930[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_p69-153] and became part of the NHS in 1948.[^cite_note-Epsom_NHS_our_story-150] Since April 1999, it has been run by Epsom and St Helier University Hospitals NHS Trust, following a merger between the Epsom Health Care and St Helier NHS Trusts.[^cite_note-154] Epsom Hospital has an [A\&E department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergency_department).[^cite_note-155]



[St Ebba's Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Ebba's_Hospital), designed by William Clifford Smith, was opened in 1903.

[^cite_note-156]

The [Epsom Cluster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Cluster) was a group of five [psychiatric hospitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychiatric_hospitals), built to the west of Epsom on land purchased by [London County Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_County_Council) in 1896.[^cite_note-Epsom_Cluster_EEHE-157] The [Manor Asylum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manor_Hospital,_Epsom) was the first to be opened in 1899[^cite_note-158] and the fifth, [West Park Asylum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Park_Hospital,_Epsom), was fully completed in June 1924, having been used by the Canadian military during WWI.[^cite_note-159] A [light railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horton_Light_Railway) was constructed to deliver building materials and coal to the construction sites.[^cite_note-160] Clean water was supplied from a borehole and a power station was constructed to provide electricity to the five institutions.[^cite_note-Epsom_Cluster_EEHE-157] All of the hospitals closed during the 1990s and 2000s and their sites have since been redeveloped for housing.[^cite_note-Epsom_Cluster_EEHE-157]

As of 2021, the town has three GP practices.[^cite_note-161]

### Independent schools



Epsom College

[Epsom College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_College) was founded in 1851 as the Medical Benevolent College by the physician, [John Propert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Propert). The school buildings were opened in 1855 by [Prince Albert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert,_Prince_Consort) and the first cohort of 100 pupils were all sons of medics.[^cite_note-Home_1901_pp41-42-189] By 1865, the school roll had grown to 300 and had been opened to those able to pay fees.[^cite_note-190] The Grade II-listed chapel, dedicated to [St Luke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luke_the_Evangelist), was built in 1857[^cite_note-EC_history-191] and was enlarged by [Arthur Blomfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Blomfield) in 1895.[^cite_note-192] Girls were first admitted to the school in 1976[^cite_note-EC_history-191] and Epsom College became fully coeducational in 1996.[^cite_note-Scadding_2004_pp130-131-193]

[Kingswood House School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingswood_House_School) is a [preparatory school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preparatory_school_\(United_Kingdom\)) to the west of the town centre. It was founded in 1899 and moved to its present site on West Hill in 1920. Since the 1960s it has operated exclusively as a [day school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Day_school). ## Places of worship

See also: [List of places of worship in Epsom and Ewell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_places_of_worship_in_Epsom_and_Ewell)

### St Martin's Church



St Martin's Church, Epsom

[\[note 12\]](#cite_note-196)

The Domesday Book entry for Epsom includes two churches,[^cite_note-Epsom_Domesday-31] one of which is thought to have been on the site of the present St Martin's Church.[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_p10-197] The oldest part of the current building is the [flint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flint) [tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower), which dates from around 1450. Much of the remainder of the church dates from a rebuilding in 1824, with the exception of the [choir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choir_\(architecture\)), [chancel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancel) and [transepts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transept), which were added by [Sir Charles Nicholson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Charles_Nicholson,_2nd_Baronet) in 1908.[^cite_note-198] The 15th century [spire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spire) was damaged in a storm in 1947 and was subsequently demolished.[^cite_note-Berry_1992_p31-199]

Following the 1824 rebuilding, many of the memorials from the medieval church were reinstalled in the new church, including several by the sculptor [John Flaxman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Flaxman).[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_p25-200][^cite_note-Home_1901_pp67-69-201] The parish chest, made from carved Spanish mahogany, is reputed to contain wood recovered from ships of the [Spanish Armada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Armada).[^cite_note-Home_1901_pp67-69-201][^cite_note-StM_internal-202] The church also holds a copy of the so-called [vinegar bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible_errata#King_James) of 1717.[^cite_note-StM_internal-202] The east window, by the local designer F.A. Oldaker, shows [Christ the Light of the World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_of_the_World), in the style of [the painting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Light_of_the_World_\(Hunt\)) by [William Holman Hunt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Holman_Hunt).[^cite_note-Berry_1992_p29-203]

### Christ Church



Christ Church, Epsom Common

Christ Church was founded as a [chapel of ease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapel_of_ease) to St Martin's in 1843.[^cite_note-Home_1901_pp73-74-204] Initially a temporary structure was provided until the first permanent building was opened in 1845.[^cite_note-Christ_Church-205] In July 1874, Epsom parish was divided into two, with the western half becoming the new parish of Epsom Common.[^cite_note-Home_1901_pp73-74-204] The chapel of ease became the parish church, but was considered too small for the [congregation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_\(congregation\)).[^cite_note-Home_1901_pp73-74-204] Its replacement, the present church, was designed in the [Gothic revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_architecture) style by [Arthur Blomfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Blomfield) and opened in 1876.[^cite_note-Christ_Church-205][^cite_note-NHLE_Christ_Church-206] It is constructed from flint with stone dressings and there is a [clerestory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clerestory) above the [nave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nave). The tower and south aisle were added in 1879 and 1887 respectively.[^cite_note-NHLE_Christ_Church-206]

The chancel arch is decorated with a [mural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mural) of [Christ](1095706.md) flanked by [angels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_angelology),[^cite_note-Christ_Church-205][^cite_note-NHLE_Christ_Church-206] beneath which is the [rood screen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rood_screen), made of [wrought iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrought_iron) and [bronze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze), which was erected 1909.[^cite_note-Berry_1992_p62-207] The alabaster [reredos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reredos), behind the [altar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altar), contains figurative scenes in [mosaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosaic) and the east wall is decorated with panels depicting the [four Evangelists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Evangelists).[^cite_note-NHLE_Christ_Church-206] The [pulpit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulpit) dates from 1880 and was originally in [St Andrew's Church, Surbiton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Andrew's_Church,_Surbiton).[^cite_note-NHLE_Christ_Church-206] The interior of the church was reordered between 1987 and 1995 to increase the versatility of the nave.[^cite_note-Christ_Church-205]

### United Reformed Church



United Reformed Church, Church Road

Following the [Act of Uniformity 1662](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Act_of_Uniformity_1662), local [nonconformists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonconformist_\(Protestantism\)) began meeting in small groups at Ewell, before establishing themselves at Epsom in around 1688.[^cite_note-Cary_1988_pp2-3-208] The Dissenting community flourished in the town in the late 17th and early 18th centuries and, in 1720, the first purpose-built meeting house was constructed on Church Street.[^cite_note-Cary_1988_pp7-8-209] As the population of the town decreased in the later 18th century, the congregation declined and the meeting house appears to have been sold by 1803, but remained in use for worship.[^cite_note-Cary_1988_pp13-14-210] A new, Gothic revival [congregational church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congregational_church) was opened in 1905 on the same site.[^cite_note-Cary_1988_pp13-14-210] Much of the church, including the roof and organ, was destroyed in fire in 1961, but the building was rebuilt two years later. It was renamed Epsom United Reformed Church in 1972.[^cite_note-Cary_1988_pp31-33-211]

### St Joseph's Catholic Church

The Catholic parish of St Joseph, traces its origins to 1859, when the congregation began to meet regularly for mass in the parlour of a house in Stanley Villas.[^cite_note-McInally_1998_p2-212] The first permanent church, in Heathcote Road, was completed in 1866 and was constructed of local red brick with [Bath stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bath_stone) dressings.[^cite_note-McInally_1998_p6-213] It was extended in 1930[^cite_note-McInally_1998_pp16-19-214] and the west end was rebuilt between 1959 and 1961,[^cite_note-McInally_1998_p22-23-215] but by 1996 it had become clear that it was inadequate for the size of the congregation. Land in St Margaret's Drive was purchased from the county council and construction of a new church began in 1999. The old church was demolished in 2000 and its replacement opened in April 2001.[^cite_note-StJoseph-216] The new church was dedicated by [Cormac Murphy-O'Connor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cormac_Murphy-O'Connor) in May 2001.[^cite_note-217]

## Culture



*View at Epsom* (1809) by John Constable

### Literature

*[Epsom Wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Wells)*, a [restoration comedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restoration_comedy) written by [Thomas Shadwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Shadwell) (c. 1642 – 1692), is one of a series of plays set in English spa towns. It received its first performance in 1673 at the [Duke's Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duke's_Company) in London.[^cite_note-218] A revival of the play in the 1690s included new [incidental music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incidental_music) by the composer, [Henry Purcell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Purcell).[^cite_note-219]

*[Epsom Downs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Downs_\(play\))*, a [comic play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_comedy) written by [Howard Brenton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Brenton) (born 1942), is set at the 1977 Epsom Derby. It received its first performance in 1977 at [The Roundhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roundhouse_\(venue\)) in London.[^cite_note-220][^cite_note-221]

### Music

Epsom Choral Society was founded in 1922 by the publisher, [Humphrey Milford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humphrey_Sumner_Milford). His son, the composer [Robin Milford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robin_Milford) was the first [conductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conducting). The choir has [commissioned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commission_\(art\)) works from the British [composers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composer) [Cecilia McDowall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecilia_McDowall) and [Jonathan Willcocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Willcocks).[^cite_note-222] Epsom Symphony Orchestra for amateur musicians was founded in 1951 and gives four concerts each year at the Epsom Playhouse.[^cite_note-223][^cite_note-224] The Epsom & Ewell Silver Band is based in Epsom. It is a traditional [British brass band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_brass_band) and was founded in 1910 as the Banstead Silver Band.[^cite_note-225]

### Paintings

The landscape painter, [John Constable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Constable) (1776–1837), visited Epsom regularly between 1806 and 1812. His works include several depictions of the town, including *View at Epsom* (1809), held by [The Tate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tate).[^cite_note-226] The gallery also owns paintings of horse racing taking place on the Downs, including works by [Alfred Munnings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Munnings) (1878–1959)[^cite_note-227] and [William Powell Frith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Powell_Frith) (1819–1909).[^cite_note-228] *[The 1821 Derby at Epsom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_1821_Derby_at_Epsom)* (1821) by [Théodore Géricault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Th%C3%A9odore_G%C3%A9ricault) (1791–1824) is held by [The Louvre](17546.md).[^cite_note-229] Paintings of [Nonsuch Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonsuch_Palace), attributed to [Hendrick Danckerts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrick_Danckerts) (c. 1625–1680),[^cite_note-230] and of the view from Epsom Downs, by William Henry David Birch (1895–1968), are held by [Epsom Town Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Town_Hall).[^cite_note-231]

### Public art



*Spectre de la Rose* by Tom Merrifield

[^cite_note-EEHE_Gilpin-232]

A [statuette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Figurine) by the [Australian](4689264.md) [sculptor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sculptor) Tom Merrifield was erected outside the Playhouse in 1999. It depicts the [dancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballet_dancer), [John Gilpin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Gilpin_\(dancer\)), performing the [title role](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title_character) of [Le Spectre de la rose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Spectre_de_la_rose) from the [ballet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballet) by [Jean-Louis Vaudoyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Louis_Vaudoyer).[^cite_note-EEHE_Gilpin-232][^cite_note-233]

It depicts two horses - [Diomed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diomed), the winner of the first Derby in 1780 and [Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_\(horse\)), the winner of the 2001 race. A statue of Emily Davison by the artist [Christine Charlesworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christine_Charlesworth), was installed in the marketplace in 2021, following a campaign by volunteers from the Emily Davison Memorial Project.[^cite_note-Orlando_2021-77]

It has two performance spaces:[^cite_note-Williamson_1983-235] The main auditorium seats a maximum of 450.[^cite_note-236] The Myers Studio, which has an [octagonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octagon) floor plan, seats 80 and is suited to smaller-scale drama, comedy and jazz performances.[^cite_note-Williamson_1983-235][^cite_note-237]

## Sport

### Rainbow Leisure Centre



Rainbow Leisure Centre

The current Rainbow Leisure Centre opened in 2003, replacing the original facility which dated from the 1930s. The centre includes [swimming pools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swimming_pool), two [dance studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dance_studio) and a sports hall with four [badminton courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Badminton_court).[^cite_note-239][^cite_note-240] In 2011, a [spa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_club) facility was added as part of an extensive refurbishment, during which the [gym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_club) equipment was also upgraded.[^cite_note-241] The centre is managed by [Greenwich Leisure Limited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenwich_Leisure_Limited) (GLL) under their "Better" brand.[^cite_note-242]

### Association Football

[Epsom & Ewell F.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_&_Ewell_F.C.) traces its origins to the Epsom Juniors Cricket Club, founded in March 1918.[^cite_note-EFC_Hist-243][^cite_note-244] A year later the team began to play football and was a founder member of the Sutton & District League in 1920. The team changed its name to Epsom Town F.C. in 1922 and played its [home games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_\(sports\)) at the Horton Hospital Sports Ground. The club became Epsom F.C. in 1934 and adopted its current name in 1960.[^cite_note-EFC_Hist-243] Since the start of the 2020/21 season, the club has been based at Fetcham Grove, the [Leatherhead F.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leatherhead_F.C.) stadium.[^cite_note-245]

### Cricket



Epsom Cricket Club ground

The earliest reference to [cricket](25675557.md) being played in the town is from 1711 and [Epsom Cricket Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Cricket_Club) is thought to have been founded in around 1800. During the early 19th century, matches appear to have taken place on the Downs and also at Stamford Green on Epsom Common. By 1860, the club had established a new ground on Woodcote Road, on land belonging to Edward Richard Northey, and plays its home games there today.[^cite_note-246]

### Hockey

Epsom Hockey Club is a [field hockey](10886.md) club that competes in the [London Hockey League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Hockey_League) and the [South East Hockey League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Counties_Hockey_Association).[^cite_note-247] It is thought to have been founded in around 1900 and was well established by the 1909/10 season. Initially, home games were played at the Court Recreation Ground, but the club established a permanent base in Woodcote Road after the Second World War.[^cite_note-248]

### Tennis



Epsom Tennis Club

Epsom Lawn Tennis Club was founded as a [croquet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croquet) club in the 1850s, based at Woodcote House. By the 1870s, [tennis](29773.md) became the more important sport and in 1913 the club had five [tennis courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tennis_court) and three croquet courts. The last remaining croquet court was converted to a [grass tennis court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grass_court) in 1923.[^cite_note-249]

### Other sports

Epsom and Ewell Harriers is a club for [track and field](194904.md) athletes, based at the Poole Road Track in Ewell.[^cite_note-250] Epsom Cycling Club was founded in 1891 and is one of the oldest in the country. The club arranges organised rides and regular [time trials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_trial) in the local area.[^cite_note-251] [Sutton and Epsom Rugby Football Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sutton_&_Epsom_RFC) was founded in 1881 and their main ground is at Rugby Lane, [Cheam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheam).[^cite_note-252]

## Notable buildings and landmarks

### The Ashley Centre

Construction of [The Ashley Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ashley_Centre) was proposed in the late 1970s, following concerns that the [retail](183515.md) sector of the local economy was in decline.[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_pp92-93-253] The [shopping centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shopping_center) was designed by [Humphrey Wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RHWL) and the building makes extensive use of steel, glass and marble. A [multistorey car park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multistorey_car_park) and an office block were included in the scheme, in addition to 23,000 m<sup>2</sup> (250,000 ft<sup>2</sup>) of retail space.[^cite_note-Williamson_1983-235] The centre was opened by Queen [Elizabeth II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_II) on 24 October 1984[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_pp92-93-253] and is named after the adjacent Ashley Road.[^cite_note-Brooks_2019-254]

### Ashley House



Ashley House

Ashley House is named after Mary Ashley, a former resident who lived at the property until 1849.[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_p112-255] The three-storey, brick townhouse was constructed in the late 1760s and is protected by a Grade II\* listing. It features an [Adam-style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_style) central doorcase with a composite [Tower of the Winds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_of_the_Winds) and [Ionic columns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionic_order).[^cite_note-256]

### Assembly Rooms

The Assembly Rooms building was constructed in around 1692, when the spa was at the height of it popularity. It functioned as a meeting place and included a tavern, [coffee house](370815.md) and [billiard room](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billiard_room). A [bowling green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowling_green) and a [cockpit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cockfight), in which fighting [cocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rooster) were set against each other for [sport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_sport), were also part of the same complex.[^cite_note-257][^cite_note-Wetherspoon-258] Following the decline of the spa in the first half of the 18th century, much of the building was converted to shops and to housing for private families.[^cite_note-Wetherspoon-258] Since April 2002, the Assembly Rooms has operated as a [public house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_house), owned by [Wetherspoons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wetherspoons).[^cite_note-259]

### Clock Tower

The [foundation stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornerstone) for the Clock Tower was laid in November 1847, following the demolition of the watch house which had stood on the site since the 17th century.[^cite_note-EEHE_Clock_Tower-260] The 70-foot-tall (21-metre)[^cite_note-EEHE_Clock_Tower-260] tower, designed by James Butler and Henry Hodge,[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_p30-261] is constructed from red and grey brick with [ashlar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashlar) dressings.[^cite_note-262] Initially four [lion sculptures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_depictions_of_lions) made of [Caen stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caen_stone) were positioned at each corner of the base, however by 1902 they had been replaced by electric lanterns, which were replaced, in 1920, by the current globe lights. In the same year, the base of the tower was extended to the east and west to provide toilet facilities.[^cite_note-EEHE_Clock_Tower-260] Several public events and civic ceremonies have taken place in the area at the base of the tower, including the official granting of the foundation [charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charter) of the Borough of Epsom and Ewell in September 1937.[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_pp71-73-39] In March 2023, following many discussions, the clock tower received a full LED lighting upgrade allowing the tower to change colour.[^cite_note-263]

### Spread Eagle Walk



Spread Eagle Walk

The Spread Eagle Walk shopping centre was constructed as a coaching inn in the late 17th century.[^cite_note-Berry_1992_p9-264] The main hotel building has two storeys with an attic and basement. The external [stucco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stucco) finish and [sash windows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sash_window) are later additions.[^cite_note-265] The distinctive black eagle sculptures above the two porticos are thought to date from the mid-late 19th century. The inn traded as a [public house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_house) until around 1990, when it closed for renovation.[^cite_note-EEHE_Spread_Eagle-266] The driveway into the former stables was converted into the main entrance to the shopping centre[^cite_note-Berry_1992_p15-267] and in 1994 the main building was occupied by Lester Bowden outfitters,[^cite_note-EEHE_Spread_Eagle-266] which had been founded by Arthur Bowden at start of the 20th century.[^cite_note-Berry_1992_p15-267]

### Town Hall

Main article: [Epsom Town Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Town_Hall)

The [neo-Georgian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Georgian_style_\(Great_Britain\)) Epsom Town Hall, designed by Hubert Moore Fairweather and William Alfred Pite, was opened in March 1934.[^cite_note-riba-268][^cite_note-269] The building has served as the headquarters of Epsom UDC, Epsom Borough Council and, since 1974, [Epsom and Ewell Borough Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_and_Ewell_Borough_Council). The New Town Hall was opened in 1992 and is connected to the older building by covered walkways.[^cite_note-Harte_2005_pp111-112-270]

### War memorials



Commonwealth War Graves Memorial, Epsom Cemetery

Epsom War Memorial at Epsom Cemetery was erected in 1921 to commemorate the 256 residents of Epsom and Ewell who had died in the First World War. The memorial consists of a [Celtic cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_cross) in [granite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granite) and the gates are dedicated to the 18th, 19th, 20th and 21st Battalions of the [Royal Fusiliers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Fusiliers), who were billeted in Epsom before the opening of the Woodcote Park camp.[^cite_note-NHLE_war_memorial-271][^cite_note-EEBC_war_memorial-272] Following the end of the Second World War, an additional inscription was added to commemorate those who had died between 1939 and 1945.[^cite_note-NHLE_war_memorial-271]

The Commonwealth War Graves Memorial was erected in Epsom Cemetery in 1925. It consists of a cross, behind which is a screen wall on which names of 148 [Commonwealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_of_Nations) soldiers are inscribed.[^cite_note-EEBC_war_memorial-272] The majority of those commemorated died in the hospitals of the Epsom Cluster.[^cite_note-273]

### Woodcote Park



*Woodcote Park* (c. 1816) engraving by [John Hassell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hassell)

[Woodcote Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodcote_Park) was created as a hunting park in the mid-12th century by the abbot of Chertsey Abbey. Following the [dissolution of the monasteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_monasteries) in the mid-16th century, the park was briefly held by [Sir Nicholas Carew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Carew_\(Henry_VIII_courtier\)), before being annexed to the [Honour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_feudal_barony) of [Hampton Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hampton_Court_Palace).[^cite_note-EEHE_Woodcote-274] In the mid-17th century, the land was inherited by Elizabeth Mynne, wife of Richard Evelyn, for whom the first mansion house was constructed. The building included decorative features by the sculptor, [Grinling Gibbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grinling_Gibbons), and a ceiling by the painter, [Antonio Verrio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_Verrio).[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_pp104-106-275] In 1715, the estate was inherited by [Charles Calvert, 5th Baron Baltimore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Calvert,_5th_Baron_Baltimore), who commissioned [Isaac Ware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Ware) to rebuild the mansion in the French [Rococo Revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rococo_Revival) style. Some of the features of the original house were reused, including the Verrio ceiling.[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_pp104-106-275] Shortly before his death, Calvert engaged [John Vardy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Vardy) to design a [Palladian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palladian_architecture) stone frontage.[^cite_note-EEHE_Woodcote-274]

Following long periods of ownership by the Tessier and Brooks families during the 19th century,[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_pp104-106-275] the [Royal Automobile Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Automobile_Club) (R.A.C.) purchased Woodcote Park in 1913.[^cite_note-Berry_1992_p83-276][^cite_note-277] Many of the historic interiors were not required by the R.A.C. and were removed[^cite_note-EEHE_Woodcote-274] and sold at auction. In 1927, wood [panelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panelling) and other decorations in the style of [Thomas Chippendale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Chippendale), from one of the [drawing rooms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drawing_room), were installed in the [Museum of Fine Arts at Boston, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Fine_Arts,_Boston).[^cite_note-278] The mansion was destroyed by fire in August 1934,[^cite_note-279] but within two years had been rebuilt with an identical exterior appearance.[^cite_note-EEHE_Woodcote-274] Today the 350-acre (140 ha) site features two 18-hole [golf courses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golf_course), sports and spa facilities, as well as restaurants and guest accommodation. The club also houses a fleet of heritage [motor vehicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motor_vehicle), owned by the R.A.C.[^cite_note-280]

## Parks and open spaces

### Epsom Common



The Great Pond, [Epsom Common](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Common)

During the medieval period, Epsom Common was the manorial waste land[^cite_note-EEBC_Common-9] and, in the 12th century, the monks of Chertsey Abbey built the Great Pond to rear fish.[^cite_note-281] The first cottages had been built around the edges of the common by 1679 and by the mid-18th century there were over 30, mostly on the land around Stamford Green.[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_pp50-51-282] The Common was purchased by Epsom Urban District Council (UDC) in 1935 and its successor body, the Borough Council, continues to own and manage it today. Together with the adjacent [Ashtead Common](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashtead_Common), Epsom Common comprises a [Site of Special Scientific Interest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Site_of_Special_Scientific_Interest) and is a [Local Nature Reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_Nature_Reserve). The 176-hectare (430-acre) site has a range of distinctive habitats and is a breeding ground for 59 bird species.[^cite_note-EEBC_Common-9]

### Epsom Downs

[Epsom Downs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsom_Downs) and the neighbouring Walton Downs are to the south east of the town and together comprise 240 hectares (600 acres) of chalk [downland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downland). The Downs are owned by Epsom Racecourse and are managed by a board of Conservators under the framework of the Epsom and Walton Downs Regulation Act 1984. A number of rare plants are present, including [round-headed rampion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phyteuma_orbiculare), [bastard-toadflax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thesium_humifusum) and [chalk hill eyebright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphrasia). The area provides a habitat for bird species including the [Eurasian skylark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasian_skylark).[^cite_note-283] Since 2013, Epsom Downs has been a [Site of Nature Conservation Importance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Site_of_Nature_Conservation_Interest).[^cite_note-284]

### Horton Country Park



[Nilgai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilgai) at Hobbledown Children's Farm, Horton Country Park

[Horton Country Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horton_Country_Park) was created in 1973 from two farms belonging to the Epsom Cluster of psychiatric hospitals and is owned and managed by the Borough Council.[^cite_note-HCP-10] Around 152 hectares (380 acres) have been designated a [Local Nature Reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_Nature_Reserve) in 2004.[^cite_note-285] The country park includes areas of [ancient woodland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_woodland) and provides a habitat for a wide range of mammal, bird and insect species including the [green woodpecker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_green_woodpecker) and [roe deer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roe_deer).[^cite_note-HCP-10] It also contains a [golf course](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golf_course), [equestrian centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equestrian_facility) and a [children's farm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petting_zoo).[^cite_note-Abdy_2001_p89-286][^cite_note-Wakefield_1997_p33-287]

### Mounthill Gardens and Rosebery Park

Mounthill Gardens, south west of the town centre, was formed from the grounds of two adjacent houses, both purchased by Epsom UDC in 1950. The smaller house, Rosebank, had been bombed during the Second World War and was subsequently demolished by the council. The larger house, Mounthill, was used as offices for a time, before it too was demolished.[^cite_note-290] The two hectares (5 acres) of steeply sloping land surrounding the two buildings was converted into a public park that opened in 1965.[^cite_note-Mounthill-288][^cite_note-Morgan_2020-289]



The lake in Rosebery Park

Rosebery Park, to the south of the town centre, was presented to Epsom UDC in 1913 by Lord Rosebery as "proof of \[his] deep and abiding affection" for the town. Rosebery's wish was for the creation of "a public pleasure ground for the resort and recreation of the inhabitants of Epsom." Work to create the formal areas of the park had begun by the outbreak of the First World War, including the enlargement of an existing pond to create the lake. Non-landscaped areas were converted into [allotments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allotment_\(gardening\)) during the two world wars. Today the 4.5-hectare (11-acre) park is owned and managed by the Borough Council.[^cite_note-291][^cite_note-Morgan_Rosebery-292]

### Recreation grounds



Alexandra Recreation Ground

Alexandra Recreation Ground was opened in 1901 on land previously owned by Chertsey Abbey.[^cite_note-Berry_1992_p52-293][^cite_note-Alex_Rec-294] The land was purchased by Epsom UDC in response to a public [petition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petition)[^cite_note-Alex_Rec-294] and was intended for local residents to use for sporting activities.[^cite_note-Berry_1992_p52-293] During WWI, the football pitches were ploughed and the land was used to grow potatoes. An adjacent chalk pit was filled in 1933 and is now the site of the children's playground.[^cite_note-Alex_Rec-294]

The Court Recreation Ground, to the north of the town centre, is the largest recreation ground in the borough. The land was acquired between 1924 and 1926 and a bowling green was created in 1934. The ground has an [artificial turf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_turf) sports pitch, five football pitches and three tennis courts.[^cite_note-295]
