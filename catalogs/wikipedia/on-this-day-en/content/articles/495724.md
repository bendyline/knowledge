The **McDonnell Douglas** (now **Boeing**) **F-15E Strike Eagle** is an American all-weather [multirole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multirole_combat_aircraft) [strike fighter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strike_fighter)[^cite_note-6] derived from the [McDonnell Douglas F-15 Eagle](11715.md). Intended for the Dual-Role Fighter (DRF) program (initially called [Enhanced Tactical Fighter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enhanced_Tactical_Fighter)), the F-15E was designed in the 1980s for long-range, high-speed [interdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_interdiction) without relying on escort or [electronic-warfare aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic-warfare_aircraft). [United States Air Force](32090.md) (USAF) F-15E Strike Eagles can be generally distinguished from other US Eagle variants by darker [aircraft camouflage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_camouflage), [conformal fuel tanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_fuel_tank) (CFTs) and [LANTIRN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LANTIRN) pods mounted behind the engine [intake ramps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intake_ramp) (although CFTs can also be mounted on earlier F-15 variants) and a tandem-seat cockpit.

Initially designed and manufactured by [McDonnell Douglas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McDonnell_Douglas), the F-15E first flew in 1986 and production continued under [Boeing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_Defense,_Space_&_Security) following the companies' merger in 1997. The aircraft became the USAF's primary strike fighter/interdictor starting near the end of the Cold War, gradually replacing the [F-111 Aardvark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Dynamics_F-111_Aardvark). The Strike Eagle has been deployed for military operations in [Iraq](7515928.md), [Afghanistan](737.md), [Syria](7515849.md), [Libya](17633.md), and [Iran](14653.md) among others. During these operations, the strike fighter has carried out deep strikes against [high-value targets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-value_target) and [combat air patrols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combat_air_patrol), and provided [close air support](600792.md) for [coalition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_War#Coalition_involvement) troops. It has also been exported to several countries. The F-15E is expected to remain in USAF service until the 2030s. Enhanced versions of the design, called the [F-15 Advanced Eagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_F-15EX_Eagle_II), remain in production. As of 2026, the F-15E is USAF's most capable [tactical nuclear weapon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tactical_nuclear_weapon) [delivery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_delivery) aircraft, able to carry up to five [B61 Mod 12 nuclear bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B61_nuclear_bomb).[^cite_note-\:1-7]

## Development

### Origins

The [McDonnell Douglas F-15 Eagle](11715.md) was introduced by the USAF to replace its fleet of [McDonnell Douglas F-4 Phantom IIs](11759.md). Unlike the F-4, the F-15 was designed for air superiority with little consideration for a ground-attack role; the F-15 Special Project Office opposed the idea of F-15s performing interdiction, giving rise to the phrase "Not a pound for air to ground."[^cite_note-D\&D_35-8]

Despite a lack of official interest, McDonnell Douglas had quietly included a basic secondary ground attack capability in the F-15's design since the beginning and worked on an F-15-derived interdictor fighter. The company envisaged the aircraft as a replacement for the [General Dynamics F-111](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Dynamics_F-111_Aardvark) and the remaining F-4s, as well as to augment the existing F-15s.[^cite_note-9] In 1978, the USAF initiated the Tactical All-Weather Requirement Study, which looked at McDonnell Douglas's proposal and other options such as the purchase of further F-111Fs, and then the Enhanced Tactical Fighter program. The studies recommended the F-15E as the USAF's future strike platform.[^cite_note-WPAJ_42-10] In 1979, McDonnell Douglas and Hughes began a close collaboration on the development of the F-15E's potential air-to-ground capabilities.[^cite_note-11]

To assist in the F-15E's development, although not yet a program of record, McDonnell Douglas modified the second TF-15A prototype, AF [serial number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_military_aircraft_serials) *71-0291*, as a demonstrator. The aircraft, known as the Advanced Fighter Capability Demonstrator, first flew on 8 July 1980.[^cite_note-WPAJ_42-10] It was previously used to test conformal fuel tanks (CFTs), initially designed for the F-15 under the designation "FAST Pack", with *FAST* standing for "Fuel and Sensor, Tactical.[^cite_note-WPAJ_42-10] It was subsequently fitted with a [Pave Tack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pave_Tack) [laser designator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_designator) [targeting pod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Targeting_pod) to allow the independent delivery of [guided bombs](3587974.md).[^cite_note-12] The demonstrator was displayed at the 1980 [Farnborough Airshow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farnborough_Airshow).[^cite_note-WAPJ_44-13]



The second TF-15A, [AF Ser. No.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_military_aircraft_serials) *71-0291*, used as an F-15E demonstrator

### Enhanced Tactical Fighter

In March 1981, the USAF announced the [Enhanced Tactical Fighter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enhanced_Tactical_Fighter) program to replace the F-111. The program was later renamed the Dual-Role Fighter (DRF) competition, while the [Advanced Tactical Fighter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_Tactical_Fighter) looked at more advanced concepts. The ETF concept envisioned an aircraft capable of launching deep [air interdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_interdiction) missions without requiring additional support by [fighter escort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escort_fighter) or [jamming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_jamming_and_deception).[^cite_note-14] [General Dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Dynamics) submitted the [F-16XL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Dynamics_F-16XL), while McDonnell Douglas submitted the F-15E. The [Panavia Tornado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panavia_Tornado) was also a candidate, but since the aircraft lacked a credible [air-superiority fighter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air-superiority_fighter) capability, coupled with the fact that it was not American-made, it was not seriously considered.[^cite_note-WAPJ_44-13]

The DRF evaluation team, under the direction of [Brigadier General](206220.md) [Ronald W. Yates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_W._Yates), ran from 1981 through 30 April 1983, during which the F-15E logged more than 200 flights, demonstrated a takeoff weight of more than 75,000 pounds (34 t), and validated 16 different weapons-carrying configurations.[^cite_note-J98_36-15][^cite_note-WAPJ_45-16] McDonnell Douglas, to assist *71-0291* in the evaluation, added to the program other F-15s, designated *78-0468*, *80-0055*, and *81-0063*. The single-engined F-16XL was a promising design, which with its radically redesigned [cranked-delta wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_wing#Design_variations), greatly boosted performance; if selected, the single- and two-seat versions were to be designated F-16E and F-16F, respectively.[^cite_note-WAPJ_45-16] On 24 February 1984, the USAF chose the F-15E; key factors in the decision were the F-15E's lower development costs compared to the F-16XL (US\$270 million versus US\$470 million), a belief that the F-15E had future growth potential, and possessing twin-engine redundancy.[^cite_note-J98_36-15][^cite_note-F15Chrono-5] The USAF was initially expected to procure 400 aircraft, a figure later revised to 392.[^cite_note-WAPJ_45-16][^cite_note-Dual_Role-17]

Construction of the first three F-15Es started in July 1985. The first of these, *86-0183*, made its [maiden flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maiden_flight) on 11 December 1986.[^cite_note-J98_36-15][^cite_note-F15Chrono-5] Piloted by Gary Jennings, the aircraft reached a maximum speed of Mach 0.9 and an altitude of 40,000 feet (12,000 m) during the 75-minute flight.[^cite_note-J98_36-15] This aircraft had the full F-15E avionics suite and the redesigned front fuselage, but not the aft fuselage and the common engine bay for more powerful Improved Performance Engine (IPE) variants of the [Pratt & Whitney F100](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_&_Whitney_F100) or [General Electric F110](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric_F110).[^cite_note-J98_36-15] The latter was featured on *86-0184*, while *86-0185* incorporated all the changes of the F-15E from the F-15.[^cite_note-J98_36-15] On 31 March 1987, the first officially completed F-15E made its first flight.[^cite_note-18]

The first production F-15E was delivered to the [405th Tactical Training Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/405th_Air_Expeditionary_Wing), [Luke Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luke_Air_Force_Base), Arizona, in April 1988.[^cite_note-F15Chrono-5] Production continued into the 2000s with 236 produced for the USAF through 2001.[^cite_note-JAWA_F-15E-19]

### Upgrade programs and replacement

Main article: [Boeing F-15EX Eagle II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_F-15EX_Eagle_II)



First production F-15E, 86-0183

The F-15E was upgraded with the Raytheon [AN/APG-82(V)1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AN/APG-63_radar_family#AN/APG-82\(V\)1) [Active Electronically Scanned Array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_Electronically_Scanned_Array) (AESA) radar after 2007, and the first test radar was delivered to Boeing in 2010.[^cite_note-20] It combines the processor of the [APG-79](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/APG-79) used on the [F/A-18E/F Super Hornet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F/A-18E/F_Super_Hornet) with the antenna of the [APG-63(V)3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/APG-63_and_APG-70) AESA being fitted on the F-15C;[^cite_note-21] it was named APG-63(V)4 until it received the APG-82 designation in 2009.[^cite_note-22] The new radar is to be part of the F-15E Radar Modernization Program,[^cite_note-23] which also includes a wideband radome (enabling operation on more radar frequencies) and environment control and electronic warfare improvements.[^cite_note-24] In 2015, Boeing and BAE Systems were awarded contracts to comprehensively upgrade of the electronic warfare system of all USAF F-15s, including the F-15E, with the AN/ALQ-250 Eagle Passive/Active Warning Survivability System (EPAWSS).[^cite_note-25] The first F-15E retrofitted with EPAWSS was delivered in 2022.[^cite_note-EPAWSS_award-26]

Having a sturdier airframe rated for 8,000 hours of service life or up to 16,000 hours with proper depot maintenance programs, twice the lifetime of earlier variants, the F-15E is expected to remain in service past 2025.[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28] As of December 2012, the USAF's F-15E fleet had an average age of 21 years and an average airframe flying time of 6,000 hours. In 2012, the USAF was reportedly considering future options, with no replacement for the F-15E being slated at that time.[^cite_note-F-15E_replacement_options-29]

The F-15E design would see some export success and Boeing continued developing upgrades for international customers. More radical upgrades to the air vehicle design resulted in the *F-15 Advanced Eagle* family that began with the F-15SA (Saudi Advanced) for the [Royal Saudi Air Force](570367.md), which first flew in 2013. It replaces the older hybrid electronic/mechanical system with a digital fly-by-wire control system that opens up two additional wing pylons and a revised wing structure for increased service life. The Advanced Eagle would have further developed variants with the F-15QA (Qatari Advanced) for the [Qatar Emiri Air Force](4014890.md) and the F-15EX Eagle II for the USAF. In FY 2020, USAF began procuring the F-15EX to replace the aging fleet of F-15C/D and supplement the [F-22 Raptor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_Martin_F-22_Raptor) to maintain air superiority fighter numbers, taking advantage of the existing export production line to quickly and affordably bring additional fighters into service; the F-15EX is also an option to replace the F-15E at a later time.[^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31] Another choice is the [F-35 Lightning II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-35_Lightning_II), set to replace other aircraft such as the [F-16 Falcon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-16_Falcon); an *F-35E* variant was studied. Adding a second seat to the F-35 is complex and costly, especially to preserve its stealth profile; providing for greater range and payload would also be difficult tasks. Alternatively, the role could be covered by a combination of fighter and bomber aircraft, such as the [B-21 Raider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B-21_Raider). The F-15E may also be replaced by a clean-sheet [sixth-generation aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sixth-generation_jet_fighter) design.[^cite_note-F-15E_replacement_options-29][^cite_note-32]

### ALASA

On 24 March 2014, Boeing won a \$30.6 million contract from [DARPA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DARPA) as part of the [Airborne Launch Assist Space Access](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airborne_Launch_Assist_Space_Access) (ALASA) program. The goal of the program is to cut the cost of putting [microsatellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miniaturized_satellite#Microsatellites) into orbit by 66% through advances in launch systems. Under the 11-month contract, Boeing will build twelve 24 ft (7.3 m) launch vehicles, each with a payload capability up to 100 lb (45 kg). An ALASA vehicle is to be fitted under an F-15E, which will climb to 40,000 ft, then be released and fire its four engines to reach low-Earth orbit. Awarding the contract to Boeing would make use of the F-15E as the carriage vehicle, as previous design contracts had been given to [Lockheed Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_Martin) to use the [F-22 Raptor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_Martin_F-22_Raptor) and [Virgin Galactic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgin_Galactic) to use their [SpaceShip Two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceShip_Two) aircraft. DARPA had previously insisted they wanted to select an aircraft they would not need to modify heavily to carry and launch the ALASA payload.[^cite_note-33][^cite_note-34] The project was terminated in late 2015.[^cite_note-35]

## Design

For the airframe overview, see [McDonnell Douglas F-15 Eagle](11715.md).

### Overview



Forward cockpit of an F-15E with three [multifunction displays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multifunction_display)

The F-15E's deep-strike mission is a radical departure from the original intent of the F-15 since it was designed as an air-superiority fighter under the mantra "not a pound for air-to-ground."[^cite_note-36] The basic airframe, however, proved versatile enough to produce a very capable strike fighter. The F-15E, while designed for ground attack, retains the air-to-air lethality of the F-15, and can defend itself against enemy aircraft.[^cite_note-Jenkins_p35-37]

The F-15E prototype was a modification of the two-seat F-15B. Despite its origins and retaining the same aerodynamic shape, it includes significant structural changes as well as more powerful engines. The aft fuselage was designed to incorporate the more powerful engines with advanced engine bay structures and doors, which incorporate [superplastic forming and diffusion bonding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superplastic_forming_and_diffusion_bonding) technologies. The back seat is equipped for a [weapon systems officer](4072511.md) (WSO, pronounced "wizzo") to work the [air-to-ground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air-to-ground) [avionics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avionics) via multiple screens; these view the [radar](25676.md), [electronic warfare](82272.md), or [thermographic cameras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermographic_camera), monitor aircraft or weapons status and possible threats, select targets, and use an electronic moving map to navigate. Two hand controls are used to select new displays and to refine targeting information; displays can be moved from one screen to another using a menu of display options. Unlike previous two-place jets (e.g. the [F-14 Tomcat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-14_Tomcat) and Navy variants of the F-4), whose back seat omitted [flying controls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_flight_control_system), the F-15E's back seat is equipped with its own stick and throttle so the WSO can take over flying, albeit with reduced visibility.[^cite_note-Davies_2003_p63-38]

For extended range, the F-15E is fitted with two [conformal fuel tanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_fuel_tank) (CFTs) that hug the fuselage to produce lower drag than conventional underwing/underbelly [drop tanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drop_tank). They carry 750 U.S. gallons (2,800 L) of fuel, and house six weapons [hardpoints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardpoint) in two rows of three in tandem. Unlike conventional drop tanks, CFTs cannot be jettisoned, thus increased range is a trade-off for increased drag and weight compared to a "clean" configuration.[^cite_note-39]

### Avionics



A wing-over maneuver displays the clean lines and high-wing design of an F-15E from [Elmendorf AFB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elmendorf_Air_Force_Base), [Alaska](624.md)

The tactical electronic warfare system (TEWS) integrates all countermeasures on the craft: radar warning receivers, radar jammer, radar and [chaff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaff_\(radar_countermeasure\))/[flare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flare_\(countermeasure\)) dispensers are all tied to the TEWS to provide comprehensive defense against detection and tracking. This system includes an externally mounted ALQ-131 ECM pod which is carried on the centerline pylon when required. The MIDS Fighter Data Link Terminal, produced by [BAE Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BAE_Systems), improves situational awareness and communications capabilities via the [Link 16](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Link_16) datalink.[^cite_note-40] The TEWS was replaced by the AN/ALQ-250 EPAWSS digital electronic warfare suite beginning in 2022; EPAWSS replaces all TEWS components with lighter and more capable digital ones for increased performance.[^cite_note-EPAWSS_award-26][^cite_note-BAE_EPAWSS-41]

The [AN/APG-70](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AN/APG-63_radar_family#AN/APG-70) radar allows crews to detect ground targets from longer ranges; one feature is that, after a sweep of a target area, the crew may freeze the air-to-ground map then switch to air-to-air mode to scan for aerial threats. During air-to-surface weapon delivery, the pilot is capable of detecting, targeting, and engaging air-to-air targets while the WSO designates ground targets. Under the Radar Modernization Program, the APG-70 has been replaced by the AN/APG-82(V)1,[^cite_note-42] which combines the AESA antenna from the AN/APG-63(V)3 with the processor from the AN/APG-79(V) as well as new Radio Frequency Tunable Filters and cooling system; APG-82(V)1 flight tests began in January 2010 and the radar achieved initial operational capability in 2014.[^cite_note-43]



USAF F-15E Strike Eagle performs a [flare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flare_\(countermeasure\)) check in March 2025

Its [inertial navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_navigation) system uses a [laser gyroscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_laser_gyroscope) to continuously monitor the aircraft's position and provide information to the central computer and other systems, including a digital moving map in both cockpits. The F-15E is commonly equipped with [LANTIRN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LANTIRN) system pods for its interdiction role. Mounted externally under the engine intakes, the LANTIRN system allows the aircraft to fly at low altitudes, at night, and in any weather conditions, to attack ground targets with a variety of precision-guided and unguided weapons. The LANTIRN system gives the F-15E exceptional accuracy in weapons delivery day or night and in poor weather, and consists of two pods attached to the exterior of the aircraft. At night, the video picture from the LANTIRN can be projected on the [head-up display](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head-up_display) (HUD), producing an infrared image of the ground.[^cite_note-Jenkins_p100-44] The digital, triple-redundant [Lear Siegler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EG\&G) [aircraft flight control system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_flight_control_system) permits coupled [automatic terrain following](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrain-following_radar), enhanced by a [ring-laser gyro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_laser_gyroscope) inertial navigation system.[^cite_note-45]

The [AN/AAQ-13](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AN/AAQ-13) navigation pod contains a [terrain-following radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrain-following_radar) which allows the pilot to safely [fly at a very low altitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nap-of-the-earth) following cues displayed on a HUD; it also can be coupled to the autopilot to provide "hands off" terrain-following capability. This pod also contains a [forward-looking infrared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forward-looking_infrared) system which is projected on the HUD, typically used during nighttime or low-visibility operations. The nav pod is installed beneath the right engine intake. The targeting pod contains a laser designator and a tracking system that mark an enemy for destruction as far away as 10 mi (16 km). Once tracking has started, targeting information is automatically handed off to [infrared homing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared_homing) [air-to-surface missiles](1544116.md) or [laser-guided bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser-guided_bomb). The targeting pod is mounted beneath the left engine intake; configurations may be either the [AN/AAQ-14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AN/AAQ-14), [AN/AAQ-28(V) Litening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Litening), or the [AN/AAQ-33 Sniper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sniper_Advanced_Targeting_Pod).[^cite_note-ISIS_strike-46][^cite_note-47]



An underside view of an F-15E Strike Eagle with [landing gear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landing_gear) down

The F-15E carries most air-to-ground weapons in the USAF inventory.[^cite_note-\:0-48] It is also armed with [AIM-9 Sidewinders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AIM-9_Sidewinder) and [AIM-120 AMRAAMs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AIM-120_AMRAAM), retaining the counter-air capabilities of its Eagle lineage, being fully capable of Offensive-Counter-Air operations. Like the F-15C, it also carries an internally mounted General Electric [M61A1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M61_Vulcan) 20 mm cannon with 500 rounds, which is effective against enemy aircraft and "soft" ground targets.

Since 2004, South Korean firm [LIG Nex1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LIG_Nex1) has been manufacturing the F-15's Head-up display; a total number of 150 HUDs were delivered by 2011.[^cite_note-DNLIG-49][^cite_note-DNLIG2-50] LIG Nex1 had been a participant in the F-15K program as a subcontractor to [Rockwell Collins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockwell_Collins).[^cite_note-DNLIG-49][^cite_note-DNLIG2-50] LIG Nex1 is also preparing to manufacture F-15's new multi-function display and flight control computer.[^cite_note-DNLIG-49] Also since 2004, [Korea Aerospace Industries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korea_Aerospace_Industries) (KAI) has produced the F-15's wings and forward fuselages; in 2008, KAI established another production line for Singapore's F-15SG.[^cite_note-KAIf-15-51] KAI is involved in the design and manufacture of the Conformal Weapons Bay (CWB) for the [F-15 Silent Eagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_F-15SE_Silent_Eagle).[^cite_note-52]

### Engines

The engines used on early aircraft are the same as on previous F-15 variants, the [Pratt & Whitney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_&_Whitney) [F100-PW-220](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_&_Whitney_F100) each producing 23,770 lbf (105.7 kN) of thrust in afterburner. However, owing to the increased weight from strike payloads and CFTs, the F-15E was designed with a common engine bay that could accept more powerful Improved Performance Engine (IPE) variants. Later batches feature the F100-PW-229 IPE with 29,160 lbf (129.7 kN) of thrust each.[^cite_note-53] Saudi Arabian and Israeli aircraft were also ordered and equipped with the F100-229 engines.[^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55]

While the F-15E had also been flight tested with the [General Electric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric) [F110-GE-129](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric_F110) with 29,500 lbf (131.2 kN) of thrust, the South Korean F-15K would be the first variant to adopt it in service. The F-15K would have two different engine types; the first batch are powered by F110-129 engines, while the second batch are powered by F100-229 engines.[^cite_note-56] In 2008, Saudi Arabia decided to re-engine their F-15S fleet with the F110-129 engines, which also enables commonality with their F-15SA fleet.[^cite_note-57][^cite_note-58] The Singapore Air Force equipped their F-15SG fleet with F110-129 engines.[^cite_note-59][^cite_note-60]

## Operational history

### United States

The F-15E was first delivered to the U.S. Air Force operational units in 1988.[^cite_note-88service-4] The F-15E reached [initial operational capability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_operational_capability) on 30 September 1989 at [Seymour Johnson Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seymour_Johnson_Air_Force_Base) in North Carolina with the [4th Tactical Fighter Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4th_Fighter_Wing), [336th Tactical Fighter Squadron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/336th_Tactical_Fighter_Squadron).[^cite_note-F15Chrono-5] As of 2026, the F-15E is USAF's most capable [tactical nuclear weapon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tactical_nuclear_weapon) [delivery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_weapons_delivery) aircraft, able to carry up to five [B61 Mod 12 nuclear bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B61_nuclear_bomb).[^cite_note-\:1-7]

#### Gulf War



USAF F-15Es, accompanied by an F-15C and two F-16s, flying over burning Kuwaiti oil wells.

The F-15E was deployed in response to Iraq's [invasion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_invasion_of_Kuwait) of [Kuwait](7515890.md) in August 1990 for [Operation Desert Shield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desert_Shield). The 336th Tactical Fighter Squadron flew to Seeb Air Base in Oman to begin training exercises in anticipation of an Iraqi attack on [Saudi Arabia](349303.md); in December, the [335th](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/335th_Tactical_Fighter_Squadron) and 336th squadrons relocated to [Prince Sultan Air Base](5553272.md) in Saudi Arabia, closer to Iraq's border.[^cite_note-61] At the start of [Operation Desert Storm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_War#Early_battles), 24 F-15Es launched an attack on five fixed [Scud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scud) installations in western Iraq on 17 January 1991. Missions against Scud sites continued through that night with a second strike of 21 F-15Es. At night-time, F-15Es flew hunter missions over western Iraq, searching for mobile SCUD launchers. By conducting random bombings in suspected areas, it was hoped to deter the Iraqis from setting up for a Scud launch.[^cite_note-62]

On the war's opening night, an F-15E failed to hit a [MiG-29](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MiG-29) with an AIM-9 Sidewinder; other F-15Es also unsuccessfully engaged this lone MiG-29, which was eventually brought down by a missile of unknown origin.[^cite_note-bennett-63][^cite_note-Davies_p17-64] On 18 January, during a strike against a petrol oil and lubricant plant near [Basrah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basrah), an F-15E was lost to enemy fire, killing both pilot and WSO. F-15E crews described this mission as the most difficult and dangerous of the war as it was heavily defended by [SA-3s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SA-3), [SA-6s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SA-6), [SA-8s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SA-8) and [Rolands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roland_\(air_defence\)) as well as by anti-aircraft artillery. Two nights later, a second and final F-15E was downed by an Iraqi [SA-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SA-2); the crew survived and evaded capture for several days and made contact with coalition aircraft, but a rescue was not launched due to security issues over an airman who failed to identify himself with proper codes. The Iraqis later captured both airmen.[^cite_note-65]

F-15Es destroyed 18 Iraqi jets on the ground at Tallil air base using [GBU-12s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GBU-12) and [CBU-87s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CBU-87). On 14 February, an F-15E scored its only air-to-air kill of the war: a [Mil Mi-24](384591.md) helicopter. While responding to a request for help by US Special Forces, five Iraqi helicopters were spotted. The lead F-15E of two, via its FLIR, acquired a helicopter in the process of unloading Iraqi soldiers, and released a [GBU-10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GBU-10) bomb. The F-15E crew thought the bomb had missed its target and were preparing to use a Sidewinder when the helicopter was destroyed. The Special Forces team estimated that the Hind was roughly 800 feet (240 m) over the ground when the 2,000 lb (910 kg) bomb hit its target.[^cite_note-66] As another Coalition bombing operation had commenced, the F-15Es disengaged from combat with the remaining helicopters.[^cite_note-bennett-63]

F-15Es struck heavily defended targets throughout Iraq, prioritizing SCUD missile sites. Missions aimed at killing [Iraqi President](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_President) [Saddam Hussein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saddam_Hussein) were undertaken by F-15Es, bombing several suspected locations. Prior to the ground war, F-15Es flew [tank plinking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tank_plinking) missions against Iraqi vehicles in Kuwait. After 42 days of combat, a cease fire came into effect on 1 March 1991, leading to the creation of [Northern and Southern no-fly zones over Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_no-fly_zones).[^cite_note-Davies_p30-1-67]

#### Operations Southern Watch and Northern Watch



An F-15E over Iraq in 1999 for Operation Northern Watch

Following the Gulf War, two no-fly zones over Iraq were set up, and enforced typically by US and UK aircraft. In one incident, an attack on up to 600 [Kurdish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurdish_people) refugees by Iraqi helicopters at [Chamchamal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamchamal), northern Iraq, was observed by a flight of F-15Es. As they were not allowed to open fire, the F-15Es instead conducted several high speed passes as close as possible to the Iraqi helicopters to create severe wake-turbulence, while aiming lasers at the helicopter's cockpits to attempt to blind their crews; this caused the crash of one Hind. Afterwards, USAF leadership ordered F-15Es not to fly below 10,000 feet (3,000 m) to deter a repetition.[^cite_note-Davies_p30-1-67]

F-15Es of the [391st Fighter Squadron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/391st_Fighter_Squadron), [492d Fighter Squadron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/492d_Fighter_Squadron), and [494th Fighter Squadron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/494th_Fighter_Squadron) regularly deployed to Turkey throughout the 1990s. In January 1993, in breach of the ceasefire agreement, [Iraqi targets below the 32nd parallel north were attacked; 10 F-15Es conducted a punitive strike days later](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/January_1993_airstrikes_on_Iraq).[^cite_note-Davies_2005,_p._33-68] Most missions were of a defensive nature; the Strike Eagles carried a flexible range of weapons on a typical mission. [AWACS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airborne_Early_Warning_and_Control) aircraft were in close contact with F-15E crews, who would receive new taskings while airborne and thus could fly unplanned attacks on Iraqi targets.[^cite_note-Davies_2005,_p._33-68] After 1993, no-fly zone violations were minimal as Iraq staged a minor withdrawal; in 1997, [Turkey](11125639.md) approved the creation of [Operation Northern Watch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Northern_Watch) (ONW) and permitted US forces to use the [Incirlik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incirlik) air base.

In December 1998, [Operation Desert Fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Desert_Fox) was conducted when Iraq refused [UNSCOM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNSCOM) inspections. On 28 December 1998, three F-15Es struck an SA-3 tracking radar and optical guidance unit, each dropping two GBU-12 500-pound precision-guided munitions (PGMs). After Desert Fox, Iraq frequently violated the no-fly zones, thus F-15Es conducted several pre-planned retaliatory strikes; in ONW alone, weapons were expended on at least 105 days.[^cite_note-69] Between 24 and 26 January 1999, F-15Es expended several [AGM-130s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-130) and GBU-12s against SAM sites near [Mosul](200475.md), northern Iraq.[^cite_note-70] They also flew in support of [Operation Provide Comfort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Provide_Comfort) and Operation Provide Comfort II.[^cite_note-Davies_p30-1-67]

#### Operations in the Balkans



F-15E departing Aviano Air Base, Italy, for a strike mission in Operation Allied Force on 28 March 1999

[Operation Deny Flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Deny_Flight) was a [United Nations](31769.md)-enforced no-fly zone over [Bosnia and Herzegovina](3463.md) due to the [deteriorating situation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yugoslav_Wars) in the [Balkans](4829.md). In August 1993, F-15Es from 492nd and 494th FS deployed to [Aviano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviano), [Italy](14532.md). In late 1993, [NATO](21133.md) ordered a limited F-15E [strike at Udbina airfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airstrike_on_Udbina_Air_Base), targeting [Serbian](29265.md) forces in neighboring [Croatia](5573.md). Eight F-15Es armed with GBU-12s took off to attack an SA-6 anti-aircraft vehicle; the mission was cancelled mid-flight over the application of stringent Rules of Engagement.[^cite_note-davies_p43-71] In December 1993, F-15Es launched to destroy a pair of SA-2s which had fired upon two [Royal Navy](26061.md) [Sea Harrier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_Harrier) FRS 1s.[^cite_note-davies_p44-72] In August 1995, F-15Es of [90th Fighter Squadron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/90th_Fighter_Squadron) were also deployed. The 492d and 494th flew over 2,500 sorties since starting Deny Flight, 2,000 of these by 492d. In August 1995, in support of NATO's [Operation Deliberate Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Deliberate_Force), F-15Es flew strike missions against Serbian armor and logistics around the Bosnian capital, [Sarajevo](26786.md). On 9 September, an F-15E deployed the type's first GBU-15 bomb; dropping nine on Bosnian-Serb ground forces and air defense targets near [Banja Luka](385109.md).[^cite_note-davies_p44-72]

In response to the displacement of [Kosovars](17391.md) and the Serbian government's rejection of a NATO ultimatum, [Operation Allied Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Allied_Force) was launched in March 1999. A total of 26 F-15Es flew the first strikes of Allied Force against Serb [surface-to-air-missile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface-to-air-missile) sites, anti-aircraft batteries and early warning radar stations.[^cite_note-73] Strike Eagles were deployed to Aviano as well as RAF Lakenheath in the UK. In-theater, F-15Es conducted [close air support](600792.md) (CAS) missions, a popular concept within the USAF.[^cite_note-autogenerated1-74] Missions typically lasted around 7.5 hours, included two aerial refuelings; F-15Es would carry a mix of air-to-air and air-to-ground munitions to perform both [combat air patrol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combat_air_patrol) duties as well as strike missions in the same mission.[^cite_note-autogenerated1-74] Mobile SAM launchers posed a considerable threat to NATO aircraft and had made successful shoot-downs, most notably of a [Lockheed F-117 Nighthawk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_F-117_Nighthawk). In order to strike from increased distances, the F-15E was equipped with the AGM-130, which provided a stand-off strike capability.[^cite_note-75]

#### War in Afghanistan



An F-15E over Afghanistan during Operation Mountain Lion, 2006

Weeks after the [September 11 attacks](5058690.md) in 2001, the 391st Fighter Squadron deployed to [Ahmad al-Jaber air base](5306887.md), Kuwait, to support [Operation Enduring Freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Enduring_Freedom) during the [War in Afghanistan](19666611.md). F-15Es met little resistance during initial missions. On the first night, the main targets were [Taliban](30635.md) military structures, supply depots, and [al-Qaeda](1921.md) training camps and caves. Both the AGM-130 and [GBU-15](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GBU-15) 2,000 lb (910 kg) bombs were expended; this was the GBU-15's first combat usage.[^cite_note-76] [GBU-24s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GBU-24) and [GBU-28s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GBU-28) were used against reinforced targets, command and control centers and cave entrances. F-15Es often operated in pairs alongside pairs of F-16Cs. Within weeks of the start of combat operations, there was a lack of targets to strike as nearly all targets had been already destroyed. The Taliban had access to [SA-7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SA-7) and [FIM-92 Stinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FIM-92_Stinger) portable [surface-to-air missiles](182664.md), posing no threat to most aircraft flying above 7,000 feet (2,100 m). Additionally, fixed SAM sites near cities as Mazar-i-Sharif and Bagram were struck early on; Afghanistan had rapidly become a low-threat environment for air operations.[^cite_note-davies_p64-77]

Aircraft commonly flew on-call support missions for allied ground forces, F-15Es usually carried [MK-82](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_82_bomb) and GBU-12 bombs in this role, other weapons were sometimes carried, during one mission a GBU-28, two GBU-24s and six GBU-12s were released.[^cite_note-davies_p64-77] Frequent targets during the rest of the war were individual insurgents, light vehicles and supply convoys; cannon fire was often expended as well as bombs from F-15Es.[^cite_note-78] It was during combat over Afghanistan that four 391st crews conducted the longest fighter mission in history; lasting a total of 15.5 hours, nine of those hours spent flying over the target area. Two F-15Es attacked two Taliban command and control facilities, two buildings suspected of being used by Taliban fighters, and a road block; the F-15Es refueled 12 times during the mission.[^cite_note-79]



An F-15E of the 391st Expeditionary Fighter Squadron launching heat decoys over Afghanistan, 2008

On 4 March, another incident known as the [Battle of Roberts' Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Anaconda) involved several F-15Es performing a CAS mission. Aircraft destroyed a Taliban observation post and responded to nearby enemy mortar fire upon Navy SEAL forces searching for an ambushed [MH-47E Chinook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MH-47E_Chinook) in the [Shah-i-Kot Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shah-i-Kot_Valley).[^cite_note-davies_p72-80] Several bombs were dropped as the SEAL team took fire, however one bomb missed due to the aircrew using incorrect coordinates.[^cite_note-davies_p72-80] An MH-47 carrying a rescue team was downed by an [RPG](26286.md) while attempting to support the SEALs.[^cite_note-davies_p73-81] Following refueling, the F-15Es dropped a further 11 GBU-12s in coordination with ground forces, and fired their cannons on Taliban forces in close proximity to the survivors of the downed MH-47.[^cite_note-davies_p73-81] F-16s of [18th Fighter Squadron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/18th_Fighter_Squadron) also made strafing passes until cannon ammunition was depleted, then resorting to further bomb drops. The F-15Es suffered technical issues involving both radio and weapon failures, several GBU-12s were dropped before returning to Al Jaber in Kuwait.[^cite_note-82]

Years later, several incidents occurred. On 23 August 2007, a [friendly fire](202484.md) incident involved an F-15E mistakenly dropping a 500 lb (230 kg) bomb on British forces, killing three soldiers;[^cite_note-83] the stated cause was confusion between the air controller and the F-15E on the bombing coordinates.[^cite_note-84] On 13 September 2009, an F-15E shot down a non-responsive [MQ-9 Reaper](4769988.md) drone over Northern Afghanistan to prevent it entering foreign airspace.[^cite_note-85]

#### Iraq War



An F-15E disengaging from a [KC-10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KC-10) during Operation Iraqi Freedom. Note the visible wingtip vortices.

In late 2002, during tension over suspected Iraqi possession of [weapons of mass destruction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weapons_of_mass_destruction), the 4th Fighter Wing at Seymour Johnson Air Force Base was ordered to maintain at least one squadron ready to deploy to the [Persian Gulf](24761.md). During January 2003, the 336th was deployed to [Al Udeid Air Base](3830783.md), Qatar, in coordination with planners of the [Combined Air Operations Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combined_Air_Operations_Center) at Prince Sultan Air Base, Saudi Arabia.[^cite_note-davies_p77-78-86] In late January, F-15Es began flying in Operation Southern Watch, typically performing surveillance and reconnaissance missions. Additional roles included simulated combat against potential Iraqi targets and regional familiarization with local procedures and [rules of engagement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rules_of_engagement).[^cite_note-davies_p77-78-86] During OSW, F-15Es struck targets in southern and western Iraq, including radars, radio stations, command and control sites, and air defences. On one night, four F-15Es released multiple GBU-24s on the [Iraqi Republican Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_Republican_Guard)/[Baath Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baath_Party) HQ in Basrah while another flight of four destroyed a nearby Air Defense Sector HQ with six GBU-10s.[^cite_note-davies_p80-87]

In late February, the 336th received additional aircrews, many drafted from the two non-deployable squadrons at Seymour Johnson (the [333d](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/333d_Fighter_Squadron) and [334th Fighter Squadrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/334th_Fighter_Squadron)) and 391st Fighter Squadron at [Mountain Home Air Force Base](55328338.md), for a total of four aircrews per F-15E.[^cite_note-davies_p80-87] In March, the 335th Fighter Squadron's personnel and aircraft joined the 336th at Al Udeid. One objective was the destruction of Iraq's air defenses and Early Warning radar network near the [Jordanian](7515964.md) border, allowing F-16s and helicopters to operate from Jordan from the war's outset. Several radar sites and radio relay stations were hit in western Iraq near the "H3" airfield, encountering heavy anti-aircraft fire.[^cite_note-davies_p82-88]

On 19 March, as [F-117 Nighthawks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-117_Nighthawk) dropped bombs over [Baghdad](4492.md), targeting a house where Saddam Hussein was believed to be; F-15Es dropped GBU-28s around the H3 airfield. On 20 March, the effective start of the war, F-15Es fired AGM-130s against communication, command and control buildings, and other key targets in Baghdad; some weapons missed their intended targets, possibly due to jamming by [EA-6B Prowlers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EA-6B_Prowler) in the vicinity.[^cite_note-89] On 3 April 2003, an F-15E mistook a USA [M270 Multiple Launch Rocket System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M270_Multiple_Launch_Rocket_System) (MLRS) for an Iraqi surface-to-air missile site and dropped a 500 lb (230 kg) laser-guided bomb, killing three and wounding five others.[^cite_note-90]

On 7 April 2003, an F-15E, crewed by Captain Eric Das and Major William Watkins, performed a key interdiction mission in support of special forces, but crashed near [Tikrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tikrit), Iraq.[^cite_note-Posthumous_Awards-91] Das and Watkins were posthumously awarded the [Distinguished Flying Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distinguished_Flying_Cross_\(United_States\)) and the [Purple Heart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purple_Heart).[^cite_note-Posthumous_Awards-91] During the war, F-15Es were credited with destroying 60% of the Iraqi Medina Republican Guard's total force; they also struck 65 MiGs on the ground,[^cite_note-davies_p82-88] and destroyed key air defense and command buildings in Baghdad. F-15Es worked with other jets deployed to Al Udeid, including [RAAF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAAF) [F/A-18s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F/A-18_Hornet), USAF F-16s and F-117s, RAF Panavia Tornados and [US Navy](20518076.md) F-14s.[^cite_note-92][^cite_note-93]

#### Libyan civil war

Following the adoption of [United Nations Security Council Resolution 1973](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Security_Council_Resolution_1973) on 17 March 2011, 18 USAF F-15Es were amongst other NATO and allied aircraft were deployed to enforce the [Libyan no-fly zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libyan_no-fly_zone) as part of [Operation Odyssey Dawn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Odyssey_Dawn). On 21 March 2011, an F-15E from the 492d Fighter Squadron crashed near Bengazi, Libya.[^cite_note-94] Both crew members parachuted into territory held by resistance elements of the Libyan population and were eventually rescued by US Marines.[^cite_note-95][^cite_note-96] Equipment problems caused a weight imbalance and contributed to the crash when leaving the target area.[^cite_note-97]

#### Operations against Islamic State (2014–present)

USAF F-15Es have participated in [Operation Inherent Resolve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_military_intervention_against_ISIS) against [Islamic State](9087364.md) (IS) militants in Iraq and Syria. On the morning of 23 September 2014, numerous American and Arab aircraft conducted air strikes in Syria against IS fighters, training compounds, headquarters and command and control facilities, storage facilities, a finance center, supply trucks, and armed vehicles.[^cite_note-98] The Pentagon released videos of targets hit by ordnance deployed by F-15Es, taken by their AN/AAQ-33 Sniper targeting pods.[^cite_note-ISIS_strike-46] Between August 2014 and January 2015, F-15Es flew 37 percent of all USAF sorties.[^cite_note-99]



A 492 FS F-15E of the [48th Fighter Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/48th_Fighter_Wing) taking off from [RAF Lakenheath](856907.md)

USAF F-15Es based at RAF Lakenheath in the United Kingdom performed several long range strikes against IS camps and prominent figures in Libya. On 13 November 2015, a pair of F-15Es killed [Abu Nabil al-Anbari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Nabil_al-Anbari), the leader of the [Islamic State of Iraq and the Levant in Libya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_State_of_Iraq_and_the_Levant_in_Libya), in a strike near [Darnah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derna,_Libya), Eastern Libya.[^cite_note-100] On 20 February 2016, USAF F-15Es[^cite_note-101] hit an IS training camp near [Sabratha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabratha) where foreign fighters were based, reportedly killing Noureddine Chouchane, a 36-year-old Tunisian jihadist linked to the [2015 Sousse attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_Sousse_attacks). Sources said that 49 people were killed and 6 wounded;[^cite_note-102][^cite_note-103] two [Serbians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbians) kidnapped by IS in 2015 were also reportedly killed.[^cite_note-104]

On 8 June 2017, an F-15E shot down a [pro-Syrian Regime](27479.md) UAV near [Al Tanf, Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Waleed_border_crossing);[^cite_note-105] according to OIR officials, it was downed after deploying "one of several weapons it was carrying near a position occupied by Coalition personnel... \[It was] similar in size to a U.S. [MQ-1 Predator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MQ-1_Predator)".[^cite_note-106] The drone may have been a [Shahed 129](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shahed_129);[^cite_note-107] on 20 June 2017, a second Shahed-129 was downed by an F-15E near the 50 mile exclusion zone around Al-Tanf.[^cite_note-108]

On 21 August 2021, a USAF F-15E shot down an unidentified drone with an [AIM-9X Sidewinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AIM-9X_Sidewinder) missile as the drone approached US forces in Eastern Syria.[^cite_note-109]

USAF F-15Es were deployed on 2 February 2024, during the [airstrikes against Iranian backed militias in Iraq and Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/February_2024_United_States_airstrikes_in_Iraq_and_Syria). USAF F-15Es helped blunt an [Iranian attack against Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/April_2024_Iranian_strikes_against_Israel) on 13 April 2024 by shooting down over 70 Iranian one-way attack UAVs.[^cite_note-110]

Concurrent with the [fall of the Assad regime](78548066.md) on 8 December, USAF F-15Es were deployed alongside [B-52s](18933037.md) and [A-10s](12502446.md) in what the USAF said were "dozens" of airstrikes against over 75 ISIS targets. The strikes were intended to prevent ISIS from benefitting from the political upheaval in Syria.[^cite_note-111]

#### Iran war (2026)

Main article: [2026 United States F-15E rescue operation in Iran](82877513.md)

During the [2026 Iran war](82533582.md), on 1-2 March 2026, videos emerged online of a [386th Air Expeditionary Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/386th_Air_Expeditionary_Wing) F-15E in a death spiral, after it suffered a catastrophic issue due to a [friendly fire incident](202484.md), after being hit by Kuwaiti air defense. Two other USAF F-15Es were also confirmed to have been shot down due to friendly fire in the same incident. All six crew members ejected from their aircraft and were safely recovered.[^cite_note-112][^cite_note-113] It was later reported that the F-15Es were shot down by one or more Kuwaiti [F/A-18s](845012.md).[^cite_note-114]

On 3 April 2026, U.S. officials reported that a F-15E had been shot down by Iranian forces. Both its pilot and [weapon systems officer](4072511.md) (WSO) ejected inside Iranian territory and a massive [search operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combat_search_and_rescue) was undertaken, utilizing [C-130s](7697.md), [Black Hawks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikorsky_UH-60_Black_Hawk) and [Apaches](37746.md). The pilot was recovered within a few hours, while it took roughly two days to recover and exfiltrate the [weapon systems officer](4072511.md). Hundreds of U.S. special operations forces were involved in the [rescue mission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_U.S._pilot_rescue_operation_in_Iran). Two [MC-130s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_MC-130) and four [MH-6](2223586.md) were destroyed by U.S. forces on a makeshift airstrip in Iran to prevent them from being seized by the Iranian military. No U.S. casualties occurred during the rescue, while there were three [IRGC](1285472.md) and four [Iranian Army](48259216.md) casualties.[^cite_note-115][^cite_note-116][^cite_note-117][^cite_note-118][^cite_note-119][^cite_note-120][^cite_note-121]

### Israel



[Israeli Air Force](177619.md) F-15I Ra'am

The F-15I is operated by the [Israeli Defense Force](39237.md)/[Air Force](177619.md) No [69 Squadron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/69_Squadron_\(Israel\)), succeeding the F-4 Phantom II. It is used akin to a strategic bomber due to its long range, high munition capacity and advanced systems.

After the [Gulf War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_War) in 1991, in which Israeli towns were attacked by [SCUD missiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SCUD_missiles) based in Iraq, the Israeli government decided a long range strike aircraft was needed, issuing a Request for Information (RFI). In response, Lockheed Martin offered a version of the [F-16 Fighting Falcon](11642.md), while McDonnell Douglas offered both the [F/A-18 Hornet](45998.md) and the F-15E. On 27 January 1994, the Israeli government announced their intention to buy 21 modified F-15Es, designated *F-15I*. On 12 May 1994, the US Government authorized the purchase of up to 25 F-15Is by Israel. In November 1995, Israel ordered four extra F-15Is; 25 were built from 1996 to 1998.[^cite_note-davies_2002-122] Some of the air-to-air missiles aircraft can carry: the [AIM-9L](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AIM-9L), [Rafael Python 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rafael_Python_4) and the [Rafael Python 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rafael_Python_5) infrared-homing missiles; and the [AIM-7 Sparrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AIM-7_Sparrow) and the AIM-120 AMRAAM radar-guided missiles. In 1999, Israel announced its intention to procure more fighters and that a possible contender was the F-15I. However, the contract went to the [F-16I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-16I_Sufa).

### Saudi Arabia

In November 2009, [Royal Saudi Air Force](570367.md) (RSAF) F-15s, along with Saudi Tornados, performed air raids amid the [Houthi insurgency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houthi_insurgency_in_Yemen) in north Yemen. It was the RSAF's first military action over hostile territory since Operation Desert Storm.[^cite_note-123] In October 2010, Saudi Arabia requested 84 F-15SA (Saudi Advanced) fighters, upgrading of its existing F-15S fleet to F-15SA standard, and related equipment and weapons through a [Foreign Military Sale](30857635.md) (FMS).[^cite_note-DSCA_F-15SA_request-124] On 29 December 2011, the U.S. signed a \$29.4 billion contract to sell 84 F-15SAs, as well as the F-15S upgrades.[^cite_note-125] In June 2012, an FMS contract for 68 F-15S to F-15SA modification kits was placed with Boeing; the upgraded aircraft are designated the F-15SR.[^cite_note-126] On 20 February 2013, the maiden flight of the first new-build F-15SA occurred.[^cite_note-AIN_First_F-15SA-127]

#### Saudi-led intervention in Yemen (2015–present)

See also: [List of aviation shootdowns and accidents during the Saudi Arabian-led intervention in Yemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_aviation_shootdowns_and_accidents_during_the_Saudi_Arabian-led_intervention_in_Yemen)

On 26 March 2015, Saudi F-15Ss, along with other Arab coalition assets, started striking targets in Yemen as part of the [Saudi Arabian-led intervention in Yemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saudi_Arabian-led_intervention_in_Yemen), called *Operation Decisive Storm*. Opposing a joint force composed of former Houthi rebels and Yemeni Army forces, the strikes, at least initially, were met by ineffective anti-aircraft fire that reportedly only caused damage when falling to the ground.[^cite_note-128] Early strikes were aimed at air defense sites, Army HQs, military airports, ballistic missiles depots, and launchers.[^cite_note-129] During these attacks, a Saudi F-15S crashed into the Gulf of Aden after circling over the sea; its two pilots ejected safely and were recovered from the sea by a USAF [HH-60G](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikorsky_HH-60_Pave_Hawk) rescue helicopter; Arab coalition reports claimed enemy fire was not involved,[^cite_note-130] while Houthi and Iranian sources claimed they had shot it down.[^cite_note-JANESWM-131] On 8 January 2018, a RSAF F-15S was reportedly shot down by a Houthi surface-to-air missile; a Houthi-released video shows the F-15 increasing speed and releasing decoy flares before being struck by a projectile and apparently suffering major damage.[^cite_note-132][^cite_note-133] On 9 January 2018, the Houthi media, Al-Masirah, announced that the F-15 had been damaged but did not crash.[^cite_note-134]

On 21 March 2018, [Houthi rebels](17240332.md) released a video allegedly showing a RSAF F-15 being hit in [Saada province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saada_Governorate) by an [R-27](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R-27_\(air-to-air_missile\)) air to air missile adapted for surface to air use.[^cite_note-135] As in the video of the previous similar hit recorded on 8 January, the target, while clearly hit, seems not falling to the ground when the video stops. Saudi forces confirmed the hit, while saying the jet safely landed at a Saudi base.[^cite_note-136] Saudi sources confirmed the incident involved a surface-to-air missile being launched at the jet from inside Saada airport.[^cite_note-137][^cite_note-138]

#### F-15I&#x20;

The F-15I is operated by the Israeli Air Force where it is known as the *Ra'am* (רעם – "Thunder"). It is a dual-seat ground attack aircraft powered by two [Pratt & Whitney F100-PW-229](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_&_Whitney_F100#F100-PW-229) engines, and is based on the F-15E.

The F-15I has featured different avionic systems from the USAF F-15E to meet Israeli requirements and also due to export restictions relating the resolution of the APG-70 system. Initially, Sharpshooter targeting pods designed for IAF F-16s were fitted for nighttime strikes but were less capable than the LANTIRN pods used on USAF F-15Es. Israel later bought 30 LANTIRN pods. The F-15Is initially lacked [Radar Warning Receivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_Warning_Receiver); Israel installed its own Elisra ASPS electronic warfare suite with a new central computer and embedded GPS/INS system. All sensors can be slaved to the [Display and Sight Helmet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmet-mounted_display) (DASH) helmet-mounted sight, providing both crew members a means of targeting which the F-15E lacks. The F-15I uses the APG-70I radar; its terrain mapping capability can locate targets difficult to spot while under adverse weather conditions and can detect large airliner-sized targets at 150 nautical miles (170 mi; 280 km), and fighter-sized targets at 56 nmi (64 mi; 104 km); it has degraded resolution one-third below the original APG-70.

### F-15 Advanced Eagle variants

For later F-15SA, F-15QA, F-15EX, F-15IA, and other Advanced Eagle variants, see [Boeing F-15EX Eagle II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_F-15EX_Eagle_II).

The F-15 Advanced Eagle represents a more substantial upgrade baseline over previous models in that it has a new [fly-by-wire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fly-by-wire) control system and wing structure that enables two additional underwing weapons hardpoints (increasing the number from nine to eleven). Additional enhancements include the option of a large area display cockpit, the Raytheon AN/APG-82(V)1 or APG-63(V)3 AESA radar, [General Electric F110-129](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric_F110#F110-GE-129) engines, digital [Joint Helmet-Mounted Cueing Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_Helmet-Mounted_Cueing_System) for pilot and WSO, and a digital electronic warfare system among other enhancements. Advanced Eagle variants include the F-15SA (Saudi Advanced), F-15QA (Qatari Advanced), F-15EX Eagle II, and F-15IA (Israeli Advanced).[^cite_note-AIN_First_F-15SA-127][^cite_note-170][^cite_note-171][^cite_note-172]
