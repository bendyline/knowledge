The **coccyx** (pl.: **coccyges** or **coccyxes**), commonly referred to as the **tailbone**, is the final segment of the [vertebral column](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertebral_column) in all [apes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ape),[^cite_note-CNN-20240323-1] and analogous structures in certain other mammals such as [horses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horse_anatomy). In tailless [primates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primates) (e.g. [humans](682482.md) and other [great apes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_ape)) since *[Nacholapithecus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nacholapithecus)* (a [Miocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miocene) [hominoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hominoid)),[^cite_note-Nakatsukasa-J-Anatomy-2][^cite_note-3] the coccyx is the remnant of a [vestigial tail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_vestigiality#Coccyx). In animals with bony tails, it is known as [*tailhead* or *dock*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rump_\(animal\)), in [bird anatomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird_anatomy) as *tailfan*. It comprises three to five separate or fused coccygeal vertebrae below the [sacrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrum), attached to the sacrum by a [fibrocartilaginous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibrocartilaginous) [joint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint), the [sacrococcygeal symphysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrococcygeal_symphysis), which permits limited movement between the sacrum and the coccyx.

## Structure



A coccyx with four vertebrae below the sacrum

The coccyx is formed of three, four or five rudimentary [vertebrae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertebra). It articulates superiorly with the [sacrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrum). In each of the first three segments may be traced a rudimentary body and [articular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Articular_processes) and [transverse processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transverse_process); the last piece (sometimes the third) is a mere nodule of bone. The transverse processes are most prominent and noticeable on the first coccygeal segment. All the segments lack [pedicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedicle_of_vertebral_arch), [laminae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamina_of_the_vertebral_arch) and [spinous processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinous_process). The first segment is the largest; it resembles the lowest [sacral vertebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacral_vertebra), and often exists as a separate piece; the remaining ones diminish in size [caudally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroanatomy#Orientation_in_neuroanatomy).

Most anatomy books incorrectly state that the coccyx is normally fused in adults. It has been shown that the coccyx may, in some people, consist of up to five separate bony segments, the most common configuration being two or three segments.[^cite_note-pmid28091426-4]

### Surfaces

The [anterior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anterior) surface is slightly concave and marked with three transverse grooves which indicate the junctions of the different segments. It gives attachment to the [anterior sacrococcygeal ligament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anterior_sacrococcygeal_ligament) and the [levatores ani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levator_ani) and supports part of the [rectum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectum). The [posterior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posterior_\(anatomy\)) surface is convex, marked by transverse grooves similar to those on the anterior surface, and presents on either side a linear row of tubercles – the undeveloped [articular processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Articular_processes) of the coccygeal vertebrae. Of these, the superior pair are the largest, and are called the **[coccygeal cornua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coccygeal_cornu)** they project [caudally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroanatomy#Orientation_in_neuroanatomy), and articulate with the cornua of the sacrum, and on either side complete the [foramen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foramen) for the transmission of the [posterior division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumbar_nerves#Posterior_divisions) of the [fifth sacral nerve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacral_spinal_nerve_5).

### Borders

The lateral borders are thin and exhibit a series of small bony protrusions, which represent the transverse processes of the coccygeal vertebrae. Of these, the first is the largest; it is flattened anteriorly, and often extends to join the lower part of the thin lateral edge of the sacrum, thus completing the foramen for the transmission of the [anterior division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ventral_ramus_of_spinal_nerve) of the fifth sacral nerve; the others diminish in size caudally, and are often lacking. The borders of the coccyx are narrow, and give attachment on either side to the [sacrotuberous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrotuberous_ligament) and [sacrospinous ligaments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrospinous_ligament), to the [coccygeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coccygeus_muscle) and [levator ani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levator_ani) in front of the ligaments, and to the [gluteus maximus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gluteus_maximus) behind them.

### Apex

The apex is rounded, and has attached to it the tendon of the [external anal sphincter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/External_anal_sphincter); it may be divided in two.

### Coccygeal fossa

The **coccygeal fossa** is a shallow depression on the posterior surface between the [sacrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrum) and the [perineum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perineum), located in the [intergluteal cleft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intergluteal_cleft) that runs from just below the sacrum to the [perineum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perineum).[^cite_note-5] It is not consistently present in all humans. The coccygeal fossa marks the deepest part of the [pelvic floor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelvic_floor), next to the coccyx. The [levator ani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levator_ani) has its [origin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origin_\(anatomy\)) here.[^cite_note-6]

### Extensor coccygis

The **extensor coccygis** is a slender [muscle fascicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muscle_fascicle), which is not always present. It extends over the caudal portion of the posterior surface of the [sacrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrum) and coccyx. It arises by tendinous fibers from the last segment of the sacrum, or first piece of the coccyx, and passes downward to be inserted into the lower part of the coccyx. It is an evolutionary relic of the extensor muscle of the caudal vertebrae of other animals, enabling limited coccygeal motion.

### Sacrococcygeal and intercoccygeal joints

The joints are variable and may be: (1) [synovial joints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synovial_joints); (2) thin discs of [fibrocartilage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibrocartilage); (3) intermediate between these two; (4) ossified.[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8]

### Attachments

The anterior side of the coccyx has attachments to the [levator ani muscle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levator_ani_muscle), [coccygeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coccygeus_muscle), [iliococcygeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iliococcygeus_muscle), and [pubococcygeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pubococcygeus_muscle), [anococcygeal raphe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anococcygeal_raphe). Attached to the posterior side is the [gluteus maximus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gluteus_maximus_muscle), which extends the [thigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thigh) at the hip joint.[^cite_note-Foye-eMed-CocPain-9] The [ligaments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ligament) attached to the coccyx include the [anterior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anterior_sacrococcygeal_ligament) and [posterior sacrococcygeal ligaments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posterior_sacrococcygeal_ligament) which are the continuations of the [anterior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anterior_longitudinal_ligament) and [posterior longitudinal ligaments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posterior_longitudinal_ligament) that extend along the entire spine.[^cite_note-Foye-eMed-CocPain-9] The [lateral sacrococcygeal ligaments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lateral_sacrococcygeal_ligament) complete the [foramina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foramina) for the [last sacral nerve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacral_spinal_nerve_5).[^cite_note-Morris-59-10] Some fibers of the [sacrospinous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrospinous_ligament) and [sacrotuberous ligaments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrotuberous_ligament) (arising from the [spine of the ischium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ischial_spine) and the [ischial tuberosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ischial_tuberosity) respectively) also attach to the coccyx.[^cite_note-Foye-eMed-CocPain-9] An extension of the [pia mater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pia_mater), the [filum terminale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filum_terminale), extends from the apex of the [conus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conus_medullaris), and inserts on the coccyx.

### Haemal arches

[Haemal arches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haemal_arch) are usually absent in adult humans, though they appear and are resorbed during the embryonic phase of development. Rarely, however, the first coccygeal vertebra bears a haemal arch on its ventral side. More rarely, the second coccygeal vertebra may also have a haemal arch.[^cite_note-11]

## Function

The coccyx is not entirely useless in humans,[^cite_note-Saladin-285-12] because it has attachments to various muscles, tendons and ligaments. However, these muscles, tendons and ligaments are also attached at many other points, to stronger structures than the coccyx. It is doubtful that the coccyx attachments are important to the well-being of humans, given the large number of [coccygectomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coccygectomy) procedures performed annually to treat [coccydynia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coccydynia). Reviews of studies covering more than 700 coccygectomies found the operation was successful in relieving pain in 84% of cases.[^cite_note-aarby-13][^cite_note-14] 12% of the time, the only major complication faced was infection due to the proximity to the anus. One notable [complication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complication_\(medicine\)) of coccygectomy is an increased risk of [perineal hernia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perineal_hernia).

## Clinical significance

Injuring the coccyx can give rise to a painful condition called [coccydynia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coccydynia) and one or more of the bones or the connections thereof may be broken, [fractured tailbone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coccyx_fracture).[^cite_note-Maigne-15][^cite_note-Foye-2006-16] A number of [tumors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tumor) are known to involve the coccyx; of these, the most common is [sacrococcygeal teratoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrococcygeal_teratoma). Both coccydynia and coccygeal tumors may require surgical removal of the coccyx ([coccygectomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coccygectomy)). One very rare complication of coccygectomy is a type of [perineal hernia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perineal_hernia) known as a coccygeal hernia.[^cite_note-pmid17889632-17]

## Etymology

The term *coccyx* is derived from the [ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) word κόκκυξ[^cite_note-Klein1971-18][^cite_note-19] *kokkyx* "cuckoo";[^cite_note-Liddell_&_Scott-20] the latter is attested in the writings of the Greek [physician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physician) [Herophilus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herophilus) to denote the end of the vertebral column.[^cite_note-Hyrtl1880-21] This Greek name for the [cuckoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuckoo) was applied as the last three or four bones of the coccyx resemble the beak of this bird,[^cite_note-Klein1971-18][^cite_note-Hyrtl1880-21][^cite_note-Kraus1844-22][^cite_note-Panourias2011-23] when viewed from the side.[^cite_note-Foye-eMed-CocPain-9][^cite_note-24]

This established etymological explanation can also be found in the writings of the 16th century anatomist [Andreas Vesalius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andreas_Vesalius) who wrote: *os cuculi, a similitudine rostri cuculi avis*[^cite_note-Hyrtl1880-21] (the cuckoo bone shows a likeness to the beak of the cuckoo bird). Vesalius used the Latin expression *os cuculi*, with *os*, bone[^cite_note-Lewis_&_Short-25] and *cuculus*, the Latin name for the cuckoo.[^cite_note-Lewis_&_Short-25] The 16th/17th century French anatomist [Jean Riolan the Younger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Riolan_the_Younger) gives a rather hilarious etymological explanation, as he writes: *quia crepitus, qui per sedimentum exeunt, ad is os allisi, cuculi vocis similitudinem effingunt*[^cite_note-Hyrtl1880-21] (because the sound of the farts that leave the anus and dash against this bone, shows a likeness to the call of the cuckoo). Riolan's explanation is not considered credible.[^cite_note-Hyrtl1880-21][^cite_note-Kraus1844-22]

Besides *os cuculi*, *os caudae*,[^cite_note-Hyrtl1880-21][^cite_note-Schreger-26] with *caudae*, of the tail[^cite_note-Lewis_&_Short-25] is attested. This Latin expression might be the source of the English, [French language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language), [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language) and [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_language) terms *tailbone*, *l'os de la queue*,[^cite_note-Schreger-26] *Schwanzbein*[^cite_note-Kraus1844-22][^cite_note-Schreger-26] and *_staartbeen_*.[^cite_note-Pinkhof2012-27] In the current official anatomic Latin nomenclature, *[Terminologia Anatomica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminologia_Anatomica)*,[^cite_note-FCAT-28] *coccyx* and *os coccygis* is used.
