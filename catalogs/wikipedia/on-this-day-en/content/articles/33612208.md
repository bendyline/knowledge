**Paul Jonathan Goldsmith** (born 1971) is a New Zealand historian and politician. The biographer of several leading right-wing political and business figures, he was first elected a list member of the [New Zealand House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_House_of_Representatives) for the [National Party](333149.md) at the [2011 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_New_Zealand_general_election).

Goldsmith is [Minister for Arts, Culture and Heritage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Arts,_Culture_and_Heritage), [Minister of Justice](12866459.md), [Minister for Treaty of Waitangi Negotiations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Treaty_of_Waitangi_Negotiations), [Minister for Media and Communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Media_and_Communications), Minister for the [Public Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Service_Commission_\(New_Zealand\)), [Minister for Digitising Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Digitising_Government), and [Minister for Pacific Peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Pacific_Peoples) in the [Sixth National Government](75059562.md). On 12 August 2026, Goldsmith received the [Minister of Defence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_of_Defence_\(New_Zealand\)), [Minister for Veterans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Veterans_\(New_Zealand\)), [Minister for Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Space) portfolios after they were dismissed from [Chris Penk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chris_Penk) following an unsuccessful leadership challenge on [Prime Minister](106321.md) [Christopher Luxon](62303596.md) that triggered a confidence vote. [^cite_note-\:3-1][^cite_note-Beehive_2_Apr_2026-2] He was previously an [Auckland City Councillor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auckland_City_Council) between 2007 and 2010, and a [Cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_of_New_Zealand) minister in the [Fifth National Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth_National_Government_of_New_Zealand).

## Early life and family

Goldsmith was born in 1971 in the [Auckland](18660332.md) suburb of [Mount Eden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Eden) to parents Lawrence, a mathematics teacher, and Margaret, a [palliative care](275509.md) nurse.[^cite_note-\:4-3] He has an older brother and sister.[^cite_note-\:4-3]

The Goldsmith family descends from Charles George Goldsmith, a migrant from Liverpool who settled in the [East Cape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Cape) area early in New Zealand's colonial history. Charles Goldsmith had four wives—two [Māori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ori_people) ([Ngāti Porou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ng%C4%81ti_Porou)), and two [Pākehā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C4%81keh%C4%81)—fathering 16 children. However, Goldsmith has clarified that he is not himself of Māori descent.[^cite_note-4]

Goldsmith attended [Auckland Grammar School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auckland_Grammar_School) and received an MA in history from the [University of Auckland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Auckland) in 1996. His thesis was on the life of missionary [William Colenso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Colenso). Dame [Judith Binney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judith_Binney) was his master's supervisor.[^cite_note-5][^cite_note-\:5-6]

Paul Goldsmith is married to Melissa. They have four children.[^cite_note-7] He is a pianist and a second-dan black belt in [taekwondo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taekwondo).[^cite_note-invisible-8]

## Career before politics

He began working as a historian for the [Waitangi Tribunal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waitangi_Tribunal) in March 1995, contributing to the Tribunal's work on historic claims in Taranaki and Wairarapa.[^cite_note-\:5-6][^cite_note-\:6-9] After a year, he moved to working for [John Banks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Banks_\(New_Zealand_politician\)), then the [National Party](333149.md) [minister of local government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_of_Local_Government_\(New_Zealand\)), as a press secretary and speech writer. Goldsmith also began a biography of Banks during this period. Banks was not reappointed a minister after the [1996 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_New_Zealand_general_election); Goldsmith moved to working for the [environment minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_for_the_Environment_\(New_Zealand\)) [Simon Upton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Upton) and, after the [1999 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1999_New_Zealand_general_election), the new [Labour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Labour_Party) [minister of foreign affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_of_Foreign_Affairs_\(New_Zealand\)) [Phil Goff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phil_Goff).[^cite_note-invisible-8][^cite_note-\:7-10]

In 2000, Goldsmith became a public relations adviser and worked for [Tranz Rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tranz_Rail) and the University of Auckland.[^cite_note-invisible-8]

### Historian and biographer

See also: [Paul Goldsmith bibliography](#Bibliography)

Goldsmith has written the biographies of John Banks, [Don Brash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Don_Brash), [William Gallagher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Gallagher_\(inventor\)), Alan Gibbs and Te Hemara Tauhia as well as a history of taxes, [Puketutu Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puketutu_Island) and a history of the [Fletcher Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fletcher_Building) construction company.[^cite_note-invisible-8] The biographies were criticised in 2021 by labour historian Ross Webb as "hagiographies" of those involved in New Zealand's economic reforms in the 1980s and 1990s.[^cite_note-\:6-9]

His Don Brash biography, *[Brash: A Biography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brash:_A_Biography)*, was a source of controversy. When it was released in 2005 he maintained it was not commissioned by the National Party,[^cite_note-\:8-11] but investigative journalist [Nicky Hager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicky_Hager) revealed it was indeed commissioned by the National Party and was in fact the party's first big-budget item in the [2005 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_New_Zealand_general_election) campaign.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHager2006192–93-12]

## Early political career

Goldsmith contested the [Maungakiekie electorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maungakiekie_\(New_Zealand_electorate\)) in the [2005 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_New_Zealand_general_election) for the [National Party](333149.md).[^cite_note-invisible-8] At this time, National was led by Don Brash, whose biography by Goldsmith was published the same year.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHager2006192–93-12][^cite_note-\:8-11] Goldsmith was defeated by the incumbent, Labour's [Mark Gosche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Gosche), and due to his low list placing (59 on the National Party list),[^cite_note-invisible-8] did not enter Parliament.[^cite_note-2005_Maungakiekie-13]

Goldsmith successfully stood for the [Auckland City Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auckland_City_Council) Hobson Ward at the [2007 local body elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_New_Zealand_local_elections) as a member of [Citizens & Ratepayers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizens_&_Ratepayers).[^cite_note-14] He was appointed deputy finance chairman by [Mayor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor_of_Auckland_City) John Banks and chaired the community services committee.[^cite_note-invisible-8][^cite_note-2010_Auckland_Council_-_Albert-Eden-Roskill_Ward-15] During his term, Goldsmith was criticised by the Auckland City Mission and the [Green Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Party_of_Aotearoa_New_Zealand) for instructing council officers to investigate removing homeless people from the city centre and refusing to rule out arresting homeless people to do so.[^cite_note-16]

He sought but missed out on the [Citizens & Ratepayers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizens_&_Ratepayers) candidacy in the [Ōrākei ward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%8Cr%C4%81kei_ward)[^cite_note-17] of the new [Auckland Council](22312574.md) at the [2010 Auckland elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Auckland_local_elections), and was instead selected to contest [Albert-Eden-Roskill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert-Eden-Roskill). He finished third, behind [Christine Fletcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christine_Fletcher) and [Cathy Casey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathy_Casey), in the two-member ward.[^cite_note-2010_Auckland_Council_-_Albert-Eden-Roskill_Ward-15]

## Opposition, 2017–2023

During the [2017 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_New_Zealand_general_election), Goldsmith was re-elected as a list MP after coming second place in the Epsom electorate.[^cite_note-27] At the beginning of the parliamentary term, as an opposition MP, he was the party spokesperson for arts, culture and heritage.[^cite_note-\:0-25] Following the March 2018 National Party portfolio reshuffle, Goldsmith became spokesperson for revenue and economic and regional development.[^cite_note-\:0-25][^cite_note-28] Later in the year, he lost the revenue portfolio, but became transport spokesperson.[^cite_note-\:0-25]

In 2019, Goldsmith assumed the spokesperson role for finance and infrastructure and the third-ranked member of the National caucus.[^cite_note-29][^cite_note-\:0-25] *Stuff.co.nz* writer Thomas Coughlan described him as "possibly the most libertarian or right-wing person to hold the shadow finance portfolio."[^cite_note-\:7-10] Political columnist Danyl McLauchlan said Goldsmith was "possibly the only remaining member of his party who could be described as 'neoliberal'."[^cite_note-30] Goldsmith was additionally spokesperson for [state-owned enterprises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_state-owned_enterprises_of_New_Zealand) between February and May 2020 and for the [Earthquake Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthquake_Commission) between May and November 2020.[^cite_note-\:0-25] Between March and May 2020, Goldsmith was a member of the [Epidemic Response Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epidemic_Response_Committee), a select committee that considered the government's response to the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_New_Zealand).[^cite_note-31]

Goldsmith's budget for the National Party's 2020 election campaign was found to have several errors, some of which Goldsmith accepted.[^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33] Re-elected for a fourth term as a list MP,[^cite_note-34][^cite_note-35] Goldsmith lost the finance role after the election and became spokesperson for education and a member of the education and workforce committee until 2021.[^cite_note-\:0-25] When [Christopher Luxon](62303596.md) became National leader in 2021, Goldsmith became justice spokesperson.[^cite_note-36]

## Sixth National Government, 2023–present

Goldsmith won a fifth term as a National list MP in October 2023. Though he failed to [unseat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unseating) [ACT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACT_New_Zealand) leader [David Seymour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Seymour_\(New_Zealand_politician\)), he was re-elected on the National Party list.[^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38]

On 27 November 2023, Goldsmith was announced as the [Minister for Arts, Culture and Heritage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Arts,_Culture_and_Heritage), [Minister of Justice](12866459.md), [Minister for State Owned Enterprises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_State_Owned_Enterprises) and [Minister for Treaty of Waitangi Negotiations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Treaty_of_Waitangi_Negotiations) in the [Sixth National Government](75059562.md).[^cite_note-\:3-1]

On 24 April 2024, Luxon appointed Goldsmith as [Minister for Media and Communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Media_and_Communications) during a [cabinet reshuffle](845103.md). Goldsmith replaced [Melissa Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melissa_Lee), who had faced criticism for her response to [Warner Bros. Discovery New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky_Free)'s closure of [Newshub](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newshub).[^cite_note-39]

During a [cabinet reshuffle](845103.md) that occurred in early April 2026, Goldsmith acquired the [Public Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Service_Commission_\(New_Zealand\)), [Digitising Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Digitising_Government), and [Pacific Peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_for_Pacific_Peoples) portfolios.[^cite_note-1News_2_Apr_2026-40][^cite_note-Beehive_2_Apr_2026-2] Following [Chris Penk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chris_Penk)'s unsuccessful leadership challenge against Luxon on 12 August, Goldsmith assumed the [defence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_of_Defence_\(New_Zealand\)), [veterans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Veterans_\(New_Zealand\)), [space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Space), [Government Communications Security Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Communications_Security_Bureau) and [New Zealand Security Intelligence Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Security_Intelligence_Service) portfolios.[^cite_note-41]

### Sentencing, law and order

Goldsmith has repeatedly criticised what he describes as “undue leniency” in sentencing, including the use of short or non-custodial sentences for serious offences. He argues that short prison sentences undermine public confidence in the justice system and fail to deter crime, especially violent and repeat offences.[^cite_note-42] He has introduced caps for sentence discounts, prevented repeat discounts for youth and remorse, and encouraged cumulative sentencing for crimes committed on bail, in custody, or on parole. He has stated that victims’ interests must be prioritised over leniency for offenders.[^cite_note-43]

On 26 June 2024, Goldsmith confirmed that the Government would encourage judges to hand down "cumulative" sentences for offences committed on parole, bail or in custody. Earlier, the National-led Government had announced it would require "cumulative" sentences for such offenses but backtracked due to concerns about increasing the prison population.[^cite_note-44]

On 15 July 2024, Goldsmith in his capacity as Justice Minister signed an extradition order against fugitive [Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet) millionaire [Kim Dotcom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kim_Dotcom). Dotcom had spent the past 12 years fighting against extradition to the United States where he is facing several charges of copyright infringement, money laundering and racketeering related to his [Megaupload](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megaupload) website.[^cite_note-45]

On 29 June 2025, Goldsmith proposed legislation introducing higher penalties for those assaulting [correctional officers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correctional_officer) and first responders.[^cite_note-46] On 30 June, Goldsmith confirmed that the Government would introduce legislation creating specific offences for [coward punching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sucker_punch) as part of the National Party's coalition agreement with [New Zealand First](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_First).[^cite_note-47]

On 1 July 2025, Goldsmith and Associate Justice Minister [Nicole McKee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicole_McKee) announced several proposed tougher penalties that they claim will combat [shoplifting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoplifting). These included introducing infringement fees for shoplifting offences above NZ\$500 and NZ\$100, increasing prison terms to one year for stolen goods worth NZ\$2,000 and seven years for goods worth NZ \$2,000 or more, and creating a new aggravated shoplifting offence. These changes were part of National's coalition agreement with NZ First.[^cite_note-48] On 3 July, Goldsmith and McKee announced that the Government would increase the maximum trespass period from two to three years, and raise the maximum fines for trespassing.[^cite_note-49]

On 13 August 2025, Goldsmith announced that the Government would amend the [Crimes Act 1961](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimes_Act_1961) to strengthen the country's [human trafficking](19333662.md) and [people smuggling](68714.md) laws.[^cite_note-50] On 15 August, Goldsmith announced the Government would introduce legislation banning protests outside people's private homes. Labour leader Hipkins indicated his party was discussing whether to support the proposed law.[^cite_note-51]

### Retail crime advisory group

On 11 July 2024, Goldsmith and Associate Justice Minister [Nicole McKee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicole_McKee) announced the formation of a new retail crime advisory group (formally, the Ministerial Advisory Group for Victims of Retail Crime) to engage with victims, workers, business owners, retail experts and advocacy groups to combat retail crime. The advisory group was allocated NZ\$1.8 million a year and expected to last two years.[^cite_note-52]

On 10 February 2026, Goldsmith confirmed that the retail crime advisory group would be dissolved in May 2026, four months ahead of its planned dissolution in September 2026.[^cite_note-\:10-53] The group had earlier faced criticism over its costs and spending habits.[^cite_note-54] Three of the group's five members had resigned due to disagreements with the group's chairman Sunny Kaushal.[^cite_note-\:10-53]

### Cultural reports

On 7 February 2024, Justice Minister Goldsmith announced that government funding for [cultural reports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_report) would be stopped under the new [National-led government](75059562.md).[^cite_note-55] A month later, the Government passed urgent legislation ending legal aid funding for [cultural reports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_report), claiming defendants were using them to get discounts off the length of their sentence with "no benefits to the real victims of crime."[^cite_note-56][^cite_note-57] The decision was criticized by lawyers and academics. Former district court judge, Dr David Harvey, said that judges would no longer have the kind of information they need to craft a proper sentence.[^cite_note-58]

### Gang insignia

On 25 February 2024, Justice Minister Goldsmith and [Police Minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_of_Police_\(New_Zealand\)) [Mark Mitchell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Mitchell_\(New_Zealand_politician\)) announced that the Government would introduce legislation to ban [gang insignia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gang_patch) in public places, enable Police to disperse gang gatherings, allow Courts to ban gang members from communicating for at least three years, and giving greater weight to gang membership during sentencing. Goldsmith argued that the Government needed to take action in response to a 51% increase in gang membership (over 3,000 individuals) over the past five years.[^cite_note-59] On 19 September 2024, the Government passed [legislation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gangs_Act_2024) banning [gang patches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gang_patch).[^cite_note-60][^cite_note-61]

### Hate speech

In early April 2024, Goldsmith ordered that work on the previous [Labour Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sixth_Labour_Government_of_New_Zealand)'s efforts to develop hate speech legislation protecting religious communities be halted, saying it undermined free speech. Goldsmith's decision drew criticism from [Federation of Islamic Associations of New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federation_of_Islamic_Associations_of_New_Zealand) (FIANZ) President Abdur Razzaq, who argued that there needed to be limits to free speech when it crossed the lines into defamation and incitement of violence and hatred.[^cite_note-62]

### Anti-stalking legislation

On 10 November 2024, Goldsmith confirmed that the Government would introduce new anti-[stalking](25272420.md) legislation with new restraining and harmful digital communications orders, and prison terms for stalking.[^cite_note-63] On 21 November, Goldsmith confirmed the Government would introduce legislation to give sexual abuse victims the power to decide whether convicted offenders should receive name suppression.[^cite_note-64]

On 10 June 2025, Goldsmith released details of the Government's proposed Crime Legislation (Stalking and Harassment) Amendment Bill. Under the proposed legislation, stalkers could be imprisoned for committing two stalking or harassment acts within 24 months instead of three acts within 12 months. In addition, [doxxing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doxxing) would be classified as a form of stalking and people convicted of stalking or harassment could be subject to Firearms Prohibition Orders.[^cite_note-65] The bill passed its third reading on 20 December 2025.[^cite_note-66] It came into effect on 26 May 2026.[^cite_note-67]

### Treaty of Waitangi issues

In late June 2025, Goldsmith confirmed that the Government would not progress with a [Treaty of Waitangi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Waitangi) settlement with the Māori [iwi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iwi) (tribe) [Te Whānau-ā-Apanui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Te_Wh%C4%81nau-%C4%81-Apanui) due to a disagreement with the tribe over an "agree to disagree" clause stating that the tribe had never ceded sovereignty to the [New Zealand Crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Crown). During a parliamentary select committee, Goldsmith stated: "The Crown's position is clear; the Crown is sovereign. The Crown is simply the representation of the democratic will of the people of New Zealand." In response, Te Whānau-ā-Apanui said that it was open to negotiating with the Crown "but it would not seek a settlement at all costs or at the price of our [rangatiratanga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tino_rangatiratanga) (chieftainship or self-determination)."[^cite_note-68] The previous Labour Government had included the "agree to disagree" clause in a [Deed of Settlement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deed_of_Settlement) that it had initialled with Te Whānau-ā-Apanui in 2023.[^cite_note-RNZ_24_Jun_2025-69]

In late June 2025, Goldsmith stated that the Government preferred signing one commercial settlement with the Māori tribe [Ngāpuhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ng%C4%81puhi) instead of having multiple settlements. Coalition partner [New Zealand First](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_First) MP [Shane Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shane_Jones) had introduced a member's bill seeking to force Ngāpuhi to sign a single commercial settlement with the Government. Labour MP [Peeni Henare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peeni_Henare), who is of Ngāpuhi descent, said that Goldsmith was motivated by financial considerations and said that Jones' bill would go against the "good faith" provisions of Treaty settlements. Labour leader [Chris Hipkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chris_Hipkins) said that the National-led government's "hostile" position towards Māori would make Treaty settlements in the next few years "very, very difficult to achieve."[^cite_note-RNZ_24_Jun_2025-69]

As Justice Minister, Goldsmith also chaired a ministerial oversight group seeking to weaken references the [Principles of the Treaty of Waitangi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principles_of_the_Treaty_of_Waitangi) across 23 laws as part of the National Party's coalition agreement with New Zealand First. Other members of this oversight group included the-then Attorney-General [Judith Collins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judith_Collins), Regional Development Minister [Shane Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shane_Jones) and Māori-Crown relations Minister [Tama Potaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tama_Potaka).[^cite_note-Rātana_21_Apr_2026-70][^cite_note-Dunlop_20_Apr_2026-71] During the consultation process, Goldsmith directed Justice Ministry officials not to hold regional [hui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hui) (meetings) with iwi but to limit their engagement to a Crown-appointed ministerial advisory group and several government agencies.[^cite_note-72] This ministerial advisory group consisting of lawyer David Cochrane as chair, iwi leader Marama Royal, lawyer John Walters and lawyer and Act Party candidate James Christmas.[^cite_note-Rātana_21_Apr_2026-70] The proposed removal of Treaty clauses drew criticism from several Māori leaders, legal and Treaty experts including [University of Auckland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Auckland) law professor [Margaret Mutu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Mutu), associate professor Andrew Erueti, [Te Pāti Māori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Te_P%C4%81ti_M%C4%81ori) co-leader [Rawiri Waititi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rawiri_Waititi), Northland leader Pita Tipene, [Victoria University of Wellington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_University_of_Wellington) law senior lecturer Luke Fitzmaurice-Brown and the [National Iwi Chairs Forum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Iwi_Chairs_Forum) who said they would breach and undermine the Crown's Treaty obligations to Māori.[^cite_note-Dunlop_20_Apr_2026-71][^cite_note-Hurihanganui_24_Apr_2026-73][^cite_note-Paewai_1_May_2026-74][^cite_note-75] Despite the Ministry of Justice advising against the removal of Treaty clauses and the ministerial advisory group recommending tweaks to make the Treaty clauses more "descriptive," Goldsmith confirmed that the Government would proceed with plans to weaken Treaty obligations in mid-April 2026.[^cite_note-Hurihanganui_24_Apr_2026-73][^cite_note-76] This review is expected to affect nine laws; with five laws having Treaty principles provisions repealed, two laws having their Treaty principles provisions consolidated, and two laws having their Treaty principles provisions amended to make them "more specific."[^cite_note-Paewai_1_May_2026-74] On 15 May 2026, Goldsmith confirmed the Government was seeking to amend Treaty clauses in 19 pieces of legislation.[^cite_note-77]

### Electoral amendment legislation

Main article: [Electoral Amendment Act 2025](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_Amendment_Act_2025)

On 30 April 2025, Goldsmith confirmed that the Government would introduce legislation to [reinstate a blanket ban on prisoners voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_rights_of_prisoners_in_New_Zealand), describing it as a reversal of the previous Labour Government's "soft on crime" policy. Goldsmith said that the Cabinet had decided to disregard a [High Court](3902269.md) ruling and recommendations from both the [Electoral Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_Commission_\(New_Zealand\)) and [Waitangi Tribunal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waitangi_Tribunal) that prisoners be allowed to vote.[^cite_note-78] In response, the Labour, Green parties and Te Pāti Māori described the government's plan to strip prisoners of voting rights as a violation of human rights, counterproductive to rehabilitation and discriminatory against [Māori people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ori_people). By contrast, the [Sensible Sentencing Trust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensible_Sentencing_Trust)'s spokesperson Louise Parsons welcomed the government's announcement, saying that prisoners had lost their rights to be a part of "functioning society" due to their crimes.[^cite_note-79]

On 24 July 2025, Goldsmith confirmed that the Government would introduce new electoral amendment legislation. Key provisions include closing voter enrollment 13 days before election day, setting a 12-day advance voting period, automatic enrollment updates, removing the postal requirements for enrollment. The Government also intends to ban free food, drink or entertainment within 100 metres of a voting place (subject to a NZ\$10,000 fine). In addition, the Government intends to ban all prisoners from voting and will raise the donation threshold from NZ\$5,000 to NZ\$6,000.[^cite_note-80][^cite_note-Newsroom_24_Jul_2025-81] In response, Labour's justice spokesperson [Duncan Webb](52931754.md) expressed disagreement with the Government's plan to eliminate voter enrollment on election day as an invalid reason for restricting the number of people able to exercise their democratic right to vote.[^cite_note-Newsroom_24_Jul_2025-81]

On 17 December 2025, Parliament passed the Government's new electoral amendment legislation into law. Its provisions include [banning prisoners from voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_rights_of_prisoners_in_New_Zealand), limiting food and entertainment around polling booths, and closing voter enrollment two weeks before election day.[^cite_note-82]

### Other

On 14 November 2024, Goldsmith introduced new legislation that criminalised covert and other subversive activities on behalf of a foreign power "to intentionally or recklessly harm New Zealand."[^cite_note-83]

On 10 February 2025, Goldsmith appointed victims advocate [Ruth Money](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruth_Money?action=edit\&redlink=1) as [Chief Victims' Advisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_Victims'_Advisor?action=edit\&redlink=1).[^cite_note-84]

On 6 May 2026 Goldsmith, as [Minister for Media and Communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Media_and_Communications), announced that the Government would enact legislation to disestablish the broadcasting regulator, the [Broadcasting Standards Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcasting_Standards_Authority).[^cite_note-85]

On 13 May 2026 Goldsmith, as Justice Minister, announced that the Government would amend existing climate legislation to prevent companies from being sued over damages caused by [greenhouse gas emissions](9528025.md). The law change is expected to apply to current and future cases including [iwi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iwi) leader [Mike Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mike_Smith_\(activist\))'s 2024 lawsuit against dairy giant [Fonterra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fonterra) and five other major emitters.[^cite_note-86] According to [Radio New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_New_Zealand), [Justice Ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Justice_\(New_Zealand\)) officials had advised the Government not to intervene in Smith's court case. In response, Goldsmith acknowledged receiving a "range of advice and potential outcomes" from officials but reiterated "it is for the elected Government to determine how it moves forward."[^cite_note-87]

## Social and political views

Goldsmith voted in favour of [legalising same-sex marriage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marriage_\(Definition_of_Marriage\)_Amendment_Act_2013) in 2012 and 2013 and in favour of [prohibiting conversion therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversion_Practices_Prohibition_Legislation_Act_2022) in 2022.[^cite_note-88] He opposed the [End of Life Choice Bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End_of_Life_Choice_Act_2019) in 2017 and 2019[^cite_note-89] and voted for the [Abortion Legislation Bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abortion_Legislation_Act_2020) in 2019 but not 2020.[^cite_note-90]

Goldsmith has stated that he would vote against the legalisation of cannabis at the [2020 referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_New_Zealand_cannabis_referendum).[^cite_note-\:2-91] He believes New Zealand should wait and observe the effects of [cannabis legalisation in Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_history_of_cannabis_in_Canada) before making a decision.[^cite_note-\:2-91]

In June 2021, Goldsmith attracted controversy for stating that colonisation had been "on balance" good for Māori because it had led to the creation of New Zealand.[^cite_note-\:1-92][^cite_note-\:9-93] He believes that New Zealand's reconnection with the rest of the world following isolation for centuries was always going to be a "traumatic experience".[^cite_note-\:1-92] Goldsmith refused to apologise for the comments, calling himself "a proud New Zealander."[^cite_note-\:9-93]
