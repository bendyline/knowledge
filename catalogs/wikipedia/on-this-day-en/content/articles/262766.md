**Paramount leader** ([Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_language): 最高领导人; [pinyin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinyin): *Zuìgāo Lǐngdǎorén*) is an informal term for the most important political figure of the [People's Republic of China](5405.md) (PRC). The paramount leader typically controls the [Chinese Communist Party](7175.md) (CCP) and the [People's Liberation Army](66890.md) (PLA), often holding the titles of [CCP General Secretary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Secretary_of_the_Chinese_Communist_Party) and [Chairman of the Central Military Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chairman_of_the_Central_Military_Commission_\(China\)) (CMC).[^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10][^cite_note-BBC:_How_China_is_ruled-11] The [state representative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_state_representatives_of_the_People's_Republic_of_China) ([president](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_China)) or [head of government](37570.md) ([premier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premier_of_China)) are not necessarily paramount leader; under China's [party-state](102459.md) system, CCP roles are politically more important than state titles.[^cite_note-WSJ20191114-12]

The current paramount leader is [Xi Jinping](2017814.md),[^cite_note-13] who is considered to have taken on the role in November 2012 since [assuming the position of CCP general secretary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_secretaryship_of_Xi_Jinping), rather than in March 2013 when he succeeded [Hu Jintao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hu_Jintao) as president.[^cite_note-14]

The paramount leader is not a formal position nor an office unto itself. The term gained prominence during the era of [Deng Xiaoping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deng_Xiaoping) (1978–1989), when he was *de facto* able to wield political power without holding any official or formally significant party or government positions at any given time ([state representative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_state_representatives_of_the_People's_Republic_of_China), [head of government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_premiers_of_the_People's_Republic_of_China) or [leader of the CCP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leader_of_the_Chinese_Communist_Party)), but the Chairman of the CCP Central Military Commission (1981–1989).[^cite_note-15] As the leader of the world's largest economy by [GDP purchasing power parity (PPP)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_\(PPP\)), the second-largest economy by [nominal GDP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GDP_\(nominal\)), and a [potential superpower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_superpower), the paramount leader is considered to be one of the world's most powerful political figures.[^cite_note-16][^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18]

There has been significant overlap between paramount leader status and [leadership core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leadership_core) status, with a majority but not all of paramount leaders being also leadership cores, though they are separate concepts. The term has been used less frequently to describe Deng's successors, [Jiang Zemin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jiang_Zemin), Hu Jintao and Xi Jinping, who have all formally held the offices of General Secretary of the Chinese Communist Party ([party leader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leader_of_the_Chinese_Communist_Party)), [President of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_China) ([state representative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_state_representatives_of_the_People's_Republic_of_China)) and Chairman of the Central Military Commission ([commander-in-chief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Military_Command_of_the_People's_Republic_of_China)). Jiang, Hu and Xi are therefore usually referred to as president in the international scene, the title used by most other republican heads of state.[^cite_note-WSJ20191114-12] However, Deng's successors derive their real power from the post of general secretary, which is the [primary position](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orders_of_precedence_in_the_People's_Republic_of_China) in the Chinese power structure[^cite_note-19] and generally regarded by scholars as the post whose holder can be considered paramount leader.[^cite_note-20] The presidency is a [largely ceremonial office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Figurehead) according to the [Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_China),[^cite_note-22] and the most powerful position in the Chinese political system is the CCP general secretary.[^cite_note-WSJ20191114-12] The general secretary has been the [highest-ranking official](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_precedence_in_China#Order_of_leaders) in China's [political system](23239.md) since 1982.[^cite_note-23] Since Mao every paramount leader developed his own philosophy.[^cite_note-24]

## History

Chairman [Mao Zedong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mao_Zedong) was the undisputed ruler of [Communist China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_China_\(1949%E2%80%931976\)) from its beginning in 1949 and held three chairman offices at once: [Chairman of the Central Committee of the Chinese Communist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chairman_of_the_Chinese_Communist_Party), [Chairman of the Central Military Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chairman_of_the_Central_Military_Commission_\(China\)) and [Chairman of the People's Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chairman_of_the_People's_Republic_of_China) (1954–59), making him the leader of the party, military and state, respectively. Following the [Cultural Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_Revolution), a rough consensus emerged within the party, that the worst excesses were caused by lack of checks and balances in the exercise of political power and the resulting "rule of personality" by Mao.[^cite_note-25]

Beginning in the 1980s, the CCP leadership desired to prevent a [single leader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictator) from rising above the party, as Mao had done. Accordingly, the post of CCP Chairman was abolished in 1982. Most of its functions were transferred to the revived post of General Secretary.[^cite_note-26] The leadership experimented with a quasi-[separation of powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separation_of_powers), whereby the offices of general secretary, president and premier were held by different people. In 1985, for example, the CCP General Secretary was [Hu Yaobang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hu_Yaobang), the [Chinese President](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_China) was [Li Xiannian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Li_Xiannian) and the [Chinese Premier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premier_of_China) was [Zhao Ziyang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhao_Ziyang). However, Deng Xiaoping was still recognized as the [core of the leadership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leadership_core). Both Hu and Zhao fell out of favour in the late 1980s, but Deng was able to retain ultimate political control.

In a discussion with Central Committee members in the lead-up 4th Plenum of the Thirteenth Central Committee (Jun. 23-24 1989), Deng Xiaoping introduced the concept of the "Core Leader". In his analysis, despite the existence of figures like [Chen Duxiu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chen_Duxiu), [Qu Qiubai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qu_Qiubai), [Xiang Zhongfa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xiang_Zhongfa), [Li Lisan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Li_Lisan), and [Wang Ming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wang_Ming), the Party did not have a proper "Core Leader" until the ascent of Mao Zedong at the [Zunyi Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zunyi_Conference) of 1935. Mao's election ushered in the "First Generation" of CCP leadership. As for the second generation, Deng conceded that in retrospect, he had himself been the "Core", but that he had been constantly planning for the transition to a third generation. For this purpose, he encouraged his audience to rally around Jiang Zemin as the core of the "Third Generation".[^cite_note-27] Despite Deng not formally relinquishing the position of Chairman of the Central Military Commission until the 5th Plenum (Nov. 6-9 1989), official histories published by the CCP regard this endorsement, at the 4th Plenum, as the transition from the Deng administration to the Jiang administration.

The paramount leader label has been applied to Deng's successors, Jiang Zemin and Hu Jintao, though it is generally recognized that they did not wield as much power as Deng despite their having held more offices of leadership. There has been a greater emphasis on [collective leadership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collective_leadership), whereby the top leader is a [first among equals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_among_equals) style figure, exercising power with the consensus of the [CCP Politburo Standing Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politburo_Standing_Committee_of_the_Chinese_Communist_Party).[^cite_note-BBC:_How_China_is_ruled-11] This was particularly apparent during the tenure of Hu Jintao.[^cite_note-28] Beginning in 1993, Jiang formally held the three offices that made him the head of the party, state, and military:

- General Secretary of the Chinese Communist Party: the [party leader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Party_leader) and the [primary position](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orders_of_precedence_in_China) of the state.

- Chairman of the Central Military Commission: [Supreme Military Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Military_Command_of_the_People's_Republic_of_China) of the [People's Liberation Army](66890.md).

- President of the People's Republic of China: the largely [ceremonial state representative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Figurehead) under the [1982 Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1982_Constitution_of_the_People's_Republic_of_China).

  [^cite_note-29]

The system of holding the three posts simultaneously has officially been referred to as the "three-in-one" leadership system (“三位一体”领导体制; *“Sān wèi yī tǐ” lǐng dǎo tǐ zhì*).[^cite_note-30] When Jiang left the offices of General Secretary and President in 2002 and 2003, respectively, he held onto the position of Chairman of the Central Military Commission. Military power had always been an important facet in the exercise of political power in [Communist-ruled China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_People's_Republic_of_China) and as such holding the top military post meant Jiang retained some formal power. When Jiang stepped down from his formal posts between 2002 and 2004, it was ambiguous who the paramount leader was. Hu Jintao held the same trio of positions during his years in power. Hu transferred all three positions onto his successor Xi Jinping between November 2012, when Xi became CCP General Secretary and Chairman of the Central Military Commission; and March 2013, when Xi became president. Since Xi's ascendance to power, two new bodies, the [National Security Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Security_Commission_of_the_Chinese_Communist_Party) and [Central Comprehensively Deepening Reforms Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Comprehensively_Deepening_Reforms_Commission), have been established, ostensibly concentrating political power in the paramount leader to a greater degree than anyone since Deng.[^cite_note-xi-31] These bodies were tasked with establishing the general policy direction for national security, as well as economic reform. Both groups are headed by the General Secretary.[^cite_note-32]
