***Nature*** is a British weekly international [scientific journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_journal) publishing [peer-reviewed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peer-reviewed) research across the natural sciences, including biology, physics, chemistry, the earth sciences, and related interdisciplinary fields. It operates editorial offices in London, the United States, continental Europe, and Asia under the international scientific publishing company [Springer Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Springer_Nature). According to the 2022 *[Journal Citation Reports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_Citation_Reports)*, *Nature* had one of the highest [impact factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_factor) among multidisciplinary science journals (50.5), reflecting its strong citation influence within the scientific literature;[^cite_note-WoS-1] some commentators also regard it as among the most influential scientific journals worldwide.[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4] In 2007, *Nature* (together with *[Science](193513.md)*) received the [Prince of Asturias Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princess_of_Asturias_Awards) for Communications and Humanity.[^cite_note-SciNat-5][^cite_note-Prince-6] As of 2012, it claimed an online readership of about three million unique readers per month.[^cite_note-\:2-7]

Founded in the autumn of 1869, *Nature* was first circulated by [Norman Lockyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Lockyer) and [Alexander MacMillan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_MacMillan_\(publisher\)) as a public forum for scientific innovations. The mid-20th century facilitated an editorial expansion for the journal; *Nature* redoubled its efforts in explanatory and [scientific journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_journalism). The late 1980s and early 1990s saw the creation of a network of editorial offices outside of Britain and the establishment of ten new supplementary, specialty publications (e.g. *[Nature Materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_Materials)*). Since the late 2000s, dedicated editorial and current affairs columns have been created weekly, and electoral endorsements are featured. The primary source of the journal remains, as established at its founding, research scientists; editing standards are primarily concerned with technical readability.

The main research published in *Nature* consists mostly of papers (articles or letters) in lightly-edited form. They are highly technical and dense, but, due to imposed text limits, they are typically summaries of larger works. Fewer than 8% of submitted papers are accepted for publication.[^cite_note-8] *Nature*’s research articles are widely cited and often attract international attention, and the journal has occasionally been involved in disputes and academic controversies, such as debates over retractions, peer-review processes, and methodological standards.

In addition to primary research papers, Nature publishes commentary, analysis, news, and features on topics such as research funding, science policy, ethical issues in science, and notable scientific developments; there are also sections on books, arts, and short science fiction stories. Innovations or breakthroughs in any scientific or technological field are featured in the journal as spotlight articles, which are news or magazine-style papers and therefore do not receive similar recognition as research articles. Some spotlight articles are also funded by partners or sponsors.[^cite_note-9]

## History

### Background

The huge progress in science and mathematics during the 19th century was recorded in journals written mostly in [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language) or [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language), as well as in [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language). Britain underwent enormous technological and industrial changes and advances, particularly in the latter half of the 19th century.[^cite_note-10] The most respected scientific journals of this time were the refereed journals of the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society), which had published many of the great works from [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton) and [Michael Faraday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Faraday) to [Charles Darwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Darwin). In addition, the number of popular science periodicals doubled from the 1850s to the 1860s.[^cite_note-Bar3-11] According to the editors of these popular science magazines, the publications were designed to serve as "organs of science", in essence, a means of connecting the public to the scientific world.[^cite_note-Bar3-11]

*Nature*, first created in 1869, was not the first magazine of its kind in Britain. One journal to precede *Nature* was *Recreative Science: A Record and Remembrancer of Intellectual Observation*,[^cite_note-12] which, founded in 1859, began as a [natural history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_history) magazine and progressed to include more physical observational science and technical subjects and less natural history.[^cite_note-Bar7-13] The journal's name changed from its original title to *Intellectual Observer: A Review of Natural History, Microscopic Research, and Recreative Science*[^cite_note-14] and then to the *Student and Intellectual Observer of Science, Literature, and Art*.[^cite_note-Bar6-15] While *Recreative Science* had attempted to include more [physical sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outline_of_physical_science) such as [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy) and [archaeology](18951655.md), the *Intellectual Observer* broadened itself further to include literature and art as well.[^cite_note-Bar6-15] Similar to *Recreative Science* was the scientific journal *Popular Science Review*, created in 1862,[^cite_note-Bar13-16] which covered different fields of science by creating subsections titled "Scientific Summary" or "Quarterly Retrospect", with book reviews and commentary on the latest scientific works and publications.[^cite_note-Bar13-16] Two other journals produced in England prior to the development of *Nature* were the *[Quarterly Journal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarterly_Journal_of_Science)* and *Scientific Opinion*, established in 1864 and 1868, respectively.[^cite_note-Bar6-15] The journal most closely related to *Nature* in its editorship and format was *[The Reader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Reader_\(weekly\))*, created in 1863; the publication mixed science with literature and art in an attempt to reach an audience outside of the scientific community, similar to *Popular Science Review*.[^cite_note-Bar6-15]

These similar journals all ultimately failed. The *Popular Science Review* survived longest, lasting 20 years and ending its publication in 1881; *Recreative Science* ceased publication as the *Student and Intellectual Observer* in 1871. The *Quarterly Journal*, after undergoing a number of editorial changes, ceased publication in 1885. *The Reader* terminated in 1867, and finally, *Scientific Opinion* lasted a mere 2 years, until June 1870.[^cite_note-Bar7-13]

### Creation



First title page, 4 November 1869

Not long after the conclusion of *The Reader*, a former editor, [Norman Lockyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Lockyer), decided to create a new scientific journal titled *Nature*,[^cite_note-B248-17] taking its name from a line by [William Wordsworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Wordsworth): "To the solid ground of nature trusts the Mind that builds for aye".[^cite_note-18] First owned and published by [Alexander Macmillan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Macmillan_\(publisher\)), *Nature* was similar to its predecessors in its attempt to "provide cultivated readers with an accessible forum for reading about advances in scientific knowledge."[^cite_note-B248-17] Janet Browne has proposed that "far more than any other science journal of the period, *Nature* was conceived, born, and raised to serve polemic purpose."[^cite_note-B248-17] Many of the early editions of *Nature* consisted of articles written by members of a group that called itself the [X Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X_Club), a group of scientists known for having liberal, progressive, and somewhat controversial scientific beliefs for their time.[^cite_note-B248-17] Initiated by [Thomas Henry Huxley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Henry_Huxley), the group consisted of such important scientists as [Joseph Dalton Hooker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Dalton_Hooker), [Herbert Spencer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Spencer), and [John Tyndall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Tyndall), along with another five scientists and mathematicians; these scientists were all avid supporters of [Darwin's theory of evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darwinism) as [common descent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_descent), a theory which, during the latter half of the 19th century, received a great deal of criticism among more conservative groups of scientists.[^cite_note-19] Perhaps it was in part its scientific liberality that made *Nature* a longer-lasting success than its predecessors. [John Maddox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maddox), editor of *Nature* from 1966 to 1973 and from 1980 to 1995, suggested at a celebratory dinner for the journal's centennial edition that perhaps it was the journalistic qualities of *Nature* that drew readers in; "journalism" Maddox states, "is a way of creating a sense of community among people who would otherwise be isolated from each other. This is what Lockyer's journal did from the start."[^cite_note-Dinner-20] In addition, Maddox mentions that the financial backing of the journal in its first years by the Macmillan family also allowed the journal to flourish and develop more freely than scientific journals before it.[^cite_note-Dinner-20]

#### Editors

[Norman Lockyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Lockyer), the founder of *Nature*, was a professor at [Imperial College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_College). He was succeeded as editor in 1919 by [Sir Richard Gregory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Richard_Gregory,_1st_Baronet).[^cite_note-NPG-21] Gregory helped to establish *Nature* in the international scientific community. His obituary by the Royal Society stated: "Gregory was always very interested in the international contacts of science, and in the columns of *Nature,* he always gave generous space to accounts of the activities of the International Scientific Unions."[^cite_note-22] During the years 1945 to 1973, editorship of *Nature* changed three times, first in 1945 to A. J. V. Gale and [L. J. F. Brimble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L._J._F._Brimble) (who in 1958 became the sole editor), then to [John Maddox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maddox) in 1965, and finally to [David Davies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_'Dai'_Davies?action=edit\&redlink=1) in 1973.[^cite_note-NPG-21] In 1980, Maddox returned as editor and retained his position until 1995. [Philip Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Campbell_\(scientist\)) became editor-in-chief of all *Nature* publications until 2018. [Magdalena Skipper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magdalena_Skipper) has since become editor-in-chief.[^cite_note-NPG-21]

#### Expansion and development

In 1970, *Nature* first opened its Washington office; other branches opened in New York in 1985, [Tokyo](30057.md) and [Munich](19058.md) in 1987, Paris in 1989, San Francisco in 2001, Boston in 2004, and [Hong Kong](13404.md) in 2005. In 1971, under [John Maddox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maddox)'s editorship, the journal split into *Nature Physical Sciences* (published on Mondays), *Nature New Biology* (published on Wednesdays), and *Nature* (published on Fridays). In 1974, Maddox was no longer editor, and the journals were merged into *Nature*.[^cite_note-23] Starting in the 1980s, the journal underwent a great deal of expansion, launching over ten new journals. These new journals comprise Nature Research, which was created in 1999 under the name Nature Publishing Group and includes *Nature*, [Nature Research Journals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Nature_Research_journals), Stockton Press Specialist Journals and Macmillan Reference (renamed NPG Reference). In 1996, *Nature* created its own website[^cite_note-24] and in 1999 Nature Publishing Group began its series of *Nature Reviews*.[^cite_note-NPG-21] Some articles and papers are available for free on the Nature website, while others require the purchase of premium access to the site. As of 2012, *Nature* claimed an online readership of about 3 million unique readers per month.[^cite_note-\:2-7]

On 30 October 2008, *Nature* endorsed an American presidential candidate for the first time when it supported [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama) during his campaign in [America's 2008 presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_United_States_presidential_election).[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26] In October 2012, an [Arabic edition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_Arabic_Edition) of the magazine was launched in partnership with [King Abdulaziz City for Science and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Abdulaziz_City_for_Science_and_Technology). As of the time it was released, it had about 10,000 subscribers.[^cite_note-NatureME-27] On 2 December 2014, *Nature* announced that it would allow its subscribers and a group of selected media outlets to share links allowing free, "read-only" access to content from its journals. These articles are presented using the [digital rights management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_rights_management) system [ReadCube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ReadCube) (which is funded by the Macmillan subsidiary Digital Science), and does not allow readers to download, copy, print, or otherwise distribute the content. While it does, to an extent, provide free online access to articles, it is not a true [open access](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_access) scheme due to its restrictions on re-use and distribution.[^cite_note-wired-naturefreeview-28][^cite_note-guardian-npgreadonly-29] On 15 January 2015, details of a proposed merger with Springer Science+Business Media were announced.[^cite_note-30]

In May 2015, it came under the umbrella of [Springer Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Springer_Nature), by the merger of [Springer Science+Business Media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Springer_Science+Business_Media) and [Holtzbrinck Publishing Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holtzbrinck_Publishing_Group)'s [Nature Publishing Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_Publishing_Group), [Palgrave Macmillan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palgrave_Macmillan), and [Macmillan Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macmillan_Education).[^cite_note-31] Since 2011, the journal has published [Nature's 10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature's_10) "people who mattered" during the year, as part of their annual review.[^cite_note-n10-32][^cite_note-n102011-33]

## Publication in *Nature*



Skewed curve of citations per article in 2015 to *Nature* articles from 2013 to 2014

According to *[Science](193513.md)*, another academic journal, being published in *Nature* has been known to carry a certain level of prestige in academia.[^cite_note-34] In particular, empirical papers are often highly cited, which can lead to promotions, grant funding, and attention from the mainstream media. Because of these [positive feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_feedback) effects, competition among scientists to publish in high-level journals like *Nature* and its closest competitor, *[Science](193513.md)*, can be very fierce. *Nature*'s [impact factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_factor), a measure of how many citations a journal generates in other works, was 42.778 in 2019 (as measured by [Thomson ISI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Scientific_Information)).[^cite_note-WoS-1][^cite_note-35][^cite_note-36] However, as with many journals, most papers receive far fewer citations than the impact factor would indicate.[^cite_note-37] *Nature*'s journal impact factor carries a long tail.[^cite_note-38]

Studies of methodological quality and reliability have found that some high-prestige journals, including *Nature*, "publish significantly substandard structures", and overall "reliability of published research works in several fields may be decreasing with increasing journal rank".[^cite_note-Brembs2018-39]

As with most other professional scientific journals, papers undergo an initial screening by the editor, followed by [peer review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peer_review). In this process, the editor selects several other scientists to read and critique articles, based on their expertise with the subject matter but who have no connection to the research under review. These critiques are then given to the original author, who makes revisions based on feedback. In the case of *Nature*, articles are generally sent for peer review if it is decided that they deal with a topical subject and are sufficiently ground-breaking in that particular field. As a consequence, the majority of submitted papers are rejected without peer review.

According to *Nature*'s original [mission statement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mission_statement):

> It is intended, FIRST, to place before the general public the grand results of Scientific Work and Scientific Discovery; and to urge the claims of Science to a more general recognition in Education and in Daily Life; and, SECONDLY, to aid Scientific men themselves, by giving early information of all advances made in any branch of Natural knowledge throughout the world, and by affording them an opportunity of discussing the various Scientific questions which arise from time to time.[^cite_note-nature1869-40]

This was later revised to:

> First, to serve scientists through prompt publication of significant advances in any branch of science, and to provide a forum for the reporting and discussion of news and issues concerning science. Second, to ensure that the results of science are rapidly disseminated to the public throughout the world, in a fashion that conveys their significance for knowledge, culture and daily life.[^cite_note-nature2000-41]

### Controversies

*Nature* has famously rejected papers that later became foundational to modern science. For example, [Enrico Fermi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrico_Fermi)'s breakthrough paper on the weak [interaction theory of beta decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi's_interaction) was rejected by Nature because it was considered too remote from reality.[^cite_note-42] (Fermi's paper was instead published by *[Zeitschrift für Physik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeitschrift_f%C3%BCr_Physik)* in 1934.[^cite_note-43]) In another example, *Nature* initially rejected [Paul Lauterbur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Lauterbur) and [Peter Mansfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Mansfield)'s research on [MRI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging), which would later win them the [Nobel Prize in Medicine](52502.md)[^cite_note-44]. Only after Lauterbur appealed the rejection did Nature publish their work.

&#x20;In a 2003 editorial, "Coping with Peer Rejection", *Nature* acknowledged several other rejection missteps:

> \[T]here are unarguable *faux pas* in our history. These include the rejection of [Cherenkov radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%8Cherenkov_radiation), [Hideki Yukawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hideki_Yukawa)'s [meson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meson), work on [photosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis) by [Johann Deisenhofer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Deisenhofer), [Robert Huber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Huber) and [Hartmut Michel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartmut_Michel), and the initial rejection (but eventual acceptance) of [Stephen Hawking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Hawking)'s [black-hole radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawking_radiation).[^cite_note-45]

The journal has also faced scrutiny for not following editorial procedure. For example, before publishing [Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_D._Watson) and [Crick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Crick)'s 1953 [paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_Structure_of_Nucleic_Acids:_A_Structure_for_Deoxyribose_Nucleic_Acid) on the [structure of DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structure_of_DNA), *Nature* did not send the paper to peer review. [John Maddox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maddox), *Nature*'s editor, stated: "The Watson and Crick paper was not peer-reviewed by *Nature* ... the paper could not have been refereed: its correctness is self-evident. No referee working in the field ... could have kept his mouth shut once he saw the structure."[^cite_note-46]

In June 1988, *Nature* published a controversial and seemingly anomalous paper detailing [Jacques Benveniste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Benveniste) and his team's work studying [water memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_memory).[^cite_note-47] The paper concluded that less than a single molecule of [antibody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibody) diluted in water could trigger an immune response in human [basophils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basophils), defying the physical [law of mass action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_mass_action). The paper gained substantial media attention in Paris, France, chiefly because their research sought funding from [homeopathic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeopathic) medicine companies. Public inquiry prompted *Nature* to mandate an extensive and stringent experimental replication in Benveniste's lab, through which his team's results were refuted.[^cite_note-48]

The journal has also been criticised for its social stances. In 2017, *Nature* published an editorial entitled "Removing Statues of Historical Figures Risks Whitewashing History: Science Must Acknowledge Mistakes as it Marks its Past". The article argued against removing monuments to scientists with controversial legacies. Specifically, the editorial cited examples of [J. Marion Sims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Marion_Sims), the 'Father of gynecology', who experimented on African American female slaves who were unable to give informed consent, and [Thomas Parran Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Parran_Jr.), who oversaw the [Tuskegee Syphilis Experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuskegee_Syphilis_Experiment). The editorial made the case that removing monuments could result in "whitewashing history" and stated, "Instead of removing painful reminders, perhaps these should be supplemented." The article drew condemnation, particularly on social media, and was quickly modified by *Nature*.[^cite_note-49] *Nature* acknowledged that the article, as originally written, was "offensive and poorly worded" and published selected letters of response.[^cite_note-50] The controversy was intensified as the editorial was published shortly after the violent [Unite the Right rally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unite_the_Right_Rally) in [Charlottesville, Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlottesville,_Virginia). In response, several scientists called for a boycott.[^cite_note-51] On 18 September 2017, the editorial was updated and edited by Philip Campbell, the editor of the journal.[^cite_note-52]

In April 2020, the journal apologized for its initial coverage of the [COVID-19 pandemic](62750956.md) in which it linked China and the city of Wuhan with the outbreak, which may have led to racist attacks.[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-54]

Before the election for the 46th President of the United States, Nature published an editorial entitled "Why Nature Supports Joe Biden for US President".[^cite_note-55] Political scientists Ffloyd Jiuyun Zhang found that this decreased trust in Nature and in the institution of science more broadly.[^cite_note-56] Philosopher of science Byron Hyde argues that repeated presidential endorsements since 2012 were a mistake and that any benefits are overshadowed by the loss of public trust.[^cite_note-57]

#### Retractions

From 2000 to 2001, a series of five fraudulent papers by [Jan Hendrik Schön](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sch%C3%B6n_scandal) were published in *Nature*. The papers, about [semiconductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductors), were revealed to contain falsified data and other scientific fraud. In 2003, *Nature* retracted the papers. The Schön scandal was not limited to *Nature*; other prominent journals, such as *[Science](193513.md)* and *[Physical Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_Review)*, also retracted papers by Schön.[^cite_note-58]

In 2024, a paper titled "Pluripotency of [Mesenchymal Stem Cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesenchymal_stem_cell) Derived from Adult Marrow", published in 2002, was [retracted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retraction_in_academic_publishing) due to concerns raised regarding some of the panels shown in a figure, making it the most-cited retracted paper ever.[^cite_note-59][^cite_note-60][^cite_note-61]

## Science fiction

In 1999, *Nature* began publishing [science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction) short stories. The brief "[vignettes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vignette_\(literature\))" are printed in a series called "Futures". The [stories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_story) appeared in 1999 and 2000, again in 2005 and 2006, and have appeared weekly since July 2007.[^cite_note-futures-62] Sister publication *[Nature Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_Physics)* also printed stories in 2007 and 2008.[^cite_note-FuturesPhysics-63] In 2005, *Nature* was awarded the [European Science Fiction Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Science_Fiction_Society)'s Best Publisher award for the "Futures" series.[^cite_note-ESFSAward-64] One hundred of the *Nature* stories between 1999 and 2006 were published as the collection *Futures from Nature* in 2008.[^cite_note-FuturesCollection-65] Another collection, *Futures from Nature 2*, was published in 2014.[^cite_note-66]

## Publication



*[Nature Materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_Materials),* a specialized journal from [Nature Portfolio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_Portfolio), 2018

*Nature* is edited and published in the United Kingdom by a division of the international [scientific publishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_publishing) company [Springer Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Springer_Nature) that publishes academic journals, magazines, online databases, and services in science and medicine. *Nature* has offices in London, New York City, San Francisco, Washington, D.C., [Boston](24437894.md), Tokyo, Hong Kong, Paris, [Munich](19058.md), and [Basingstoke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basingstoke). [Nature Portfolio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_Portfolio) also publishes other specialized journals, including *[Nature Neuroscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_Neuroscience)*, *[Nature Biotechnology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_Biotechnology),* *[Nature Methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_Methods)*, the *[Nature Clinical Practice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_Clinical_Practice)* series of journals, *[Nature Structural & Molecular Biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_Structural_&_Molecular_Biology)*, *[Nature Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_Chemistry)*, and the *Nature Reviews* series of journals.[^cite_note-67]

Since 2005, each issue of *Nature* has been accompanied by a *[Nature Podcast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_Podcast)*[^cite_note-68] featuring highlights from the issue and interviews with the articles' authors and the journalists covering the research. It is presented by Kerri Smith and features interviews with scientists on the latest research, as well as news reports from *Nature*'s editors and journalists. The Nature Podcast was founded – and the first 100 episodes were produced and presented – by clinician and virologist [Chris Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chris_Smith,_The_Naked_Scientist) of Cambridge and *[The Naked Scientists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Naked_Scientists)*.[^cite_note-69]

Nature Portfolio actively supports the self-archiving process and, in 2002, was one of the first publishers to allow authors to post their contributions on their personal websites by requesting an exclusive licence to publish, rather than requiring authors to transfer copyright. In December 2007, Nature Publishing Group introduced the Creative Commons attribution-non-commercial-share alike unported licence for those articles in Nature journals that are publishing the primary sequence of an organism's genome for the first time.[^cite_note-70]

In 2008, a collection of articles from *Nature* was edited by John S. Partington under the title *H. G. Wells in Nature, 1893–1946: A Reception Reader* and published by [Peter Lang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Lang_\(publisher\)).[^cite_note-71]
