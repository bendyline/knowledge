***One China*** is a phrase with variant meanings, adopted by many states and other actors to describe their stance on the relationship between the [People's Republic of China](5405.md) (PRC) based on [mainland China](55079.md), and the [Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_China) (ROC) based on the [Taiwan Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan_Area). "One China" asserts that there is only one *[de jure](43594.md)* Chinese nation, despite the *[de facto](43594.md)* division between the [two rival governments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Chinas) in the aftermath of the [Chinese Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Civil_War). The term may refer, in alphabetical order, to one of the following:

- The ***One China policy*** refers to a [United States](3434750.md) policy of [strategic ambiguity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Policy_of_deliberate_ambiguity) regarding Taiwan.

  [^cite_note-2]

  &#x20;It reaffirms the U.S. interest in a peaceful settlement of the [Taiwan question](66555.md).

  [^cite_note-Wilson_Center_Digital_Archive-3]

  &#x20;The United States has formal relations with the PRC, recognizes the PRC as the sole legal government of China, and simultaneously maintains [its unofficial relations with Taiwan](15996902.md) while taking no official position on Taiwanese sovereignty.

After the [Chinese Communist Party](7175.md) (CCP) defeated the [Kuomintang](16903.md) (KMT) in the [Chinese Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Civil_War) and the subsequent [retreat of the ROC to Taiwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retreat_of_the_government_of_the_Republic_of_China_to_Taiwan), the CCP [established the PRC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proclamation_of_the_People's_Republic_of_China) in mainland China while the ROC ruled over Taiwan and several outlying islands. During this time, both governments continued to claim [legitimacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legitimacy_\(political\)) as the government of all of China. The KMT legally designated the [Chinese Communist Party](7175.md) as a "rebellious group".[^cite_note-25] Initially, international recognition of the two was split, but most countries [began to recognize the PRC over the ROC in the 70s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_Taiwan#Relations_switched_from_the_ROC_to_the_PRC), including the United States [in 1979](15996902.md#Post-democratization). The language in the United States' One China policy first arose in its joint [1972 Communiqué](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Communiqu%C3%A9s) with the PRC.

Under ROC President [Lee Teng-hui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_Teng-hui) in the 1990s, the [Additional Articles of the Constitution of the Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Additional_Articles_of_the_Constitution_of_the_Republic_of_China) were passed which effectively transformed Taiwan from a [one-party state](102459.md) into a democracy, and limited civil and political rights to citizens in the "[free area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_area_of_the_Republic_of_China)" (the area under its *de facto* control, consisting of the island groups of Taiwan, Penghu, Kinmen, Matsu and some minor islands), but did not alter language regarding territorial claims or national territory.[^cite_note-lee1999-26]

Although the 1991 constitutional amendments localized governance to the "free area," the 1947 Constitution still claims all of China as ROC territory, aligning with the Kuomintang's (KMT) original "One China" stance.[^cite_note-27] The Constitution defines ROC territory as encompassing all of China, based on its "existing national boundaries" at the time of adoption (Article 4), asserting the ROC as the legitimate government of the entirety of China, not solely Taiwan. This constitutional stance supports the KMT's historical vision, creating a legal-political disconnect under Democratic Progressive Party (DPP) governance. Despite the DPP's political dominance, it has not legally amended the Constitution to formally redefine Taiwan as a separate nation or change the national boundaries to exclude the mainland, not least because this could provoke PRC retaliation or even invasion.[^cite_note-28][^cite_note-29][^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31] This deadlock reflects a split along party lines: [Pan-Blue coalition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-Blue_coalition) parties (including the [Kuomintang](16903.md)) adhere to "One China with respective interpretations", while [Pan-Green coalition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-Green_coalition) parties (including the [Democratic Progressive Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party)) reject it. Meanwhile, the PRC has maintained its One China principle.[^cite_note-TDSGL-32]

Territory controlled by the [People's Republic of China](5405.md) (purple) and the [Republic of China](25734.md) (orange). The size of minor islands has been exaggerated in this map for ease of identification.

The [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Empire) established a colony on Taiwan in 1624 based in present-day [Tainan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tainan). Shortly after, the [Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Empire) established a colony in Northern Taiwan in 1626, but were driven out by the Dutch in 1642. It was during this time that large-scale Chinese migration from nearby [Fujian Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fujian_Province) began.[^cite_note-33][^cite_note-34] The Dutch colony was later conquered by [Zheng Chenggong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zheng_Chenggong) (Koxinga), a [Ming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ming_dynasty)-loyalist, in 1662 as the [Kingdom of Tungning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Tungning), before being incorporated by the [Qing dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qing_dynasty) in 1683 as part of Fujian Province. In 1887, it was officially made a separate [Fujian-Taiwan Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fujian-Taiwan_Province). Taiwan remained a province for eight years until it was ceded to Japan under the [Treaty of Shimonoseki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Shimonoseki) in 1895 following the [First Sino-Japanese War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Sino-Japanese_War).

While Taiwan remained under Japanese control, the Qing dynasty was [ousted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xinhai_Revolution) and the [First](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beiyang_government) and [Second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalist_government) [Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_China_\(1912%E2%80%931949\)) (ROC) were established from the Beiyang regime to the [Kuomintang](16903.md) (KMT) from 1928. After the conclusion of [World War II](32927.md) in 1945, the Republic of China was given control of Taiwan.[^cite_note-35][^cite_note-36][^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40] In 1949, after losing control of most of mainland China following the [Chinese Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Civil_War), and before the post-war peace treaties had come into effect, the ROC government under the KMT [withdrew to Taiwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Nationalist_Party_retreat_to_Taiwan), and [Chiang Kai-shek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiang_Kai-shek) declared [martial law](50477.md).

An argument has been made that Japan formally renounced all territorial rights to Taiwan in 1952 in the [San Francisco Peace Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_Peace_Treaty), but neither in that treaty nor in the peace treaty signed between Japan and China was the territorial sovereignty of Taiwan awarded to the Republic of China.[^cite_note-\:0-41][^cite_note-\:1-42] The treaties left the status of Taiwan—as ruled by the ROC or PRC—[deliberately vague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deliberate_ambiguity), and the question of [legitimate sovereignty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legitimacy_\(political\)) over China is why China was not included in the San Francisco Peace Treaty.[^cite_note-\:0-41][^cite_note-\:1-42] This argument is not accepted by those who view the sovereignty of Taiwan as having been legitimately [returned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrocession_Day) to the Republic of China at the end of the war.[^cite_note-43] Some argue that the ROC is a government in exile,[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46][^cite_note-47] while others maintain it is a [rump state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rump_state).[^cite_note-48]

The ROC continued to claim itself as the rightful ruler of the entirety of China under the single-party KMT regime, and the PRC made a symmetric claim. In 1971, the [United Nations General Assembly Resolution 2758](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_General_Assembly_Resolution_2758) replaced the ROC's seat in the [United Nations](31769.md) with the PRC. From 30 April 1991, the ROC officially recognized the PRC, thus abandoning the [Hallstein Doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallstein_Doctrine), while maintaining the claim of an [exclusive mandate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exclusive_mandate) as the legitimate ruler of China.[^cite_note-49] The ROC transformed into a free and democratic state in the 1990s following decades of martial law with the passage of the [Additional Articles of the Constitution of the Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Additional_Articles_of_the_Constitution_of_the_Republic_of_China).[^cite_note-50] Afterwards, the legal and [political status of Taiwan](66555.md) has become more contentious, with increasing public expressions in favor of [Taiwan independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan_independence), which were formerly outlawed.

## Viewpoints within Taiwan

Within Taiwan, there is a distinction between the positions of the Kuomintang (KMT) and the [Democratic Progressive Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party) (DPP).

The Kuomintang holds the "One-China principle" and maintains its claim that under the ROC Constitution (passed by the Kuomintang government in 1947 in [Nanjing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanjing)) the ROC has sovereignty over most of China, including, by their interpretation, both mainland China and Taiwan. After the [Chinese Communist Party](7175.md) expelled the ROC in the Chinese Civil War from most of Chinese territory in 1949 and founded the PRC, the ROC's [Chinese Nationalist](16903.md) government, which still held Taiwan, continued to claim [legitimacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legitimacy_\(political\)) as the government of all of China. Under former President [Lee Teng-hui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_Teng-hui), additional articles were appended to the [ROC constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_the_Republic_of_China) in 1991 so that it applied effectively only to the [Taiwan Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan_Area).[^cite_note-lee1999-26] The Kuomintang proclaims a modified form of the "One-China" principle known as the "[1992 Consensus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1992_Consensus)". Under this "consensus", both governments "agree" that there is only one single sovereign state encompassing both mainland China and Taiwan, but disagree about which of the two governments is the [legitimate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legitimacy_\(political\)) government of this state. Former ROC President [Ma Ying-jeou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ma_Ying-jeou) had re-asserted claims on mainland China as late as 8 October 2008.[^cite_note-51]

The [Democratic Progressive Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party) rejects the One China principle, and its official position currently is that Taiwan is an independent and sovereign country whose territory consists of Taiwan and its surrounding smaller islands and whose sovereignty derives only from the ROC citizens living in Taiwan (similar to the philosophy of [self-determination](29269.md)), based on the 1999 "[Resolution on Taiwan's Future](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resolution_on_Taiwan's_Future)". It considers Taiwan as an independent nation under the name of Republic of China, making a formal declaration of independence unnecessary.[^cite_note-resolution-52] Though calls for drafting a new constitution and a declaration of a Republic of Taiwan was written into the party charter in 1991,[^cite_note-1991article-53] the 1999 resolution has practically superseded the earlier charter.[^cite_note-92c-54]

At least one observer of the [Taiwan independence movement](31156.md) believes it runs counter to the PRC's sovereignty claims over Taiwan.[^cite_note-55] A [Brookings Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brookings_Institution) survey indicates that while Taiwan people overwhelmingly reject unification with the PRC, the vast majority do not support immediate formal independence of a Republic of Taiwan.[^cite_note-56]

## Evolution of the One China principle



[Flag of the Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_the_Republic_of_China) (right) and [People's Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_China) flying together in [Chinatown, San Francisco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinatown,_San_Francisco), revealing different political views from [overseas Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overseas_Chinese)

One interpretation, which was adopted during the [Cold War](325329.md), is that either the PRC or the ROC is the sole rightful government of all [China](5405.md) and that the other government is illegitimate. While much of the [western bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_bloc) maintained relations with the ROC until the 1970s under this policy, much of the [eastern bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_bloc) maintained relations with the PRC. While the government of the ROC considered itself the remaining holdout of the legitimate government of a country overrun by what it thought of as "[Communist bandits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_bandit)", the PRC claimed to have succeeded the ROC in the [Chinese Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Civil_War). Though the ROC no longer portrays itself as the sole legitimate government of China, the position of the PRC remained unchanged until the early 2000s, when the PRC began to soften its position on this issue to promote [Chinese unification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_unification).

One interpretation of one China is that only one geographical region of China exists, which was split between two Chinese governments during the Chinese Civil War. This is largely the position of current supporters of Chinese unification in mainland China, who believe that "one China" should eventually unite under a single government.[^cite_note-\:2-57] Starting in 2005, this position has become close enough to the position of the PRC, allowing high-level dialogue between the CCP and the [Pan-Blue Coalition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-Blue_Coalition) of the ROC.

The revised position of the PRC was made clear in the [Anti-Secession Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Secession_Law) of 2005, which although stating that there is one China whose [sovereignty](28791.md) is indivisible, does not explicitly identify this China with the PRC. Almost all PRC laws have a suffix "of the People's Republic of China" (prefix in Chinese grammar) in their official names, but the Anti-Secession Law is an exception. Beijing has made no major statements after 2004 which identify one China with the PRC and has shifted its definition of one China slightly to encompass a concept called the '[1992 Consensus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1992_Consensus)': both sides of the Taiwan strait recognize there is only one China—both mainland China and Taiwan belong to the same China but agree to differ on the definition of which China.[^cite_note-\:2-57] According to Taiwanese lawyer Chen Chang-wen, the new version changed from a subordinate relationship to an equal relationship, and the legislation only legalized its constitutional obligations.[^cite_note-58] Placing less emphasis on which government should represent One China is more compatible with KMT's position as well as the current ROC Constitution. This reformulation was also reflected in the 2022 PRC white paper on Taiwan.[^cite_note-59]

### Policy position in the PRC

See also: [Taiwan Province, People's Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan_Province,_People's_Republic_of_China)

In practice, official sources and state-owned media never refer to the "ROC government", and seldom to the "government of Taiwan". Instead, the government in Taiwan is referred to as the "Taiwan authorities". The PRC does not accept or stamp [Republic of China passports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_China_passport). Instead, a Taiwan resident visiting [Mainland China](55079.md) must use a [Taiwan Compatriot Entry Permit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan_Compatriot_Entry_Permit). [Hong Kong](13404.md) grants visa-free entry to holders of a Permit, while holders of a ROC passport must apply for a Pre-arrival Registration. [Macau](19068.md) grants visa-free entry to holders of both the permit and the passport.

The [United Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Front_\(China\)), which consists of the eight other political parties in the PRC subordinate to the CCP, has adhered to the One-China policy and opposes Taiwan independence. Among the parties that accepted it are the [Revolutionary Committee of the Chinese Kuomintang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutionary_Committee_of_the_Chinese_Kuomintang) (a splinter left-wing party that broke away from the main Kuomintang) and the [Taiwan Democratic Self-Government League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan_Democratic_Self-Government_League).[^cite_note-TDSGL-32]

In 1950, Premier [Zhou Enlai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhou_Enlai) stated that the principle that Taiwan is part of China is "not only a historical fact but affirmed by the [Cairo Declaration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1943_Cairo_Declaration), the [Potsdam Declaration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potsdam_Declaration), and the conditions after Japan's surrender."[^cite_note-60]

In its foreign relations, the PRC does not object to other countries having Taiwan trade offices, so long as those countries are not formally engaged in diplomatic activity.[^cite_note-\:32-61]<sup>: 34 </sup>

### Policy position in the ROC

See also: [Taiwan (Republic of China province)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan_\(Republic_of_China_province\)) and [Free area of the Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_area_of_the_Republic_of_China)

Chiang Kai-shek held the view that there was One China that should be united under the government of the Republic of China; his adversary Mao praised him for rejecting the idea of 'two Chinas,' stating that Chiang "dared to defy the U.S. policy for 'two Chinas' in front of \[John Foster] [Dulles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Foster_Dulles), proving that he is still a great nationalist."[^cite_note-\:92-62]<sup>: 43 </sup>

On 1 August 1992, the ROC's [National Unification Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Unification_Council) passed the "Definition of One China Resolution", stating: "The two sides of the Taiwan Strait uphold the One China principle, but the interpretations of the two sides are different ... Our side believes that one China should mean the Republic of China, established in 1912 and existing today, and its sovereignty extends throughout China, but its current governing authority is only over Taiwan, Penghu, Kinmen, and Matzu. Admittedly, Taiwan is part of China, but the mainland is also a part of China."[^cite_note-\:Chen-63]<sup>: 229 </sup> This resolution provided the basis for quasi-governmental talks between the ROC's [Strait Exchange Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straits_Exchange_Foundation) (SEF) and the PRC's [Association for Relations Across the Taiwan Straits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_for_Relations_Across_the_Taiwan_Straits) (ARATS).[^cite_note-\:Chen-63]<sup>: 229 </sup>

During these discussions, SEF stated that "each side expresses its own interpretation verbally in order to solve this sticky problem of \[one China] and thereby reaffirmed the August 1st NUC resolution as SEF's interpretation of one China."[^cite_note-\:Chen-63]<sup>: 229 </sup> ARATS agreed to expressing these interpretations verbally and stated that "both sides of the strait uphold the principle of one China, and actively seek national unification, but the political interpretation of the one China will not be referred to in the cross-strait negotiations on functional issues."[^cite_note-\:Chen-63]<sup>: 229–230 </sup> This position later became known as the [1992 Consensus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1992_Consensus), a phrase coined in early 2000 by [Su Chi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Su_Chi).[^cite_note-\:Chen-63]<sup>: 230 </sup>

There is significant difference on Taiwan regarding recognition and understanding of the One-China principle. The Pan-Blue Coalition parties, led by the Kuomintang, generally accept the One-China principle. In particular, former [president](61155.md) [Ma Ying-jeou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ma_Ying-jeou) has stated that "One China is the Republic of China".[^cite_note-64] The [Pan-Green Coalition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-Green_Coalition) parties, led by the Democratic Progressive Party (DPP), do not accept the policy and view Taiwan as a country separate from China. Former DPP president [Chen Shui-bian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chen_Shui-bian) believes the 1992 Consensus forsakes Taiwan's national sovereignty, effectively ceding it to the PRC.[^cite_note-65] President [Tsai Ing-wen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsai_Ing-wen) rejected the 1992 Consensus categorically in 2019.[^cite_note-Horton-66]

When the ROC established diplomatic relations with [Kiribati](16674.md) in 2003, it did not require that Kiribati sever relations with the PRC.[^cite_note-67] However, the PRC did not accept dual recognition and severed ties with Kiribati as a result.[^cite_note-68] In 2024, the ROC Ministry of Foreign Affairs announced that it had no preconditions for maintaining diplomatic relations with other countries, in particular that it was not opposed to simultaneous recognition of the ROC and PRC.[^cite_note-69]

The ROC does not recognize or stamp PRC passports. Instead, mainland Chinese residents visiting Taiwan and other territories under ROC jurisdiction must use an [Exit and Entry Permit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exit_&_Entry_Permit_\(Republic_of_China\)) issued by the ROC authorities.

Not formally recognizing the ROC is a requirement for any political entity to establish diplomatic relations with the People's Republic of China, in effect forcing other governments to choose between Beijing and Taipei.[^cite_note-70][^cite_note-71] At times, the PRC has used financial incentives to entice smaller countries to recognize it over the ROC, and both the ROC and PRC have accused each other of dollar diplomacy.[^cite_note-72] Most countries that recognize Beijing circumvent the diplomatic language by establishing [trade and cultural missions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_diplomatic_missions_in_Taiwan) that represent their interests on Taiwanese soil, while the ROC government represents its interests abroad with [reciprocal missions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_diplomatic_missions_of_Taiwan).

The PRC has, in the past, attempted to get nations to recognize that "the Government of the People's Republic of China is the sole legal government of China ... and Taiwan is an inalienable part of the territory of the People's Republic of China." However, many nations are unwilling to make this particular statement and there was often a protracted effort to find language acceptable to both sides, for example that they "respect", "acknowledge", "understand", or "take note of" the PRC's One China principle (but do not say they "recognize" it). This [strategic ambiguity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_ambiguity) in the language used provides the basis for countries to have formal ties with People's Republic of China and maintain unofficial ties to the Republic of China.

Names such as "[Chinese Taipei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Taipei)" (e.g. in the Olympics) or "Separate Customs Territory of Taiwan, Penghu, Kinmen, and Matsu" (e.g. in the [World Trade Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Trade_Organization)) are sometimes used in some international arenas since "Taiwan" suggests that Taiwan is a separate country and "Republic of China" suggests that there are [two Chinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Chinas), and thus both violate the One-China principle.

### United States policy

See also: [China–United States relations](277880.md) and [Taiwan–United States relations](15996902.md)



[Mao Zedong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mao_Zedong) greets U.S. President [Richard Nixon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Nixon) during his [visit to China in 1972](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1972_Nixon_visit_to_China).

The United States' One-China policy was first stated in the [Shanghai Communiqué](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanghai_Communiqu%C3%A9) of 1972: "the United States acknowledges that Chinese on either side of the Taiwan Strait maintain there is but one China and that Taiwan is a part of China.[^cite_note-Wilson_Center_Digital_Archive-3] The United States does not challenge that position." The United States has not expressed an explicitly immutable statement regarding whether it believes Taiwan is independent or not. Instead, Washington simply states that they understand the PRC's claims on Taiwan as its own. Nancy Bernkopf Tucker asserts that U.S. One-China policy was not intended to please the PRC government, but as a way for Washington to conduct international relations in the region, which Beijing fails to state.[^cite_note-73] A more recent study suggests that this wording reflected the Nixon administration's desire to shift responsibility for resolving the dispute to the "people most directly involved" – that is, China and Taiwan. At the same time, the United States would avoid "prejudic\[ing] the ultimate outcome" by refusing to explicitly support the claims of one side or the other.[^cite_note-74]

At the height of the [Sino-Soviet split](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Soviet_split) and [Sino-Vietnamese conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Vietnamese_War), and at the start of the [reform and opening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reform_and_opening) of the PRC, the United States strategically switched diplomatic recognition from the Republic of China (ROC) to the People's Republic of China (PRC) on 1 January 1979 under the administration of [Jimmy Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Carter). Congress quickly responded by passing the [Taiwan Relations Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan_Relations_Act) that defined relations with the ROC, but stopped short of full recognition. It also required the United States to provide Taiwan with arms sufficient to maintain its self-defense, but did not commit to defending Taiwan in the event of an invasion.

In 1982, President [Ronald Reagan](25433.md) also saw that the [Six Assurances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six_Assurances) were adopted, the fifth being that the United States would not formally recognize Chinese sovereignty over Taiwan. Still, United States policy has remained ambiguous. In the [House International Relations Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._House_Committee_on_International_Relations) on 21 April 2004, the Assistant Secretary of State for East Asian and Pacific Affairs, [James A. Kelly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_A._Kelly), was asked by Rep. [Grace Napolitano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grace_Napolitano) (D-CA) whether the United States government's commitment to Taiwan's democracy conflicted with the so-called One-China policy.[^cite_note-75] He stated "In my testimony, I made the point "our One China," and I didn't really define it, and I'm not sure I very easily could define it. I can tell you what it is not. It is not the One-China policy or the One-China principle that Beijing suggests, and it may not be the definition that some would have in Taiwan. But it does convey a meaning of solidarity of a kind among the people on both sides of the straits that has been our policy for a very long time."[^cite_note-76]

When President [Bill Clinton](3356.md) visited Shanghai during his June 1998 visit to China, Clinton articulated the "three nos" for United States foreign policy towards China: (1) not recognizing [two Chinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Chinas), (2) not supporting Taiwanese independence, and (3) not supporting Taiwanese efforts to join international organizations for which sovereignty is a membership requirement.[^cite_note-\:9-77]



U.S. President [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama) and Chinese leader [Xi Jinping](2017814.md). Obama supported the "One-China" policy during his administration.

[^cite_note-78]

The position of the United States, as clarified in the *China/Taiwan: Evolution of the "One China" Policy* report of the Congressional Research Service (date: 9 July 2007) is summed up in five points:

1. The United States did not explicitly state the sovereign status of Taiwan in the three US-PRC Joint Communiqués of 1972, 1979, and 1982.

2. The United States "acknowledged" the "One China" position of both sides of the Taiwan Strait.

3. U.S. policy has not recognized the PRC's sovereignty over Taiwan;

4. U.S. policy has not recognized Taiwan as a sovereign country; and

5. U.S. policy has considered Taiwan's status as unsettled.

These positions remained unchanged in a 2013 report of the Congressional Research Service.[^cite_note-79]

On 2 December 2016, US President-elect Donald Trump and ROC President Tsai Ing-wen conducted a short phone call regarding "the close economic, political and security ties between Taiwan and the US".[^cite_note-80] On 6 December, a few days after the call, Trump said that the US is not necessarily bound by its "one China" policy.[^cite_note-81][^cite_note-82][^cite_note-83] On 9 February 2017, in a lengthy phone call, US President [Donald Trump](4848272.md) and PRC [Paramount leader](262766.md) Xi Jinping discussed numerous topics and President Trump agreed, at the request of Xi Jinping, to honor the "one China" policy.[^cite_note-84]

On 23 May 2022, U.S. President Joe Biden announced the United States would intervene militarily if China were to unilaterally invade Taiwan. Speaking in Japan, President Biden stated, "That’s the commitment we made," an apparent reference to the Taiwan Relations Act, which ensures military support for Taiwan, although the Act does not specifically guarantee direct military action by the United States in Taiwan. President Biden emphasized that Russia's military invasion of Ukraine created an "even stronger" burden to protect Taiwan.[^cite_note-Boak_2022-85] China criticized Biden's statement as part of a "hypocritical and futile" pattern of encouragement to "'Taiwan independence' forces."[^cite_note-86] Biden later stated that his remarks did not represent a change from the status quo and the U.S. position of [strategic ambiguity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Policy_of_deliberate_ambiguity).[^cite_note-87] Secretary of State [Antony Blinken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antony_Blinken) also delivered a speech in which he stated that U.S. policy regarding the island had not changed, and the State Department updated its fact sheet to reinstate a line stating "we do not support Taiwan independence."[^cite_note-88][^cite_note-89]

In May 2026, US President [Donald Trump](4848272.md) announced he would have a discussion with Chinese leader [Xi Jinping](2017814.md) on the matter of arms sales to Taiwan during his [visit to China](83136377.md), potentially breaking with the Six Assurances.[^cite_note-90] After the visit concluded, Trump confirmed that the matter of arms sales have been brought up, and said "He talked about that to me, obviously. So what am I going to do, say ‘I don't want to talk to you about it because I have an agreement that was signed in 1982?’"[^cite_note-91] In another interview, Trump said he was holding the arm sales to Taiwan "in abeyance and it depends on China" and said the arm sales are a "very good negotiating chip" with China.[^cite_note-92]

### Japanese position

See also: [China–Japan relations](13106128.md) and [Japan–Taiwan relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan%E2%80%93Taiwan_relations)

Further information: [2025–2026 China–Japan diplomatic crisis](81642186.md)

The 1972 [Japan-China Joint Communiqué](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan%E2%80%93China_Joint_Communiqu%C3%A9) entered into as the basis of diplomatic normalization in [Sino-Japanese relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Japanese_Relations) states that the Government of Japan fully understands and respects the stance of the Government of the People's Republic of China that "Taiwan is an inalienable part of the territory of the People's Republic of China." and that it firmly maintains its stand under Article 8 of the [Potsdam Declaration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potsdam_Declaration).[^cite_note-93]

Japan has recognized the People's Republic of China "as the sole legal Government of China" since 1975 but has maintained an ambiguous stance regarding the PRC's sovereignty claim over the island of Taiwan.[^cite_note-94]

### Russian position

See also: [China–Russia relations](30964882.md) and [Russia–Taiwan relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia%E2%80%93Taiwan_relations)

In 1949, the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) recognized the People's Republic of China as the only lawful government of China. The ROC on Taiwan had cancelled the [Sino-Soviet Treaty of Friendship and Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Soviet_Treaty_of_Friendship_and_Alliance) treaty in response. The Soviet Union voted to admit the PRC into the UN in 1971.

As with the past leaderships, the Russian government has accepted its support for the One-China policy that Taiwan is "an inalienable part of China, and \[the Russian side] opposes any forms of independence", as stated in Article 5 of the [2001 Sino-Russian Treaty of Friendship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001_Sino-Russian_Treaty_of_Friendship). This was reaffirmed in January 2022, when Russian and PRC leaders [Vladimir Putin](32817.md) and [Xi Jinping](2017814.md) discussed the situation in the [prelude to the Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prelude_to_the_Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine), and again in July 2022, by Russian foreign minister [Sergei Lavrov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergei_Lavrov), as [United States Speaker of the House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speaker_of_the_United_States_House_of_Representatives) [Nancy Pelosi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nancy_Pelosi) visited Taiwan.[^cite_note-95][^cite_note-96][^cite_note-97]

### Philippine policy

See also: [China–Philippines relations](33734272.md) and [Philippines–Taiwan relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippines%E2%80%93Taiwan_relations)

Similar to other countries, the Philippines maintain a One-China policy.[^cite_note-PH-bane-98] However, despite officially recognizing the People's Republic of China as the sole legitimate government of China since 1975, the country also maintains economic and cultural relations with the Republic of China (or Taiwan).[^cite_note-PH-bane-98][^cite_note-99] [Lito Banayo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lito_Banayo), chair of the Manila Economic and Cultural Office in Taiwan remarked that the country's One-China policy only proscribes the Philippines to enter into political and military agreements with the ROC.[^cite_note-PH-bane-98] High-ranking Filipino officials have suggested the reviewal of the country's position on the One China policy after repeated interference by the People's Republic of China (PRC) against the foreign affairs and territorial integrity of the Philippines.[^cite_note-100]

### African countries

The One China principle is an important factor in [China-Africa relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-African_relations).[^cite_note-\:32-61]<sup>: 34 </sup> In 1971, 26 African countries supported the United Nations General Assembly vote through which the PRC became the sole representative of China.[^cite_note-\:32-61]<sup>: 34 </sup> The sole African country which does not recognize the PRC (and consequently which the PRC maintains no relations with) is [Eswatini](27451.md).[^cite_note-\:32-61]<sup>: 22 </sup> All African countries except Eswatini recognize the People's Republic of China as the sole government of China and Taiwan as an integral part of Chinese territory, and support all efforts by the PRC to "achieve national reunification".[^cite_note-101]

## Cross-strait relations

Main article: [Cross-strait relations](2596399.md)

|  | This section needs to be **updated**. Please help update this article to reflect recent events or newly available information. *(September 2023)* |
| --- | --- |



A propaganda sign on [Dadan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dadan_Island) (ROC) facing [Xiamen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xiamen) (PRC) proclaiming "[Three Principles of the People](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Principles_of_the_People) unites China"



A propaganda sign on [Mawei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mawei) (PRC) facing [Matsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matsu_Islands) (ROC) proclaiming "Peaceful reunification, [One country, two systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_country,_two_systems)."

The People's Republic of China demands Taiwan acknowledge the One-China principle as a prerequisite to resume any cross-strait dialogue.[^cite_note-102] The PRC offers the chance for open talks and "unobstructed exchanges" with Taiwan as long as it moves to accept the 1992 Consensus.[^cite_note-\:0-41] The PRC's One-China policy rejects formulas which call for "[two Chinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Chinas)" or "[one China, one Taiwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_China,_one_Taiwan)"[^cite_note-103] and has stated that efforts to divide the sovereignty of China could be met with military force.[^cite_note-104]

The PRC has explicitly stated that it is flexible about the meaning "one China", and that "one China" may not necessarily be synonymous with the PRC, and has offered to talk with parties on Taiwan and the government on Taiwan on the basis of the [Consensus of 1992](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consensus_of_1992) which states that there is one China, but that there are different interpretations of that one China. For example, in Premier [Zhu Rongji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhu_Rongji)'s statements prior to the [2000 Presidential Election in Taiwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2000_Taiwan_presidential_election), he stated that as long as any ruling power in Taiwan accepts the One-China principle, they can negotiate and discuss anything freely.

However, the One-China principle would apparently require that Taiwan formally give up any possibility of [Taiwanese independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan_independence), and would preclude any "one nation, two states" formula similar to ones used in German *[Ostpolitik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ostpolitik)* or in [Korean reunification](61104.md). [Chen Shui-bian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chen_Shui-bian), president of the Republic of China between 2000 and 2008 repeatedly rejected the demands to accept the One-China principle and instead called for talks to discuss One China itself. With the [January](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Republic_of_China_legislative_election) and [March](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Republic_of_China_presidential_election) 2008 elections in Taiwan, and the election of [Ma Ying-jeou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ma_Ying-jeou) as the President of the ROC, who was inaugurated on 20 May, a new era of better relations between both sides of the Taiwan Strait was established.[^cite_note-105] KMT officials visited mainland China, and the Chinese [ARATS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_for_Relations_Across_the_Taiwan_Straits) met in [Beijing](18603746.md) with its Taiwanese counterpart, the [Straits Exchange Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straits_Exchange_Foundation). [Direct charter flights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-Strait_charter) were therefore established.

One China was the formulation held by the ROC government before the 1990s, but it was asserted that the one China was the Republic of China rather than PRC. However, in 1991, President [Lee Teng-hui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_Teng-hui) indicated that he would not challenge the Communist authorities to rule mainland China. This is a significant point in the history of [Cross-Strait relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-Strait_relations) in that a president of the ROC no longer claims administrative authority over mainland China. Henceforth, the [Taiwan independence movement](31156.md) gained a political boost, and under Lee's administration the issue is no longer who rules mainland China, but who claims legitimacy over Taiwan and the surrounding islands. Over the course of the 1990s, President Lee appeared to drift away from the One-China formulation, leading many to believe that he was actually sympathetic to Taiwan independence. In 1999, Lee proposed a [special state-to-state relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_state-to-state_relations) for mainland China–Taiwan relations which was received angrily by [Beijing](18603746.md), which ended semi-official dialogue until June 2008, when ARATS and SEF met, and in which President [Ma Ying-jeou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ma_Ying-jeou) reiterated the 1992 Consensus and the different interpretation on "One China".

After the election of [Chen Shui-bian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chen_Shui-bian) in 2000, the policy of the ROC government was to propose negotiations without preconditions. While Chen did not explicitly reject Lee's two states theory, he did not explicitly endorse it either. Throughout 2001, there were unsuccessful attempts to find an acceptable formula for both sides, such as agreeing to "abide by the 1992 consensus". Chen, after assuming the Democratic Progressive Party chairmanship in July 2002, moved to a somewhat less ambiguous policy, and stated in early August 2002 that "it is clear that both sides of the straits are separate countries". This statement was strongly criticized by opposition [Pan-Blue Coalition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-Blue_Coalition) parties on Taiwan, which support a One-China principle, but oppose defining this "One China" as the PRC.

The One-China policy became an issue during the [2004 ROC Presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_ROC_Presidential_election). Chen Shui-bian abandoned his earlier ambiguity and publicly rejected the One-China principle claiming it would imply that Taiwan is part of the PRC. His opponent [Lien Chan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lien_Chan) publicly supported a policy of "one China, different interpretations", as done in 1992. At the end of the 2004 election, Lien Chan and his running mate, [James Soong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Soong), later announced that they would not put ultimate unification as the goal for their cross-strait policy and would not exclude the possibility of an independent Taiwan in the future. In an interview with Time Asia bureau prior to the 2004 presidential elections, Chen used the model of Germany and the European Union as examples of how countries may come together, and the Soviet Union as illustrating how a country may fragment.

In March 2005, the PRC passed an [Anti-Secession Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Secession_Law) which authorized the use of force to prevent a "serious incident" that breaks the One-China policy, but which at the same time did not identify one China with the People's Republic and offered to pursue political solutions. At the same session of the PRC Congress, a large increase in military spending was also passed, leading blue team members to interpret those measures as forcing the ROC to adhere to the One-China policy or else the PRC would attack.

In April and May 2005, Lien Chan and [James Soong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Soong) made separate trips to mainland China,[^cite_note-106] during which both explicitly supported the [Consensus of 1992](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consensus_of_1992) and the concept of one China and in which both explicitly stated their parties' opposition to [Taiwan independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan_independence). Although President Chen at one point supported the trips of Lien and Soong for defusing cross-strait tensions,[^cite_note-107] he also attacked them for working with the "enemy" PRC. On 28 April 2008, Honorary Chairman [Lien Chan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lien_Chan) of the then opposition [Kuomintang](16903.md) visited Beijing and met with [Hu Jintao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hu_Jintao) for the fourth time since their historic encounter on 29 April 2005 in their respective capacity as party leaders of both the Chinese Communist Party and the KMT. Lien also met [Chen Yunlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chen_Yunlin), director of the PRC's [Taiwan Affairs Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan_Affairs_Office) of the State Council.[^cite_note-108]

On 28 May 2008, Kuomintang Chairman [Wu Po-hsiung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wu_Po-hsiung) made a landmark visit to [Beijing](18603746.md),[^cite_note-109] and met and shook hands with the Communist General Secretary [Hu Jintao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hu_Jintao), at the [Great Hall of the People](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Hall_of_the_People). He also visited the mausoleum of [Sun Yat-sen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Yat-sen). Hu Jintao called for resuming exchanges and talks, based on the [1992 Consensus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1992_Consensus).
