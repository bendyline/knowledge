**Primate** ([/ˈpraɪmət/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English); [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language): *Primas*) is a title or rank bestowed on some important archbishops in certain Christian churches. Depending on the particular tradition, it can denote either jurisdictional authority ([title of authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title_of_authority)) or (usually) ceremonial precedence ([title of honour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title_of_honour)).

## Catholic Church

See also: [Catholic Church hierarchy § Primates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_hierarchy#Primates), and [Bishop (Catholic Church) § Primate](8799424.md#Primate)

In the [Latin Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Church), a primate is an [archbishop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop)—or, rarely, a [suffragan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suffragan) or [exempt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exemption_\(church\)) [bishop](8799424.md)—of a specific (mostly metropolitan) [episcopal see](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episcopal_see) (called a *primatial see*) who has precedence over the bishoprics of one or more [ecclesiastical provinces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesiastical_province) of a particular historical, political or cultural area. Historically, primates of particular sees were granted privileges including the authority to call and preside at national [synods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synod), jurisdiction to hear appeals from [metropolitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_bishop) tribunals, the right to crown the sovereign of the nation, and presiding at the [investiture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Investiture) (installation) of archbishops in their sees.[^cite_note-ce-1]



Catholic Primate (non-cardinal) coat of arms

The office is generally found only in older Catholic countries, and is now purely honorific, enjoying no effective powers under [canon law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_law_\(Catholic_Church\))—except for the [archbishop of Esztergom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop_of_Esztergom) (Gran) in Hungary.[^cite_note-ce-1] Thus, e.g., the [primate of Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primate_of_Poland) holds no jurisdictional authority over other Polish bishops or their dioceses, but is *durante munere* a member of the standing committee of the episcopal conference, and has honorary precedence among Polish bishops (e.g., in liturgical ceremonies). The Holy See has also granted Polish primates the privilege of wearing cardinal's crimson attire, except for the skullcap and [biretta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biretta), even if they have not been made [cardinals](6221.md).[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3]

Where the title of primate exists, it may be vested in one of the oldest archdioceses in a country, often based in a city other than the present capital, but which was the capital when the country was first Christianized. The city may no longer have the prominence it had when the title was granted. The political area over which primacy was originally granted may no longer exist: for example, the [archbishop of Toledo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop_of_Toledo) was designated "Primate of the [Visigothic Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visigothic_Kingdom)", and the [archbishop of Lyon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop_of_Lyon) is the "[primate of the Gauls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primate_of_the_Gauls)".[^cite_note-ce-1] The title of primate can, therefore, also be disputed between different archdioceses who, at some point, held proeminence over a shifting territory; such is the dispute over the [Primacy of the Spains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primacy_of_the_Spains) that was fought over by the Archdioceses of [Braga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Braga), Toledo and [Santiago de Compostela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Santiago_de_Compostela). After the founding of [Portugal](23033.md), the archbishop of [Braga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braga) held precedence over all other archbishops in the country, though his role declined under the rise of the [Archdiocese of Lisbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarchate_of_Lisbon), which culminated in 1716, when Archbishop [Tomás de Almeida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom%C3%A1s_de_Almeida) (1670–1754) was elevated to [patriarch](175409.md).

Some of the leadership functions once exercised by primates, specifically presiding at meetings of the bishops of a nation or region, are now exercised by the president of the [conference of bishops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conference_of_bishops): "The president of the Conference or, when he is lawfully impeded, the vice-president, presides not only over the general meetings of the Conference but also over the permanent committee."[^cite_note-4] The president is generally elected by the conference, but by exception the president of the [Italian Episcopal Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Episcopal_Conference) is appointed by the pope, and the [Irish Catholic Bishops' Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Catholic_Bishops'_Conference) has the [primate of All Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primate_of_All_Ireland) as president and the [primate of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primate_of_Ireland) as vice-president. Other former functions of primates, such as hearing appeals from [metropolitan tribunals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesiastical_court), were reserved to the [Holy See](13393.md) by the early 20th century.[^cite_note-ce-1] Soon after, by the norm of the Code of Canon Law of 1917, confirmed in the 1983 Code, the tribunal of second instance for appeals from a metropolitan tribunal is "the tribunal which the metropolitan has designated in a stable manner with the approval of the Apostolic See".[^cite_note-5]

The closest equivalent position in the Eastern Churches in 1911 was an [Exarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exarch#Ecclesiastical_exarchs).[^cite_note-ce-1]

The Holy See has continued in modern times to grant the title of primate. With the [papal decree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papal_decree) *Sollicitae Romanis Pontificibus* of 24 January 1956 it granted the title of Primate of Canada to the Archbishop of [Quebec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Quebec).[^cite_note-6] As stated above, this is merely an honorary title involving no additional power.[^cite_note-7]<sup>: 131 </sup>

A right of precedence over other bishops and similar privileges can be granted even to a bishop who is not a primate. Thus, in 1858, the Holy See granted the [archbishop of Baltimore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop_of_Baltimore) precedence in meetings of the United States bishops.[^cite_note-8] The [archbishop of Westminster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Westminster) has not been granted the title of primate of England and Wales, which is sometimes applied to him, but his position has been described as that of "chief metropolitan" and as "similar to" that of the archbishop of Canterbury.[^cite_note-9]

The title of primate is sometimes applied loosely to the archbishop of a country's capital, as in the case of the archbishops of [Seoul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Seoul) in South Korea and of [Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_St_Andrews_and_Edinburgh) in Scotland.

The pre-reformation metropolitan [Archbishop of Nidaros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archdiocese_of_Nidaros) was sometimes referred to as Primate of Norway,[^cite_note-10] even though it is unlikely that this title ever was officially granted to him by the Holy See.

### Regular clergy equivalent

In the modern confederation of the [Benedictine Order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedictine_Order), all the Black Monks of St. Benedict were united under the presidency of an **Abbot Primate** (Leo XIII, *Summum semper*, 12 July 1893); but the unification, fraternal in its nature, brought no modification to the abbatial dignity, and the various congregations preserved their autonomy intact. The loose structure of the Benedictine Confederation is claimed to have made [Pope Leo XIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Leo_XIII) exclaim that the Benedictines were *ordo sine ordine* ("an order without order"). The powers of the [Abbot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbot) Primate are specified, and his position defined, in a decree of the [Sacred Congregation of Bishops and Regulars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacred_Congregation_of_Bishops_and_Regulars) dated 16 September 1893. The primacy is attached to the global [Benedictine Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedictine_Confederation) whose Primate resides at [Sant'Anselmo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sant'Anselmo) in [Rome](25458.md). He takes precedence of all other abbots, is empowered to pronounce on all doubtful matters of discipline, to settle difficulties arising between monasteries, to hold a canonical visitation, if necessary, in any congregation of the order, and to exercise a general supervision for the regular observance of monastic discipline. The Primatial powers are only vested in the Abbot Primate to act by virtue of the proper law of its autonomous Benedictine congregation, which at the present is minimal to none. However, certain branches of the Benedictine Order seem to have lost their original autonomy to some extent.

In a similar way the Confederation of [Canons Regular of St. Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canons_Regular_of_St._Augustine), elects an Abbot Primate as figurehead of the Confederation and indeed the whole Canonical Order. The Abbots and Superiors General of the nine congregations of confederated congregations of [Canons Regular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canons_Regular) elect a new Abbot Primate for a term of office lasting six years. The Current Abbot Primate is Rt Rev. Fr Jean-Michel Girard, CRB, Abbot General of the Canons Regular of the Grand St Bernard.

## Anglicanism

Anglican usage styles the bishop who heads an independent church as its "primate", though commonly they hold some other title (e.g. archbishop, presiding bishop, or moderator). The primates' authority within their churches varies considerably: some churches give the primate some executive authority, while in others they may do no more than preside over church councils and represent the church ceremonially.

### Anglican Communion

Main article: [Primates in the Anglican Communion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primates_in_the_Anglican_Communion)

In the context of the [Anglican Communion Primates' Meeting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Communion_Primates'_Meeting), the chief [bishop](4092.md) of each of the thirty-nine churches (also known as provinces) that compose the [Anglican Communion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Communion) acts as its primate, though this title may not necessarily be used within their own provinces. Thus the [United Churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Church) of Bangladesh, of North India, of Pakistan and of South India, which are united with other originally non-Anglican churches, are represented at the meetings by their moderators.[^cite_note-AC-41]

In both the [Church of England](5955.md) and the [Church of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Ireland), two bishops have the title of primate: the archbishops of [Canterbury](2345.md) and [York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop_of_York) in England and of [Armagh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop_of_Armagh_\(Church_of_Ireland\)) and [Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop_of_Dublin_\(Church_of_Ireland\)) in Ireland. Only the bishop of the senior primatial see of each of these two churches participates in the meetings.

The archbishop of Canterbury, who is considered *[primus inter pares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primus_inter_pares)* of all the participants, convokes the meetings and issues the invitations.[^cite_note-AC-41]

Primates and archbishops are styled "The Most Reverend". All other bishops are styled "The Right Reverend",[^cite_note-AC-41] with the exception of the [Bishop of Meath and Kildare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_Meath_and_Kildare) in the Church of Ireland.

## Eastern Orthodox equivalent

Historically, the primatial title in [Western Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Christianity) corresponded to the title and office of supra-metropolitan [exarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesiastical_exarch) in [Eastern Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Christianity). Such exarchs, or primates, were archbishops of [Ephesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephesus) (for the [Diocese of Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_Asia)), [Heraclea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraclea_Lyncestis) (for the [Diocese of Thrace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_Thrace)) and [Caesarea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caesarea_in_Cappadocia) (for the [Diocese of Pontus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_Pontus)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMeyendorff198956,_58-42]
