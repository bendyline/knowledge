The **Baltic Sea** is an arm of the [Atlantic Ocean](698.md) that is enclosed by the countries of [Denmark](76972.md), [Estonia](28222445.md), [Finland](10577.md), [Germany](11867.md), [Latvia](17514.md), [Lithuania](17675.md), [Poland](22936.md), [Russia](25391.md), and [Sweden](5058739.md). Geophysically, it is surrounded by the [Scandinavian Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scandinavian_Peninsula) and the [North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_European_Plain) and Central [European Plain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Plain) regions.[^cite_note-\:3-3] It is the world's largest [brackish water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brackish_water) basin.

The sea stretches from 53°N to 66°N latitude and from 10°E to 30°E longitude. It is a [shelf sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_shelf#Shelf_seas) and [marginal sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marginal_sea) of the Atlantic with limited water exchange between the two, making it an [inland sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inland_sea). The Baltic Sea drains through the [Danish Straits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_Straits) into the [Kattegat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kattegat) by way of the [Øresund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%98resund), [Great Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Belt) and [Little Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Belt). It includes the [Gulf of Bothnia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Bothnia) (divided into the [Bothnian Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bothnian_Bay) and the [Bothnian Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bothnian_Sea)), the [Gulf of Finland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Finland), the [Gulf of Riga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Riga) and the [Bay of Gdańsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bay_of_Gda%C5%84sk).

The "Baltic Proper" is bordered on its northern edge, at latitude 60°N, by [Åland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%85land) and the Gulf of Bothnia, on its northeastern edge by the Gulf of Finland, on its eastern edge by the Gulf of Riga, and in the west by the Swedish part of the southern Scandinavian Peninsula.

The Baltic Sea is connected by [artificial waterways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canal) to the [White Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Sea) via the [White Sea–Baltic Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Sea%E2%80%93Baltic_Canal) and to the [German Bight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Bight) of the [North Sea](21179.md) via the [Kiel Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiel_Canal).

### Administration

The [Helsinki Convention on the Protection of the Marine Environment of the Baltic Sea Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helsinki_Convention_on_the_Protection_of_the_Marine_Environment_of_the_Baltic_Sea_Area) includes the Baltic Sea and the [Kattegat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kattegat), without calling Kattegat a part of the Baltic Sea, "For the purposes of this Convention the 'Baltic Sea Area' shall be the Baltic Sea and the Entrance to the Baltic Sea, bounded by the parallel of the Skaw in the Skagerrak at 57°44.43'N."[^cite_note-4]

### Traffic history

Historically, the [Kingdom of Denmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Denmark) collected [Sound Dues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_Dues) from ships at the border between the ocean and the land-locked Baltic Sea, in tandem: in the [Øresund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%98resund) at [Kronborg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kronborg) castle near [Helsingør](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helsing%C3%B8r); in the [Great Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Belt) at [Nyborg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nyborg); and in the [Little Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Belt) at its narrowest part then [Fredericia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fredericia), after that stronghold was built. The narrowest part of Little Belt is the "Middelfart Sund" near [Middelfart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middelfart).[^cite_note-5]

### Oceanography

Geographers widely agree that the preferred physical border between the Baltic and North Seas is the Langelandsbælt (the southern part of the [Great Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Belt) strait near [Langeland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langeland)) and the [Drogden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drogden)-Sill strait.[^cite_note-6] The Drogden Sill is situated north of [Køge Bugt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B8ge_Bugt) and connects [Dragør](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drag%C3%B8r) in the south of [Copenhagen](5166.md) to [Malmö](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malm%C3%B6); it is used by the [Øresund Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%98resund_Bridge), including the *Drogden Tunnel*. By this definition, the [Danish Straits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_Straits) is part of the entrance, but the [Bay of Mecklenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bay_of_Mecklenburg) and the [Bay of Kiel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bay_of_Kiel) are parts of the Baltic Sea. Another usual border is the line between [Falsterbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falsterbo), Sweden, and [Stevns Klint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stevns_Klint), Denmark, as this is the southern border of Øresund. It is also the border between the shallow southern Øresund (with a typical depth of 5–10 meters only) and notably deeper water.

### Hydrography and biology

[Drogden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drogden) Sill (depth of 7 m (23 ft)) sets a limit to Øresund and [Darss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darss) Sill (depth of 18 m (59 ft)), and a limit to the Belt Sea.[^cite_note-7] The shallow [sills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sill_\(geology\)) are obstacles to the flow of heavy salt water from the Kattegat into the basins around [Bornholm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bornholm) and [Gotland](284507.md).

The Kattegat and the southwestern Baltic Sea are well oxygenated and have a rich biology. The remainder of the Sea is brackish, poor in oxygen, and in species. Thus, statistically, the more of the entrance that is included in its definition, the healthier the Baltic appears; conversely, the more narrowly it is defined, the more endangered its biology appears.

## Etymology and nomenclature

The precise origin of the name is unknown. [Tacitus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacitus) called it the Suebic Sea, Latin: *Mare Suebicum* after the [Germanic people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_people) of the [Suebi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suebi),[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9] and [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy) *Sarmatian Ocean* after the [Sarmatians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarmatians),[^cite_note-10] but the first to name it the *Baltic Sea* ([Medieval Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Latin_language): *[Mare Balticum](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Mare%20Balticum)*) was the eleventh-century German chronicler [Adam of Bremen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_of_Bremen). It might be connected to the Germanic word *belt*, a name used for two of the Danish Straits, [the Belts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Belts), while others claim it to be directly derived from the source of the Germanic word, [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) *balteus* "belt".[^cite_note-Latin-etymology-11] Adam of Bremen himself compared the sea with a belt, stating that it is so named because it stretches through the land as a belt (*Balticus, eo quod in modum baltei longo tractu per [Scithicas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scythians) regiones tendatur usque in [Greciam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece)*).

He might also have been influenced by the name of a legendary island mentioned in the [*Natural History*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_History_\(Pliny\)) of [Pliny the Elder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliny_the_Elder). Pliny mentions an island named *[Baltia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltia)* (or *Balcia*) with reference to accounts of [Pytheas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pytheas) and [Xenophon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenophon). It is possible that Pliny refers to an island named Basilia ("the royal") in *On the Ocean* by Pytheas. *Baltia* also might be derived from "belt", and therein mean "near belt of sea, strait".

Others have suggested that the name of the island originates from the [Proto-Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-European_language) root \**bʰel* meaning "white, fair",[^cite_note-12] which may echo the naming of seas after colours relating to the cardinal points (as per [Black Sea](3386.md) and [Red Sea](26191.md)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchmitt1989310–313-13] This \**bʰel* root and basic meaning were retained in [Lithuanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuanian_language) (as *baltas*), [Latvian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latvian_language) (as *balts*) and [Slavic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavic_languages) (as *bely*). On this basis, a related hypothesis holds that the name originated from this Indo-European root via a [Baltic language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltic_language) such as Lithuanian.[^cite_note-14] Another explanation is that, while derived from the aforementioned root, the name of the sea is related to names for various forms of water and related substances in several European languages, that might have been originally associated with colors found in swamps (compare Proto-Slavic *[\*bolto](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Reconstruction\:Proto-Slavic/bolto#Proto-Slavic)* "swamp"). Yet another explanation is that the name originally meant "enclosed sea, bay" as opposed to open sea.[^cite_note-baltu_valodas-15]

In the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) the sea was known by a variety of names. The name Baltic Sea became dominant after 1600. Usage of *Baltic* and similar terms to denote the region east of the sea started only in the 19th century.

## History

### Classical world

At the time of the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire), the Baltic Sea was known as the *Mare Suebicum* or *Mare Sarmaticum*. [Tacitus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaius_Cornelius_Tacitus) in his AD 98 *Agricola* and *Germania* described the Mare Suebicum, named for the [Suebi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suebi) tribe, during the spring months, as a [brackish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brackish) sea where the ice broke apart and chunks floated about. The Suebi eventually migrated southwest to temporarily reside in the Rhineland area of modern Germany, where their name survives in the historic region known as [Swabia](616237.md). [Jordanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordanes) called it the *Germanic Sea* in his work, the *[Getica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getica)*.[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-22]

### Middle Ages



[Cape Arkona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Arkona) on the island of [Rügen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%BCgen) in Germany

In the early [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), Norse (Scandinavian) merchants built a trade empire all around the Baltic. Later, the Norse fought for control of the Baltic against [Wendish tribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wends) dwelling on the southern shore. The Norse also used the rivers of [Russia](25391.md) for trade routes, finding their way eventually to the [Black Sea](3386.md) and southern Russia. This Norse-dominated period is referred to as the [Viking Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viking_Age).[^cite_note-23]

Since the [Viking Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viking_Age), the Scandinavians have referred to the Baltic Sea as *Austmarr* ("Eastern Sea"). "Eastern Sea", appears in the *[Heimskringla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heimskringla)* and *Eystra salt* appears in *[Sörla þáttr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%B6rla_%C3%BE%C3%A1ttr)*. [Saxo Grammaticus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saxo_Grammaticus) recorded in *[Gesta Danorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gesta_Danorum)* an older name, *[Gandvik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gandvik)*, *-vik* being [Old Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse) for "bay", which implies that the Vikings correctly regarded it as an inlet of the sea. Another form of the name, "Grandvik", attested in at least one English translation of *Gesta Danorum*, is likely to be a misspelling.

In addition to fish the sea also provides [amber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amber), especially from its southern shores within today's borders of [Poland](22936.md), [Russia](25391.md) and [Lithuania](17675.md). First mentions of amber deposits on the South Coast of the Baltic Sea date back to the 12th century.[^cite_note-24] The bordering countries have also traditionally exported lumber, [wood tar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pine_tar), [flax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flax), [hemp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemp) and furs by ship across the Baltic. Sweden had from early medieval times exported [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron) and [silver](27119.md) mined there, while Poland had and still has extensive [salt](1605200.md) mines. Thus, the Baltic Sea has long been crossed by much merchant shipping.[^cite_note-25]

The lands on the Baltic's eastern shore were among the last in Europe to be converted to [Christianity](5211.md). This finally happened during the [Northern Crusades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Crusades): [Finland](10577.md) in the twelfth century by Swedes, and what are now [Estonia](28222445.md) and [Latvia](17514.md) in the early thirteenth century by Danes and Germans ([Livonian Brothers of the Sword](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Livonian_Brothers_of_the_Sword)). The [Teutonic Order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teutonic_Order) gained control over parts of the southern and eastern shore of the Baltic Sea, where they set up [their monastic state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monastic_state_of_the_Teutonic_Knights). [Lithuania](17675.md) was [the last European state to convert to Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianization_of_Lithuania).[^cite_note-26]

### An arena of conflict



Main trading routes of the [Hanseatic League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanseatic_League) (*Hanse*)

In the period between the 8th and 14th centuries, there was much piracy in the Baltic from the coasts of [Pomerania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pomerania) and [Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussia), and the [Victual Brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victual_Brothers) held [Gotland](284507.md).

Starting in the 11th century, the southern and eastern shores of the Baltic were settled by migrants mainly from [Germany](11867.md), a movement called the *[Ostsiedlung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ostsiedlung)* ("east settling"). Other settlers were from the [Netherlands](21148.md), [Denmark](76972.md), and [Scotland](26994.md). The [Polabian Slavs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polabian_Slavs) were gradually assimilated by the Germans.[^cite_note-27] Denmark gradually gained control over a large portion of the Baltic coast, until it lost many of its possessions after being defeated in the 1227 [Battle of Bornhöved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Bornh%C3%B6ved_\(1227\)).



The naval [Battle of the Sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Sound) took place on 8 November 1658 during the [Dano-Swedish War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dano-Swedish_War_\(1658%E2%80%9360\)).



Nautical chart of the Baltic Sea in 1919



The burning *[Cap Arcona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cap_Arcona)* shortly after the attacks, 3 May 1945. Only 350 survived of the 4,500 prisoners who had been aboard.

Between the 13th and 17th centuries the Baltic Sea was, depending on the period, dominated by the Netherlands, Germany, Sweden and Russia.[^cite_note-28] In the 13th to 16th centuries, the strongest economic force in Northern Europe was the [Hanseatic League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanseatic_League), a federation of merchant cities around the Baltic Sea and the [North Sea](21179.md). In the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Lithuanian_Commonwealth), [Denmark](76972.md), and [Sweden](5058739.md) fought wars for *[Dominium maris baltici](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominium_maris_baltici)* ("Lordship over the Baltic Sea"). Eventually, it was Sweden that [virtually encompassed the Baltic Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Empire). In Sweden, the sea was then referred to as *Mare Nostrum Balticum* ("Our Baltic Sea"). The goal of Swedish warfare during the 17th century was to make the Baltic Sea an all-Swedish sea (*Ett Svenskt innanhav*), something that was accomplished except the part between Riga in Latvia and [Stettin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Szczecin) in Pomerania. However, the [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Republic) dominated the Baltic trade in the seventeenth century.

In the eighteenth century, [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire) and [Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussia) became the leading powers over the sea. Sweden's defeat in the [Great Northern War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Northern_War) brought Russia to the eastern coast. Russia became and remained a dominating power in the Baltic. Russia's [Peter the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_I_of_Russia) saw the strategic importance of the Baltic and decided to found his new capital, [Saint Petersburg](24320051.md), at the mouth of the [Neva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neva) river at the east end of the [Gulf of Finland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Finland). There was much trading not just within the Baltic region but also with the North Sea region, especially eastern [England](9316.md) and the [Netherlands](21148.md): their fleets needed the Baltic timber, tar, flax, and hemp.

During the [Crimean War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_War), a joint British and French fleet attacked the Russian fortresses in the Baltic; the case is also known as the [Åland War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%85land_War). They bombarded [Sveaborg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sveaborg), which guards [Helsinki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helsinki); and [Kronstadt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kronstadt,_Russia), which guards Saint Petersburg; and they destroyed [Bomarsund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bomarsund,_%C3%85land) in [Åland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%85land). After the unification of [Germany](11867.md) in 1871, the whole southern coast became German. [World War I](4764461.md) was partly fought in the Baltic Sea. After 1920 [Poland](22936.md) was granted access to the Baltic Sea at the expense of Germany by the [Polish Corridor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_Corridor) and enlarged the port of [Gdynia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gdynia) in rivalry with the port of the [Free City of Danzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_City_of_Danzig).[^cite_note-29]

After the Nazis' rise to power, Germany reclaimed the [Memelland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memelland) and after the outbreak of the [Eastern Front (World War II)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Front_\(World_War_II\)) occupied the Baltic states. In 1945, the Baltic Sea became a mass grave for retreating soldiers and refugees on torpedoed [troop transports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Hannibal). The sinking of the *[Wilhelm Gustloff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Gustloff_\(ship\))* remains the worst maritime disaster in history, killing (very roughly) 9,000 people. In 2005, a Russian group of scientists found over five thousand airplane wrecks, sunken warships, and other material, mainly from World War II, on the bottom of the sea.

### Since World War II

#### Ammunition dumping

Since the end of [World War II](32927.md), various nations, including the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union), the United Kingdom and the United States have disposed of [chemical weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_warfare) in the Baltic Sea, raising concerns of environmental contamination.[^cite_note-30] Today, fishermen occasionally find some of these materials: the most recent available report from the Helsinki Commission notes that four small scale catches of chemical munitions representing approximately 105 kg (231 lb) of material were reported in 2005. This is a reduction from the 25 incidents representing 1,110 kg (2,450 lb) of material in 2003.[^cite_note-31] Until now, the [U.S. Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Government) refuses to disclose the exact coordinates of the wreck sites. Deteriorating bottles leak [mustard gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mustard_gas) and other substances, thus slowly poisoning a substantial part of the Baltic Sea.

In addition to chemicals, tons of German ammunition were dumped into the Baltic after the war at the behest of the Allies, who wanted to ensure that they would not start another war. By 2025, these were leaking contaminants into the water and the German government was piloting solutions.[^cite_note-32]

#### Territorial changes

After 1945, the [German population was expelled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_and_expulsion_of_Germans_\(1944%E2%80%931950\)) from all areas east of the [Oder-Neisse line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oder-Neisse_line), [making room](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_cleansing) for new Polish and Russian settlement. Poland [gained most of the southern shore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Former_eastern_territories_of_Germany). The Soviet Union gained another access to the Baltic with the [Kaliningrad Oblast](17311.md), that had been part of German-settled [East Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Prussia). The Baltic states on the eastern shore were annexed by the Soviet Union. The Baltic then separated opposing military blocs: [NATO](21133.md) and the [Warsaw Pact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warsaw_Pact). During the [Cold War](325329.md) the Baltic Sea remained on the periphery of the main axis of confrontation between these two blocs.[^cite_note-33] Neutral Sweden developed [incident weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incident_weapon) to defend its [territorial waters](496983.md) after the [Swedish submarine incidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_submarine_incidents).[^cite_note-34] This border status restricted trade and travel. It ended only after the collapse of the [Communist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist) regimes in Central and Eastern Europe in the late 1980s.



In 1981, the [Soviet submarine *S-363*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_submarine_S-363) ran on ground deep inside Swedish territorial waters, an incident which illustrated the impact of the [Cold War](325329.md) on the Baltic Sea.

Finland and Sweden joined NATO in 2023 and 2024, respectively, making the Baltic Sea almost entirely surrounded by the alliance's members, leading some commentators to label the sea a "NATO lake".[^cite_note-Kirby_2023-35][^cite_note-RFI_2024_i241-36][^cite_note-France_24_2024_r479-37][^cite_note-Financial_Times_n260-38][^cite_note-Kayali_2023_y072-39] However, the legal status of the sea has not changed and it is still open to all nations.[^cite_note-40] Such an arrangement has also existed for the [European Union](9317.md) (EU) since May 2004 following the accession of the Baltic states and Poland. The remaining non-NATO and non-EU shore areas are Russian: the Saint Petersburg area and the [Kaliningrad Oblast](17311.md) [exclave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exclave).

#### Infrastructure

The Baltic Sea today is of significant economic and security importance due to its dense network of [submarine cables](79454.md), [energy pipelines](40024054.md), ports and offshore energy platforms. In recent years, there have been a number of incidents of sabotage in the Baltic Sea, resulting in damage to [critical infrastructures](521286.md).[^cite_note-41][^cite_note-42][^cite_note-\:7-43] The most notable incidents include the [Nord Stream pipelines sabotage](71856702.md) in 2022, where a series of underwater explosions destroyed both [Nord Stream 1 and 2](71965880.md).[^cite_note-44] In 2023, there was another incident involving the [Balticconnector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balticconnector) gas pipeline and a nearby data cable, which were damaged by the [Hong Kong](13404.md)-flagged container ship [NewNew Polar Bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newnew_Polar_Bear).[^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46]

Other significant incidents include the recent damage to several [undersea communication cables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_communications_cable). The most recent incident of relevance was the [rupture of the Estlink 2 cable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Estlink_2_incident) in late 2024. It is suspected that the oil tanker [Eagle S](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eagle_S), believed to be part of a [Russian shadow fleet](78631435.md), is responsible.[^cite_note-47]

These events followed a series of responses from both [NATO](21133.md) and the [EU](9317.md). In response, NATO Baltic Sea states have increased their [naval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navy) presence in the Baltic Sea, and the NATO operation [Baltic Sentry](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/NATO-Operation%20Baltic%20Sentry) was established. Simultaneously, the EU has implemented a series of measures designed to enhance the protection of critical maritime infrastructure. The EU has also underscored the commitment to strengthening cooperation with NATO.[^cite_note-48]

### Storms and storm floods

Winter storms begin arriving in the region during October. These have caused numerous [shipwrecks](272778.md), and contributed to the extreme difficulties of rescuing passengers of the ferry [MS *Estonia*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MS_Estonia) en route from [Tallinn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tallinn), Estonia, to [Stockholm](26741.md), Sweden, in September 1994, which claimed the lives of 852 people. Older, wood-based shipwrecks such as the *[Vasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vasa_\(ship\))* tend to remain well-preserved, as the Baltic's cold and brackish water does not suit the [shipworm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shipworm).

[Storm surge](449744.md) floods are generally taken to occur when the water level is more than one metre above normal. In Warnemünde about 110 floods occurred from 1950 to 2000, an average of just over two per year.[^cite_note-49]

Historic flood events were the [All Saints' Flood of 1304](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_Saints'_Flood_of_1304) and other floods in the years 1320, 1449, 1625, 1694, 1784 and 1825. Little is known of their extent.[^cite_note-50] From 1872, there exist regular and reliable records of water levels in the Baltic Sea. The highest was the [flood of 1872](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1872_Baltic_Sea_flood) when the water was an average of 2.43 m (8 ft 0 in) above sea level at Warnemünde and a maximum of 2.83 m (9 ft 3 in) above sea level in Warnemünde. In the last very heavy floods the average water levels reached 1.88 m (6 ft 2 in) above sea level in 1904, 1.89 m (6 ft 2 in) in 1913, 1.73 m (5 ft 8 in) in January 1954, 1.68 m (5 ft 6 in) on 2–4 November 1995 and 1.65 m (5 ft 5 in) on 21 February 2002.[^cite_note-51]

## Geography

### Geophysical data



Baltic drainage basins (catchment area), with depth, elevation, major rivers and lakes



[Curonian Lagoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curonian_Lagoon), [Spit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curonian_Spit) and [Klaipėda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klaip%C4%97da)

An arm of the [North Atlantic Ocean](698.md), the Baltic Sea is enclosed by [Sweden](5058739.md) and [Denmark](76972.md) to the west, [Finland](10577.md) to the northeast, and the [Baltic countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltic_states) to the southeast.

It is about 1,600 km (990 mi) long, an average of 193 km (120 mi) wide, and an average of 55 metres (180 ft) deep. The maximum depth is 459 m (1,506 ft) which is on the Swedish side of the centre. The surface area is about 349,644 km<sup>2</sup> (134,998 sq mi) [^cite_note-52] and the volume is about 20,000 km<sup>3</sup> (4,800 cu mi). The periphery amounts to about 8,000 km (5,000 mi) of coastline.[^cite_note-53]

The Baltic Sea is one of the largest [brackish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brackish_water) inland seas by area, and occupies a basin (a *[Zungenbecken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zungenbecken)*) formed by glacial erosion during the last few [ice ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_age).

The northern part of the Baltic Sea is known as the [Gulf of Bothnia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Bothnia), of which the northernmost part is the Bay of Bothnia  or [Bothnian Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bothnian_Bay). The more rounded southern basin of the gulf is called [Bothnian Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bothnian_Sea) and immediately to the south of it lies the [Sea of Åland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_of_%C3%85land). The [Gulf of Finland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Finland) connects the Baltic Sea with [Saint Petersburg](24320051.md). The [Gulf of Riga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Riga) lies between the [Latvian](17514.md) capital city of [Riga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riga) and the [Estonian](28222445.md) island of [Saaremaa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saaremaa).

The Northern Baltic Sea lies between the [Stockholm](26741.md) area, southwestern Finland, and Estonia. The [Western and Eastern Gotland basins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gotland_Basin) form the major parts of the Central Baltic Sea or Baltic proper. The [Bornholm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bornholm) Basin is the area east of Bornholm, and the shallower [Arkona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Arkona) Basin extends from Bornholm to the Danish isles of [Falster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falster) and [Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zealand_\(Denmark\)).

In the south, the [Bay of Gdańsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bay_of_Gda%C5%84sk) lies east of the [Hel Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hel_Peninsula) on the Polish coast and west of the [Sambia Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sambia_Peninsula) in [Kaliningrad Oblast](17311.md). The [Bay of Pomerania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bay_of_Pomerania) lies north of the islands of [Usedom/Uznam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usedom) and [Wolin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolin), east of [Rügen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%BCgen). Between Falster and the German coast lie the [Bay of Mecklenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bay_of_Mecklenburg) and [Bay of Lübeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bay_of_L%C3%BCbeck). The westernmost part of the Baltic Sea is the [Bay of Kiel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bay_of_Kiel). The three [Danish Straits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_Straits), the [Great Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Belt), the [Little Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Belt) and [The Sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%96resund) (*Öresund*/*Øresund*), connect the Baltic Sea with the [Kattegat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kattegat) and [Skagerrak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skagerrak) strait in the [North Sea](21179.md).

### Temperature and ice



Satellite image of the Baltic Sea in a mild winter



Traversing Baltic Sea and ice



On particularly cold winters, the coastal parts of the Baltic Sea freeze into ice thick enough to walk or ski on.

The water temperature of the Baltic Sea varies significantly depending on exact location, season and depth. At the Bornholm Basin, which is located directly east of the island of the same name, the surface temperature typically falls to 0–5 °C (32–41 °F) during the peak of the winter and rises to 15–20 °C (59–68 °F) during the peak of the summer, with an annual average of around 9–10 °C (48–50 °F).[^cite_note-OurBalticSea-57] A similar pattern can be seen in the [Gotland Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gotland_Basin), which is located between the island of Gotland and Latvia. In the deep of these basins the temperature variations are smaller. At the bottom of the Bornholm Basin, deeper than 80 m (260 ft), the temperature typically is 1–10 °C (34–50 °F), and at the bottom of the Gotland Basin, at depths greater than 225 m (738 ft), the temperature typically is 4–7 °C (39–45 °F).[^cite_note-OurBalticSea-57] Generally, offshore locations, lower latitudes and islands maintain [maritime climates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanic_climate), but adjacent to the water [continental climates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humid_continental_climate) are common, especially on the [Gulf of Finland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Finland). In the northern tributaries the climates transition from moderate continental to [subarctic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subarctic_climate) on the northernmost coastlines.

On the long-term average, the Baltic Sea is ice-covered at the annual maximum for about 45% of its surface area. The ice-covered area during such a typical winter includes the [Gulf of Bothnia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Bothnia), the [Gulf of Finland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Finland), the [Gulf of Riga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Riga), the archipelago west of Estonia, the [Stockholm archipelago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm_archipelago), and the [Archipelago Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archipelago_Sea) southwest of Finland. The remainder of the Baltic does not freeze during a normal winter, except sheltered bays and shallow lagoons such as the [Curonian Lagoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curonian_Lagoon). The ice reaches its maximum extent in February or March; typical ice thickness in the northernmost areas in the [Bothnian Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bothnian_Bay), the northern basin of the Gulf of Bothnia, is about 70 cm (28 in) for landfast sea ice. The thickness decreases farther south.

Freezing begins in the northern extremities of the Gulf of Bothnia typically in the middle of November, reaching the open waters of the Bothnian Bay in early January. The [Bothnian Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bothnian_Sea), the basin south of [Kvarken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kvarken), freezes on average in late February. The Gulf of Finland and the Gulf of Riga freeze typically in late January. In 2011, the Gulf of Finland was completely frozen on 15 February.[^cite_note-58]

The ice extent depends on whether the winter is mild, moderate, or severe. In severe winters ice can form around southern [Sweden](5058739.md) and even in the [Danish Straits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_Straits). According to the 18th-century natural historian [William Derham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Derham), during the severe winters of 1703 and 1708, the ice cover reached as far as the Danish Straits.[^cite_note-59] Frequently, parts of the Gulf of Bothnia and the Gulf of Finland are frozen, in addition to coastal fringes in more southerly locations such as the Gulf of Riga. This description meant that the whole of the Baltic Sea was covered with ice.

Since 1720, the Baltic Sea has frozen over entirely 20 times, most recently in early 1987, which was the most severe winter in Scandinavia since 1720. The ice then covered 400,000 km<sup>2</sup> (150,000 sq mi). During the winter of 2010–11, which was quite severe compared to those of the last decades, the maximum ice cover was 315,000 km<sup>2</sup> (122,000 sq mi), which was reached on 25 February 2011. The ice then extended from the north down to the northern tip of [Gotland](284507.md), with small ice-free areas on either side, and the east coast of the Baltic Sea was covered by an ice sheet about 25 to 100 km (16 to 62 mi) wide all the way to [Gdańsk](12099.md). This was brought about by a stagnant [high-pressure area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-pressure_area) that lingered over central and northern Scandinavia from around 10 to 24 February. After this, strong southern winds pushed the ice further into the north, and much of the waters north of Gotland were again free of ice, which had then packed against the shores of southern Finland.[^cite_note-60] The effects of the aforementioned high-pressure area did not reach the southern parts of the Baltic Sea, and thus the entire sea did not freeze over. However, floating ice was additionally observed near [Świnoujście](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9Awinouj%C5%9Bcie) harbour in January 2010.

In recent years before 2011, the Bothnian Bay and the Bothnian Sea were frozen with solid ice near the Baltic coast and dense floating ice far from it. In 2008, almost no ice formed except for a short period in March.[^cite_note-61]



Piles of drift ice on the shore of Puhtulaid, near [Virtsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtsu), Estonia, in late April

During winter, [fast ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_ice), which is attached to the shoreline, develops first, rendering ports unusable without the services of [icebreakers](483060.md). [Level ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Level_ice?action=edit\&redlink=1), [ice sludge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_sludge?action=edit\&redlink=1), [pancake ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pancake_ice), and [rafter ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rafter_ice?action=edit\&redlink=1) form in the more open regions. The gleaming expanse of ice is similar to the [Arctic](36971.md), with wind-driven pack ice and ridges up to 15 m (49 ft). Offshore of the landfast ice, the ice remains very dynamic all year, and it is relatively easily moved around by winds and therefore forms [pack ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pack_ice), made up of large piles and ridges pushed against the landfast ice and shores.

In spring, the Gulf of Finland and the Gulf of Bothnia normally thaw in late April, with some ice ridges persisting until May in the eastern extremities of the Gulf of Finland. In the northernmost reaches of the Bothnian Bay, ice usually stays until late May; by early June it is practically always gone. However, in the famine year of [1867](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Famine_of_1866-68) remnants of ice were observed as late as 17 July near [Uddskär](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uddsk%C3%A4r).[^cite_note-62] Even as far south as [Øresund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%98resund), remnants of ice have been observed in May on several occasions; near [Taarbaek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taarbaek) on 15 May 1942 and near Copenhagen on 11 May 1771. Drift ice was also observed on 11 May 1799.[^cite_note-63][^cite_note-64][^cite_note-65]

The ice cover is the main habitat for two large mammals, the [grey seal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grey_seal) (*Halichoerus grypus*) and the Baltic [ringed seal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ringed_seal) (*Pusa hispida botnica*), both of which feed underneath the ice and breed on its surface. Of these two seals, only the Baltic ringed seal suffers when there is not adequate ice in the Baltic Sea, as it feeds its young only while on ice. The grey seal is adapted to reproducing also with no ice in the sea. The sea ice also harbours several species of algae that live in the bottom and inside unfrozen brine pockets in the ice.

Due to the often fluctuating winter temperatures between above and below freezing, the saltwater ice of the Baltic Sea can be treacherous and hazardous to walk on, in particular in comparison to the more stable fresh water-ice sheets in the interior lakes.

### Hydrography



Depths of the Baltic Sea in meters

The Baltic Sea flows out through the [Danish Straits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_Straits); however, the flow is complex. A surface layer of brackish water discharges 940 km<sup>3</sup> (230 cu mi) per year into the [North Sea](21179.md). Due to the difference in [salinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salinity), by salinity permeation principle, a sub-surface layer of more saline water moving in the opposite direction brings in 475 km<sup>3</sup> (114 cu mi) per year. It mixes very slowly with the upper waters, resulting in a salinity gradient from top to bottom, with most of the saltwater remaining below 40 to 70 m (130 to 230 ft) deep. The general circulation is anti-clockwise: northwards along its eastern boundary, and south along with the western one.[^cite_note-Alhonen-66]

The difference between the outflow and the inflow comes entirely from [fresh water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresh_water). More than 250 streams drain a basin of about 1,600,000 km<sup>2</sup> (620,000 sq mi), contributing a volume of 660 km<sup>3</sup> (160 cu mi) per year to the Baltic. They include the major rivers of north Europe, such as the [Oder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oder), the [Vistula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vistula), the [Neman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neman_River), the [Daugava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daugava_River) and the [Neva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neva). Additional fresh water comes from the difference of [precipitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precipitation_\(meteorology\)) less evaporation, which is positive.

An important source of salty water is infrequent inflows (also known as [major Baltic inflows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_Baltic_Inflow) or MBIs) of [North Sea](21179.md) water into the Baltic. Such inflows, important to the Baltic ecosystem because of the oxygen they transport into the Baltic deeps, happen on average once per year, but large pulses that can replace the anoxic deep water in the [Gotland Deep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gotland_Basin) occur about once in ten years. Previously, it was believed that the frequency of MBIs had declined since 1980, but recent studies have challenged this view and no longer display a clear change in the frequency or intensity of saline inflows. Instead, a decadal variability in the intensities of MBIs is observed with a main period of approximately 30 years.[^cite_note-67][^cite_note-68]

The water level is generally far more dependent on the regional wind situation than on tidal effects. However, tidal currents occur in narrow passages in the western parts of the Baltic Sea. Tides can reach 17 to 19 cm (6.7 to 7.5 in) in the Gulf of Finland.[^cite_note-69]

The [significant wave height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Significant_wave_height) is generally much lower than that of the [North Sea](21179.md). Quite violent, sudden storms sweep the surface ten or more times a year, due to large transient temperature differences and a long reach of the wind. Seasonal winds also cause small changes in sea level, of the order of 0.5 m (1 ft 8 in) .[^cite_note-Alhonen-66] According to the media, during a storm in January 2017, an extreme wave above 14 m (46 ft) has been measured and significant wave height of around 8 m (26 ft) has been measured by the [FMI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finnish_Meteorological_Institute). A numerical study has shown the presence of events with 8 to 10 m (26 to 33 ft) significant wave heights. Those extreme waves events can play an important role in the coastal zone on erosion and sea dynamics.[^cite_note-70]

### Salinity



Baltic Sea near [Klaipėda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klaip%C4%97da) ([Karklė](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karkl%C4%97?action=edit\&redlink=1))

The Baltic Sea is the world's largest [brackish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brackish) sea.[^cite_note-Leijonmalm2017-71] Only two [other brackish waters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_brackish_bodies_of_water) are larger according to some measurements: The [Black Sea](3386.md) is larger in both surface area and water volume, but most of it is located outside the [continental shelf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_shelf) (only a small fraction is inland). The [Caspian Sea](19653787.md) is larger in water volume, but—despite its name—it is a lake rather than a sea.[^cite_note-Leijonmalm2017-71]

The Baltic Sea's [salinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salinity) is much lower than that of ocean water (which averages 3.5%), as a result of abundant freshwater runoff from the surrounding land (rivers, streams and alike), combined with the shallowness of the sea itself; runoff contributes roughly one-fortieth its total volume per year, as the volume of the basin is about 21,000 km<sup>3</sup> (5,000 cu mi) and yearly runoff is about 500 km<sup>3</sup> (120 cu mi).

The open surface waters of the Baltic Sea "proper" generally have a salinity of 0.3 to 0.9%, which is border-line [freshwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freshwater). The flow of freshwater into the sea from approximately two hundred rivers and the introduction of salt from the southwest builds up a gradient of salinity in the Baltic Sea. The highest surface salinities, generally 0.7–0.9%, are in the southwestern most part of the Baltic, in the Arkona and Bornholm basins (the former located roughly between southeast [Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zealand) and Bornholm, and the latter directly east of Bornholm). It gradually falls further east and north, reaching the lowest in the [Bothnian Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bothnian_Bay) at around 0.3%.[^cite_note-Hydrogeography\&Oxygen-72] Drinking the surface water of the Baltic as a means of survival would actually hydrate the body instead of [dehydrating](155056.md), as is the case with ocean water.[^cite_note-73]

As saltwater is denser than freshwater, the bottom of the Baltic Sea is saltier than the surface. This creates a vertical stratification of the water column, a [halocline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halocline), that represents a barrier to the exchange of [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen) and nutrients, and fosters completely separate maritime environments.[^cite_note-74] The difference between the bottom and surface salinities varies depending on location. Overall it follows the same southwest to east and north pattern as the surface. At the bottom of the Arkona Basin (equalling depths greater than 40 m or 130 ft) and Bornholm Basin (depths greater than 80 m or 260 ft) it is typically 1.4–1.8%. Further east and north the salinity at the bottom is consistently lower, being the lowest in Bothnian Bay (depths greater than 120 m or 390 ft) where it is slightly below 0.4%, or only marginally higher than the surface in the same region.[^cite_note-Hydrogeography\&Oxygen-72]

In contrast, the salinity of the [Danish Straits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_Straits), which connect the Baltic Sea and Kattegat, tends to be significantly higher, but with major variations from year to year. For example, the surface and bottom salinity in the [Great Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Belt) is typically around 2.0% and 2.8% respectively, which is only somewhat below that of the Kattegat.[^cite_note-Hydrogeography\&Oxygen-72] The water surplus caused by the continuous inflow of rivers and streams to the Baltic Sea means that there generally is a flow of brackish water out through the Danish Straits to the Kattegat (and eventually the Atlantic).[^cite_note-Muus1999-75] Significant flows in the opposite direction, salt water from the Kattegat through the Danish Straits to the Baltic Sea, are less regular and are known as [major Baltic inflows (MBIs)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_Baltic_Inflow).

Population density in the Baltic Sea catchment area

Countries that border the sea: Denmark, Estonia, Finland, Germany, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, Russia, Sweden.

Countries lands in the outer [drainage basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage_basin): Belarus, Czech Republic, Norway, Slovakia, Ukraine.

The Baltic Sea drainage basin is roughly four times the surface area of the sea itself. About 48% of the region is forested, with Sweden and Finland containing the majority of the forest, especially around the Gulfs of Bothnia and Finland.

About 20% of the land is used for agriculture and pasture, mainly in Poland and around the edge of the Baltic Proper, in Germany, Denmark, and Sweden. About 17% of the basin is unused open land with another 8% of wetlands. Most of the latter are in the Gulfs of Bothnia and Finland.

The rest of the land is heavily populated. About 85 million people live in the Baltic drainage basin, 15 million within 10 km (6 mi) of the coast and 29 million within 50 km (31 mi) of the coast. Around 22 million live in population centres of over 250,000. 90% of these are concentrated in the 10 km (6 mi) band around the coast. Of the nations containing all or part of the basin, Poland includes 45% of the 85 million, Russia 12%, Sweden 10% and the others less than 6% each.[^cite_note-76]

The Baltic Sea somewhat resembles a [riverbed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River), with two tributaries, the [Gulf of Finland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Finland) and [Gulf of Bothnia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Bothnia). [Geological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology) surveys show that before the [Pleistocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleistocene), instead of the Baltic Sea, there was a wide plain around a great river that palaeontologists call the [Eridanos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eridanos_\(geology\)). Several Pleistocene [glacial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glaciation) episodes scooped out the river bed into the sea basin. By the time of the last, or [Eemian Stage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eemian_Stage) ([MIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_isotopic_stage) 5e), the Eemian Sea was in place. Sometimes the Baltic Sea is considered a very large [estuary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estuary), with freshwater outflow from numerous rivers.[^cite_note-o502-78]

From that time the waters underwent a geologic history summarized under the names listed below. Many of the stages are named after marine animals (e.g. the [Littorina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Littorina) [mollusc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mollusk)) that are clear markers of changing water temperatures and salinity.

The factors that determined the sea's characteristics were the submergence or emergence of the region due to the weight of ice and subsequent isostatic readjustment, and the connecting channels it found to the [North Sea](21179.md)-[Atlantic](698.md), either through the straits of [Denmark](76972.md) or at what are now the large lakes of [Sweden](5058739.md), and the [White Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Sea)-[Arctic Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_Sea). There are a number of named and dated stages in the evolution of the Baltic Sea:[^cite_note-79]

- [Eemian Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eemian_Sea), about 130,000–115,000 years [BP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Before_Present)

- [Baltic Ice Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltic_Ice_Lake), 16,000–11,700 years cal. BP

- [Yoldia Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoldia_Sea), 11,700–10,700 years cal. BP

- [Ancylus Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancylus_Lake), 10,700–9,800 years cal. BP

- [Mastogloia Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mastogloia_Sea), 9,800–8,500 years cal. BP

- [Littorina Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Littorina_Sea), 8,500–4,000 years cal. BP

- Post-Littorina Sea, 4,000–present

The land is still emerging [isostatically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isostasy) from its depressed state, which was caused by the weight of ice during the last glaciation. The phenomenon is known as [post-glacial rebound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-glacial_rebound). Consequently, the surface area and the depth of the sea are diminishing. The uplift is about eight millimetres per year on the Finnish coast of the northernmost Gulf of Bothnia. In the area, the former seabed is only gently sloping, leading to large areas of land being reclaimed in what are, geologically speaking, relatively short periods (decades and centuries).

## Biology

### Fauna and flora

See also: [List of fish in Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fish_in_Sweden)

The fauna of the Baltic Sea is a mixture of marine and freshwater species. Among marine fishes are [Atlantic cod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_cod), [Atlantic herring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_herring), [European hake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_hake), [European plaice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_plaice), [European flounder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_flounder), [shorthorn sculpin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shorthorn_sculpin) and [turbot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbot), and examples of freshwater species include [European perch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_perch), [northern pike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_pike), [whitefish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coregonus) and [common roach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_roach). Freshwater species may occur at outflows of rivers or streams in all coastal sections of the Baltic Sea. Otherwise, marine species dominate in most sections of the Baltic, at least as far north as [Gävle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%A4vle), where less than one-tenth are freshwater species. Further north the pattern is inverted. In the Bothnian Bay, roughly two-thirds of the species are freshwater. In the far north of this bay, saltwater species are almost entirely absent.[^cite_note-OurBalticSea-57] For example, the [common starfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_starfish) and [shore crab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shore_crab), two species that are very widespread along European coasts, are both unable to cope with the significantly lower salinity. Their range limit is west of Bornholm, meaning that they are absent from the vast majority of the Baltic Sea.[^cite_note-OurBalticSea-57] Some marine species, like the Atlantic cod and European flounder, can survive at relatively low salinities but need higher salinities to breed, which therefore occurs in deeper parts of the Baltic Sea.[^cite_note-Nissling1997-80][^cite_note-Momigliano2018-81] The common [blue mussel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_mussel) is the dominating animal species, and makes up more than 90% of the total animal biomass in the sea.[^cite_note-82]

There is a decrease in species richness from the Danish belts to the [Gulf of Bothnia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Bothnia). The decreasing salinity along this path causes restrictions in both physiology and habitats.[^cite_note-83] At more than 600 species of invertebrates, fish, aquatic mammals, aquatic birds and [macrophytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macrophyte), the Arkona Basin (roughly between southeast Zealand and Bornholm) is far richer than other more eastern and northern basins in the Baltic Sea, which all have less than 400 species from these groups, with the exception of the Gulf of Finland with more than 750 species. However, even the most diverse sections of the Baltic Sea have far fewer species than the almost-full saltwater Kattegat, which is home to more than 1600 species from these groups.[^cite_note-OurBalticSea-57] The lack of [tides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tide) has affected the marine species as compared with the Atlantic.

Since the Baltic Sea is so young there are only two or three known [endemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endemism) species: the brown alga *[Fucus radicans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fucus_radicans)* and the flounder *[Platichthys solemdali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platichthys_solemdali)*. Both appear to have evolved in the Baltic basin and were only recognized as species in 2005 and 2018 respectively, having formerly been confused with more widespread relatives.[^cite_note-Momigliano2018-81][^cite_note-Pereyra2009-84] The tiny [Copenhagen cockle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parvicardium_hauniense) (*Parvicardium hauniense*), a rare mussel, is sometimes considered endemic, but has now been recorded in the Mediterranean.[^cite_note-85] However, some consider non-Baltic records to be misidentifications of juvenile [lagoon cockles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagoon_cockle) (*Cerastoderma glaucum*).[^cite_note-86] Several widespread marine species have distinctive subpopulations in the Baltic Sea adapted to the low salinity, such as the Baltic Sea forms of the Atlantic herring and [lumpsucker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclopterus_lumpus), which are smaller than the widespread forms in the North Atlantic.[^cite_note-Muus1999-75]

A peculiar feature of the fauna is that it contains a number of glacial [relict species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glacial_relict), isolated populations of arctic species which have remained in the Baltic Sea since the last [glaciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glaciation), such as the large isopod *[Saduria entomon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saduria_entomon)*, the Baltic subspecies of [ringed seal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ringed_seal), and the [fourhorn sculpin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourhorn_sculpin). Some of these relicts are derived from [glacial lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glacial_lake), such as *[Monoporeia affinis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoporeia_affinis)*, which is a main element in the [benthic fauna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benthos) of the low-salinity [Bothnian Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bothnian_Bay).

[Cetaceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cetacean) in the Baltic Sea are monitored by the countries bordering the sea and data compiled by various intergovernmental bodies, such as [ASCOBANS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASCOBANS). A critically endangered population of [harbour porpoise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harbor_porpoise) inhabit the Baltic proper, whereas the species is abundant in the outer Baltic (Western Baltic and [Danish Straits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_Straits)) and occasionally oceanic and out-of-range species such as [minke whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minke_whale),[^cite_note-87] [bottlenose dolphins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bottlenose_dolphin),[^cite_note-88] [beluga whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beluga_whale),[^cite_note-89] [orcas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orca),[^cite_note-90] and [beaked whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaked_whale)[^cite_note-91] visit the waters. In recent years, very small, but with increasing rates, [fin whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fin_whale)[^cite_note-92][^cite_note-93][^cite_note-94][^cite_note-95] and [humpback whales](231728.md) migrate into Baltic sea including mother and calf pair.[^cite_note-96] Now extinct Atlantic [grey whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grey_whale) (remains found from [Gräsö](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gr%C3%A4s%C3%B6) along [Bothnian Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bothnian_Sea)/southern [Bothnian Gulf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bothnian_Gulf)[^cite_note-97] and [Ystad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ystad)[^cite_note-98]) and eastern population of [North Atlantic right whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Atlantic_right_whale) that is facing [functional extinction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_extinction)[^cite_note-99] once migrated into Baltic Sea.[^cite_note-100]

Other notable [megafauna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megafauna) include the [basking sharks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basking_shark).[^cite_note-101]

### Environmental status

Further information: [Baltic Sea hypoxia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltic_Sea_hypoxia)



Satellite photo of the Baltic Sea surrounding [Gotland](284507.md), Sweden, with algae bloom (phytoplankton) swirling in the water

Satellite images taken in July 2010 revealed a massive [algal bloom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algal_bloom) covering 377,000 square kilometres (146,000 mi<sup>2</sup>) in the Baltic Sea. The area of the bloom extended from Germany and Poland to Finland. Researchers of the phenomenon have found that algal blooms have occurred every summer for decades. Fertilizer runoff from surrounding agricultural land has exacerbated the problem and led to increased [eutrophication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eutrophication).[^cite_note-102]

Approximately 100,000 km<sup>2</sup> (38,610 sq mi) of the Baltic's seafloor (a quarter of its total area) is a variable [dead zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_zone_\(ecology\)). The more saline (and therefore denser) water remains on the bottom, isolating it from surface waters and the atmosphere. This leads to decreased oxygen concentrations within the zone. It is mainly bacteria that grow in it, digesting organic material and releasing [hydrogen sulfide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_sulfide). Because of this large anaerobic zone, the seafloor ecology differs from that of the neighbouring Atlantic.

Plans to artificially oxygenate areas of the Baltic that have experienced eutrophication have been proposed by the [University of Gothenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Gothenburg) and Inocean AB. The proposal intends to use wind-driven pumps to pump oxygen-rich surface water to a depth of around 130 m.[^cite_note-103]

After [World War II](32927.md), Germany had to be disarmed, and large quantities of ammunition stockpiles were disposed directly into the Baltic Sea and the North Sea. Environmental experts and marine biologists warn that these ammunition dumps pose an environmental threat, with potentially life-threatening consequences to the health and safety of humans on the coastlines of these seas.[^cite_note-104]

## Future change

[Climate change](5042951.md), and [pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_pollution) from agriculture and forestry, impose such strong effects on the ecosystems of the Baltic sea, that there is a concern the sea will turn from a [carbon sink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_sink) to a source of [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) and [methane](18582230.md).[^cite_note-105] Modelling climate change and the impact of well characterised factors such as post-glacial rebound before the year 2050, is complicated by the unique properties of the Baltic Sea area compared to say the adjacent North Sea and controversy as to the relative contributions of socio-economic factors such as land use to any warming component.[^cite_note-Meier2022-106]<sup>: 537 </sup> These include its current brackish water, the southern subbasin tendency to have a vertical stratification of the halocline, and the northern subbasin seasonal sea ice cover.[^cite_note-Meier2022-106]<sup>: 458 </sup> [High confidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_confidence) future projections include: air temperature warming, more heavy precipitation episodes, less snow with less perifrost and glacial ice mass in northern catchment areas, more mild winters, raised mean water temperature with more marine heatwaves, intensified seasonal [thermoclines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermocline) without change in the [thermohaline circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermohaline_circulation), and [sea level rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level_rise).[^cite_note-Meier2022-106]<sup>: 547, 458–9 </sup> There are many more projections but these have lower confidence.[^cite_note-Meier2022-106]<sup>: 547, 458–9 </sup>[^cite_note-107]

## Economy

Pedestrian pier in [Sellin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sellin), Germany

Construction of the [Great Belt Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Belt_Bridge) in Denmark (completed 1997) and the [Øresund Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%98resund_Bridge)-Tunnel (completed 1999), linking Denmark with Sweden, provided a highway and railroad connection between Sweden and the Danish mainland (the [Jutland Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jutland_Peninsula), precisely the [Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zealand)). The undersea tunnel of the Øresund Bridge-Tunnel provides for navigation of large ships into and out of the Baltic Sea. The Baltic Sea is the main trade route for the export of Russian petroleum. Countries neighbouring the Baltic Sea have expressed concerns about this since a major oil leak in a seagoing tanker would be especially disastrous for the Baltic given the slow exchange of water in the ecosystem.[^cite_note-108][^cite_note-109][^cite_note-110] The tourism industry surrounding the Baltic Sea is naturally concerned about [oil pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_pollution).

Much shipbuilding is carried out in the shipyards around the Baltic Sea. The largest shipyards are at [Gdańsk](12099.md), [Gdynia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gdynia), and [Szczecin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Szczecin), Poland; [Kiel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiel), Germany; [Karlskrona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karlskrona) and [Malmö](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malm%C3%B6), Sweden; [Rauma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rauma,_Finland), [Turku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turku), and [Helsinki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helsinki), Finland; [Riga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riga), [Ventspils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ventspils), and [Liepāja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liep%C4%81ja), Latvia; [Klaipėda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klaip%C4%97da), Lithuania; and [Saint Petersburg](24320051.md), Russia.

Construction of the [Fehmarn Belt Fixed Link](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fehmarn_Belt_Fixed_Link) between Denmark and Germany is due to finish in 2029. It will be a three-bore tunnel carrying four motorway lanes and two rail tracks.

Through the development of [offshore wind power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offshore_wind_power) the Baltic Sea is expected to become a major source of energy for countries in the region. According to the Marienborg Declaration, signed in 2022, all EU Baltic Sea states have announced their intentions to have 19.6 gigawatts of offshore wind in operation by 2030.[^cite_note-111]

## Critical Maritime Infrastructure (CMI)

Critical maritime infrastructure (CMI) include pipelines, ports, undersea cables and energy installations. Following a series of incidents between 2022 and 2025, critical infrastructure in the Baltic Sea has drawn growing political attention. In September 2022, both [Nord Stream I and Nord Stream II](71856702.md) were damaged by explosives close to [Bornholm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bornholm) in Denmark.[^cite_note-\:5-112] In October 2023, the [Balticconnector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balticconnector) gas pipeline was damaged by the anchor of the Chinese container vessel *[Newnew Polar Bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newnew_Polar_Bear)*.[^cite_note-113] In November 2024, telecoms cables were damaged, another case of suspected sabotage from a Chinese bulk carrier, departing from a Russian port.[^cite_note-114] In December 2024, a ship registered in the [Cook Islands](7067.md) which was thought to be part of a Russian shadow fleet, the *[Estlink 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estlink)*, was suspected to have damaged internet cables.[^cite_note-115] These incidents have led to responses from [NATO](21133.md), the [European Union](9317.md) and national governments.[^cite_note-\:7-43] NATO has increased its air and naval presence, as well as agreed to establish the Maritime Centre for the Security of Critical Undersea Infrastructure within NATO's Allied Maritime Command (MARCOM), among other cooperation efforts.[^cite_note-\:8-116] The EU has updated its Maritime Security Strategy, launched an action plan and a coordination group for infrastructure protection, while national governments have strengthened surveillance, legal tools, and seabed defence capabilities.[^cite_note-\:9-117] Aside from technical standards, political decisions influence what is deemed as "critical" infrastructure. Critical maritime infrastructure is deemed as critical since economies in today's society are dependent on this infrastructure.[^cite_note-118] Therefore, they require an extra layer of protection, whether by security policies or military protection.[^cite_note-\:7-43]

### Hybrid warfare

In the current geopolitical climate, CMI is facing challenges posed by hybrid warfare threats.[^cite_note-\:8-116] Hybrid threats in the Baltic Sea are often associated with Russian actions and operate below the official threshold of war, which poses a political challenge.[^cite_note-\:12-119] The challenge posed by hybrid threats is that they operate in a grey area between peace and violence. For example, the Nord Stream pipeline sabotage demonstrated this problem by exploitation of legal ambiguities,[^cite_note-\:7-43][^cite_note-\:10-120] the complexity of attribution, and the disruption of alliance cohesion.[^cite_note-\:8-116] The incident highlights the vulnerability of critical infrastructure and the absence of coherent political responses.[^cite_note-\:8-116] Responding to hybrid threats requires sustained and coordinated efforts between civilian and military actors.[^cite_note-121] However, the maritime domain presents unique difficulties, including jurisdictional overlaps, fragmented responsibilities, and the challenge of adapting land-based security frameworks to the sea.[^cite_note-\:7-43] Hybrid tactics, such as uncrewed aerial vehicle (drone) surveillance, covert sabotage, and information manipulation, aim not only to damage infrastructure but also to undermine public trust and create strategic instability in the region.[^cite_note-\:11-122]

### Geopolitical Dimensions of Critical Maritime Infrastructure

The Baltic Sea region in the post-Cold War era has for a long time been regarded as an area with little geopolitical tensions. With the Soviet presence in the south, the American influence through the NATO members Denmark and Germany and the neutral states Sweden and Finland, an equilibrium existed, often referred to as the "Nordic balance". This balance also prolonged after the steady integration of the region into western institutions.[^cite_note-\:12-119]

However, in recent years this geopolitical reality has increasingly been challenged by the neo-imperial ambitions of Russia, manifesting itself in aggression against Ukraine. Russia has also pursued a strategy of regional dominance in the Baltic Sea, designating the regional sea as a zone of strategic influence in its naval doctrine published in 2022. Yet, such a positioning in itself was significantly complicated by the accession of Sweden and Finland to NATO.[^cite_note-\:11-122]

Against this geopolitical background, it becomes clear, why many of the recent infrastructure projects in the Baltic Sea were subject to big political debates. Projects such as the [Balticconnector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balticconnector), which links the Finnish and Estonian gas markets and has been described by the European Commission as an expression of European solidarity,[^cite_note-123] whilst the Gas Interconnection Poland–Lithuania (GIPL) which connects the Polish and Lithuanian gas networks,[^cite_note-124] and the development of multiple LNG terminals, have all played a significant role in reducing European reliance on Russian energy supplies.[^cite_note-\:7-43] These initiatives form part of broader efforts to enhance regional integration and bring the Baltic Sea states into closer alignment with the European Union.[^cite_note-125] In contrast, the Nord Stream pipelines, particularly [Nord Stream 2](50335419.md), became a source of political controversy.[^cite_note-\:5-112] Critics argued that the project would increase European dependence on Russian gas, bypass transit countries such as Ukraine and Poland, and undermine EU energy solidarity by strengthening Russia's leverage over countries like Germany.[^cite_note-126] Nord Stream 2 experienced prolonged delays and was ultimately suspended following the imposition of international sanctions against Russia after the invasion of Ukraine in 2022.[^cite_note-\:5-112] In addition to political and economic controversies, the Nord Stream pipelines also became the subject of security-related concerns regarding their potential strategic implications in the Baltic Sea region.[^cite_note-\:5-112] Prior to the 2022 sabotage of the Nord Stream pipelines, security experts and several Eastern European states had raised concerns that such infrastructure could be exploited by Russia for intelligence gathering and military purposes in the Baltic Sea.[^cite_note-\:13-127] These concerns gained renewed attention following the sabotage incident, which highlighted challenges in the legal and regulatory frameworks governing the protection of critical infrastructure.[^cite_note-\:7-43][^cite_note-\:9-117][^cite_note-\:10-120] The difficulty in conclusively attributing the attack also drew attention to the limitations of existing mechanisms for responding to hybrid threats in the maritime domain.[^cite_note-\:8-116][^cite_note-\:7-43]

## Helsinki Convention

Main article: [Helsinki Convention on the Protection of the Marine Environment of the Baltic Sea Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helsinki_Convention_on_the_Protection_of_the_Marine_Environment_of_the_Baltic_Sea_Area)

### 1974 Convention

For the first time ever, all the sources of pollution around an entire sea were made subject to a single convention, signed in 1974 by the then seven Baltic coastal states. The 1974 Convention entered into force on 3 May 1980.

### 1992 Convention

In the light of political changes and developments in international environmental and maritime law, a new convention was signed in 1992 by all the states bordering on the Baltic Sea, and the European Community. After ratification, the [Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convention_on_the_Protection_of_the_Marine_Environment_of_the_Baltic_Sea_Area) entered into force on 17 January 2000. The Convention covers the whole of the Baltic Sea area, including inland waters and the water of the sea itself, as well as the seabed. Measures are also taken in the whole catchment area of the Baltic Sea to reduce land-based pollution. The convention on the Protection of the Marine Environment of the Baltic Sea Area, 1992, entered into force on 17 January 2000.

The governing body of the convention is the [Helsinki Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HELCOM),[^cite_note-128] also known as HELCOM, or Baltic Marine Environment Protection Commission. The present contracting parties are Denmark, Estonia, the European Community, Finland, Germany, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, Russia, and Sweden.

The ratification instruments were deposited by the European Community, Germany, Latvia and Sweden in 1994, by Estonia and Finland in 1995, by Denmark in 1996, by Lithuania in 1997, and by Poland and Russia in November 1999.

## Coordination in the Baltic Sea region

### European Union

The [European Union](9317.md) (EU) is one core framework shaping regional security coordination in the [Baltic Sea region.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltic_region) The EU has recognised this area as one of thirteen designated zones for territorial cooperation. Following the accession of the [Baltic States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltic_states) in 2004, the Baltic Sea is now considered an EU internal sea.[^cite_note-129] The following initiatives form the basis of the EU's engagement in [Maritime domain awareness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maritime_domain_awareness) and Maritime Situational Awareness in the Baltic Sea:

- **2006**: Maritime Surveillance Network (**MARSUR**), a project aiming at facilitating communication between maritime information systems in Europe, that is undertaken by the European Defence Agency (EDA).

  [^cite_note-130]

- **2009**: Sea Surveillance Co-Operation Baltic Sea (**SUCBAS**), a Maritime Situational Awareness cooperation between Baltic Sea countries with the objective of sharing information effectively.

  [^cite_note-131]

- **2009**: EU Strategy for the Baltic Sea Region (**EUSBSR**), a macro-regional strategy that involves EU member states bordering the Baltic Sea and the EU Commission. The strategy is centred on four core pillars: the environment, prosperity, accessibility and maritime security.

  [^cite_note-132]

  [^cite_note-133]

  [^cite_note-134]

- **2021–2027**: Interreg Baltic Sea Region, an EU co-funded transnational cooperation network.

  [^cite_note-135]

  [^cite_note-136]

### NATO

The [North Atlantic Treaty Organisation (NATO)](21133.md) is the primary provider of a [collective defence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collective_defense) system.[^cite_note-137] Following the accession of Finland in 2023 and Sweden in 2024, the majority of the states bordering the Baltic Sea have become members of NATO, simplifying the organisational geography of the region.[^cite_note-138]

The following NATO initiatives and bodies are particularly relevant for the Baltic Sea region:

- **2023**: Critical Undersea Infrastructure Coordination Cell, a centre aiming at connecting military and civilian stakeholders.

  [^cite_note-139]

- **2023**: Maritime Centre for the Security of Critical Undersea Infrastructure, a centre aiming at protecting the allies' critical undersea infrastructure.

  [^cite_note-140]

- **2023**: EU-NATO Task Force on the Resilience of Critical Infrastructure, a cooperation on increasing the resilience of [critical infrastructure](521286.md), [supply chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supply_chain) and technology.

  [^cite_note-141]

- **2025**: Baltic Sentry, a NATO [military operation](666301.md) aiming at increasing the military presence in the Baltic Sea to improve the safety of critical infrastructure.

  [^cite_note-142]

  [^cite_note-143]

- Baltic Operations (**[BALTOPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BALTOPS)**), a multinational naval manoeuvre that is held annually in the Baltic Sea.

  [^cite_note-144]

### Nordic Defence Cooperation

The [Nordic Defence Cooperation (NORDEFCO)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nordic_Defence_Cooperation) is a [military alliance](250766.md) comprising the [Nordic countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nordic_countries) of Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Norway and Sweden. It was established in 2009. The objectives of this cooperation structure include improving the national defence of each country, identifying shared strategic interests, and promoting the development of coordinated, effective responses. The strategy paper 'Vision 2025' outlines plans to enhance [collaboration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collaboration) with the [Baltic states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltic_states) and transatlantic allies.[^cite_note-145]

### Council of the Baltic Sea States

The [Council of the Baltic Sea States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_the_Baltic_Sea_States) is an [intergovernmental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intergovernmentalism) political organisation that focuses on regional cooperation. It was established in 1992 and comprises ten European states and the [European Union](9317.md).[^cite_note-146] The organisation serves as a forum for political dialogue in the region and follows three main objectives: Regional Identity, Safe & Secure Region, and Sustainable & Prosperous Region. It holds annual regional and international meetings.[^cite_note-147]
