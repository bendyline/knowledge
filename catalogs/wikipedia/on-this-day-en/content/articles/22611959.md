[Capital punishment](5902.md) is a legal penalty in North Korea. It is used for many offenses, such as [grand theft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_theft), [murder](20171.md), [rape](7980471.md), [drug smuggling](218449.md), [treason](31292.md), [espionage](10501.md), [political dissent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_dissent), [defection](383663.md), [piracy](50715.md), consumption of [media not approved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship_in_North_Korea) by the government and [proselytizing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proselytism) religious beliefs that contradict the practiced *[Juche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juche)* ideology.[^cite_note-United_Nations_Human_Rights_Council_2014-1] Owing to the secrecy of the North Korean government, working knowledge of the topic depends heavily on anonymous sources, accounts of [defectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Korean_defectors) (both relatives of victims, and former members of the government) and reports by [Radio Free Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_Free_Asia), a United States government-funded news service that operates in East Asia.[^cite_note-United_Nations_Human_Rights_Council_2014-1] The country allegedly carries out public executions, which, if true, makes North Korea one of the last four countries that still performs [public executions](4733727.md), the other three being [Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_executions_in_Iran), [Saudi Arabia](11817227.md), and [Somalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_punishment_in_Somalia), but this has been disputed by some defector accounts.[^cite_note-Guardian-2]

## Reported executions

The South-Korean-based [Database Center for North Korean Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Database_Center_for_North_Korean_Human_Rights) has collected unverified testimony on 1,193 historic executions in North Korea through 2009.[^cite_note-3] [Amnesty International](18947898.md) reported that there were 105 executions between 2007 and 2012.[^cite_note-guardian-2016-4] The *[Foreign Policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Policy)* periodical estimated there were 60 executions in 2010.[^cite_note-foreignpolicy-20110922-5] In October 2001, the North Korean government told the UN Human Rights Committee that "only 13" executions had occurred since 1998 and that no public execution had occurred since 1992.[^cite_note-6]

On December 13, 2013, North Korean state media announced the execution of [Jang Sung-taek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jang_Sung-taek), the uncle by marriage of North Korea's leader [Kim Jong Un](5962511.md).[^cite_note-7] The [South Korean National Intelligence Service](991544.md) believes that two of [Jang Sung-taek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jang_Sung-taek) closest aides, Lee Yong-ha and Jang Soo-keel, were executed in mid-November 2013.[^cite_note-8]

In 2014, the [United Nations Human Rights Council](635790.md) created a [Commission of inquiry on human rights in the Democratic People's Republic of Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commission_of_Inquiry_on_Human_Rights_in_the_Democratic_People's_Republic_of_Korea), investigating and documenting alleged instances of executions carried out with or without trial, publicly or secretly, in response to political and other crimes that are often not among the most serious. The Commission determined that these systematic acts, if true, rise to the level of [crimes against humanity](61099.md).[^cite_note-United_Nations_Human_Rights_Council_2014-1]

## Public executions

North Korea was alleged to have resumed public executions in October 2007 after they had declined in the years following 2000 amidst international criticism. Prominent supposedly executed criminals include officials convicted of drug trafficking and embezzlement. Common criminals convicted of crimes such as murder, robbery, rape, drug dealing, smuggling, piracy, vandalism, etc. have also been reported to be executed, mostly by firing squad. The country does not publicly release national crime statistics or reports on the levels of crimes.[^cite_note-USDOS-HRR2012-58] As of 2012, North Korea is allegedly one of four countries carrying out executions in public, the other three being [Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_executions_in_Iran), [Saudi Arabia](349303.md) and [Somalia](27358.md).[^cite_note-Guardian-2] However, according to defectors interviewed by *[The Diplomat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Diplomat_\(magazine\))* in 2014, the practice of such activities had not occurred, at least in [Hyesan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyesan) since 2000.[^cite_note-59]

In October 2007, a South Pyongan province factory chief convicted of making international phone calls from 13 phones he installed in his factory basement was supposedly executed by firing squad in front of a crowd of 150,000 people in a stadium, according to an unverified report from a South Korean aid agency called Good Friends.[^cite_note-foreignpolicy-20110922-5][^cite_note-60] Good Friends also reported that six were killed in the rush as spectators left. In another unverified instance, 15 people were allegedly publicly executed for crossing the border into China.[^cite_note-61]

A U.N. General Assembly committee has adopted a draft resolution, co-sponsored by more than 50 countries, expressing "very serious concern" at reports of widespread human rights violations in North Korea, including public executions. North Korea has condemned the draft, saying it is inaccurate and biased. The report was sent to the then 192-member General Assembly for a final vote.[^cite_note-62]

In 2011, two people were allegedly executed in front of 500 spectators for handling propaganda leaflets floated across the border from South Korea, reportedly as part of an unverified campaign by former North Korean leader [Kim Jong Il](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kim_Jong_Il) to tighten ideological control as he groomed his youngest son, [Kim Jong Un](5962511.md), as the eventual successor.[^cite_note-63]

In June 2019, a South Korean NGO the Transitional Justice Working Group released an unverified report "Mapping the Fate of the Dead" that suggested 318 sites in North Korea supposedly used by the government for public executions.[^cite_note-64] According to the NGO, public executions have taken place near rivers, fields, markets, schools, and sports grounds. The report alleges that family members and children of those sentenced to death were forced to watch their executions.[^cite_note-65]

## Capital punishment in prison camps

[Amnesty International](18947898.md) has alleged that [torture](47702.md) and executions are widespread in [political prisons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights_in_North_Korea#Internment_camps_for_political_prisoners) in North Korea.[^cite_note-66] Unverified testimonies describe secret and public executions in North Korean prisons by firing squad, [decapitation](70802.md) or by [hanging](155019.md).[^cite_note-67] Executions are allegedly used as a means of deterrence, often accompanied by torture.[^cite_note-autogenerated1-68]
