A **majority government** is a [government](12229.md) by one or more [governing parties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruling_party) that hold an [absolute majority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_majority) of seats in a [legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislature). Such a government can consist of one party that holds a majority on its own, or be a [coalition government](6036.md) of multiple parties. This is as opposed to a [minority government](271434.md), where the government does not have a majority, and needs to cooperate with opposition parties to get legislation passed. A **government majority** determines the balance of power.[^cite_note-1] A government is not a majority government if it only has a majority when counting parties outside the government that have a [confidence agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence_and_supply) with it.

A majority government is usually assured of having its legislation passed and rarely if ever, has to fear being defeated in parliament, a state also known as a **working majority**.[^cite_note-2] In contrast, a minority government must constantly bargain for support from other parties in order to pass legislation and avoid being defeated on [motions of no confidence](208101.md). Single-party majority governments tend to be formed in the aftermath of strong election performances.

The term "majority government" may also be used for a stable long-term coalition of two or more parties to form an absolute majority. One example of such an electoral coalition is in [Australia](4689264.md), where the [Liberal](18453.md) and [National](21927.md) parties have run as an electoral bloc, known simply as [the Coalition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coalition_\(Australia\)), for decades. The largest majority government in Australia was elected in [1975](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1975_Australian_federal_election), when the Coalition won 71.65% of the seats, 91 out of 127, in a [landslide victory](41053306.md). Majority governments with a larger absolute number of seats have been elected since, with the Coalition and [Labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Labor_Party) each winning 94 seats in [1996](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_Australian_federal_election) and [2025](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Australian_federal_election) respectively. This however amounted to a smaller majority and seat share due to the [House of Representatives](61565.md) being considerably larger than in 1975.

In [electoral systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_systems) where one party usually wins a majority of seats on their own, such as [first past the post](261709.md), coalitions are rare, but may happen when an election returns a [hung parliament](652214.md). An example of this was the [2010–2015 coalition government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cameron%E2%80%93Clegg_coalition) in the [United Kingdom](31717.md), which was composed of the [Conservative Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Party_\(UK\)) and the [Liberal Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Democrats_\(UK\)). The Conservatives won the most seats of any single party in the 2010 election, but fell short of an absolute majority. However, by combining with the Liberal Democrats a solid majority in the [House of Commons](13828.md) was created. This was the first true coalition government in the UK since [World War II](32927.md).

Majority government differs from [consensus government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consensus_democracy) or [national unity government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_unity_government) in not requiring a consensus or supermajority.
