**Maritime transport** (or **ocean transport**) or more generally **waterborne transport**, is the transport of people (passengers) or goods (cargo) via [waterways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterways). [Freight transport](28362.md) by [watercraft](301664.md) has been widely used throughout recorded history, as it provides a higher-capacity mode of transportation for passengers and cargo than land transport, the latter typically being more costly per unit [payload](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Payload) due to it being affected by [terrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrain) conditions and road/rail infrastructures. The advent of aviation during the 20th century has diminished the importance of sea travel for passengers, though it is still popular for [short trips](50771.md) and [pleasure cruises](314855.md). Transport by watercraft is much cheaper than transport by aircraft or land vehicles (both road and rail),[^cite_note-1] but is significantly slower for longer journeys and heavily dependent on adequate port facilities. Maritime transport accounts for roughly 80% of international trade, according to [UNCTAD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Conference_on_Trade_and_Development) in 2020.

Maritime transport can be realized over any distance as long as there are connecting [bodies of water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodies_of_water) that are navigable to boats, ships or [barges](4177.md) such as oceans, lakes, rivers and canals. Shipping may be for commerce, [recreation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recreation), or military purposes, and is an important aspect of logistics in human societies since early shipbuilding and [river engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_engineering) were developed, leading to [canal ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canal_age) in various civilizations. While extensive inland shipping is less critical today, the major waterways of the world including many canals are still very important and are integral parts of worldwide economies. Particularly, especially any material can be moved by water; however, water transport becomes impractical when material delivery is time-critical such as various types of perishable [produce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Produce). Still, water transport is highly cost effective with regular schedulable cargoes, such as trans-oceanic shipping of consumer products – and especially for heavy loads or [bulk cargos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulk_cargo), such as coal, [coke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coke_\(fuel\)), [ores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ore) or grains. Arguably, the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution) had its first impacts where cheap water transport by canal, navigations, or [shipping](28362.md) by all types of watercraft on natural waterways supported cost-effective [bulk transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulk_transport).

[Containerization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Containerization) revolutionized maritime transport starting in the 1970s. "General cargo" includes goods packaged in boxes, cases, pallets, and barrels. When a cargo is carried in more than one mode, it is [intermodal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermodal_freight_transport) or [co-modal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Co-modality).

A nation's shipping fleet (variously called [merchant navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merchant_navy), merchant marine, or merchant fleet) consists of the ships operated by civilian crews to transport passengers or cargo from one place to another. Merchant shipping also includes water transport over the river and canal systems connecting inland destinations, large and small. For example, during the [early modern era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_modern_era), cities in the [Hanseatic League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanseatic_League) began taming Northern Europe's rivers and harbors. Similarly, the [Saint Lawrence Seaway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Lawrence_Seaway) connects the port cities on the [Great Lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Lakes) in Canada and the United States with the Atlantic Ocean shipping routes, while the [various Illinois canals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illinois_and_Michigan_Canal) connect the Great Lakes and Canada with [New Orleans](53842.md). Ores, coal, and grains can travel along the rivers of the [American midwest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_midwest) to [Pittsburgh](25101.md) or to [Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham,_Alabama), Alabama. Professional mariners are known as merchant seamen, merchant sailors, and merchant mariners, or simply seamen, sailors, or mariners. The terms "seaman" or "sailor" may also refer to a member of a country's martial navy.

According to the 2005 [CIA World Factbook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_World_Factbook), the total number of merchant ships of at least 1,000 [gross register tons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_register_ton) in the world was 30,936. In 2010, it was 38,988, an increase of 26%, across [many countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_merchant_navy_capacity_by_country).[^cite_note-3] As of December 2018, a quarter of all merchant mariners were born in the Philippines.[^cite_note-4]

### Liners and tramps

A ship may also be categorized as to how it is operated.

- A [**liner**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_liner) will have a regular run and operate to a schedule. The scheduled operation requires that such ships are better equipped to deal with causes of potential delay such as bad weather. They are generally higher powered than tramp ships with better seakeeping qualities, thus they are significantly more expensive to build. Liners are typically built for passenger and container operation though past common uses also included mail and general cargo.

- A [**tramp (trader)**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tramp_trade) has no fixed run but will go wherever a suitable cargo takes it. Thus a ship and crew may be chartered from the ship owner to fetch a cargo of grain from Canada to Latvia, the ship may then be required to carry a cargo of coal from Britain to Melanesia. Bulk carriers and some cruise ships are examples of ships built to operate in this manner.

### Ships and watercraft&#x20;

Further information: [Ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship) and [Watercraft](301664.md)

Ships and other [watercraft](301664.md) are used for maritime transport. Types can be distinguished by [propulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_propulsion), size or cargo type. [Recreational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recreation) or educational craft still use wind power, while some smaller craft use [internal combustion engines](41228673.md) to drive one or more [propellers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propeller), or in the case of jet boats, an inboard water jet. In shallow-draft areas, such as the [Everglades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everglades), some craft, such as the [hovercraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hovercraft), are propelled by large pusher-prop fans.

### Typical in-transit times

A cargo ship sailing from a [European port](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_busiest_ports_in_Europe) to a US one will typically take 10–12 days depending on water currents and other factors.[^cite_note-6] To make [container ship](262084.md) transport more economical, ship operators sometimes reduce cruising speed, thereby increasing transit time, to reduce fuel consumption, a strategy referred to as "[slow steaming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slow_steaming)".[^cite_note-inti2013-7]

## History

Main articles: [Maritime history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maritime_history) and [Maritime timeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maritime_timeline)

Maritime transport began in [prehistory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prehistory): around 4000 BCE, when people living in river-valley and coastal societies, such as in [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia), developed basic watercraft to move people, food and goods along [waterways](174851.md).[^cite_note-8] Over millennia, the invention of sails and refined hull-building techniques enabled increasingly complex [seafaring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seafaring), laying the foundations for ancient maritime trade routes and long-distance [commerce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commerce).&#x20;

For much of history, maritime trade relied on relatively simple ships transporting [bulk cargo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulk_cargo) or loose goods ("break-bulk" cargo), loaded and unloaded manually at ports. With the rise of global trade in the 20th century, this labor-intensive method became a bottleneck for efficiency.[^cite_note-9]

From then onward, standardised containers and purpose-built container ships replaced much of the older trade methods, boosting the speed, scale, and reliability of international maritime freight and enabling interconnected [global supply chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_supply_chain) of today.

### Deck department

Further information: [Deck department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deck_department)



An able seaman stands iceberg lookout on the bow of the freighter USNS *Southern Cross* during a re-supply mission to [McMurdo Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McMurdo_Station), Antarctica; circa 1981.

&#x20;Officer positions in the [deck department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deck_department) include but not limited to the [Master](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_Mariner) and his [Chief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_Mate), [Second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Mate), and [Third](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Mate) officers. The official classifications for unlicensed members of the deck department are [Able Seaman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Able_Seaman_\(occupation\)) and [Ordinary Seaman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_Seaman_\(occupation\)).

A common deck crew for a ship includes:

- (1) [Chief Officer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_Mate)/Chief Mate

- (1) [Second Officer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Mate)/Second Mate

- (1) [Third Officer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Mate)/Third Mate

- (1) [Boatswain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boatswain)

- (2–6) [Able Seamen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Able_Seaman_\(occupation\))

- (0–2) [Ordinary Seamen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_Seaman_\(occupation\))

A [deck cadet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deck_cadet) is a person who is carrying out mandatory sea time to achieve their officer of the watch certificate. Their time on board is spent learning the operations and tasks of everyday life on a merchant vessel.

### Engine department

Further information: [Engine department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engine_department)

A ship's engine department consists of the members of a ship's crew that operate and maintain the propulsion and other systems on board the vessel. Engine staff also deal with the "Hotel" facilities on board, notably the sewage, lighting, air conditioning, and water systems. They deal with bulk fuel transfers, and require training in firefighting and first aid, as well as in dealing with the ship's boats and other nautical tasks- especially with cargo loading/discharging gear and safety systems, though the specific cargo discharge function remains the responsibility of deck officers and deck workers. On [LPG](361384.md) and [LNG](832128.md) [tankers](2506366.md), however, a cargo engineer works with the deck department during cargo operations, as well as being a watchkeeping engineer.

A common engine crew for a ship includes:

- (1) [Chief engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_engineer)

- (1) [Second engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_engineer) / first assistant engineer

- (1) [Third engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_engineer) / second assistant engineer

- (1 or 2) [Fourth engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_engineer) / third assistant engineer

- (0–2) Fifth engineer / junior engineer

- (1–3) [Oiler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oiler_\(occupation\)) (unlicensed qualified rating)

- (0–3) Greaser (unlicensed qualified rating)

- (1–5) Entry-level rating (such as [wiper (occupation)](6704145.md), utilityman, etc.)

Many American ships also carry a [motorman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motorman_\(ship\)). Other possible positions include [machinist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machinist), [electrician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrician), refrigeration engineer, and tankerman. Engine cadets are engineer trainees who are completing sea time necessary before they can obtain a watchkeeping license.

### Steward's department

Further information: [Steward's department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steward's_department)

A typical [steward's department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steward's_department) for a [cargo ship](786056.md) would be composed of a [chief steward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_steward), a [chief cook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_cook), and a [steward's assistant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steward's_assistant). All three positions are typically filled by unlicensed personnel. The chief steward directs, instructs, and assigns personnel performing such functions as preparing and serving meals; cleaning and maintaining officers' quarters and steward department areas; and receiving, issuing, and inventorying stores. On large passenger vessels, the Catering Department is headed by the chief [purser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purser) and managed by assistant pursers. Although they enjoy the benefits of having officer rank, they generally progress through the ranks to become pursers. Under the pursers are the department heads – such as chief cook, head waiter, head barman etc. They are responsible for the administration of their own areas.

The chief steward also plans menus and compiles supply, overtime, and cost control records. They may requisition or purchase stores and equipment. They may bake bread, rolls, cakes, pies, and pastries. A chief steward's duties may overlap with those of the [steward's assistant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steward's_assistant), the chief cook, and other Steward's Department crewmembers.

In the [United States Merchant Marine](307054.md), a chief steward must have a [Merchant Mariner's Document](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merchant_Mariner's_Document) issued by the [United States Coast Guard](32223.md). Because of [international law](8195726.md), conventions, and agreements, all chief cooks who sail internationally are similarly documented by their respective countries.

### Other departments

Further information: [Seafarer's professions and ranks § Other](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seafarer's_professions_and_ranks#Other)

Staff officer positions on a ship, including [junior assistant purser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junior_assistant_purser?action=edit\&redlink=1), [senior assistant purser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senior_assistant_purser?action=edit\&redlink=1), [purser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purser), [chief purser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_purser?action=edit\&redlink=1), medical doctor, professional nurse, [marine physician sssistant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_physician_sssistant?action=edit\&redlink=1), and [hospital corpsman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hospital_corpsman), are considered administrative positions and are therefore regulated by certificates of tegistry issued by the [United States Coast Guard](32223.md). [Pilots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harbour_pilot) are also merchant marine officers and are licensed by the Coast Guard. Formerly, there was also a radio department, headed by a chief radio officer and supported by a number of radio officers. Since the introduction of GMDSS (Satellite communications) and the subsequent exemptions from carrying radio officers if the vessel is so equipped, this department has fallen away, although many ships do still carry specialist radio officers, particularly passenger vessels. Many radio officers became 'electro-technical officers', and transferred into the engine department.

### Life at sea

Mariners spend much of their life beyond the reach of land. They sometimes face dangerous conditions at sea or on lakes – the fishing port of [Gloucester, Massachusetts](116744.md) has a seaside memorial listing over 10,000 fishermen who lost their lives to the sea, and the [Great Lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Lakes) have seen over 10,000 lost vessels since the 1800s.&#x20;

Findings by the Seafarer's International Research Center indicate a leading cause of mariners leaving the industry is "almost invariably because they want to be with their families." U.S. merchant ships typically do not allow family members to accompany seafarers on voyages. Industry experts increasingly recognize isolation, stress, and fatigue as occupational hazards. Advocacy groups such as International Labour Organization, a United Nations agency, and the Nautical Institute are seeking improved international standards for mariners.[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11] [Satellite phones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_phone) have improved communication and efficiency aboard sea-faring ships. This technology has contributed to crew welfare, although both equipment and fees are expensive.[^cite_note-12]

Ocean voyages are steeped in routine. Maritime tradition dictates that each day be divided into six four-hour periods. Three groups of watch keepers from the engine and deck departments work four hours on then have eight hours off watch keeping. However, there are many overtime jobs to be done daily. This cycle repeats endlessly, 24 hours a day while the ship is at sea. Members of the steward department typically are day workers who put in at least eight-hour shifts. Operations at sea, including repairs, safeguarding against [piracy](50715.md), securing cargo, [underway replenishment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underway_replenishment), and other duties provide opportunities for overtime work. Service aboard ships typically extends for months at a time, followed by protracted shore leave. However, some seamen secure jobs on ships they like and stay aboard for years.

The quick turnaround of many modern ships, spending only a few hours in port, limits a seafarer's free-time ashore. Moreover, some foreign seamen entering U.S. ports from a watch list of 25 countries face restrictions on shore leave due to [maritime security](8111405.md) concerns. However, shore leave restrictions while in U.S. ports impact American seamen as well. For example, the [International Organization of Masters, Mates & Pilots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Organization_of_Masters,_Mates_&_Pilots) notes a trend of U.S. shipping terminal operators restricting seamen from traveling from the ship to the terminal gate. Furthermore, in cases where transit is allowed, special "security fees" are at times assessed.

Such restrictions on shore leave, coupled with reduced time in port, translate into longer periods at sea. Mariners report that extended periods at sea living and working with shipmates, who for the most part are strangers, takes getting used to. At the same time, there is an opportunity to meet people from other ethnic and cultural backgrounds. Recreational opportunities have improved aboard some U.S. ships, which may feature gyms and day rooms for watching movies, swapping [sea stories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nautical_fiction), and other activities. And in some cases, especially tankers, it is possible for a mariner to be accompanied by members of his family. However, a mariner's off-duty time is largely a solitary affair, pursuing hobbies, reading, writing letters, and sleeping.

On modern ocean-going vessels, typically registered with a [flag of convenience](402049.md), life has changed immensely in the last 20 years. Most large vessels include a gym and often a swimming pool for use by the crew. Since the [*Exxon Valdez* incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exxon_Valdez_oil_spill), the focus of leisure time activity has shifted from having officer and crew bars, to simply having lounge-style areas where officers or crew can sit to watch movies. With many companies now providing TVs and DVD players in cabins, and enforcing strict smoking policies, it is not surprising that the bar is now a much quieter place on most ships. In some instances games consoles are provided for the officers and crew. The officers enjoy a much higher standard of living on board ocean-going vessels.

Crews are generally poorly paid, poorly qualified and have to complete contracts of approximately 9 months before returning home on leave. They often come from countries where the average industrial wage is still very low, such as the Philippines or India. Officers however, come from all over the world and it is not uncommon to mix the nationality of the officers on board ships. Officers are often the recipients of university degrees and have completed vast amounts of training to reach their rank. Officers benefit e.g. by having larger, more comfortable cabins and table service for their meals.

Contracts average at the 4-month mark for officers, with generous leave. Most ocean-going vessels now operate an unmanned engine room system allowing engineers to work days only. The engine room is computer controlled by night, although the duty engineer will make inspections during unmanned operation. Engineers work in a hot, humid, noisy atmosphere. Communication in the engine room is therefore by hand signals and lip-reading, and good teamwork often stands in place of any communication at all.

## Infrastructure

Further information: [Port](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port)



Container port facilities in [Newark Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newark_Bay), seen from [Bayonne, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayonne,_New_Jersey), United States

For a port to efficiently send and receive cargo, it requires infrastructure: docks, bollards, pilings, cranes, bulk cargo handling equipment, and so on – equipment and organization supporting the role of the facilities. From pier to pier these may differ, one dock handling [intermodal transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermodal_freight_transport) needs (container-ships linked to rail by cranes); another bulk handling capabilities (such as conveyors, elevators, tanks, pumps) for loading and unloading bulk cargoes like grain, coal, or fuels. Others may be outfitted as passenger terminals or for mixed mode operations.

Generally, [Harbors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harbor), [seaports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seaport) and [marinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marina) all host watercraft, and consist of components such as [piers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pier), [wharfs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wharf), [docks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dock_\(maritime\)) and [roadsteads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roadstead).

## Environmental impact

Main article: [Environmental impact of shipping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_of_shipping)

The environmental impact of shipping includes [greenhouse gas emissions](9528025.md), [acoustic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_of_shipping#Sound_pollution), and [oil pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_pollution).[^cite_note-Walker_et_al_2019_Environmental_Effects_of_Marine_Transportation-13] The [International Maritime Organization](14986.md) (IMO) estimates that [Carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) emissions from shipping were equal to 2.2% of the global human-made emissions in 2012[^cite_note-14] and expects them to rise 50 to 250 percent by 2050 if no action is taken.[^cite_note-15] The IEA forecasts that ammonia will meet approximately 45% of shipping fuel demands by 2050.[^cite_note-16]

## Innovation and development

The global economy relies heavily on maritime transportation. Maritime transport accounts for over 80% of international trade, according to [UNCTAD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Conference_on_Trade_and_Development) in 2021, and the percentage is even higher for most developing countries.[^cite_note-review-17] In its Review of Maritime transport 2024, UNCTAD, points out that «  the sector, whose greenhouse gas emissions have risen 20% over the last decade, operates an ageing fleet that runs almost exclusively on fossil fuels ». Besides, maritime trade is expected to grow 2.4% in 2023 and more than 2% between 2024 and 2028.[^cite_note-review-17]



Technology trends in Sea transportation: patent landscape in maritime transport

Two significant megatrends are observed in terms of maritime transport innovation, sustainability and digitalization. A report published by [WIPO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Intellectual_Property_Organization) in 2025 show a steady increase of [patents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patent) publication in maritime transportation, the majority of which being related to sustainable [propulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propulsion), followed by communication and security.[^cite_note-modalities-18]

Transitioning to more sustainable forms of propulsion will help reach [decarbonization](2119179.md) targets. Technological breakthroughs are advancing low and [zero-emission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_emission) solutions, including more sustainable carbon-based fuels, hydrogen, methanol, ammonia, electric propulsion, and optimizing energy consumption through efficient [ship design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_architecture). However, according to the report, scaling up production and ensuring the cost-effectiveness of carbon-neutral fuels remains a challenge.[^cite_note-modalities-18]

Between 2000 and 2023, the number of published patent families in sustainable propulsion increased annually from less than 300 to more than 2,800. In this field, the most important area in terms of patent families is efficient ship design. This includes research activities focused on optimizing hull shape so as to minimize drag, designing efficient propellers or introducing air bubbles under the [hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hull_\(watercraft\)). China is the leader in efficient ship design, [batteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_battery) and electric propulsion, and South Korea has published the most patent families in hydrogen/[fuel cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_cell) research and [sustainable fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_energy). Another important research area is sustainable [carbon-based fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon-based_fuel). The vast majority of patents in this area relate to [liquified natural gas](832128.md) (LNG) fuels for ships.[^cite_note-modalities-18]

Communication and Security technologies are making [ships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship) smarter and safer, with key research areas including navigation, device-to-device communication, low-latency communications, [cloud](19541494.md) platforms, and [cybersecurity](7398.md). Piracy and terror attacks remain a threat in international shipping, with global piracy and armed robbery incidents having increased in 2023 compared to 2022.[^cite_note-19]

Automation and Circularity technologies could boost productivity and enable more energy-efficient ships, with key research themes including efficient material use, smart production and robotics technology, and recycling, but represent a very small area of patenting in the last decade.

[Human-Machine-Interface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/User_interface) technologies are emerging as useful tools to improve the way we interact with ships. However, all HMI technologies are still in the early stages of development with little adoption in shipping. Research areas include extended reality technologies, [speech recognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speech_recognition) technology, [facial recognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facial_recognition_system), and touch displays.[^cite_note-modalities-18]



Map of the main shipping routes, choke points, straits, transoceanic canals and the largest container ports (in 2018)

Over 70% of all Sea transport patents come from Asia, led by China (36%), South Korea (25%) and Japan (9%). China's growth has been on an upward trajectory since 2016, whereas the increase from the South Korea started earlier in 2010 but since 2012 annual filings from Korean inventors have remained relatively stable. Denmark is the most dynamic European nation, followed by Finland, and France, although absolute patent numbers remain relatively small. Only 14% of patents are from Europe and 11% from North America. Top patents owners are companies or universities, such as [Harbin Engineering University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harbin_Engineering_University), [Mitsubishi Heavy Industries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_Heavy_Industries), [Wuhan University of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wuhan_University_of_Technology), [Gaztransport & Technigaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaztransport_&_Technigaz), or [Dalian Maritime University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalian_Maritime_University).[^cite_note-modalities-18]
