# Casalis

&#x20;In the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), a ***casalis*** or ***casale*** ([Medieval Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Latin) and [Italian](14708.md); [Old French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_French) and [Old Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Spanish) *casal*), plural ***casalia*** (*casali*, *casales*), was "a cluster of houses in a rural setting".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoas2010364-1] The word is not [classical Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Latin), but derives from the Latin word *[casa](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/casa#Latin)*, meaning "house". The term originated in western Europe and was also employed in the [Crusader states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusader_states). Depending on the situation, the terms *[feudum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feudum)*, *[villa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villa)* and *[locum suburbanum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suburb)* could be synonyms.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoas2010364-1] The word *casale* came into use in the eighth century to refer to an isolated rural tenement or [demesne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demesne).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin200231–32-2]

## Italy

The *casale* was the basic village unit in [Tuscany](21967242.md) from the tenth century on. They were highly discrete and stable units. During the eleventh century, churches (both public and private) proliferated and by the twelfth each *casale* seems to have had one, which probably fostered social cohesion and identity.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWickham1988175–76-3]

In the eleventh century, the [Norman conquest of southern Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_conquest_of_southern_Italy) brought disruption to settlement patterns. At the same time *casalia* begin appear in peninsular southern Italy. They were "interstitial" sites, located between walled villages and fortified towns, and being either undefended or protected by at most a ditch. The term *casale* was also used in Latin documents to refer to the small rural settlements of [Islamic Sicily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emirate_of_Sicily), called *manzil* or *raḥl* in Arabic.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin200231–32-2]

In some cases, the establishment of *casalia* was undertaken by noblemen seeking to claim new land by resettling vacant areas. Successful *casalia* could grow to become *[castra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castra)* (castles) or even walled towns with suburbs of their own.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin200231–32-2] The established towns, however, successfully asserted their jurisdiction over *casalia* in their vicinity. The *casalia* only ceased to exist in the 18th and 19th centuries, when their inhabitants, the *casalini*, sought and received their own administrations.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMarin2001319–29-4]

## Crusader states

In the Crusader states in the Levant (1098–1291), the *casale* was the basic unit of rural settlement. There were about 600 in the [Kingdom of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Jerusalem), almost all of them bearing names of local origin. Most probably corresponded to previously existing divisions.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPreston19035–10-5] In the Kingdom of Jerusalem, the *[curtile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtile)*, an isolated farmstead, was rare. The *casalia* could have European, local Christian or Muslim inhabitants, and at least one is recorded as being inhabited by [Samaritans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaritans). The smallest had just a few houses, while the largest were practically towns, although they lacked municipal institutions. Each had a [manor house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manor_house) and a church, while most possessed common [mills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mill_\(grinding\)), ovens, [cisterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cistern), [dovecotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dovecote), [threshing floors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threshing_floor), [crofts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croft_\(land\)) and [pastures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasture).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoas2010364-1] Some were associated with [vineyards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vineyard), [springs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_\(hydrology\)), [Bedouins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bedouins) and even defensive towers.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPreston19035–10-5] The inhabitants were called [villeins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villein) (*villani* or *rustici*) and each possessed a house and one or two *[carrucae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carruca)*, the basic unit of arable land.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoas2010364-1]

In the Levant, villeins were typically free (i.e. non-[servile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serf)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoas2010364-1] In practice, native villeins were tied to the land could not leave, and all villeins were required to use the communal installations, which belonged to the lord. Each *casale* had a [headman](3121665.md), called a *[raʾīs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ra%CA%BE%C4%ABs)* in Arabic (*raicius* in Latin), elected by the families (*ḥamāyil*, singular *ḥamūla*). There was sometimes more than one *raʾīs*. He was an intermediary, representing the villeins to their usually absentee landlord and representing the lord to his fellow villeins. All administration was in the hands of the *raʾīs*, who supervised farming, collected taxes, administered justice and mediated disputes. He may have been assisted by a [dragoman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragoman) (which office was often hereditary) and sometimes a [scribe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scribe) (*scribanus*).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoas201762-6]
