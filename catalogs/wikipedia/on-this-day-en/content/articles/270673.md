**Sterling** ([symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound_sign): **£**; [code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_4217): **GBP**) is the [currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency) of the [United Kingdom](31717.md) and nine of its [dependent territories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dependent_territories_of_the_United_Kingdom)[^cite_note-12] and formerly of the [Kingdom of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_England). The **pound** is the base [unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_of_account) of sterling,[^cite_note-Can_Ox_D-13][^cite_note-16] and the word *[pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound_\(currency\))* is also used to refer to the British currency generally,[^cite_note-HbIFT-17] often qualified in international contexts as the **British pound** or **pound sterling**.[^cite_note-Can_Ox_D-13] [Since 1971](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_Day), each pound has been divided into 100 [pence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penny) (singular is 'penny').

Sterling is the world's oldest currency in continuous use since its inception.[^cite_note-Rendall-18] In 2025, it was the fourth-most-traded currency in the [foreign exchange market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_exchange_market), after the [United States dollar](18717338.md), the [euro](9472.md), and the [Japanese yen](34392.md).[^cite_note-19] Together with those three currencies and the [renminbi](66958.md), it forms the [basket of currencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basket_of_currencies) that [calculate the value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_drawing_rights#Value_definition) of [IMF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IMF) [special drawing rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_drawing_rights). As of early 2026, sterling is also the fourth  most-held [reserve currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reserve_currency) in [global reserves](1015869.md).[^cite_note-IMFCOFER-20]

The [Bank of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_England) is the [central bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_bank) for sterling, issuing its [own banknotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_England_note_issues) (as used in England and Wales) and regulating issuance of banknotes [by private banks in Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banknotes_of_Scotland), such as the [Bank of Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_Scotland) and [Clydesdale Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clydesdale_Bank), and [in Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banknotes_of_Northern_Ireland), such as by [Danske Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danske_Bank_\(Northern_Ireland\)). Sterling banknotes issued by other jurisdictions are not regulated by the Bank of England; their governments [guarantee convertibility at par](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency_board). Historically, sterling was also used to varying degrees by the colonies and territories of the [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire).

## Names

### Etymology



Penny of [William I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_the_Conqueror), showing the king's head between two stars

&#x20;The basic unit of currency in medieval England was the [silver penny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penny_\(English_coin\)) or sterling, weighing about 1⁄240 of a [tower pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_pound).[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-23] Two hundred and forty of these coins made a "pound of sterlings". This term (shortened to "pound sterling" in later usage) continued to be used in accounting even after the sterling had ceased to circulate. The earliest known instances of the term occur in [Orderic Vitalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orderic_Vitalis)'s 12th-century *Historia Ecclesiastica*, which makes mention of *librae sterilensium*.[^cite_note-oed-24][^cite_note-26]

The origin of the word *sterling* itself is unclear. It first appears in the 11th and 12th centuries, suggesting that it was coined to describe the new, heavier penny introduced by the [Normans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Normans). According to the *[Oxford English Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_English_Dictionary)*, the most plausible theory is that it represents [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English) \**steorling*, meaning "coin with a star" (a pair of stars having appeared on certain Norman pennies).[^cite_note-oed-24] The numismatist [Philip Grierson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Grierson) rejects this, however, noting that the starred penny was only minted between 1077 and 1080 and formed a tiny fraction of the total coinage issued by the Normans. Grierson suggests instead that the first element is \**stēre*, meaning "stout" or "strong". In support of this theory, he notes that the Roman [solidus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solidus) had a name with a very similar meaning and was also introduced to replace a lighter coin.[^cite_note-grierson-27]

An old theory that the sterling was so named because it was introduced to England by [Easterlings](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Easterling), i.e. [Hanseatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanseatic) merchants, is untenable on historical and phonological grounds.[^cite_note-grierson-27]

### Symbol

Main article: [Pound sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound_sign)

The [currency sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency_sign) for the pound unit of sterling is £, which (depending on typeface) may be drawn [with one or two bars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound_sign#Double_bar_style):[^cite_note-BoE-FoI-28] the Bank of England has exclusively used the single bar variant since 1975.[^cite_note-BoEoldnotes-29][^cite_note-30] Historically, a simple capital L (in the historic [black-letter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black-letter) typeface,       L     {\displaystyle {\mathfrak {L}}}  {\displaystyle {\mathfrak {L}}}) placed before the numerals, or an italic *l.* after them, was used in newspapers, books and letters.[^cite_note-31] The [Royal Mint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Mint) was still using this style of notation as late as 1939.[^cite_note-32] Use of the letter ⟨L⟩ for pound derives from medieval Latin documents: "L" was the abbreviation for *[libra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_units_of_weight)*, the [Roman pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_pound) (weight), which in time became an [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_England) unit of weight defined as the [tower pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_pound). A "pound sterling" was literally a tower pound (weight) of [sterling silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sterling_silver).[^cite_note-ViewOfCoinage-33][^cite_note-34] In the British pre-decimal ([duodecimal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duodecimal)) currency system, the term [£sd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C2%A3sd) (or Lsd) for pounds, [shillings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shilling) and [pence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pence) referred to the Roman *libra*, *[solidus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solidus_\(coin\))*, and *[denarius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denarius)*.[^cite_note-britannica1-35]

Notable style guides recommend that the pound sign be used without any abbreviation or qualification to indicate sterling (e.g., £12,000).[^cite_note-36][^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38] The [ISO 4217](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_4217) code "GBP" (e.g., GBP 12,000) may also be seen should disambiguation become necessary.

### Currency code

The [ISO 4217](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_4217) currency code for sterling is "GBP",[^cite_note-39] formed from the [ISO 3166-1 alpha-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_3166-1_alpha-2) code for the United Kingdom ("GB") and the first letter of "pound".[^cite_note-40]  In historical sources and some specialist banking uses, the abbreviation **stg** (in various styles) has been used to indicate sterling.[^cite_note-Leach-41][^cite_note-42] Many stocks on the [London Stock Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Stock_Exchange) are quoted in **penny sterling**, using the unofficial code "GBX".[^cite_note-43]

### Cable

Main article: [Cable (foreign exchange)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cable_\(foreign_exchange\))

The exchange rate of sterling against the [US dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_dollar) is referred to as "cable" in the wholesale [foreign exchange markets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_exchange_market).[^cite_note-44] The origins of this term are attributed to the fact that from the mid-19th century, the sterling/dollar exchange rate was transmitted via transatlantic cable.[^cite_note-45]

### Slang terms&#x20;

Historically almost every British coin had a widely recognised nickname, such as "tanner" for the [sixpence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sixpence_\(British_coin\)) and "bob" for the [shilling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shilling_\(British_coin\)).[^cite_note-UON-46] Since decimalisation these have mostly fallen out of use except as parts of proverbs.

A common[^cite_note-slang-47] slang term for the pound unit is "**quid**" (singular and plural, except in the common phrase "quids in").[^cite_note-48] Its origin is unknown: possible derivations include *[scudo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_scudo)*, the name for a number of currency units used in Italy until the 19th century, introduced by Italian immigrants; or from Latin *quid* via the common phrase *[quid pro quo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quid_pro_quo)*, literally, "what for what", or, figuratively, "An equal exchange or substitution".[^cite_note-49] The term "nicker" (also both singular and plural) may also refer to the pound.[^cite_note-slang-47]

## Crown Dependencies and British Overseas Territories

The currency of all the [Crown Dependencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_Dependencies) ([Guernsey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bailiwick_of_Guernsey), [Jersey](15693.md), [Isle of Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isle_of_Man)) and a third of [British Overseas Territories](207114.md) ([British Antarctic Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Antarctic_Territory);[^cite_note-50][^cite_note-51] [Falkland Islands](7550182.md) and [South Georgia and the South Sandwich Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Georgia_and_the_South_Sandwich_Islands); [Gibraltar](7607314.md); and [Saint Helena, Ascension and Tristan da Cunha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Helena,_Ascension_and_Tristan_da_Cunha))[^cite_note-52] is either sterling or [pegged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency_peg) to sterling at par.

The other British Overseas Territories have a local currency that is [pegged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency_peg) to the [U.S. dollar](18717338.md) or the [New Zealand dollar](161585.md). The [Sovereign Base Areas of Akrotiri and Dhekelia](1025144.md) (in [Cyprus](5593.md)) use the euro.

## Subdivisions and other units

### Decimal coinage

Since [decimalisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimalisation) on [Decimal Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_Day) in 1971, the pound has been divided into 100 pence (denoted on coinage, until 1981, as "new pence"). The symbol for the penny is "p"; hence an amount such as 50p (£0.50) properly pronounced "fifty pence" is often pronounced "fifty pee" /fɪfti piː/. The old sign *d* was not reused for the *new penny* in order to avoid confusion between the two units. A decimal [halfpenny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halfpenny_\(British_decimal_coin\)) (⁠1/2⁠p, worth 1.2 old pennies) was issued until 1984 but was [withdrawn due to inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Withdrawal_of_low-denomination_coins).[^cite_note-53]

Before [decimalisation in 1971](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C2%A3sd#Decimalisation), the pound was divided into 20 [shillings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_shilling_coin), and each shilling into 12 [pence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_penny_sterling), making 240 pence to the pound. The symbol for the shilling was "*s*." –  not from the first letter of "shilling", but from the Latin *[solidus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solidus_\(coin\))*. The symbol for the penny was "*d*.", from the French *denier*, from the Latin *[denarius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denarius)* (the solidus and denarius were Roman coins). A mixed sum of shillings and pence, such as 3 shillings and 6 pence, was written as "3/6" or "3*s*. 6*d*." and spoken as "three and six" or "three and sixpence" except for "1/1", "2/1" etc., which were spoken as "one and a penny", "two and a penny", etc. Five shillings, for example, was written as "5*s*." or, more commonly, "5/–" (five shillings, no pence). Various coin denominations had, and in some cases continue to have, special names, such as [florin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florin_\(British_coin\)) (2/–), [crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_\(British_coin\)) (5/–), [farthing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farthing_\(British_coin\)) (1⁄4*d*), [sovereign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovereign_\(British_coin\)) (£1) and [guinea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinea_\(British_coin\)) (21s, 21/–, £1–1–0 or £1.05 in decimal notation).&#x20;

By the 1950s, the coins of kings [George III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_III), [George IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_IV) and [William IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_IV) had disappeared from circulation, but coins (at least the penny) bearing the head of every British monarch from [Queen Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Victoria) onwards could be found in circulation. Silver coins were replaced by those in [cupro-nickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cupro-nickel) in 1947, and by the 1960s the silver coins were rarely seen. Silver/cupro-nickel sixpences, shillings (from any period after 1816) and [florins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florin_\(British_coin\)) (2 shillings) remained legal tender after decimalisation (as 2½p, 5p and 10p respectively) until 1980, 1990 and 1993 respectively, but are now officially demonetised.[^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55]

The pound was a unit of account in [Anglo-Saxon England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_England). By the ninth century it was equal to 240 silver [pence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_English_penny_\(c._600_%E2%80%93_1066\)).[^cite_note-59]

The accounting [system of dividing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C2%A3sd) one pound into twenty [shillings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shilling), a shilling into twelve pence, and a penny into four [farthings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farthing_\(British_coin\)) was adopted from the [livre carolingienne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Livre_carolingienne) system introduced by [Charlemagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlemagne) to the [Frankish Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francia). The penny was abbreviated to "d" –  from *[denarius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denarius)*, the Roman equivalent of the penny; the shilling to "s" –  from [solidus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solidus) (written with a [long s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_s), ſ, later evolving into a simple [slash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slash_\(punctuation\)), /), another Roman coin; and the pound to "L" (subsequently [£](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C2%A3)) –  from *[Libra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_units_of_weight)*, the Roman pound weight.

The origins of sterling lie in the reign of [King Offa of Mercia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offa_of_Mercia) (757–796), who introduced a "sterling" coin made by physically dividing a [Tower pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_pound) (5,400 grains, 349.9 grams) of silver into 240 parts.[^cite_note-60] In practice, the weights of the coins were not consistent, 240 of them seldom added up to a full pound; there were no shilling or pound coins and these units were used only as an [accounting convenience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_of_account).[^cite_note-61]

[Halfpennies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halfpenny_\(British_pre-decimal_coin\)) and [farthings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farthing_\(English_coin\)) worth 1⁄2 and 1⁄4 penny respectively were also minted, but small change was more commonly produced by cutting up a whole penny.[^cite_note-62]

### Medieval, 1158



Penny of Henry III, 13th century

The early pennies were struck from fine silver (as pure as was available). [In 1158, a new coinage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_English_penny_\(1154%E2%80%931485\)) was introduced by King [Henry II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_II_of_England) (known as the *[Tealby penny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_English_penny_\(1154%E2%80%931485\))*), with a Tower Pound (5,400 grains, 349.9 g) of 92.5% silver minted into 240 pennies, each penny containing 20.82 grains (1.349 g) of fine silver.[^cite_note-History1300-56] Called [sterling silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sterling_silver), the alloy is harder than the 99.9% [fine silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine_silver) that was traditionally used, and sterling silver coins did not wear down as rapidly as fine silver ones.

The introduction of the larger French *[gros tournois](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gros%20tournois)* coins in 1266, and their subsequent popularity, led to additional denominations in the form of [groats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groat_\(coin\)) worth four pence and [half groats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twopence_\(British_pre-decimal_coin\)) worth two pence.[^cite_note-old_denoms-63] A [gold penny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_penny) weighing twice the silver penny and valued at 20 silver pence was also issued in 1257 but was not successful.[^cite_note-64]

The English penny remained nearly unchanged from 800 and was a prominent exception in the progressive debasements of coinage which occurred in the rest of Europe. The Tower Pound, originally divided into 240 pence, devalued to 243 pence by 1279.[^cite_note-240pence-65]

### Edward III, 1351



Edward III noble (80 pence), 1354–55

During the reign of [King Edward III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Edward_III), the introduction of gold coins received from [Flanders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_of_Flanders) as payment for English wool provided substantial economic and trade opportunities but also unsettled the currency for the next 200 years.[^cite_note-History1300-56]<sup>: 41 </sup> The first monetary changes in 1344 consisted of

- English pennies reduced to 20+1⁄4 grains (1.312 g; 0.042 ozt) of sterling silver (or 20.25gr @ 0.925 fine = 18.73 gr pure silver) and

- Gold [double florins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florin_\(English_coin\)) weighing 108 gr (6.998 g; 0.225 ozt) and valued at 6 shillings (or 72 pence).

  [^cite_note-History1300-56]

  &#x20;(or 108gr @ 0.9948 fine = 107.44 gr pure gold).

The resulting gold-silver ratio of 1\:12.55 was much higher than the ratio of 1\:11 prevailing in the Continent, draining England of its silver coinage and requiring a more permanent remedy in 1351 in the form of

- Pennies reduced further to 18 gr (1.2 g; 0.038 ozt) of sterling silver (or 18 @ 0.925 fine = 15.73 gr pure silver) and

- New [gold nobles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_\(English_coin\)) weighing 120 grains (7.776 grams; 0.250 troy ounces) of the finest gold possible at the time (191/192 or 99.48% fine),

  [^cite_note-Noble-66]

  &#x20;(meaning 120gr @ 0.9948 fine = 119.38 gr pure gold) and valued at 6 shillings and 8 pence (80 pence, or 1⁄3rd of a pound). The pure gold-silver ratio was thus 1:(80 × 15.73 / 119.38) = 1\:10.5.

These gold nobles, together with half-nobles (40 pence) and farthings or quarter-nobles (20 pence),[^cite_note-Noble-66] became the first English gold coins produced in quantity.[^cite_note-67]

### Henry IV, 1412

The exigencies of the [Hundred Years' War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hundred_Years'_War) during the reign of [King Henry IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_IV_of_England) resulted in further debasements toward the end of his reign, with the English penny reduced to 15 grains sterling silver (0.899 g fine silver) and the half-noble reduced to 54 grains (3.481 g fine gold).[^cite_note-History1300-56] The gold-silver ratio went down to 40 × 0.899 / 3.481 = 10.3.

After the [French monetary reform of 1425](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_franc#Lancastrian_War,_1425), the gold half-noble (1⁄6th pound, 40 pence) was worth close to one [Livre Parisis (French pound)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_franc#History) or 20 sols, while the silver half-groat (2 pence, fine silver 1.798 g) was worth close to 1 sol parisis (1.912 g).[^cite_note-68] Also, after the Flemish monetary reform of 1434, the new [Dutch florin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_florin) was valued close to 40 pence while the Dutch [stuiver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuiver) (shilling) of 1.63 g fine silver was valued close to 2 pence sterling at 1.8 g.[^cite_note-Vierlander_stuiver-69] This approximate pairing of English half-nobles and half-groats to Continental *livres* and *sols* persisted up to the 1560s.

### Great slump, 1464

The [Great Bullion Famine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Bullion_Famine) and the [Great Slump of the mid-15th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Slump_\(15th_century\)) resulted in another reduction in the English penny to 12 grains sterling silver (0.719 g fine silver) and the introduction of a new [half-angel gold coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angel_\(coin\)) of 40 grains (2.578 g), worth 1⁄6th pound or 40 pence.[^cite_note-History1300-56] The gold-silver ratio rose again to 40 × 0.719⁄2.578 = 11.2. The reduction in the English penny approximately matched those with the French *sol Parisis* and the Flemish *stuiver*; furthermore, from 1469 to 1475 an agreement between England and the [Burgundian Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burgundian_Netherlands) made the English groat (4-pence) mutually exchangeable with the Burgundian *double patard* (or 2-*stuiver*) minted under [Charles the Bold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_the_Bold).[^cite_note-foreign_coins-70][^cite_note-71]

40 pence or 1⁄6th pound sterling made one [Troy ounce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troy_ounce) (480 grains, 31.1035 g) of sterling silver. It was approximately on a par with France's [livre parisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_franc#History) of one French ounce (30.594 g), and in 1524 it would also be the model for a standardised German currency in the form of the [Guldengroschen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guldengroschen), which also weighed 1 German ounce of silver or 29.232 g (0.9398 ozt).[^cite_note-History1300-56]<sup>: 361 </sup>

### Tudor, 1551



Crown (5/–) of Edward VI, 1551

The last significant depreciation in sterling's [silver standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_standard) occurred amidst the 16th century influx of precious metals from the Americas arriving through the [Habsburg Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habsburg_Netherlands). Enforcement of monetary standards amongst [its constituent provinces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provinces_of_the_Netherlands) was loose, spending under King [Henry VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_VIII) was extravagant, and England loosened the importation of cheaper continental coins for exchange into full-valued English coins.[^cite_note-foreign_coins-70][^cite_note-72] All these contributed to [the Great Debasement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Great_Debasement) which resulted in a significant 1⁄3rd reduction in the bullion content of each pound sterling in 1551.[^cite_note-73][^cite_note-History1500-57]

The troy ounce of sterling silver was henceforth raised in price by 50% from 40 to 60 silver pennies (each penny weighing 8 grains sterling silver and containing 0.4795 g (0.01542 ozt) fine silver).[^cite_note-History1500-57] The gold half-angel of 40 grains (2.578 g (0.0829 ozt) fine gold) was raised in price from 40 pence to 60 pence (5 shillings or 1⁄4 pound) and was henceforth known as the [Crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_\(British_coin\)).

Prior to 1551, English coin denominations closely matched with corresponding sol (2*d*) and livre (40*d*) denominations in the Continent, namely:

- Silver; see [farthing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farthing_\(English_coin\)) (1⁄4*d*), [halfpenny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halfpenny_\(British_pre-decimal_coin\)) (1⁄2*d*), [penny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penny_\(English_coin\)) (1*d*), [half-groat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twopence_\(British_pre-decimal_coin\)) (2*d*), and [groat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groat_\(coin\)) (4*d*)

- Gold; see [*1351*](#Edward_III,_1351): 1⁄4 noble (20*d*), 1⁄2 noble (40*d*) and noble or [angel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angel_\(coin\)) (80*d*).

After 1551 new denominations were introduced,[^cite_note-new_denoms-74] weighing similarly to 1464-issued coins but increased in value 1+1⁄2 times, namely:

- In silver: the [threepence (3*d*)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threepence_\(British_coin\)), replacing the half-groat; the [sixpence (6*d*)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sixpence_\(British_coin\)), replacing the groat; and a new [shilling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shilling_\(English_coin\)) or testoon (1s or 1/–).

- In silver or gold: the [crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_\(English_coin\)) (5/– (5s) or 60*d*), replacing the 1⁄2 angel of 40*d*; and the [half crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half_crown_\(British_coin\)) (2/6*d* or 30*d*), replacing the 1⁄4 angel of 20*d*

- And in gold: the new [half sovereign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half_sovereign) (10/–) and [sovereign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovereign_\(British_coin\)) (£1 or 20/–)

The "Elephant and Castle" motif below his head is the symbol of the [Royal African Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_African_Company), Britain's foremost slave trading company.

&#x20;The RAC transported the gold used in the coin from West Africa to England after purchasing it from African merchants in the [Guinea region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinea_region), who in turn sourced it from the [Ashanti Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashanti_Empire).

[^cite_note-76]

The silver basis of sterling remained essentially unchanged until the 1816 introduction of the Gold Standard, save for the increase in the number of pennies in a troy ounce from 60 to 62 (hence, 0.464 g fine silver in a penny). Its gold basis remained unsettled, however, until the gold guinea was fixed at 21 shillings in 1717.

The [guinea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinea_\(coin\)) was introduced in 1663 with 44+1⁄2 guineas minted out of 12 troy ounces of 22-karat gold (hence, 7.6885 g fine gold) and initially worth £1 or 20 shillings. While its price in shillings was not legally fixed at first, its persistent trade value above 21 shillings reflected the poor state of clipped underweight silver coins tolerated for payment. Milled shillings of full weight were hoarded and exported to [the Continent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Continent), while clipped, hand-hammered shillings stayed in circulation (as [Gresham's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gresham's_law) describes).[^cite_note-77]

In the 17th century, English merchants tended to pay for imports in silver but were generally paid for exports in gold. This effect was notably driven by trade with the Far East, as the Chinese insisted on payments for their exports being settled in silver. From the mid-17th century, around 28,000 metric tons (27,600 long tons) of silver were received by China, principally from European powers, in exchange for Chinese tea and other goods. In order to be able to purchase Chinese exports in this period, England initially had to export to other European nations and request payment in silver, until the [British East India Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_East_India_Company) was able to foster the [indirect sale of opium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_opium_in_China) to the Chinese.[^cite_note-78]

Domestic demand for silver bullion in Britain further reduced silver coinage in circulation, as the improving fortunes of the merchant class led to increased demand for tableware. Silversmiths had always regarded coinage as a source of raw material, already verified for fineness by the government. As a result, sterling silver coins were being melted and fashioned into "sterling silverware" at an accelerating rate. An Act of the [Parliament of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_England) in 1697 tried to stem this tide by raising the minimum acceptable fineness on wrought plate from sterling's 92.5% to a new [Britannia silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Britannia_silver) standard of 95.83%. Silverware made purely from melted coins would be found wanting when the silversmith took his wares to the [assay office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assay_office), thus discouraging the melting of coins.

During the time of [Sir Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Isaac_Newton), Master of the [Mint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Mint), the gold guinea was fixed at 21 shillings (£1/1/–) in 1717. But without addressing the problem of underweight silver coins, and with the high resulting gold-silver ratio of 15.2, it gave sterling a firmer footing in gold guineas rather than silver shillings, resulting in a de facto [gold standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_standard). Silver and copper tokens issued by private entities partly relieved the problem of small change until the [Great Recoinage of 1816](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Recoinage_of_1816).[^cite_note-79]

### Establishment of modern currency

The [Bank of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_England) was founded in 1694,[^cite_note-80] followed by the [Bank of Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_Scotland) a year later.[^cite_note-rampant-81] The Bank of England began to issue [banknotes](208286.md) from "the late 1600s".[^cite_note-82] The Bank of Scotland became the first bank in [Europe](9239.md) to issue its own paper currency in the form of banknotes whilst [Pound Scots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound_Scots) was the legal tender currency of the [Kingdom of Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Scotland), making the Bank of Scotland the longest continuous issuer of banknotes in the world.[^cite_note-83]

### Currency of Great Britain (1707) and the United Kingdom (1801)

In the 17th century [Scots currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound_Scots) was [pegged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency_peg) to sterling at a value of £12 Scots = £1 sterling.[^cite_note-84]

In 1707, the kingdoms of [England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_England) and [Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Scotland) merged into the [Kingdom of Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Great_Britain). In accordance with the [Treaty of Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Union), the currency of Great Britain was sterling, with the [pound Scots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound_Scots) soon being replaced by sterling at the pegged value.

In 1801, Great Britain and the [Kingdom of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Ireland) were united to form the [United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_of_Great_Britain_and_Ireland). However, the [Irish pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_pound#First_pound) was not replaced by sterling until January 1826.[^cite_note-85] The conversion rate had long been £13 Irish to £12 sterling. In 1928, six years after the [Anglo-Irish Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Irish_Treaty) restored Irish autonomy within the British Empire, the [Irish Free State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Free_State) established a [new Irish pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_pound#Second_pound), initially pegged at par to sterling.[^cite_note-86]

### Use in the Empire

Main article: [Sterling area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sterling_area)

Sterling circulated in much of the [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire). In some areas it was used alongside local currencies. For example, the gold sovereign was legal tender in Canada despite the use of the [Canadian dollar](101846.md). Several colonies and dominions adopted the pound as their own currency. These included [Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_pound), [Barbados](3455.md),[^cite_note-87] [British West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_West_African_pound), [Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cypriot_pound), [Fiji](10707.md), [British India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_India), the [Irish Free State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_pound), [Jamaica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamaican_pound), [New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_pound), [South Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_African_pound) and [Southern Rhodesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Rhodesia). Some of these retained parity with sterling throughout their existence (e.g. the South African pound), while others deviated from parity after the end of the gold standard (e.g. the Australian pound). These currencies and others tied to sterling constituted the core of the [sterling area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sterling_area).

The original English colonies on [mainland North America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_North_America) were not party to the sterling area because the above-mentioned silver shortage in England coincided with these colonies' formative years. As a result of equitable trade (and rather less equitable piracy), the [Spanish milled dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_milled_dollar) became the most common coin within the English colonies.

### Gold standard



"Shield reverse" sovereign of Queen Victoria, 1842

During the [American War of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_War_of_Independence) and the [Napoleonic Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars), [Bank of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_England) notes were [legal tender](267793.md), and their value floated relative to gold. The Bank also issued silver tokens to alleviate the shortage of silver coins. In 1816, the [gold standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_standard) was adopted officially, with silver coins minted at a rate of 66 shillings to a troy pound (weight) of sterling silver, thus rendering them as "token" issues (i.e. not containing their value in precious metal). In 1817, the [sovereign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovereign_\(British_coin\)) was introduced, valued at 20/–. Struck in 22‑carat gold, it contained 113 grains or 7.32238 g (0.235420 ozt) of fine gold and replaced the [guinea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinea_\(coin\)) as the standard British gold coin without changing the gold standard.

By the 19th century, sterling notes were widely accepted outside Britain. The American journalist [Nellie Bly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nellie_Bly) carried Bank of England notes on [her 1889–1890 trip around the world in 72 days](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Around_the_World_in_Seventy-Two_Days).[^cite_note-bly1890-88] During the late 19th and early 20th centuries, many other countries adopted the gold standard. As a consequence, conversion rates between different currencies could be determined simply from the respective gold standards. £1 sterling was equal to US\$4.87 in the [United States](3434750.md), [Can\$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Can\$)4.87 in [Canada](5042916.md), [ƒ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_guilder)12.11 in [Dutch territories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Empire), [F](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_franc) 25.22 in [French territories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Colonial_Empire) (or equivalent currencies of the [Latin Monetary Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Monetary_Union)), 20[ℳ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E2%84%B3) 43[₰](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pfennig) in [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Empire), [Rbls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruble) 9.46 in [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire) or [K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Hungarian_crown) 24.02 in [Austria-Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria-Hungary). After the [International Monetary Conference of 1867](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Monetary_Conference_\(1867\)) in Paris, the possibility of the UK joining the [Latin Monetary Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Monetary_Union) was discussed, and a [Royal Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Commission) on International Coinage examined the issues,[^cite_note-89] resulting in a decision against joining it.

### First World War: suspension of the gold standard

The gold standard was suspended at the outbreak of [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War) in 1914, with Bank of England and Treasury notes becoming legal tender. Before that war, the United Kingdom had one of the world's strongest [economies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_the_United_Kingdom), holding 40% of the world's overseas investments. But after the end of the war, the country was highly indebted: Britain owed £850 million (about £41.8 billion in 2025)[^cite_note-inflation-UK-90] with interest costing the country some 40% of all government spending.[^cite_note-91] The British government under Prime Minister [David Lloyd George](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Lloyd_George) and Chancellor of the Exchequer [Austen Chamberlain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austen_Chamberlain) tried to make up for the deficit with a deflationary policy, but this only led to the [Depression of 1920–21](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depression_of_1920%E2%80%9321).[^cite_note-92]

By 1917, production of gold sovereigns had almost halted (the remaining production was for collector's sets and other very specific occasions), and by 1920, the silver coinage was debased from its original [.925 fine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fineness) to just .500 fine. That was due to a drastic increase in silver prices from an average 27/6*d*. \[£1.375] per [troy pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troy_pound) in the period between 1894 and 1913, to 89/6*d*. \[£4.475] in August 1920.[^cite_note-93]

### Interwar period: gold standard reinstated

To try to resume stability, a version of the gold standard was reintroduced in 1925, under which the currency was fixed to gold at its pre-war peg, but one could only exchange currency for gold bullion, not for coins. On 21 September 1931, this was abandoned during the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression_in_the_United_Kingdom), and sterling suffered an initial devaluation of some 25%.[^cite_note-94]

Since the suspension of the gold standard in 1931, sterling has been a [fiat currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiat_currency), with its value determined by its continued acceptance in the national and international economy.

### World War II

In 1940, an agreement with the US pegged sterling to the [US dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_dollar) at a rate of £1 = US\$4.03. (Only the year before, it had been US\$4.86.)[^cite_note-95] This rate was maintained through the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War) and became part of the [Bretton Woods system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bretton_Woods_system) which governed post-war exchange rates.

## History (1946–present)

### Bretton Woods

See also: [Economic history of the United Kingdom § 1945–1951: Age of Austerity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_history_of_the_United_Kingdom#1945%E2%80%931951:_Age_of_Austerity), and [Anglo-American loan § Terms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-American_loan#Terms)

Under continuing economic pressure, and despite months of denials that it would do so, on 19 September 1949 the government devalued the pound by 30.5% to US\$2.80.[^cite_note-srplumt-96] The [1949 sterling devaluation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1949_sterling_devaluation) prompted several other currencies to be devalued against the dollar.

In 1961, 1964, and 1966, sterling came under renewed pressure, as speculators were selling pounds for dollars. In summer 1966, with the value of the pound falling in the [currency markets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency_markets), [exchange controls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange_Controls_in_the_United_Kingdom) were tightened by the [Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Wilson) government. Among the measures, tourists were banned from taking more than £50 out of the country in travellers' cheques and remittances, plus £15 in cash;[^cite_note-97] this restriction was not lifted until 1979. Sterling was [devalued by 14.3%](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1967_sterling_devaluation) to £1 = US\$2.40 on 18 November 1967.[^cite_note-srplumt-96][^cite_note-srdev67-98]

### Decimalisation

Main article: [Decimal Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_Day)

Until decimalisation, amounts in sterling were expressed in [pounds, shillings, and pence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pounds,_shillings,_and_pence), with various widely understood notations. The same amount could be stated as 32*s*. 6*d*., 32/6, £1. 12*s*. 6*d*., or £1/12/6. It was customary to specify some prices (for example professional fees and auction prices for works of art) in guineas (abbr: gn. or gns.), although [guinea coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinea_\(British_coin\)) were no longer in use.

Formal parliamentary proposals to decimalise sterling were first made in 1824 when [Sir John Wrottesley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wrottesley,_1st_Baron_Wrottesley), MP for [Staffordshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staffordshire_\(UK_Parliament_constituency\)), asked in the [House of Commons](13828.md) whether consideration had been given to decimalising the currency.[^cite_note-99] Wrottesley raised the issue in the House of Commons again in 1833,[^cite_note-100] and it was again raised by [John Bowring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Bowring), MP for [Kilmarnock Burghs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilmarnock_Burghs_\(UK_Parliament_constituency\)), in 1847[^cite_note-101] whose efforts led to the introduction in 1848 of what was in effect the first decimal coin in the United Kingdom, the florin, valued at one-tenth of a pound. However, full decimalisation was resisted, although the florin coin, re-designated as *ten new pence*, survived the transfer to a full decimal system in 1971, with examples surviving in British coinage until 1993.

[John Benjamin Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Benjamin_Smith), MP for [Stirling Burghs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stirling_Burghs), raised the issue of full decimalisation again in [Parliament](13964.md) in 1853,[^cite_note-102] resulting in the Chancellor of the Exchequer, [William Gladstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Gladstone), announcing soon afterwards that "the great question of a decimal coinage" was "now under serious consideration".[^cite_note-103] A full proposal for the decimalisation of sterling was then tabled in the [House of Commons](13828.md) in June 1855, by [William Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_William_Brown,_1st_Baronet,_of_Richmond_Hill), MP for [Lancashire Southern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Lancashire_\(UK_Parliament_constituency\)), with the suggestion that the pound sterling be divided into one thousand parts, each called a "mil", or alternatively a farthing, as the pound was then equivalent to 960 farthings which could easily be rounded up to one thousand farthings in the new system.[^cite_note-104] This did not result in the conversion of sterling into a decimal system, but it was agreed to establish a [Royal Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Commission) to look into the issue.[^cite_note-105] However, largely due to the hostility to decimalisation of two of the appointed commissioners, [Lord Overstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Overstone) (a banker) and [John Hubbard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hubbard,_1st_Baron_Addington) ([Governor of the Bank of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governor_of_the_Bank_of_England)), decimalisation in Britain was effectively quashed for over a hundred years.[^cite_note-106]

However, sterling was decimalised in various British colonial territories before the United Kingdom (and in several cases in line with William Brown's proposal that the pound be divided into 1,000 parts, called mils). These included [Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Hong_Kong) from 1863 to 1866;[^cite_note-107] [Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Cyprus) from 1955 until 1960 (and continued on the island as the division of the [Cypriot pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cypriot_pound) until 1983); and the [Palestine Mandate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestine_Mandate) from 1926 until 1948.[^cite_note-108]

Later, in 1966, the UK Government decided to include in the [Queen's Speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen's_Speech) a plan to convert sterling into a decimal currency.[^cite_note-109] As a result of this, on 15 February 1971, the UK decimalised sterling, replacing the shilling and the penny with a single subdivision, the *new penny*, which was worth 2.4*d*. For example, a price tag of £1/12/6. became £1.621⁄2p.[^cite_note-110] The word "new" was omitted from coins minted after 1981.

### Free-floating pound



The cost of 1 pound sterling in [United States dollars](18717338.md) 1971–2022

With the breakdown of the [Bretton Woods system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bretton_Woods_system), sterling [floated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating_currency) from August 1971 onwards. At first, it [appreciated](5258706.md) a little, rising to almost US\$2.65 in March 1972 from US\$2.42, the upper bound of the band in which it had been fixed. The [sterling area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sterling_area) effectively ended at this time, when the majority of its members also chose to float freely against sterling and the dollar.

### 1976 sterling crisis

Main article: [1976 sterling crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1976_sterling_crisis)



UK inflation history



UK bonds 1960–2022: the yield on UK Government benchmark ten-year bonds increased to over 15% in the 1970s and early 1980s.

[James Callaghan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Callaghan) became [Prime Minister](24150.md) in 1976. He was immediately told the economy was facing huge problems, according to documents released in 2006 by the [National Archives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_National_Archives_\(United_Kingdom\)).[^cite_note-111] The effects of the failed [Barber Boom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barber_Boom) and the [1973 oil crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_oil_crisis) were still being felt,[^cite_note-112] with [inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stagflation_of_the_1970s) rising to nearly 27% in 1975.[^cite_note-113] Financial markets were beginning to believe the pound was overvalued, and in April that year *[The Wall Street Journal](173070.md)* advised the sale of sterling investments in the face of high taxes, in a story that ended with "goodbye, Great Britain. It was nice knowing you".[^cite_note-114] At the time the [UK Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_Government) was running a budget deficit, and the Labour government's strategy emphasised high public spending.[^cite_note-srplumt-96] Callaghan was told there were three possible outcomes: a disastrous free fall in sterling, an internationally unacceptable siege economy, or a deal with key allies to prop up the pound while painful economic reforms were put in place. The [US Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Government) feared the crisis could endanger [NATO](21133.md) and the [European Economic Community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Economic_Community) (EEC), and in light of this, the [US Treasury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Treasury) set out to force domestic policy changes. In November 1976, the [International Monetary Fund](15251.md) (IMF) announced the conditions for a loan, including deep cuts in [public expenditure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_expenditure).[^cite_note-115]

### 1979–1989

The [Conservative Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Party_\(UK\)) was elected to office in [1979](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1979_United_Kingdom_general_election), on a programme of fiscal austerity. Initially, sterling rocketed, moving above £1 to US\$2.40, as interest rates rose in response to the [monetarist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monetarist) policy of targeting [money supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Money_supply). The high exchange rate was widely blamed for the deep [recession of 1981](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_1980s_recession). Sterling fell sharply after 1980; at its lowest, £1 stood at just US\$1.03 in March 1985, before rising to US\$1.70 in December 1989.[^cite_note-Samson-116]

### Following the Deutsche Mark

In 1988, the [Chancellor of the Exchequer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancellor_of_the_Exchequer), [Nigel Lawson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Lawson), decided that sterling should "shadow" the [Deutsche Mark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsche_Mark) (DM), with the unintended result of a rapid rise in inflation as the economy boomed due to low interest rates.[^cite_note-117]

Following [German reunification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_reunification) in 1990, the reverse held true, as high German borrowing costs to fund Eastern reconstruction, exacerbated by the political decision to convert the [Ostmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_German_mark) to the D–Mark on a 1\:1 basis, meant that interest rates in other countries shadowing the D–Mark, especially the UK, were far too high relative to domestic circumstances, leading to a housing decline and recession.

### Following the European Currency Unit

On 8 October 1990 the Conservative government ([Third Thatcher ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Thatcher_ministry)) decided to join the [European Exchange Rate Mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Exchange_Rate_Mechanism) (ERM), with £1 set at [DM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsche_Mark) 2.95. However, the country was forced to withdraw from the system on "[Black Wednesday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Wednesday)" (16 September 1992) as Britain's economic performance made the exchange rate unsustainable. The event was also triggered by comments by Bundesbank president [Helmut Schlesinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmut_Schlesinger) who suggested the pound would eventually have to be devalued.[^cite_note-118][^cite_note-119]

"Black Wednesday" saw interest rates jump from 10% to 15% in an unsuccessful attempt to stop the pound from falling below the ERM limits. The exchange rate fell to DM 2.20. Those who had argued[^cite_note-120] for a lower GBP/DM exchange rate were vindicated since the cheaper pound encouraged exports and contributed to the economic prosperity of the 1990s.

### Following inflation targets

In 1997, the newly elected [Labour](19279158.md) government handed over day-to-day control of interest rates to the [Bank of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_England) (a policy that had originally been advocated by the [Liberal Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Democrats_\(UK\))).[^cite_note-121] The Bank is now responsible for setting its base rate of interest so as to keep inflation (as measured by the [Consumer Price Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumer_Price_Index_\(United_Kingdom\)) (CPI)) very close to 2% per annum. Should CPI inflation be more than one percentage point above or below the target, the Governor of the Bank of England is required to write an open letter to the [Chancellor of the Exchequer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancellor_of_the_Exchequer) explaining the reasons for this and the measures which will be taken to bring this measure of inflation back in line with the 2% target. On 17 April 2007, annual CPI inflation was reported at 3.1% (inflation of the [Retail Prices Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retail_Prices_Index) was 4.8%). Accordingly, and for the first time, the Governor had to write publicly to the UK Government explaining why inflation was more than one percentage point higher than its target.[^cite_note-122]

### Euro

Main article: [United Kingdom and the euro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_and_the_euro)

In 2007, [Gordon Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Brown), then [Chancellor of the Exchequer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancellor_of_the_Exchequer), ruled out membership in the eurozone for the foreseeable future, saying that the decision not to join had been right for Britain and for Europe.[^cite_note-123]

On 1 January 2008, with the [Republic of Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Cyprus) switching its currency from the [Cypriot pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cypriot_pound) to the euro, the British sovereign bases on Cyprus ([Akrotiri and Dhekelia](1025144.md)) followed suit, making the [Sovereign Base Areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovereign_Base_Areas) the only territory under British sovereignty to officially use the euro.[^cite_note-124]

The government of former Prime Minister [Tony Blair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Blair) had pledged to hold a public referendum to decide on the adoption of the Euro should "[five economic tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_economic_tests)" be met, to increase the likelihood that any adoption of the euro would be in the national interest. In addition to these internal (national) criteria, the UK would have to meet the European Union's economic [convergence criteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergence_criteria) (Maastricht criteria) before being allowed to adopt the euro. The [Conservative and Liberal Democrat coalition government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cameron%E2%80%93Clegg_coalition) (2010–2015) ruled out joining the euro for that parliamentary term.

The idea of replacing sterling with the euro was always controversial with the British public, partly because of sterling's identity as a symbol of British sovereignty and because it would, according to some critics, have led to suboptimal interest rates, harming the British economy.[^cite_note-125] In December 2008, the results of a [BBC](19344654.md) poll of 1,000 people suggested that 71% would vote no to the euro, 23% would vote yes, while 6% said they were unsure.[^cite_note-126] Sterling did not join the Second European Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM II) after the euro was created. Denmark and the UK had opt-outs from entry to the euro. Theoretically, every EU nation but Denmark must eventually sign up.

As a member of the [European Union](9317.md), the United Kingdom could have adopted the [euro](9472.md) as its currency. However, the subject was always politically controversial, and the UK negotiated an opt-out on this issue. Following the [UK's withdrawal from the EU](41688778.md), on 31 January 2020, the Bank of England ended its membership of the [European System of Central Banks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_System_of_Central_Banks),[^cite_note-127] and shares in the [European Central Bank](9474.md) were reallocated to other EU banks.[^cite_note-128]

### Recent exchange rates

Main article: [Economy of the United Kingdom § Exchange rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_the_United_Kingdom#Exchange_rates)



The cost of £1 sterling in [US dollars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_dollar) (from 1990)



The cost of one [Euro](9472.md) in sterling (from 1999)

Sterling and the euro fluctuate in value against one another, although there may be correlation between movements in their respective exchange rates with other currencies such as the US dollar. Inflation concerns in the UK led the Bank of England to raise interest rates in late 2006 and 2007. This caused sterling to appreciate against other major currencies and, with the US dollar depreciating at the same time, sterling hit a 15-year high against the US dollar on 18 April 2007, with £1 reaching US\$2 the day before, for the first time since 1992. Sterling and many other currencies continued to appreciate against the dollar; sterling hit a 26-year high of £1 to US\$2.1161 on 7 November 2007 as the dollar fell worldwide.[^cite_note-129] From mid-2003 to mid-2007, the pound/euro rate remained within a narrow range (€1.45 ± 5%).[^cite_note-130]

Following the [2008 financial crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_financial_crisis), sterling depreciated sharply, declining to £1 to US\$1.38 on 23 January 2009[^cite_note-131] and falling below £1 to €1.25 against the euro in April 2008.[^cite_note-132] There was a further decline during the remainder of 2008, most dramatically on 29 December when its euro rate hit an all-time low at €1.0219, while its US dollar rate depreciated.[^cite_note-133][^cite_note-134] Sterling appreciated in early 2009, reaching a peak against the euro of £1 to €1.17 in mid-July. In the following months sterling remained broadly steady against the euro, with £1 valued on 27 May 2011 at €1.15 and US\$1.65.

On 5 March 2009, the [Bank of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_England) announced that it would pump £75 billion of new [capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_\(economics\)) into the [British economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_economy), through a process known as [quantitative easing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantitative_easing) (QE). This was the first time in the United Kingdom's history that this measure had been used, although the Bank's [Governor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governor_of_the_Bank_of_England) [Mervyn King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mervyn_King,_Baron_King_of_Lothbury) suggested it was not an experiment.[^cite_note-BBC-135]

The process saw the Bank of England creating new money for itself, which it then used to purchase [assets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asset) such as [government bonds](93768.md), secured [commercial paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_paper), or [corporate bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_bond).[^cite_note-CNN1-136] The initial amount stated to be created through this method was £75 billion, although [Chancellor of the Exchequer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancellor_of_the_Exchequer) [Alistair Darling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alistair_Darling) had given permission for up to £150 billion to be created if necessary.[^cite_note-Times-137] It was expected that the process would continue for three months, with results only likely in the long term.[^cite_note-BBC-135] By 5 November 2009, some £175 billion had been injected using QE, and the process remained less effective in the long term. In July 2012, the final increase in QE meant it had peaked at £375 billion, then holding solely UK Government bonds, representing one third of the UK national debt.[^cite_note-BofEAPF-138]

The result of the [2016 UK referendum on EU membership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_UK_referendum_on_EU_membership) caused a major decline in sterling against other world currencies as the future of international trade relationships and domestic political leadership became unclear.[^cite_note-139] The referendum result weakened sterling against the euro by 5% overnight. The night before the vote, sterling was trading at £1 to €1.30; the next day, this had fallen to £1 to €1.23. By October 2016, the exchange rate was £1 to €1.12, a fall of 14% since the referendum. By the end of August 2017 sterling was even lower, at £1 to €1.08.[^cite_note-140] Against the US dollar, meanwhile, sterling fell from £1 to \$1.466 to £1 to \$1.3694 when the referendum result was first revealed, and down to £1 to \$1.2232 by October 2016, a fall of 16%.[^cite_note-141]

In September 2022, following [a 'mini-budget' of tax cuts funded by borrowing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/September_2022_United_Kingdom_mini-budget),[^cite_note-142] sterling's exchange rate reached an all-time low of just over \$1.03.[^cite_note-143]

## Coins

Main article: [Coins of the pound sterling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coins_of_the_pound_sterling)

### Pre-decimal coins

The silver penny (plural: *pence*; abbreviation: *d*) was the principal and often the only coin in circulation from the 8th century until the 13th century. Although some fractions of the penny were struck (see [farthing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farthing_\(British_coin\)) and [halfpenny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halfpenny_\(British_coin\))), it was more common to find pennies cut into halves and quarters to provide smaller change. Very few gold coins were struck, with the [gold penny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_penny) (equal in value to 20 silver pennies) a rare example. However, in 1279, the *[groat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groat_\(coin\))*, worth 4*d*, was introduced, with the half groat following in 1344. 1344 also saw the establishment of a gold coinage with the introduction (after the failed [gold florin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florin_\(English_coin\))) of the *[noble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_\(English_coin\))* worth six shillings and eight pence (6/8*d*) (i.e. 3 nobles to the pound), together with the half and quarter noble. Reforms in 1464 saw a reduction in value of the coinage in both silver and gold, with the noble renamed the *ryal* and worth 10/– (i.e. 2 to the pound) and the *[angel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angel_\(coin\))* introduced at the noble's old value of 6/8*d*.

The reign of [Henry VII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_VII_of_England) saw the introduction of two important coins: the [shilling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shilling) (abbr.: *s*; known as the *testoon*, equivalent to twelve pence) in 1487 and the pound (known as the *[sovereign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovereign_\(British_coin\))*, abbr.: *£* before numerals or "*l*." (lower-case L) after them, equivalent to twenty shillings) in 1489. In 1526, several new denominations of gold coins were added, including the *[crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_\(British_coin\))* and *[half crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half_crown_\(British_coin\))*, worth five shillings (*5/–*) and two shillings and six pence (*2/6*, *two and six*) respectively. [Henry VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_VIII)'s reign (1509–1547) saw a high level of [debasement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debasement) which continued into the reign of [Edward VI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_VI) (1547–1553). This debasement was halted in 1552, and new silver coinage was introduced, including coins for 1*d*, 2*d*, [3d](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threepence_\(British_coin\)), 4*d* and [6*d*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sixpence_\(British_coin\)), 1/–, 2/6*d* and 5/–. In the reign of [Elizabeth I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_I) (1558–1603), silver 3⁄4*d* and 1+1⁄2*d* coins were added, but these denominations did not last. Gold coins included the half-crown, crown, angel, half-sovereign (10/–) and sovereign (£1). Elizabeth's reign also saw the introduction of the horse-drawn screw press to produce the first "milled" coins.

Following the succession of the Scottish King [James VI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_VI) to the English throne, a new gold coinage was introduced, including the *[spur ryal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spur_ryal)* (15/–), the *[unite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unite_\(English_coin\))* (20/–) and the *rose ryal* (30/–). The *[laurel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurel_\(English_coin\))*, worth 20/–, followed in 1619. The first base metal coins were also introduced: [tin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tin) and [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper) [farthings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farthing_\(British_coin\)). Copper [halfpenny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halfpenny_\(British_coin\)) coins followed in the reign of [Charles I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_I_of_England). During the [English Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Civil_War), a number of siege coinages were produced, often in unusual denominations.

Following the restoration of the monarchy in 1660, the coinage was reformed, with the ending of production of hammered coins in 1662. The *[guinea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinea_\(British_coin\))* was introduced in 1663, soon followed by the 1⁄2, 2 and 5 guinea coins. The silver coinage consisted of denominations of 1*d*, 2*d*, 3*d*, 4*d* and 6*d*, 1/–, 2/6*d* and 5/–. Due to the widespread export of silver in the 18th century, the production of silver coins gradually came to a halt, with the half crown and crown not issued after the 1750s, and the 6*d* and 1/– stopping production in the 1780s. In response, copper 1*d* and 2*d* coins and a gold 1⁄3 guinea (7/–) were introduced in 1797. The copper penny was the only one of these coins to survive long.

To alleviate the shortage of silver coins, between 1797 and 1804, the Bank of England counterstamped [Spanish dollars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_dollar) (8 reales) and other [Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_real) and [Spanish colonial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_colonial_real) coins for circulation. A small counterstamp of the King's head was used. Until 1800, these circulated at a rate of 4/9*d* for 8 reales. After 1800, a rate of 5/– for 8 reales was used. The Bank then issued silver tokens for 5/– (struck over Spanish dollars) in 1804, followed by tokens for 1/6*d* and 3/– between 1811 and 1816.

In 1816, a new silver coinage was introduced in denominations of 6*d*, 1/–, 2/6*d* (half-crown) and 5/– (crown). The crown was only issued intermittently until 1900. It was followed by a new gold coinage in 1817 consisting of 10/– and £1 coins, known as the half sovereign and [sovereign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovereign_\(British_coin\)). The silver 4*d* coin was reintroduced in 1836, followed by the 3*d* in 1838, with the 4*d* coin issued only for colonial use after 1855. In 1848, the 2/– *[florin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florin_\(British_coin\))* was introduced, followed by the short-lived [double florin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_florin) in 1887. In 1860, copper was replaced by bronze in the farthing (quarter penny, 1⁄4*d*), halfpenny and penny.

During the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War), production of the sovereign and [half-sovereign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-sovereign) was suspended, and although the [gold standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_standard) was later restored, the coins saw little circulation thereafter. In 1920, the silver standard, maintained at .925 since 1552, was reduced to .500. In 1937, a nickel-brass 3*d* coin was introduced; the last silver 3*d* coins were issued seven years later. In 1947, the remaining silver coins were replaced with [cupro-nickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cupro-nickel), with the exception of [Maundy coinage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maundy_coinage) which was then restored to .925. Inflation caused the farthing to cease production in 1956 and be demonetised in 1960. In the run-up to decimalisation, the halfpenny and half-crown were demonetised in 1969.

British coinage timeline:

- 1968: The first [decimal coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coins_of_the_pound_sterling) were introduced. These were [cupro-nickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cupro-nickel) [5p](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_pence_\(British_decimal_coin\)) and [10p coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/10p_coin) which were the same size as, equivalent in value to, and circulated alongside, the [one shilling coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shilling_\(British_coin\)) and the [florin (two shilling coin)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florin_\(British_coin\)) respectively.

- 1969: The curved equilateral [heptagonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heptagon) cupro-nickel [50p coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifty_pence_\(British_decimal_coin\)) replaced the [ten shilling note](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_shilling_note) (10/–).

- 1970: The [half crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half_crown_\(British_coin\)) (2/6*d*, 12.5p) was [demonetised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demonetised).

- 1971: The decimal coinage was completed when decimalisation came into effect in 1971 with the introduction of the bronze [half new penny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halfpenny_\(British_decimal_coin\)) (1⁄2p), [new penny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_penny) (1p), and [two new pence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_pence_\(British_decimal_coin\)) (2p) coins and the withdrawal of the [(old) penny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penny_\(British_pre-decimal_coin\)) (1*d*) and [(old) threepence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threepence_\(British_coin\)) (3*d*) coins.

- 1980: Withdrawal of the [sixpence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sixpence_\(British_coin\)) (6*d*) coin, which had continued in circulation at a value of 2+1⁄2p.

- 1982: The word "new" was dropped from the coinage and a [20p coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/20p_coin) was introduced.

- 1983: A (round, brass) [£1 coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C2%A31_coin) was introduced.

- 1983: The 1⁄2p coin was last produced.

- 1984: The 1⁄2p coin was withdrawn from circulation.

- 1990: The [crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_\(British_coin\)), historically valued at five shillings (25p), was re-tariffed for future issues as a commemorative coin at £5.

- 1990: A new, smaller [5p coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_pence_\(British_decimal_coin\)) was introduced, replacing the original size that had been the same as the [shilling coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shilling_\(British_coin\)) of the same value that it had in turn replaced. These first generation 5p coins and any remaining old shilling coins were withdrawn from circulation in 1991.

- 1992: A new, smaller [10p coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/10p_coin) was introduced, replacing the original size that had been the same as the [florin or two shilling coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florin_\(British_coin\)) of the same value that it had in turn replaced. These first generation 10p coins and any remaining old florin coins were withdrawn from circulation over the following two years.

- 1992: [1p](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penny_\(British_decimal_coin\)) and [2p](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_pence_\(British_decimal_coin\)) coins began to be minted in [copper-plated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper_electroplating) [steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel) (the original [bronze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze) coins continued in circulation).

- 1997: A new [50p](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifty_pence_\(British_coin\)) coin was introduced, replacing the original size that had been in use since 1969, and the first generation 50p coins were withdrawn from circulation.

- 1998: The [bi-metallic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bi-metallic_coins) [£2 coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C2%A32_coin) was introduced.

- 2007: By now the value of copper in the pre-1992 [1p](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penny_\(British_decimal_coin\)) and [2p](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_pence_\(British_decimal_coin\)) coins (which are 97% copper) exceeded those coins' face value to such an extent that melting down the coins by [entrepreneurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entrepreneur) was becoming worthwhile (with a premium of up to 11%, with smelting costs reducing this to around 4%)—although this is illegal, and the [market value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_value) of copper has subsequently fallen dramatically from these earlier peaks.

- In April 2008, an extensive redesign of the coinage was unveiled. The [1p](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penny_\(British_decimal_coin\)), [2p](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_pence_\(British_decimal_coin\)), [5p](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_pence_\(British_decimal_coin\)), [10p](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_pence_\(British_decimal_coin\)), [20p](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twenty_pence_\(British_coin\)), and [50p](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifty_pence_\(British_coin\)) coins feature parts of the [Royal Shield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_coat_of_arms_of_the_United_Kingdom) on their reverse; and the reverse of the pound coin showed the whole shield. The coins were issued gradually into circulation, starting in mid-2008. They have the same sizes, shapes and weights as those with the old designs which, apart from the round pound coin which was withdrawn in 2017, continue to circulate.

- 2012: The [5p](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_pence_\(British_decimal_coin\)) and [10p](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_pence_\(British_decimal_coin\)) coins were changed from cupro-nickel to [nickel-plated steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel_electroplating).

- 2017: A more secure twelve-sided bi-metallic £1 coin was introduced to reduce forgery. The old round £1 coin ceased to be legal tender on 15 October 2017.

  [^cite_note-newpoundcoin-146]

As of 2020, the oldest circulating coins in the UK are the [1p](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penny_\(British_decimal_coin\)) and [2p](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_pence_\(British_decimal_coin\)) copper coins introduced in 1971. No other coins from before 1982 are in circulation. Prior to the withdrawal from circulation in 1992, the oldest circulating coins usually dated from 1947: although older coins were still legal tender, inflation meant that their silver content was worth more than their face value, so they tended to be [removed from circulation and hoarded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gresham's_law). Before decimalisation in 1971, a handful of change might have contained coins over 100 years old, bearing any of five monarchs' heads, especially in the copper coins.

## Banknotes

Main article: [Banknotes of the pound sterling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banknotes_of_the_pound_sterling)



Selection of current sterling banknotes printed by all banks

The first [sterling notes were issued](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_England_note_issues) by the [Bank of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_England) shortly after its foundation in 1694. Denominations were initially handwritten on the notes at the time of issue. From 1745, the notes were printed in denominations between £20 and £1,000, with any odd shillings added by hand. £10 notes were added in 1759, followed by £5 in 1793 and £1 and £2 in 1797. The lowest two denominations were withdrawn after the end of the [Napoleonic wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_wars). In 1855, the notes were converted to being entirely printed, with denominations of £5, £10, £20, £50, £100, £200, £300, £500 and £1,000 issued.

The [Bank of Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_Scotland) began issuing notes in 1695. Although the [pound Scots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound_Scots) was still the currency of Scotland, these notes were denominated in sterling in values up to £100. From 1727, the [Royal Bank of Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Bank_of_Scotland) also issued notes. Both banks issued some notes denominated in guineas as well as pounds. In the 19th century, regulations limited the smallest note issued by Scottish banks to be the £1 denomination, a note not permitted in England.

With the extension of sterling to Ireland in 1825, the [Bank of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_Ireland) began issuing sterling notes, later followed by other Irish banks. These notes included the unusual denominations of 30/– and £3. The highest denomination issued by the Irish banks was £100.

In 1826, banks at least 65 miles (105 km) from London were given permission to issue their own paper money. From 1844, new banks were excluded from issuing notes in England and Wales but not in Scotland and Ireland. Consequently, the number of private banknotes dwindled in England and Wales but proliferated in Scotland and Ireland. The last English private banknotes were issued in 1921.

In 1914, the [Treasury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HM_Treasury) introduced notes for 10/– and £1 to replace gold coins. These circulated until 1928 when they were replaced by Bank of England notes. Irish independence reduced the number of Irish banks issuing sterling notes to five operating in Northern Ireland. The [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War) had a drastic effect on the note production of the Bank of England. Fearful of mass forgery by the [Nazis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi) (see [Operation Bernhard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Bernhard)), all notes for £10 and above ceased production, leaving the bank to issue only 10/–, £1 and £5 notes. Scottish and Northern Irish issues were unaffected, with issues in denominations of £1, £5, £10, £20, £50 and £100.

Due to repeated devaluations and spiralling inflation the Bank of England reintroduced £10 notes in 1964. In 1969, the 10/– note was replaced by the 50p coin, again due to inflation. £20 Bank of England notes were reintroduced in 1970, followed by £50 in 1981.[^cite_note-147] A £1 coin was introduced in 1983, and Bank of England £1 notes were withdrawn in 1988. Scottish and Northern Irish banks followed, with only the Royal Bank of Scotland continuing to issue this denomination.

UK notes include raised print (e.g. on the words "Bank of England"); watermarks; embedded metallic thread; holograms; and fluorescent ink visible only under [UV lamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UV_lamp). Three printing techniques are involved: [offset litho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offset_litho), [intaglio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intaglio_\(printmaking\)) and [letterpress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letterpress); and the notes incorporate a total of 85 specialized inks.[^cite_note-148]

The Bank of England produces notes named "[giant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_England_%C2%A31,000,000_note)" and "[titan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_England_%C2%A3100,000,000_note)". A giant is a one million pound note, and a titan is a one hundred million pound bank note.[^cite_note-149] Giants and titans are used only within the [banking system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banking_system).[^cite_note-150]

### Polymer banknotes

The [Northern Bank £5 note](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Bank_%C2%A35_note), issued by Northern Ireland's [Northern Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Bank) (now [Danske Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danske_Bank_\(Northern_Ireland\))) in 2000, was the only [polymer banknote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymer_banknote) in circulation until 2016. The Bank of England introduced £5 polymer banknotes in September 2016, and the paper £5 notes were withdrawn on 5 May 2017. A polymer £10 banknote was introduced on 14 September 2017, and the paper note was withdrawn on 1 March 2018. A polymer £20 banknote was introduced on 20 February 2020, followed by a polymer £50 in 2021.[^cite_note-151]

## Monetary policy

As the [central bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_bank) of the United Kingdom which has been delegated authority by the government, the [Bank of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_England) sets the [monetary policy](297032.md) for the British pound by controlling the amount of money in circulation. It has a monopoly on the issuance of banknotes in England and Wales and regulates the amount of banknotes issued by seven authorized banks in Scotland and Northern Ireland.[^cite_note-152] [HM Treasury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HM_Treasury) has reserve powers to give orders to the committee "if they are required in the public interest and by extreme economic circumstances" but such orders must be endorsed by Parliament within 28 days.[^cite_note-153]

Unlike banknotes which have separate issuers in Scotland and Northern Ireland, all British coins are issued by the [Royal Mint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Mint), an independent enterprise (wholly owned by the [Treasury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HM_Treasury)) which also mints coins for other countries.

## Legal tender and national issues



The [British Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Islands) (red) and overseas territories (blue) using sterling or their local issue

[Legal tender](267793.md) in the United Kingdom is defined such that "a debtor cannot successfully be sued for non-payment if he pays into court in legal tender." Parties can alternatively settle a debt by other means with mutual consent. Strictly speaking, it is necessary for the debtor to offer the exact amount due as there is no obligation for the other party to provide change.[^cite_note-Mint_LT-154]

Throughout the UK, £1 and £2 coins are legal tender for any amount, with the other coins being legal tender only for limited amounts. [Bank of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_England) notes are legal tender for any amount in [England and Wales](275187.md), but not in Scotland or Northern Ireland.[^cite_note-Mint_LT-154] (Bank of England 10/– and £1 notes were legal tender, as were Scottish banknotes, during [World War II](32927.md) under the [Currency (Defence) Act 1939](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency_\(Defence\)_Act_1939?action=edit\&redlink=1), which was repealed on 1 January 1946.) [Channel Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_Islands) and [Manx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isle_of_Man) banknotes are legal tender only in their respective jurisdictions, because they are different currencies, set at parity with sterling.[^cite_note-155]

Bank of England, Scottish, Northern Irish, Channel Islands, Isle of Man, Gibraltar, and Falkland banknotes may be offered anywhere in the UK, although there is no obligation to accept them as a means of payment, and acceptance varies. For example, merchants in England generally accept Scottish and Northern Irish notes, but some unfamiliar with them may reject them.[^cite_note-156] However, Scottish and Northern Irish notes both tend to be accepted in Scotland and Northern Ireland, respectively. Merchants in England generally do not accept Jersey, Guernsey, Manx, Gibraltarian, and Falkland notes but Manx notes are generally accepted in Northern Ireland.[^cite_note-157] Bank of England notes are generally accepted in the Falklands and Gibraltar, but for example, Scottish and Northern Irish notes are not.[^cite_note-158] Since all of the notes are denominated in sterling, banks will exchange them for locally issued notes at face value,[^cite_note-159] though some in the UK have had trouble exchanging Falkland Islands notes.[^cite_note-Falklands-160]

Commemorative [£5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_pounds_\(British_decimal_coin\)) and [25p](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_twenty-five_pence_coin) (crown) coins, and [decimal sixpences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sixpence_\(British_coin\)#2016_decimal_sixpence) (6p, not the [pre-decimalisation 6*d*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sixpence_\(British_coin\)), equivalent to 2+1⁄2p) made for traditional wedding ceremonies and Christmas gifts, although rarely if ever seen in circulation, are formally [legal tender](267793.md),[^cite_note-161] as are the [bullion coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bullion_coin) issued by the Mint.

### Pegged currencies

In Britain's [Crown Dependencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_Dependencies), the [Manx pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manx_pound), [Jersey pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jersey_pound), and [Guernsey pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guernsey_pound) are unregulated by the Bank of England and are issued independently.[^cite_note-163] However, they are maintained at a [fixed exchange rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_exchange_rate) by their respective governments, and Bank of England notes have been made legal tender on the islands, forming a sort of one-way de facto [currency union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency_union). Internationally they are considered local issues of sterling so do not have [ISO 4217](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_4217) codes. "GBP" is usually used to represent all of them; informal abbreviations resembling ISO codes are used where the distinction is important.

[British Overseas Territories](207114.md) are responsible for the monetary policy of their own currencies (where they exist),[^cite_note-164] and have their own ISO 4217 codes. The [Falkland Islands pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falkland_Islands_pound), [Gibraltar pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibraltar_pound), and [Saint Helena pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Helena_pound) are set at a fixed 1\:1 exchange rate with the British pound by local governments.

## Value

In 2006, the [House of Commons Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Commons_Library) published a research paper which included an index of prices for each year between 1750 and 2005, where 1974 was indexed at 100.[^cite_note-165]

Regarding the period 1750–1914 the document states: "Although there was considerable year on year fluctuation in price levels prior to 1914 (reflecting the quality of the harvest, wars, etc.) there was not the long-term steady increase in prices associated with the period since 1945". It goes on to say that "Since 1945 prices have risen in every year with an aggregate rise of over 27 times".

The value of the index in 1751 was 5.1, increasing to a peak of 16.3 in 1813 before declining very soon after the end of the [Napoleonic Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars) to around 10.0 and remaining in the range 8.5–10.0 at the end of the 19th century. The index was 9.8 in 1914 and peaked at 25.3 in 1920, before declining to 15.8 in 1933 and 1934—prices were only about three times as high as they had been 180 years earlier.[^cite_note-166]

[Inflation](38286.md) has had a dramatic effect during and after [World War II](32927.md): the index was 20.2 in 1940, 33.0 in 1950, 49.1 in 1960, 73.1 in 1970, 263.7 in 1980, 497.5 in 1990, 671.8 in 2000 and 757.3 in 2005. The smallest coin in 1971 was the 1⁄2p, worth about 6.4p in 2015 prices.
