**India's telecommunication network** is the [second largest in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_mobile_network_operators) by number of telephone users[^cite_note-3] (both fixed and mobile phones) with over 1,337.54 million subscribers as of April 2026.

Major sectors of the Indian telecommunication industry are the telephone, internet, and television broadcast industries in the country which are involved in an ongoing process of developing into a [next-generation network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Next-generation_network), increasingly employing an extensive array of modern network infrastructure such as digital [telephone exchanges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephone_exchange), [network switching subsystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_switching_subsystem), [media gateways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_gateway) and [signaling gateways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signaling_gateway) at the core, interconnected by a wide variety of transmission systems using [optical fiber](3372377.md) or [microwave radio relay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave_transmission) networks. The [access network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Access_network), which connects the subscriber to the core, is highly diversified with different copper-pair, [optical fiber](3372377.md) and wireless technologies. [Satellite television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_television), a relatively new broadcasting technology has attained significant popularity in the Television segment. The introduction of private FM has boosted [radio broadcasting in India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FM_broadcasting_in_India). Telecommunication in India has been greatly supported by the [Indian National Satellite System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_National_Satellite_System) system of the country, one of the largest domestic satellite systems in the world. India possesses a diversified communications system, which links all parts of the country by telephone, Internet, radio, television, and satellite. India's participation in global telecommunications and spectrum policy discussions is supported by the [ITU-APT Foundation of India (IAFI)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITU-APT_Foundation_of_India), a sector member of ITU-R, ITU-T, and ITU-D.[^cite_note-http-4]

The Indian telecom industry underwent a high rate of market liberalisation and growth since the 1990s and has now become the world's most competitive and one of the fastest growing telecom markets.[^cite_note-5][^cite_note-bbcnews.com-6]

Telecommunication has supported the socioeconomic development of India and has played a significant role in narrowing down the rural-urban [digital divide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_divide) to an extent. It has also helped to increase the transparency of governance with the introduction of [e-governance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E-governance) in India. The government has pragmatically used modern telecommunication facilities to deliver mass education programmes for rural communities in India.[^cite_note-Raju_Thomas-7]

According to the London-based telecom trade body [GSMA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GSMA), the telecom sector accounted for 6.5% of India's GDP in 2015, or about ₹9 lakh crore (US\$93 billion), and supported direct employment for 2.2 million people in the country. GSMA estimates that the Indian telecom sector will contribute ₹14.5 lakh crore (US\$150 billion) to the economy and support 3 million direct jobs and 2 million [indirect jobs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct,_indirect,_and_induced_employment) by 2020.[^cite_note-8]

In today's period of progress and wealth, technological modernization is increasingly seen as a foreseen necessity for every country. With better technology and more competition from established businesses, telecommunications has entered a new era of development. The continuous rise of the mobile industry is linked to technological advancements in the telecommunications sector. The service providers' primary goal is to build a loyal customer base by measuring their performance and maintaining existing consumers in order to profit from their loyalty. The purpose of the paper is to address these concerns.[^cite_note-9]

## History

### Beginning



A microwave tower for short distance (\~50 km) communication

Prof. [S. P. Chakravarti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S._P._Chakravarti) is known as the father of electronics and [telecommunications engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunications_engineering) in India.[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11][^cite_note-auto-12] He started electronics and telecommunications education, training and research in India.

Telecommunications in India began with the introduction of [telegraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telegraphy). The Indian postal and telecom sectors are one of the world's oldest. In 1850, the first experimental electric telegraph line was started between [Kolkata](47905.md) and [Diamond Harbour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Harbour).[^cite_note-13] In 1851, it was opened for the use of the [East India Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_India_Company). The Posts and Telegraphs department occupied a small corner of the Public Works Department at that time.[^cite_note-14]

The construction of 4,000 miles (6,400 km) of telegraph lines was started in November 1853. These connected Kolkata (then Calcutta) and [Peshawar](66230.md) in the north; [Agra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agra), [Mumbai](19189.md) (then Bombay) through Sindwa Ghats, and [Chennai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chennai) (then Madras) in the south; [Ooty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ooty) and [Bengaluru](44275267.md). [William O'Shaughnessy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Brooke_O'Shaughnessy), who pioneered the [Telegraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telegraphy) and telephone in India, belonged to the Public Works Department, and worked towards the development of telecom throughout this period. A separate department was opened in 1854 when telegraph facilities were opened to the public.

In 1880, two telephone companies, namely the [Oriental Telephone Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriental_Telephone_Company) Ltd. and the Anglo-Indian Telephone Company Ltd. approached the [Government of India](553883.md) to establish a [telephone exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephone_exchange) in India. Permission was refused on the grounds that the establishment of telephone networking was a government monopoly and that the government itself should undertake the work. In 1881, the government later reversed its earlier decision and a licence was granted to the [Oriental Telephone Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriental_Telephone_Company) Limited of England for opening telephone exchanges at [Kolkata](47905.md), [Mumbai](19189.md), [Chennai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chennai) and [Ahmedabad](206337.md) and the first formal telephone service was established in the country.[^cite_note-ind_telecom_iim-15] On 28 January 1882, Major E. Baring, Member of the [Governor General of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governor_General)'s Council declared open the Telephone Exchanges in Calcutta, Bombay and Madras. The exchange in Calcutta named the "Central Exchange" had a total of 93 subscribers in its early stage. Later that year, Bombay also witnessed the opening of a telephone exchange.[^cite_note-16]

**Development of Broadcasting:** Radio broadcasting was initiated in 1927 but became state responsibility only in 1930. In 1937 it was given the name *[All India Radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_India_Radio)* and since 1957 it has been called *Akashvani*.[^cite_note-Schwartzberg-22] Limited duration of television programming began in 1959, and complete broadcasting followed in 1965. The [Ministry of Information and Broadcasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Information_and_Broadcasting_\(India\)) owned and maintained the audio-visual apparatus—including the television channel *[Doordarshan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doordarshan)*—in the country prior to the [economic reforms of 1991](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_liberalisation_in_India). In 1997, an autonomous body was established in the name of [Prasar Bharti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prasar_Bharti) to take care of the public service broadcasting under the Prasar Bharti Act. All India Radio and Doordarshan, which earlier were working as media units under the Ministry of I\&B became constituents of the body.[^cite_note-Raju_Thomas-7]

**Pre-liberalisation statistics:** While all the major cities and towns in the country were linked with telephones during the [British](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Raj) period, the total number of telephones in 1948 numbered only around 80,000. Post-independence, growth remained slow because the telephone was seen more as a status symbol rather than being an instrument of utility. The number of telephones grew leisurely to 980,000 in 1971, 2.15 million in 1981 and 5.07 million in 1991, the year economic reforms were initiated in the country.

### Liberalisation and privatisation



A mobile phone tower in Leh, Ladakh, India, surrounded by Buddhist prayer flags

The liberalisation of the Indian telecommunications industry started in 1981 when then Prime Minister [Indira Gandhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indira_Gandhi) signed contracts with Alcatel CIT of France to merge with the state owned Telecom Company (ITI), in an effort to set up 5 million telecom lines per year. Soon after the contracts were signed, the policy was left unfufilled due to political opposition.[^cite_note-Dash-23] Attempts to liberalise the telecommunication industry were continued by the following government under the prime-minister-ship of [Rajiv Gandhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rajiv_Gandhi). He invited [Sam Pitroda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Pitroda), a US-based [Non-resident Indian (NRI)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-resident_Indian_and_person_of_Indian_origin) and a former [Rockwell International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockwell_International) executive to set up a [Centre for Development of Telematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C-DOT) (C-DOT) which manufactured electronic telephone exchanges in India for the first time.[^cite_note-Kohli2006-24] Sam Pitroda had a significant role as a consultant and adviser in the development of telecommunication in India.[^cite_note-25]

In 1985, the [Department of Telecom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Telecom) (DoT) was separated from Indian Post & Telecommunication Department. DoT was responsible for telecom services in entire country until 1986 when [Mahanagar Telephone Nigam Limited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahanagar_Telephone_Nigam_Limited) (MTNL) and [Videsh Sanchar Nigam Limited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Videsh_Sanchar_Nigam_Limited) (VSNL) were carved out of DoT to run the telecom services of metro cities ([Delhi](37756.md) and [Mumbai](19189.md)) and international long-distance operations respectively.[^cite_note-Kohli2006-24]

The demand for telephones continued to increase and in the 1990s the Indian government was under mounting pressure to open up the telecom sector for private investment as a part of the [Liberalisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberalisation)-[Privatisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privatisation)-[Globalisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globalisation) policies that the government accepted to overcome the severe fiscal crisis and resultant [balance of payments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balance_of_payments) issue in 1991. Consequently, private investment in the sector of Value Added Services (VAS) was allowed and cellular telecom sector were opened up for competition from private investments. It was during this period that the [Narsimha Rao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narsimha_Rao)-led government introduced the *National Telecommunications policy (NTP)* in 1994 which brought changes in the ownership, service and regulation of the national telecommunications infrastructure. The policy introduced the concept of *telecommunication for all* and its vision was to expand the telecommunication facilities to all the villages in India.[^cite_note-Arasu2008-26] Liberalisation in the basic telecom sector was also envisaged in this policy.[^cite_note-Dossani2002-27] They were also successful in establishing joint ventures between state owned telecom companies and international players. Foreign firms were eligible to 49% of the total stake. The multi-nationals were just involved in technology transfer, and not policy making.[^cite_note-Dash-23]

During this period, the World Bank and [ITU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITU) had advised the Indian Government to liberalise long-distance services to release the monopoly of the state-owned DoT and VSNL and to enable competition in the long-distance carrier business which would help reduce tariff's and better the economy of the country. The Rao run government instead liberalised the local services, taking the opposite political parties into confidence and assuring foreign involvement in the long-distance business after 5 years. The country was divided into 20 telecommunication circles for basic telephony and 18 circles for mobile services. These circles were divided into category A, B and C depending on the value of the revenue in each circle. The government threw open the bids to one private company per circle along with government-owned DoT per circle. For cellular service two service providers were allowed per circle and a 15 years licence was given to each provider. During all these improvements, the government did face oppositions from [ITI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Telephone_Industries), DoT, MTNL, VSNL and other labour unions, but they managed to keep away from all the hurdles.[^cite_note-Dash-23]

In 1997, the government set up TRAI ([Telecom Regulatory Authority of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecom_Regulatory_Authority_of_India)) which reduced the interference of Government in deciding tariffs and policymaking. The political powers changed in 1999 and the new government under the leadership of [Atal Bihari Vajpayee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atal_Bihari_Vajpayee) was more pro-reforms and introduced better liberalisation policies. In 2000, the [Vajpayee government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premiership_of_Atal_Bihari_Vajpayee) constituted the [Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecom_Disputes_Settlement_and_Appellate_Tribunal) (TDSAT) through an amendment of the TRAI Act, 1997.[^cite_note-TDSAT_-_profile-28][^cite_note-TRAI_Act-29] The primary objective of TDSAT's establishment was to release TRAI from adjudicatory and dispute settlement functions in order to strengthen the regulatory framework. Any dispute involving parties like licensor, licensee, service provider and consumers are resolved by TDSAT. Moreover, any direction, order or decision of TRAI can be challenged by appealing in TDSAT.[^cite_note-30] The government corporatised the operations wing of DoT on 1 October 2000 and named it as *Department of Telecommunication Services (DTS)* which was later named as [Bharat Sanchar Nigam Limited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bharat_Sanchar_Nigam_Limited) (BSNL). The proposal of raising the stake of foreign investors from 49% to 74% was rejected by the opposite political parties and leftist thinkers. Domestic business groups wanted the government to privatise VSNL. Finally in April 2002, the government decided to cut its stake of 53% to 26% in VSNL and to throw it open for sale to private enterprises. TATA finally took 25% stake in VSNL.[^cite_note-Dash-23]

This was a gateway to many foreign investors to get entry into the Indian telecom markets. After March 2000, the government became more liberal in making policies and issuing licences to private operators. The government further reduced licence fees for \[cellular service providers and increased the allowable stake to 74% for foreign companies. Because of all these factors, the service fees finally reduced and the call costs were cut greatly enabling every common middle-class family in India to afford a cell phone. Nearly 32 million handsets were sold in India. The data reveals the real potential for growth of the Indian mobile market.[^cite_note-31] Many private operators, such as [Reliance Communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reliance_Communications), [Jio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jio), [Tata Indicom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tata_Indicom), [Vodafone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vodafone_Essar), [Loop Mobile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loop_Mobile_India), [Airtel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airtel_India), [Idea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idea_Cellular) etc., successfully entered the high potential Indian telecom market. In the initial 5–6 years the average monthly subscribers additions were around 0.05 to 0.1 million only and the total mobile subscribers base in December 2002 stood at 10.5 million. However, after a number of proactive initiatives taken by regulators and licensors, the total number of mobile subscribers has increased rapidly to over 929 million subscribers as of May 2012.

In March 2008, the total [GSM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GSM) and [CDMA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CDMA) mobile subscriber base in the country was 375 million, which represented a nearly 50% growth when compared with previous year.[^cite_note-32] As the unbranded Chinese cell phones which do not have [International Mobile Equipment Identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Mobile_Equipment_Identity) (IMEI) numbers pose a serious security risk to the country, [Mobile network operators](481601.md) therefore suspended the usage of around 30 million mobile phones (about 8% of all mobiles in the country) by 30 April 2009. Phones without valid IMEI cannot be connected to cellular operators.[^cite_note-33]

India has opted for the use of both the [GSM (global system for mobile communications)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_System_for_Mobile_Communications) and [CDMA (code-division multiple access)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_division_multiple_access) technologies in the [mobile](19644137.md) sector. In addition to [landline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landline) and mobile phones, some of the companies also provide the [WLL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless_local_loop) service. The mobile tariffs in India have also become the lowest in the world. A new mobile connection can be activated with a monthly commitment of US\$0.15 only.

Decentralisation has been the new push by the government through PM WANI scheme launched in Dec 2020 to push Internet penetration above the current 50% threshold into smaller towns and villages.[^cite_note-34] This opens up opportunities for multiple small and medium scale local business models[^cite_note-35] as well as decentralized solutions using [Blockchain](36662188.md).

### Licence cancellation

Main article: [2G spectrum case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2G_spectrum_case)

On 2 February 2012, the Supreme Court ruled on petitions filed by [Subramanian Swamy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subramanian_Swamy) and the Centre for Public Interest Litigation (CPIL) represented by [Prashant Bhushan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prashant_Bhushan), challenging the 2008 allotment of 2G licenses,[^cite_note-license-36] cancelling all 122 spectrum licences granted during [A. Raja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._Raja)'s ([Minister of Communications & IT from 2007 to 2009](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Communications_and_Information_Technology_\(India\))) term as communications minister,[^cite_note-license-36] and described the allocation of 2G spectrum as "unconstitutional and arbitrary".[^cite_note-37] The bench of [GS Singhvi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GS_Singhvi) and [Asok Kumar Ganguly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asok_Kumar_Ganguly) imposed a fine of ₹50 million (US\$520,000) on Unitech Wireless, Swan Telecom and Tata Teleservices and a ₹5 million (US\$52,000) fine on [Loop Telecom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loop_Telecom), [S Tel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S_Tel), Allianz Infratech and [Sistema Shyam Tele Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MTS_India).[^cite_note-SC_quashes_122_licences-38] According to the ruling, the then-granted licences would remain in place for four months, after which time the government would reissue the licences.[^cite_note-39]

### Consolidation

Post starting of the commercial operation of [Reliance Jio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reliance_Jio) in September 2016, the telecom market saw a huge change in terms of falling tariff rates and reduction of data charges, which changed the economics for some of the telecom players. This resulted in exit of many smaller players from the market. Players like Videocon and Systema sold their spectrum under spectrum trading agreements to Airtel and RCOM respectively in Q4 2016.

On 23 February 2017, [Telenor India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telenor_India) announced that [Bharti Airtel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bharti_Airtel) will take over all its business and assets in India and deal will be completed in 12 months timeframe.[^cite_note-40] On 14 May 2018, [Department of Telecom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Telecommunications) approved the merger of [Telenor India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telenor_India) with Bharti Airtel paving the way for final commercial closing of the merger between the two companies.[^cite_note-41] [Telenor India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telenor_India) has been acquired by Airtel almost without any cost.

On 12 October 2017, [Bharti Airtel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bharti_Airtel) announced that it would acquire the consumer mobile businesses of [Tata Teleservices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tata_Teleservices) Ltd (TTSL) and Tata Teleservices Maharastra Ltd (TTML) in a debt-free cash-free deal. The deal was essentially free for [Airtel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airtel_India) which incurred TTSL's unpaid spectrum payment liability. TTSL will continue to operate its enterprise, fixed line and broadband businesses and its stake in tower company Viom Networks.[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44] The consumer mobile businesses of Tata Docomo, Tata Teleservices (TTSL) and Tata Teleservices Maharashtra Limited (TTML) have been merged into [Bharti Airtel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bharti_Airtel) from 1 July 2019[^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46][^cite_note-47]

[Reliance Communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reliance_Communications) had to shut down its [2G](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2G) and [3G](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3G) services including all voice services and only offer [4G](486547.md) data services from 29 December 2017, as a result of debt and a failed merger with [Aircel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircel).[^cite_note-48][^cite_note-49] Surprisingly, the shut down was shortly after completion of acquisition of [MTS India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MTS_India) on 31 October 2017.[^cite_note-50][^cite_note-51] In February 2019, the company filed for bankruptcy as it was unable to sell assets to repay its debt.[^cite_note-52] It has an estimated debt of ₹ 57,383 crore against assets worth ₹18,000 crore.[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-54]

## Next-generation networks (NGN)

&#x20;Historically, the role of telecommunication has evolved from that of plain information exchange to a multi-service field, with *Value Added Services (VAS)* integrated with various discrete networks like [PSTN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_switched_telephone_network), [PLMN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_land_mobile_network), Internet Backbone etc. However, with decreasing [average revenue per user](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Average_revenue_per_user) and increasing demand for VAS has become a compelling reason for the service providers to think of the convergence of these parallel networks into a single core network with service layers separated from network layer.[^cite_note-TRAINGN-93] [Next-generation networking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Next-generation_network) is such a convergence concept which according to [ITU-T](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITU-T) is:[^cite_note-94]

> A next-generation network (NGN) is a [packet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_packet)-based [network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_network) which can provide services including Telecommunication Services and able to make use of multiple broadband, [quality of service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quality_of_service)-enabled transport technologies and in which service-related functions are independent from underlying transport-related technologies. It offers unrestricted access by users to different service providers. It supports generalised mobility which will allow the consistent and ubiquitous provision of services to users.

Access network: The user can connect to the IP-core of NGN in various ways, most of which use the standard Internet Protocol (IP). User terminals such as mobile phones, [personal digital assistants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_digital_assistant) (PDAs) and computers can register directly on NGN-core, even when they are [roaming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roaming) in another network or country. The only requirement is that they can use IP and [Session Initiation Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Session_Initiation_Protocol) (SIP). Fixed access (e.g., [digital subscriber line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_subscriber_line) (DSL), [cable modems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cable_modem), [Ethernet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethernet)), mobile access (e.g. [UMTS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UMTS), [CDMA2000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CDMA2000), GSM, [GPRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Packet_Radio_Service)) and wireless access (e.g.[WLAN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless_LAN), [WiMAX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WiMAX)) are all supported. Other phone systems like [plain old telephone service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plain_old_telephone_service) and non-compatible [VoIP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voice_over_IP) systems, are supported through [gateways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gateway_\(telecommunications\)). With the deployment of the NGN, users may subscribe to many simultaneous access-providers providing telephony, internet or entertainment services. This may provide end-users with virtually unlimited options to choose between service providers for these services in NGN environment.[^cite_note-TRAINGN-93]

The hyper-competition in the telecom market, which was effectively caused by the introduction of *Universal Access Service (UAS)* licence in 2003 became much tougher after 3G and 4G [competitive auction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Telecom_Spectrum_Auction). About 670,000 route-kilometres (420,000 mi) of [optical fibre](3372377.md) has been laid in India by the major operators, including in the financially nonviable rural areas and the process continues. Keeping in mind the viability of providing services in rural areas, the government of India also took a proactive role to promote the NGN implementation in the country; an expert committee called *NGN eCO* was constituted in order to deliberate on the licensing, interconnection and quality of service (QoS) issues related to NGN and it submitted its report on 24 August 2007. Telecom operators found the NGN model advantageous, but huge investment requirements have prompted them to adopt a multi-phase migration and they have already started the migration process to NGN with the implementation of IP-based core-network.[^cite_note-TRAINGN-93]

## Regulatory environment

[LIRNEasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LIRNEasia)'s Telecommunications Regulatory Environment (TRE) index, which summarises stakeholders' perception on certain TRE dimensions, provides insight into how conducive the environment is for further development and progress. The most recent survey was conducted in July 2008 in eight Asian countries, including Bangladesh, India, Indonesia, Sri Lanka, Maldives, Pakistan, Thailand, and the Philippines. The tool measured seven dimensions: i) market entry; ii) access to scarce resources; iii) interconnection; iv) tariff regulation; v) anti-competitive practices; and vi) universal services; vii) quality of service, for the fixed, mobile and broadband sectors.

The results for India, point out to the fact that the stakeholders perceive the TRE to be most conducive for the mobile sector followed by fixed and then broadband. Other than for Access to ScarceResources the fixed sector lags behind the mobile sector. The fixed and mobile sectors have the highest scores for Tariff Regulation. Market entry also scores well for the mobile sector as competition is well entrenched with most of the circles with 4–5 mobile service providers. The broadband sector has the lowest score in the aggregate. The low penetration of broadband of mere 3.87 against the policy objective of 9 million at the end of 2007 clearly indicates that the regulatory environment is not very conducive.[^cite_note-lirneasia_tre-95]

In 2013 the [home ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Home_Affairs_\(India\)) stated that legislation must ensure that law enforcement agencies are empowered to intercept communications.[^cite_note-96]

## S-band spectrum scam

In India, [electromagnetic spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_spectrum), being a scarce resource for wireless communication, is auctioned by the Government of India to telecom companies for use. As an example of its value, in 2010, 20 [Hertz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hertz) of [3G](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3G) spectrum was [auctioned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Telecom_Spectrum_Auction) for ₹677 billion (US\$7.0 billion). This part of the spectrum is allocated for terrestrial communication (cell phones). However, in January 2005, Antrix Corporation (commercial arm of ISRO) signed an agreement with Devas Multimedia (a private company formed by former ISRO employees and venture capitalists from USA) for lease of [S band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S_band) transponders (amounting to 70 MHz of spectrum) on two ISRO satellites (GSAT 6 and GSAT 6A) for a price of ₹14 billion (US\$150 million), to be paid over a period of 12 years. The spectrum used in these satellites (2500 MHz and above) is allocated by the [International Telecommunication Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Telecommunication_Union) specifically for satellite-based communication in India. Hypothetically, if the spectrum allocation is changed for utilisation for terrestrial transmission and if this 70 MHz of spectrum were sold at the 2010 auction price of the 3G spectrum, its value would have been over ₹2,000 billion (US\$21 billion). This was a hypothetical situation. However, the [Comptroller and Auditor General of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comptroller_and_Auditor_General_of_India) considered this hypothetical situation and estimated the difference between the prices as a loss to the Indian Government.[^cite_note-Thakur,_Pradeep-97][^cite_note-Behind_the_S-band_spectrum_scandal-98]

There were lapses on implementing Government of India procedures. Antrix/ISRO had allocated the capacity of the above two satellites to Devas Multimedia on an exclusive basis, while rules said it should always be non-exclusive. The [Union Council of Ministers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_Council_of_Ministers) was misinformed in November 2005 that several service providers were interested in using satellite capacity, while the Devas deal was already signed. Also, the Space Commission was kept in the dark while taking approval for the second satellite (its cost was diluted so that Cabinet approval was not needed). ISRO committed to spending ₹7.66 billion (US\$80 million) of public money on building, launching, and operating two satellites that were leased out for Devas.

In late 2009, some ISRO insiders exposed information about the Devas-Antrix deal,[^cite_note-Behind_the_S-band_spectrum_scandal-98][^cite_note-99] and the ensuing investigations resulted in the deal being annulled. [G. Madhavan Nair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._Madhavan_Nair) (ISRO Chairperson when the agreement was signed) was barred from holding any post under the Department of Space. Some former scientists were found guilty of "acts of commission" or "acts of omission". Devas and Deutsche Telekom demanded US\$2 billion and US\$1 billion, respectively, in damages.[^cite_note-100]

The [Central Bureau of Investigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Bureau_of_Investigation) concluded investigations into the Antrix-Devas scam and registered a case against the accused in the Antrix-Devas deal under Section 120-B, besides Section 420 of IPC and Section 13(2) read with 13(1)(d) of PC Act, 1988 on 18 March 2015 against the then executive director of Antrix Corporation, two officials of USA-based company, Bangalore based private multimedia company, and other unknown officials of Antrix Corporation or Department of Space.[^cite_note-101][^cite_note-102]

Devas Multimedia started arbitration proceedings against Antrix in June 2011. In September 2015, the International Court of Arbitration of the [International Chamber of Commerce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Chamber_of_Commerce) ruled in favour of Devas, and directed Antrix to pay US\$672 million (Rs 44.35 billion) in damages to Devas.[^cite_note-103] Antrix opposed the Devas plea for tribunal award in the [Delhi High Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delhi_High_Court).[^cite_note-104]
