**Strike action**, also called **labor strike**, **labour strike** in [British English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_English), or simply **strike**, is a form of direct action in which [employees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Employees) collectively refuse to work. It is usually a response to [employee grievances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Employee_grievances). Generally, strikes are intended to bring an employer to accede to employees' demands for improved working conditions or [unionisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unionization), particularly when other avenues of change, such as negotiations or [legal redress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_redress), have been exhausted. Strike actions can be called by trade unions or be instigated by rank-and-file activists.

Strikes became common during the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution), when [mass labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_economics) became important in factories and mines. As striking became a more common practice, governments were often pushed to act (either by private business or by union workers). When government intervention occurred, it was rarely neutral or amicable. Early strikes were often deemed unlawful conspiracies or anti-competitive cartel action, and many were subject to massive legal repression by state police, federal military power, and federal courts.[^cite_note-1] Many Western nations legalized striking under certain conditions in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

Strikes are sometimes used to pressure governments to change policies. Occasionally, strikes destabilize the rule of a particular political party or ruler; in such cases, strikes are often part of a broader social movement taking the form of a campaign of [civil resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_resistance). Notable examples are the 1980 [Gdańsk Shipyard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gda%C5%84sk_Shipyard) and the [1981 Warning Strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1981_warning_strike_in_Poland) led by [Lech Wałęsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lech_Wa%C5%82%C4%99sa). These strikes were significant in the long campaign of civil resistance for political change in [Poland](22936.md), and were an important mobilizing effort that contributed to [the fall of the Iron Curtain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_fall_of_the_Iron_Curtain) and the end of communist party rule in Eastern Europe.[^cite_note-2] Another example is the [general strike](41138909.md) in [Weimar Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weimar_Germany) in response to the [Kapp Putsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kapp_Putsch) in March 1920. It was called by the [Social Democratic Party](60585.md) (SPD) and received such broad support that it resulted in the collapse of the putsch.[^cite_note-3]

There are a variety of different types of strikes, as well as responses to them or actions taken against them. They are the subject of various schools of thought, as well as creative works, such as films.

## History

See also: [List of strikes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_strikes)

### Etymology

The use of the English word "strike" to describe a work protest was first seen in 1768, when sailors, in support of [demonstrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demonstration_\(political\)) in [London](17867.md), "struck" or removed the [topgallant sails](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topgallant_sail) of merchant ships at port, thus crippling the ships.[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6] The 1797 [Spithead and Nore mutinies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spithead_and_Nore_mutinies) have been compared to labor strikes, and anticipated elements that would later become widespread, including use of the red flag in the context of labor disputes.[^cite_note-7]

### Pre-industrial strikes



The so-called "Strike Papyrus" written by Amunnakht, between 1187 and 1157 BC, [New Kingdom of Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Kingdom_of_Egypt). [Museo Egizio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museo_Egizio), Turin

The first historically certain account of strike action was in [ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt) on 14 November in 1152 BCE, when artisans of the Royal Necropolis at [Deir el-Medina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deir_el-Medina) walked off their jobs in protest at the failure of the government of [Ramesses III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramesses_III) to pay their wages on time and in full.[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9] The royal government ended the strike by raising the artisans' wages.

The first [Jewish](25955086.md) source for the idea of a labor strike appears in the [Talmud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talmud), which records that the bakers who prepared [showbread](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Showbread) for the [altar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altar) went on strike.[^cite_note-10]

An early predecessor of the [general strike](41138909.md) may have been the *[secessio plebis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secessio_plebis)* in [ancient Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome). In *[The Outline of History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Outline_of_History)*, [H. G. Wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._G._Wells) characterized this event as "the general strike of the [plebeians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plebeian); the plebeians seem to have invented the strike, which now makes its first appearance in history."[^cite_note-H.G._Wells_1920,_page_225-11] Their first strike occurred because they "saw with indignation their friends, who had often served the state bravely in the legions, thrown into chains and [reduced to slavery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debt_bondage) at the demand of [patrician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrician_\(ancient_Rome\)) creditors".[^cite_note-H.G._Wells_1920,_page_225-11]

### During and after the Industrial Revolution



Agitated workers face the factory owner in *The Strike*. Painted by [Robert Koehler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Koehler) in 1886.



*Strike action* (1879), painting by [Theodor Kittelsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Kittelsen)

For the first time in history during the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution), large numbers of people were members of the industrial working class; they lived in towns and cities, exchanging their labor for payment. By the 1830s, when the [Chartist movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chartist_movement) was at its peak in Britain, a true and widespread 'workers consciousness' was awakening. In 1838, a [Statistical Society of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_Society_of_London) committee "used the first written questionnaire… The committee prepared and printed a list of questions 'designed to elicit the complete and impartial history of strikes.'"[^cite_note-12]

In 1842 the demands for fairer wages and conditions across many different industries finally exploded into the first modern [general strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1842_General_Strike). After the second [Chartist Petition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chartism#1842) was presented to Parliament in April 1842 and rejected, a strike began in the coal mines of [Staffordshire](58733.md), [England](9316.md), and soon spread through Britain, affecting [factories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factories), [cotton mills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton_mills) in Lancashire and [coal mines](208413.md) from [Dundee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dundee) to [South Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Wales) and [Cornwall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornwall).[^cite_note-Plug_Plot-13] The strike was politically motivated, and was driven by an agenda to win concessions. As much as half of the then-industrial work force were on strike at its peak–over 500,000 men.[^cite_note-14] The local leadership marshaled a growing working class tradition to politically organize their followers to mount an articulate challenge to the capitalist, political establishment. [Friedrich Engels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Engels), an observer in [London](17867.md) at the time, wrote:

> *by its numbers, this class has become the most powerful in England, and woe betide the wealthy Englishmen when it becomes conscious of this fact … The English proletarian is only just becoming aware of his power, and the fruits of this awareness were the disturbances of last summer.*[^cite_note-15]

As the 19th century progressed, strikes became a fixture of industrial relations across the industrialized world, as workers organized themselves to [collectively bargain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collectively_bargain) for better wages and standards with their employers. [Karl Marx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Marx) condemned the theory of [Pierre-Joseph Proudhon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre-Joseph_Proudhon) criminalizing strike action in his work *[The Poverty of Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Poverty_of_Philosophy)*.[^cite_note-16]

In 1937, there were 4,740 strikes in the United States.[^cite_note-17] This was the greatest strike wave in [American labor history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_labor_history). The number of major strikes and lockouts in the US fell by 97% from 381 in 1970, to 187 in 1980, to only 11 in 2010. Companies countered the threat of a strike by threatening to close or move a plant.[^cite_note-census-18][^cite_note-19]

The [International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Covenant_on_Economic,_Social_and_Cultural_Rights), adopted in 1967 by the [United Nations General Assembly](31957.md), ensures the right to strike in Article 8. The [European Social Charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Social_Charter), adopted in 1961, also ensures the right to strike in Article 6.

[The Farah Strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farah_strike), 1972–1974, labeled the "strike of the century," was organized and led by Mexican American women predominantly in El Paso, Texas.[^cite_note-20]

## Frequency and duration



Female tailors on strike, New York City, February 1910

Strikes are rare, in part because many workers are not covered by a [collective bargaining agreement](12728750.md).[^cite_note-Kennan-21] Strikes that do occur are generally fairly short in duration.[^cite_note-Kennan-21] Labor economist John Kennan notes:

> In Britain in 1926 (the year of [the general strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1926_United_Kingdom_general_strike)) about 9 workdays per worker were lost due to strikes. In 1979, the loss due to strikes was a little more than one day per worker. These are the extreme cases. In the 79 years following 1926, the number of workdays lost in Britain was less than 2 hours per year per worker. In the U.S., idleness due to strikes never exceeded one half of one percent of total working days in any year during the period 1948-2005; the average loss was 0.1% per year. Similarly, in Canada over the period 1980-2005, the annual number of work days lost due to strikes never exceeded one day per worker; on average over this period lost worktime due to strikes was about one-third of a day per worker. Although the data are not readily available for a broad sample of developed countries, the pattern described above seems quite general: days lost due to strikes amount to only a fraction of a day per worker per annum, on average, exceeding one day only in a few exceptional years.[^cite_note-Kennan-21]

Since the 1990s, strike actions have generally further declined, a phenomenon that might be attributable to lower information costs (and thus more readily available access to information on [economic rents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_rent)) made possible by computerization and rising personal indebtedness, which increases the cost of job loss for striking workers.[^cite_note-Kennan-21][^cite_note-22][^cite_note-23] In the United States, the number of workers involved in major work stoppages (including strikes and, less commonly, lockouts) that involved at least a thousand workers for at least one full shift generally declined from 1973 to 2017 (coinciding with a general decrease in overall union membership), before substantially increasing in 2018 and 2019.[^cite_note-\:0-24] In the 2018 and 2019 period, 3.1% of union members were involved in a work stoppage each year on average, these strikes also contained more workers than ever recorded with an average of 20,000 workers participating in each major work stoppage in 2018 and 2019.[^cite_note-\:0-24]

## Variations

### Black ban

The black ban is strike action taken against a particular job or employer, in order to protect the economic interests of the strikers.

### Byline strike

A [byline strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byline_strike) is when [newspaper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newspaper) writers choose to withhold their names from their stories, as a way to protest actions of their employer.[^cite_note-26]

### Flying squads

Activists may form "flying squad" groups for strikes or other actions, a form of [picketing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picketing), to disrupt the workplace or another aspect of capitalist production. Examples include supporting other strikers or unemployed workers, participating in protests against globalization, or opposing abusive landlords.[^cite_note-27]

### General strikes

Main article: [General strike](41138909.md)



[Teamsters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teamsters) wielding pipes, clash with [armed police](1148542.md) in the streets of [Minneapolis](6097240.md) during a [1934 strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minneapolis_Teamsters_Strike_of_1934)

Strikes may be specific to a particular workplace, unit within a workplace, or employer; or, they may encompass an entire industry, or every worker within a given area. Strikes that involve all workers, or a number of large and important groups of workers, in a particular community or region are known as general strikes. Under some circumstances, strikes may take place in order to put pressure on the State or other authorities, or may be a response to unsafe conditions in the workplace.

### Green ban

Main article: [Green ban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_ban)

During the development boom of the 1970s in Australia, the [Green ban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_ban) was developed from the black ban. This is a form of strike action taken by a trade union or other organized labor group for [environmentalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmentalism) or conservationist purposes.

### Hunger strike

A [hunger strike](222292.md) is a deliberate refusal to eat. Hunger strikes are often used in prisons as a form of political protest. Like student strikes, a hunger strike aims to worsen the public image of the target.

### Jurisdictional strike

A [jurisdictional strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisdictional_strike) in United States labor law refers to a concerted refusal to work undertaken by a union to assert its members' right to particular job assignments, and to protest the assignment of disputed work to members of another union, or to unorganized workers.

### Overtime ban

Main article: [Overtime ban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overtime_ban)

The [overtime ban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overtime_ban) is when employees refuse to work overtime with the intent to frustrate business. Such strikes may in some cases be a form of "partial strike" or "slowdown".

### Recognition strikes

&#x20;A recognition strike is an industrial strike implemented in order to force a particular employer or industry to recognize a [trade union](17626.md) as the legitimate collective bargaining agent for a company's workers.[^cite_note-uslegal-28][^cite_note-incessant-29][^cite_note-manager-30] In 1949, their use in the United States was described as "a weapon used with varying results by labor for the last forty years or more"; one example cited was the successful formation of the [United Auto Workers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Auto_Workers), which achieved recognition from [General Motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Motors) through the [Flint sit-down strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flint_sit-down_strike) of 1936–37.[^cite_note-hein-31] They were more common prior to the advent of modern American [labor law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labor_law) (including the [National Labor Relations Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Labor_Relations_Act)), which introduced processes legally compelling an employer to recognize the legitimacy of properly certified unions.[^cite_note-hein-31][^cite_note-uslegal-28]

Two other examples include the [US Steel recognition strike of 1901](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Steel_recognition_strike_of_1901), and the subsequent [coal strike of 1902](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_strike_of_1902).[^cite_note-anthracite-32] A 1936 study of strikes in the United States indicated that about one third of the total number of strikes between 1927 and 1928, and over 40 percent in 1929, were due to "demands for union recognition, closed shop, and protest against union discrimination and violation of union agreements".[^cite_note-peterson-33] A 1988 study of strike activity and unionization in non-union municipal [police departments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_departments) between 1972 and 1978 found that recognition strikes were carried out "primarily where bargaining laws \[provided] little or no protection of bargaining rights."[^cite_note-police-34]

### Rolling strike

A [rolling strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolling_strike) refers to a strike where only some employees in key departments or locations go on strike. These strikes are performed in order to increase stakes as negotiations draw on, and to be unpredictable to the employer. Rolling strikes also serve to conserve [strike funds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strike_funds).

### Sickout

A "sickout" is a type of strike action in which the strikers [call in sick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malingering), using an accepted excuse to perform a strike. This is used in cases where laws prohibit certain employees from declaring a strike. Police, firefighters, air traffic controllers, and teachers in some US states are among the groups commonly barred from striking, usually by state and federal laws meant to ensure the safety or security of the general public.

When a sickout is conducted by police officers, it is sometimes called a ["blue flu"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_flu), receiving its name from the uniforms worn by police officers, who are traditionally prohibited from striking. The term "red flu" has sometimes been used to describe this action when undertaken by firefighters.

### Sit-down strike

Main article: [Sitdown strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sitdown_strike)

A strike may consist of workers refusing to attend work, or [picketing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picketing) outside the workplace, to prevent or dissuade people from working for, or conducting business with, their employer. Less frequently, workers may occupy the workplace, but refuse to work. This is known as a [sit-down strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sit-down_strike).

### Student strike

Main article: [Student protest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Student_protest)

A [student strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Student_strike) involves students (sometimes supported by faculty) refusing to attend classes. In some cases, the strike is intended to draw media attention to the institution, so that the grievances that are causing the students to strike can be aired before the public; this usually damages the institution's (or government's) public image. In other cases, especially in government-supported institutions, the student strike can cause a budgetary imbalance and have actual economic repercussions for the institution.

### Sympathy strike

A [sympathy strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sympathy_strike) is a strike action in which one group of workers refuses to cross a picket line established by another, as a means of supporting the striking workers. Sympathy strikes in the United States were once the norm in the construction industry, but have since been made much more difficult to conduct. The [National Labor Relations Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Labor_Relations_Board) has made decisions permitting employers to establish separate or "reserved" gates for particular trades. Consequentially, if a union establishes a picket line at any gate other than the one reserved for the employer it is picketing, that makes it an unlawful [secondary boycott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_boycott). Still, the practice continues to occur; for example, some [Teamsters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teamsters) contracts often protect members from disciplinary action if a member refuses to cross a picket line.[^cite_note-35] Sympathy strikes may be undertaken by a union as an organization, or by individual union members choosing not to cross a picket line.

### Warning strike

Main article: [Warning strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warning_strike)

A warning strike is a temporary, brief work stoppage in the course of the [collective bargaining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collective_bargaining) process, lasting for a few hours or up to a day.[^cite_note-\:02-36]

### Wildcat strike

Main article: [Wildcat strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildcat_strike)

Occasionally, workers decide to strike without the sanction of a labor union, either because the union refuses to endorse such a tactic, or because the workers involved are non-unionized. Strikes without formal union authorization are also known as [wildcat strikes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildcat_strikes).

In many countries, wildcat strikes do not enjoy the same legal protections as recognized union strikes, and may result in penalties for the union members who participate, or for their union. The same often applies in the case of strikes conducted without an official ballot of the union membership, as is required in some countries, such as the [United Kingdom](31717.md).

### Work-in strike



Work-in strike at Gasworks, Sydney, 21 August 1939

A similar tactic is the [work-in](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Work-in), where employees occupy the workplace but still continue work, often without pay. They attempt to show they employees are still useful, or that [worker self-management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worker_self-management) can be successful. For instance, this occurred with factory occupations in the *[Biennio Rosso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biennio_Rosso)* strikes–the "two red years" of Italy from 1919 to 1920.

### Work-to-rule

Main article: [Work-to-rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Work-to-rule)

Another unconventional tactic (like work-in strikes) and less-than-full-strike labor action (like overtime bans) is called [work-to-rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Work-to-rule). It is also known as an *Italian strike*–in [Italian](14708.md): *Sciopero bianco*. In this strategy, workers perform their tasks exactly as they are required to, but no better. For example, workers might follow safety protocols with excessive zeal with the intent of reducing productivity.

The [workers' right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labor_rights) to strike is guaranteed by the [Constitution of Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Argentina) since it was [reformed in 1949](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentine_Constitution_of_1949) during the government of [Juan Domingo Perón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Per%C3%B3n). The Constitution also guarantees other rights such as [minimum wage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum_wage), limited work hours, free vacations, [fair wage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Living_wage), [equal pay for equal work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equal_pay_for_equal_work), protection against workers being fired arbitrarily and the right to [unionize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unionization).

Only workers that provide "essential services" have some limitations to their right to strike, such as policemen and those related to water and power supply, due to the Law 14.786. If they strike, they still have to guarantee a minimum provision of those services.

### Canada

On 30 January 2015, the [Supreme Court of Canada](123468.md) ruled that there is a constitutional right to strike.[^cite_note-37] In this 5–2 majority decision, [Justice Rosalie Abella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosalie_Abella) ruled that "\[a]long with their right to associate, speak through a bargaining representative of their choice, and bargain collectively with their employer through that representative, the right of employees to strike is vital to protecting the meaningful process of collective bargaining…" \[paragraph 24]. This decision adopted the dissent by [Chief Justice Brian Dickson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Dickson) in a 1987 Supreme Court ruling on a [reference case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_case) brought by the province of [Alberta](717.md) (*[Reference Re Public Service Employee Relations Act (Alta)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_Re_Public_Service_Employee_Relations_Act_\(Alta\))*). The exact scope of this right to strike remains unclear.[^cite_note-38]

Prior to this Supreme Court decision, the federal and provincial governments had the ability to introduce "back-to-work legislation", a [special law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_law) that blocks the strike action (or a lockout) from happening or continuing. Canadian governments could also have imposed [binding arbitration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binding_arbitration) or a new contract on the disputing parties. Back-to-work legislation was first used in 1950 during a railway strike, and as of 2012 had been used 33 times by the federal government for those parts of the economy that are regulated federally (grain handling, rail and air travel, and the postal service), and in more cases provincially. In addition, certain parts of the economy can be proclaimed "[essential services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essential_services)" in which case all strikes are illegal.[^cite_note-39]

Examples include when the government of Canada passed back-to-work legislation during the 2011 [Canada Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canada_Post) lockout and the 2012 [CP Rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CP_Rail) strike, thus effectively ending the strikes. In 2016, the former use was ruled unconstitutional, with the judge specifically referencing the Supreme Court of Canada's 2015 decision in *[Saskatchewan Federation of Labour v Saskatchewan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saskatchewan_Federation_of_Labour_v_Saskatchewan)*.[^cite_note-40]

### Former Soviet Union

Main article: [Trade unions in the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_unions_in_the_Soviet_Union)



[Lenin Shipyard workers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gda%C5%84sk_Shipyard), Poland, on strike in August 1980, with the name of the state-controlled trade union crossed out in protest

In the Soviet Union, strikes occurred throughout the existence of the [Union of Soviet Socialist Republics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) (USSR), most notably in the 1930s. After [World War II](32927.md), they diminished both in number and in scale.[^cite_note-41] [Trade unions in the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_unions_in_the_Soviet_Union) served in part as a means to educate workers about the country's economic system. Russian revolutionary, politician and political theorist [Vladimir Lenin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Lenin) referred to trade unions as "Schools of Communism".

In [France](5843419.md), the first law aimed at limiting the ability of workers to take collective action was the [Le Chapelier Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Chapelier_Law), passed by the [National Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Assembly_\(French_Revolution\)) on 14 June 1791, which introduced the "crime of coalition." In his speech in support of the law, the titular author [Isaac René Guy le Chapelier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Ren%C3%A9_Guy_le_Chapelier) explained that it "must be without a doubt permitted for all citizens to assemble," but he maintained that it "must not be permitted for citizens from certain professions to assemble for their so-called common interests."[^cite_note-42]

Strike actions were specifically banned with the passage of [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon)'s [French Penal Code of 1810](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Penal_Code_of_1810). Article 415 of the Code declared that participants in an attempted strike action would be subject to an imprisonment of between one and three months, and that the organizers of the attempted strike action would be subject to an imprisonment of between two and five years.[^cite_note-43]

In 1971, the [Constitutional Council](1141259.md) made a [decision on the freedom of association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A9cision_Libert%C3%A9_d'association), which recognized the [preamble to the French Constitution of 27 October 1946](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Constitution_of_27_October_1946#Preamble) as being invested with constitutional value. This recognized and guaranteed the right to strike under the current [French Fifth Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Fifth_Republic).

A "minimum service" during strikes in public transport was a promise of [Nicolas Sarkozy](557007.md) during his campaign for the French presidential election. A law "on social dialogue and continuity of public service in regular terrestrial transports of passengers" was adopted on 12 August 2007, and it took effect on 1 January 2008.

### Germany

In Germany, a freedom to strike is not an explicit part of the constitution, but derived from the constitutional freedom of association in Article 9 of the [Basic Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_Law_for_the_Federal_Republic_of_Germany).[^cite_note-\:2-44] However, the Basic Law bans civil servants from going on strike, and the Federal Constitutional Court confirmed that teachers were not permitted to strike.[^cite_note-\:1-45] As of December 2023, the matter whether the decision violated teachers' human rights under the European Convention of Human Rights (ECHR) is pending at the European Court of Human Rights.[^cite_note-\:1-45]

Legal rules concerning strikes have been developed mainly by court decisions: strikes have to be organized to be organized by a trade union, they need to aim at wages or working conditions, follow a principle of reasonableness and only be a last resort in a labor dispute.[^cite_note-\:2-44][^cite_note-\:3-46] Strikes are explicitly linked to the negotiation of collective agreements.[^cite_note-\:4-47] Strikes with political goals not resolvable by collective agreements are generally considered illegal in Germany,[^cite_note-\:2-44][^cite_note-\:3-46] however, the extent of this prohibition and its compatibility with European law and especially the [European Social Charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Social_Charter) have remained a debated question.[^cite_note-\:4-47]

### Italy

In Italy, the right to strike is guaranteed by the [Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Italy) ([article 40](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Constitution%20of%20Italy#Article_40)). The law number 146 of 1990 and law number 83 of 2000[^cite_note-48] regulate the strike actions. In particular, they impose limitations for the strikes of workers in public essential services, i.e., the ones that "guarantee the [personality rights](225178.md) of life, health, freedom and security, movements, assistance and welfare, education, and communications". These limitations provide a minimum guarantee for these services, and punish violations. Similar limitations are applied to workers in the private sector whose strike can affect public services. The employer is explicitly forbidden to apply sanctions to employees participating in the strikes, with the exception of the aforementioned essential services cases.

The [government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Italy), under exceptional circumstances, can impose the *precettazione* of the strike, i.e., can force the postponement, cancellation or duration reduction of a national-wide strike. The [prime minister](273741.md) has to justify the decision of applying the *precettazione* in front of the [parliament](1417859.md). For local strikes, *precettazione* can also be applied by a decision of the [prefect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefect). The employees refusing to work after the *precettazione* takes effect may be subject of a sanction, or even a penal action (for a maximum of 4 years of prison), if the illegal strike causes the suspension of an essential service.

*Precettazione* has been rarely applied, usually after several days of strikes affecting transport, fuel services, or extraordinary events. Recent cases include the cancellation of the 2015 strike of the [company providing transportation services in Milan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azienda_Trasporti_Milanesi) during [Expo 2015](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expo_2015), and the 2007 *precettazione* to stop the strike of the [truck drivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truck_driver) that was causing food and fuel shortage after several days of strike.

### People's Republic of China

Main article: [All-China Federation of Trade Unions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-China_Federation_of_Trade_Unions)

In some [Marxist–Leninist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxist%E2%80%93Leninist) states, such as the [People's Republic of China](5405.md), striking was illegal and viewed as [counter-revolutionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counter-revolutionary), and labor strikes are considered to be [taboo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taboo) in most East Asian cultures. In 1976, China signed the [International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Covenant_on_Economic,_Social_and_Cultural_Rights), which guaranteed the right to unions and striking, but Chinese officials declared that they had no interest in allowing these liberties.[^cite_note-49] In June 2008, the municipal government in the [Shenzhen Special Economic Zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shenzhen_Special_Economic_Zone) introduced draft labor regulations, which a [labor rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labor_rights) advocacy group says would, if implemented and enforced, virtually restore Chinese workers' right to strike.[^cite_note-50]

### United Kingdom

The [Industrial Relations Act 1971](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Relations_Act_1971) was repealed through the [Trade Union and Labour Relations Act 1974](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_Union_and_Labour_Relations_Act_1974), sections of which were repealed by the [Employment Act 1982](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Employment_Act_1982).

The Code of Practice on Industrial Action Ballots and Notices, and sections 22 and 25 of the [Employment Relations Act 2004](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Employment_Relations_Act_2004), which concern industrial action notices, commenced on 1 October 2005.

In January 1951 during the [Labour](19279158.md) [Attlee ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attlee_ministry), [Attorney-General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attorney-General) [Hartley Shawcross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartley_Shawcross) left [his name to a Parliamentary principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shawcross_principle) in a defense of his conduct regarding an illegal strike: that the Attorney-General "is not to be put, and is not put, under pressure by his colleagues in the matter" of whether or not to establish [criminal proceedings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_proceedings).[^cite_note-hsp-51][^cite_note-rhhsp-52]

Legislation was enacted in the aftermath of the [1919 police strikes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_police_strikes_in_1918_and_1919), forbidding [British police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_police) from both taking industrial action, and discussing the possibility with colleagues.[^cite_note-bbcsp-53]

The [Police Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_Federation_of_England_and_Wales), which was created at the time to deal with employment grievances, and to provide representation to police officers, attempted to put pressure on the [Blair ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Blair_ministry), and at the time repeatedly threatened strike action.[^cite_note-bbcsp-53]

Prison officers have gained and lost the right to strike over the years; despite it being illegal, they walked out on 15 November 2016,[^cite_note-54] and again on 14 September 2018.[^cite_note-55]

### United States



A strike leader addressing strikers in [Gary, Indiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary,_Indiana) in 1919

The [National Labor Relations Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Labor_Relations_Act) generally permits strikes, but provides a mechanism to enjoin from striking workers in industries in which a strike would create a national emergency. As of 2021, the federal government most recently invoked these statutory provisions to obtain an injunction requiring the [International Longshore and Warehouse Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Longshore_and_Warehouse_Union) to return to work in 2002 after having been locked out by the employer group, the [Pacific Maritime Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Maritime_Association).

Some jurisdictions prohibit all strikes by public employees, under laws such as the "[Taylor Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_Law)" in [New York](8210131.md). Other jurisdictions impose strike bans only on certain categories of workers, particularly those regarded as critical to society: [police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police), [teachers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teachers) and [firefighters](146303.md) are among the groups commonly barred from striking in these jurisdictions. Some states, such as [New Jersey](21648.md), [Michigan](18859.md), [Iowa](26810748.md) and [Florida](18933066.md), do not allow teachers in public schools to strike. Workers have sometimes circumvented these restrictions by falsely claiming inability to work due to illness–this is sometimes called a "sickout", "blue flu" when employed by police officers, or "red flu" when employed by firefighters.

The [Railway Labor Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railway_Labor_Act) bans strikes by [United States airline](17111897.md) and railroad employees, except in narrowly defined circumstances. Under federal law, federal employees who participate in a strike, or who assert the right to strike against the US government, are barred from retaining their employment.[^cite_note-56]

Often, specific regulations on strike actions exist for employees in prisons. The [Code of Federal Regulations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_of_Federal_Regulations) declares "encouraging others to refuse to work, or to participate in a work stoppage" by prisoners to be a "High Severity Level Prohibited Act", and authorizes [solitary confinement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solitary_confinement) for periods of up to a year for each violation.[^cite_note-57] The [California Code of Regulations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Code_of_Regulations) states that "\[p]articipation in a strike or work stoppage", "\[r]efusal to perform work or participate in a program as ordered or assigned", and "\[r]ecurring failure to meet work or program expectations within the inmate's abilities when lesser disciplinary methods failed to correct the misconduct" by prisoners is "serious misconduct" under §3315(a)(3)(L), leading to [gang](80408.md) affiliation under CCR §3000.[^cite_note-58]

Postal workers involved in 1978 wildcat strikes in [Jersey City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jersey_City), [Kearny, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kearny,_New_Jersey), [San Francisco](49728.md), and [Washington, D.C.](108956.md) were fired under the presidency of [Jimmy Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Carter), and President [Ronald Reagan](25433.md) fired [air traffic controllers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_traffic_controller) and the [PATCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professional_Air_Traffic_Controllers_Organization_\(1968\)) union after the [air traffic controllers’ strike of 1981](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1981_Professional_Air_Traffic_Controllers_Organization_strike).

The [West Virginia teacher's strike in 2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_West_Virginia_teachers'_strike) inspired [teachers in other states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018%E2%80%9319_education_workers'_strikes_in_the_United_States), including [Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Oklahoma_teachers'_strike), [Colorado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Colorado_teachers'_strike), and [Arizona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Arizona_teachers'_strike), to take similar action.[^cite_note-59]

The U.S. [Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_of_Labor_Statistics) recorded 30 major work stoppages beginning in 2025, involving 306,800 workers. Its series defines a major stoppage as a strike or lockout involving at least 1,000 workers and lasting at least one work shift, so the figures do not represent all strikes in the country.[^cite_note-60][^cite_note-61]

## Jurisprudence and philosophy

Strike actions have also been discussed from the perspective of [jurisprudence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisprudence) and [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy), with issues being raised such as whether people have a right to strike, the interaction of strikes with other rights, civil order, coercion, justice and the interplay between striking and contracts.[^cite_note-62][^cite_note-63][^cite_note-64][^cite_note-65][^cite_note-66]

## Anti-strike action

Most strikes called by unions are somewhat predictable; they typically occur after the contract has expired. However, not all strikes are called by union organizations–some strikes have been called in an effort to pressure employers to recognize unions. Other strikes may be spontaneous actions by working people, such as wildcat strikes, which were the key fighting point in [May 1968 in France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_1968_in_France). Most commonly, these are responses to serious (often life-threatening) safety hazards in the workplace rather than wage or hour disputes, etc.

Whatever the cause of the strike, employers are generally motivated to take measures to prevent them, mitigate the impact, or to undermine strikes when they do occur.



To bring public attention, a giant [inflatable rat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflatable_rat) (named 'Scabby') is used in the US at the site of a labor dispute. The rat represents strike-breaking replacement workers, otherwise known as 'scabs'.

### Conscription

Critical infrastructure workers who are on strike may be forced back to work under military law and/or [civil conscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_conscription) in countries which allow conscription. In 2010, the Spanish government invoked emergency powers to conscript air traffic controllers who were [on strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_air_traffic_controllers'_strike).[^cite_note-telegraph1-67]

### Lockout

Another counter to a strike is a [lockout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockout_\(industry\)), a form of work stoppage in which an employer refuses to allow employees to work. Two of the three employers involved in the Caravan park grocery workers strike of 2003–2004 locked out their employees in response to a strike against the third member of the employer bargaining group. Lockouts are, with certain exceptions, lawful under United States labor law.

### No-strike clauses



A rally of the trade union [UNISON](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNISON) in [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford) during a strike in March 2006



The [2005 New York City transit strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_New_York_City_transit_strike)



A teachers' strike in [Tartu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tartu), [Estonia](28222445.md) in front of the [Ministry of Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Education_and_Research_\(Estonia\)) building, March 2012



Metal workers doing motorized strike in [Hyvinkää](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyvink%C3%A4%C3%A4), Finland in March 1971

Most strikes are organized by [labor unions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labor_unions) during collective bargaining as a last resort. The object of collective bargaining is for the employer and the union to come to an agreement over wages, benefits, and working conditions. A collective bargaining agreement may include a clause (a contractual "no-strike clause") which prohibits the union from striking during the term of the agreement.[^cite_note-FeldackerHayes-68] Under US labor law, a strike in violation of a no-strike clause is not a [protected concerted activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protected_concerted_activity).[^cite_note-FeldackerHayes-68]

The scope of a no-strike clause varies; generally, the US courts and [National Labor Relations Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Labor_Relations_Board) have determined that a collective bargaining agreement's no-strike clause has the same scope as the agreement's [arbitration clauses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arbitration_clause), such that "the union cannot strike over an arbitrable issue."[^cite_note-FeldackerHayes-68] The US Supreme Court case *Jacksonville Bulk Terminals Inc. v. International Longshoremen's Association* (1982), which involved the [International Longshoremen's Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Longshoremen's_Association) refusal to work with goods for export to the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) in protest against its [invasion of Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet%E2%80%93Afghan_War), held that a no-strike clause does not bar unions from refusing to work as a political protest (since that is not an "arbitrable" issue), although such activity may lead to damages for a [secondary boycott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_boycott).[^cite_note-FeldackerHayes-68] Whether a no-strike clause applies to [sympathy strikes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sympathy_strikes) depends on the context.[^cite_note-FeldackerHayes-68] Some in the labor movement consider no-strike clauses to be an unnecessary detriment to unions in the collective bargaining process.[^cite_note-69]

### Strikebreaking

Main article: [Strikebreaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strikebreaker)



A strikebreaking driver and cart being stoned during sanitation worker strike. [New York City](645042.md), 1911.

A *strikebreaker* (sometimes derogatorily called a *scab*, *blackleg*, or *knobstick*) is a person who works despite an ongoing strike. Strikebreakers are usually individuals who are not employed by the company prior to the [trade union](17626.md) dispute, but rather hired after or during the strike to keep the organization running. "Strikebreakers" may also refer to workers (union members or not) who cross [picket lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picketing_\(protest\)) to work.

Irwin, Jones, McGovern (2008) believe that the term "scab" is part of a larger metaphor involving strikes. They argue that the picket line is symbolic of a wound, and those who break its borders to return to work are the scabs who bond that wound. Others have argued that the word is not a part of a larger metaphor, but rather, was an old-fashioned English insult whose meaning narrowed over time.

"Blackleg" is an older word and is found in the 19th-century folk song "[Blackleg Miner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackleg_Miner)" which originated in [Northumberland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northumberland). The term does not necessarily originate from this tune of unknown origin.



Strike breakers, *[Chicago Tribune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Tribune)* strike, 1986, [Chicago](6886.md), Illinois

#### Hired strikebreakers

When established unions commence strike action, some companies may decline entirely to negotiate with the union, and respond to the strike by hiring replacement workers. For strikers, this may be concerning for multiple reasons. For example, they may fear that the strike will be lost. The length of time that the strike may last could cause many workers to cease striking, which would likely cause it to fail. They may also be concerned that they will lose their jobs entirely. Companies that hire strikebreakers typically use these concerns to attempt to convince union members to abandon the strike, and cross the union's [picket line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picketing_\(protest\)).

Unions faced with a strikebreaking situation may try to inhibit the use of strikebreakers by a variety of methods–establishing picket lines where strikebreakers enter the workplace; discouraging strike breakers from taking, or from keeping, strikebreaking jobs; raising the cost of hiring strikebreakers for the company; or employing public relations tactics. Companies may respond by increasing security forces and seeking court injunctions.

Examining conditions in the late 1990s, John Logan, professor and director of Labor and Employment Studies at [San Francisco State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_State_University), observed that union busting agencies helped to "transform economic strikes into a virtually suicidal tactic for US unions". Logan further observed, "as strike rates in the United States have plummeted to historic low levels, the demand for strike management firms has also declined."[^cite_note-Logan_651-675-70]

The concept of *union strikebreaking* or *union scabbing* refers to any circumstance in which union workers themselves cross picket lines to work.

Unionized workers are sometimes required to cross the picket lines established by other unions due to their organizations having signed contracts which include no-strike clauses. The no-strike clause typically requires that members of the union not conduct any strike action for the duration of the contract; such actions are called *sympathy* or *secondary strikes*. Members who honor the picket line in spite of the contract frequently face discipline, for their action may be viewed as a violation of provisions of the contract.

Therefore, any union conducting a strike action typically seeks in the agreement that settles the strike to include a provision of amnesty for all who honored the picket line. No-strike clauses may also prevent unionized workers from engaging in solidarity actions for other workers, even when no picket line is crossed. For example, striking workers in manufacturing or mining produce a product which must be transported. In a situation where the factory or mine owners have replaced the strikers, unionized transport workers may feel inclined to refuse to haul any product that is produced by strikebreakers, yet their own contract obligates them to do so.

Historically the practice of union strikebreaking has been a contentious issue in the union movement, and a point of contention between adherents of different union philosophies. For example, supporters of [industrial unions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_unions), which have sought to organize entire workplaces without regard to individual skills, have criticized [craft unions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Craft_unions) for organizing workplaces into separate unions according to skill, a circumstance that makes union strikebreaking more common. Union strikebreaking is not unique to craft unions.

### Strike preparation

Companies which produce products for sale will frequently increase inventories prior to a strike. Salaried employees may be called upon to take the place of strikers, which may entail advance training. If the company has multiple locations, personnel may be redeployed to meet the needs of reduced staff. Companies may also take out strike insurance, to help offset the losses which a strike would cause.

### Union busting

Main article: [Union busting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_busting)



*Strike*, painting by [Stanisław Lentz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanis%C5%82aw_Lentz)

One method of inhibiting or ending a strike is firing union members who are striking, which can result in elimination of the union. Although this has happened, it is rare due to laws regarding firing and "right to strike" having a wide range of differences in the US, depending on whether union members are public or private sector. Laws also vary country-to-country. In the UK, "It is important to understand that there is no right to strike in UK law."[^cite_note-71] Employees who strike risk dismissal, unless it is an official strike (one called or endorsed by their union) in which case they are protected from unlawful dismissal, and cannot be fired for at least 12 weeks. UK laws regarding work stoppages and strikes are defined within the [Employment Relations Act 1999](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Employment_Relations_Act_1999) and the [Trade Union and Labour Relations (Consolidation) Act 1992](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_Union_and_Labour_Relations_\(Consolidation\)_Act_1992).

A significant case of mass-dismissals in the UK in 2005 involved the firing of over 600 [Gategourmet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gategourmet) employees at [Heathrow Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heathrow_Airport).[^cite_note-72] The firing prompted a walkout by [British Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Airways) ground staff, leading to cancelled flights and thousands of delayed passengers. The walkout was illegal under UK law, and the [Transport and General Workers' Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_and_General_Workers'_Union) (T\&GWU) quickly brought it to an end. A subsequent court case ruled that demonstrations on a grass verge approaching the Gategourmet premises were not illegal, but limited the number and made the T\&GWU responsible for their action.[^cite_note-BBC_GG-73]

In 1962, US President [John F. Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Kennedy) issued Executive Order #10988[^cite_note-74], which permitted federal employees to form trade unions, but prohibited strikes (codified in 1966 at 5 U.S.C. 7311 – Loyalty and Striking). In 1981, after public sector union [Professional Air Traffic Controllers Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professional_Air_Traffic_Controllers_Organization_\(1968\)) (PATCO) went on strike illegally, President [Ronald Reagan](25433.md) fired all of the controllers. His action resulted in the dissolution of the union. PATCO reformed to become the [National Air Traffic Controllers Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Air_Traffic_Controllers_Association).



Victims of a clash between striking workers and the army in [Prostějov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prost%C4%9Bjov), Austria-Hungary, April 1917

In the US, as established in the [National Labor Relations Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Labor_Relations_Act), there is a legally protected right for private sector employees to strike to gain better wages, benefits, or working conditions and they cannot be fired. Striking for economic reasons (i.e., protesting workplace conditions or supporting a union's bargaining demands) allows an employer to hire permanent replacements. The replacement worker can continue in the job, and then the striking worker must wait for a vacancy. But if the strike is due to [unfair labor practices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unfair_labor_practice) (ULP), the strikers replaced can demand immediate reinstatement when the strike ends. If a collective bargaining agreement is in effect, and it contains a "no-strike clause", a strike during the life of the contract could result in the firing of all striking employees, which could in turn result in dissolution of that union. Although this is legal, it could be viewed as union busting.

[United States labor law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_labor_law) also draws a distinction, in the case of private sector employers covered by the [National Labor Relations Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Labor_Relations_Act), between "economic" and "unfair labor practice" strikes. An employer may not fire, but may permanently replace, workers who engage in a strike over economic issues.

Amazon has used the Law firm Wilmerhale to legally end worker strikes at its locations.

### Violence

Main article: [Anti-union violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-union_violence)



*The Charge* by [Ramon Casas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramon_Casas) (1899)

Historically, some employers have attempted to break union strikes by force. One of the most famous examples of this occurred during the [Homestead Strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homestead_Strike) of 1892. Industrialist [Henry Clay Frick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Clay_Frick) sent private security agents from the [Pinkerton National Detective Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinkerton_National_Detective_Agency) to break [a strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homestead_strike), organised by the [Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amalgamated_Association_of_Iron_and_Steel_Workers) at a [Homestead, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homestead,_Pennsylvania), steel mill. Two strikers were killed, twelve wounded, along with two Pinkertons killed and eleven wounded.
