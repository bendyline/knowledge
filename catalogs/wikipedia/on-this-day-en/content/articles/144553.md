A projectile being fired from [artillery](2508.md)

A **projectile** is launched by the application of an external force and then moves freely under the influence of gravity and air resistance.[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-2] Although any objects in [motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_\(physics\)) through space are projectiles, they are commonly found in [warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warfare) and [sports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sport) (for example, a thrown [baseball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baseball_\(ball\)), kicked [football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ball_\(association_football\)), fired [bullet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bullet), shot [arrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrow), a ball from a [cannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannon), stone released from [catapult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catapult), etc.).[^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4]

In [ballistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballistics), mathematical [equations of motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equations_of_motion) are used to analyze projectile [trajectories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajectory) through launch, [flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight), and [impact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_ballistics).[^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6]

## Motive force

See also: [Projectile motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projectile_motion)



Projectile and cartridge case for the huge [World War II](32927.md) *[Schwerer Gustav](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwerer_Gustav)* artillery piece. The majority of projectile weapons use the compression or expansion of gases as their motive force.

[Blowguns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blowgun) and [pneumatic rifles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pneumatic_rifle) use compressed gases, while most other guns and cannons utilize expanding gases liberated by sudden chemical reactions by [propellants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propellant) like [smokeless powder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smokeless_powder). [Light-gas guns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light-gas_gun) use a combination of these mechanisms.

[Railguns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railgun) utilize electromagnetic fields to provide acceleration along the entire length of the device, greatly increasing the [muzzle velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muzzle_velocity).

Some projectiles provide [propulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propulsion) during flight by means of a [rocket engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_engine) or [jet engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_engine). In military terminology, a [rocket](26301.md) is unguided, while a [missile](19594.md) is [guided](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guided_missile). Note the two meanings of "rocket" (weapon and engine): an [ICBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intercontinental_ballistic_missile) is a guided missile with a rocket engine.

An explosion, whether or not by a weapon, causes the debris to act as multiple high velocity projectiles. An explosive weapon or device may also be designed to produce numerous high velocity projectiles by the break-up of its casing; these are correctly termed *[fragments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fragmentation_\(weaponry\))*.

In projectile motion, the most important force applied to the projectile mass is the propelling force.[^cite_note-8] In many sports, the propelling forces are provided by muscles that accelerate the projectile (ball, disc, javelin, hammer, dart), and the stronger the propelling force, the faster and further the projectile will travel. See [pitching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_\(baseball\)), [bowling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowling).

## As a weapon

### Delivery projectiles

Many projectiles, e.g. [shells](337004.md), may carry an explosive charge or another chemical or biological substance. Aside from explosive payload, a projectile can be designed to cause special damage, e.g. fire (see also [early thermal weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_thermal_weapons)), or poisoning (see also [arrow poison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrow_poison)).

### Kinetic projectiles

This section is an excerpt from [Kinetic energy weapon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_energy_weapon).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kinetic_energy_weapon\&action=edit)]



The [Homing Overlay Experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_Defense_Initiative#Homing_Overlay_Experiment_\(HOE\)) used a metal fan that was rolled up during launch and expanded during flight. The metal has five times as much destructive power as an explosive warhead of the same weight.



Sample from a kinetic energy weapon test. A piece of [polycarbonate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polycarbonate) plastic weighing 7 grams (1⁄4 oz) was fired at an [aluminium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium) block at 7 km/s (23,000 ft/s), giving it a [muzzle energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muzzle_energy) of 171,500 J (126,500 ft⋅lbf); a typical rifle [bullet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bullet) has a muzzle energy in the range of a few thousand joules, with the anti materiel [.50 BMG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/.50_BMG) reaching 20,000 J (15,000 ft⋅lbf).

A [kinetic energy weapon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_energy_weapon) (also known as kinetic weapon, kinetic energy warhead, kinetic warhead, kinetic projectile, kinetic kill vehicle) is a projectile weapon based solely on a projectile's [kinetic energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_energy) to inflict [damage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damage) to a target, instead of using any [explosive](10192.md), [incendiary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incendiary_ammunition), [chemical](27179600.md) or [radiological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiological_warfare) [payload](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Payload). All kinetic weapons work by attaining a high flight speed – generally [supersonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supersonic) or even up to [hypervelocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypervelocity) – and [collide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collision) with their targets, converting their kinetic energy and relative [impulse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impulse_\(physics\)) into destructive [shock waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shock_wave), [heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat) and [cavitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penetrating_trauma#Cavitation). In kinetic weapons with [unpowered flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unpowered_flight), the [muzzle velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muzzle_velocity) or [launch velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projectile_motion#Velocity) often determines the [effective range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_range) and [potential damage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_ballistics) of the kinetic projectile.

Kinetic weapons are the oldest and most common ranged weapons used in [human history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_history), with the projectiles varying from blunt projectiles such as [rocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_\(geology\)) and [round shots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Round_shot), pointed missiles such as [arrows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrow), [bolts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crossbow_bolt), [darts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dart_\(missile\)), and [javelins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Javelin), to modern tapered high-velocity impactors such as [bullets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bullet), [flechettes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flechette), and [penetrators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_energy_penetrator). Typical kinetic weapons accelerate their projectiles mechanically (by [muscle power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_power), [mechanical advantage devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_advantage_device), [elastic energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elastic_energy) or [pneumatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pneumatics)) or chemically (by [propellant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propellant) [combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion), as with [firearms](11966.md)), but newer technologies are enabling the development of potential weapons using [electromagnetically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic) launched projectiles, such as [railguns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railgun), [coilguns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coilgun) and [mass drivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_driver). There are also concept weapons that are accelerated by [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity), as in the case of [kinetic bombardment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_bombardment) weapons designed for [space warfare](956982.md).

The term hit-to-kill, or kinetic kill, is also used in the military [aerospace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerospace) field to describe kinetic energy weapons accelerated by a [rocket engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_engine). It has been used primarily in the [anti-ballistic missile](1791.md) (ABM) and [anti-satellite weapon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-satellite_weapon) (ASAT) fields, but some modern [anti-aircraft missiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-aircraft_missile) are also kinetic kill vehicles. Hit-to-kill systems are part of the wider class of kinetic projectiles, a class that has widespread use in the [anti-tank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-tank_warfare) field.

## Wired projectiles

Some projectiles stay connected by a cable to the launch equipment after launching it:

- for guidance: [wire-guided missile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wire-guided_missile) (range up to 4,000 metres or 13,000 feet)

- to administer an electric shock, as in the case of a [Taser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taser) (range up to 10.6 metres or 35 feet); two projectiles are shot simultaneously, each with a cable.

- to make a connection with the target, either to tow it towards the launcher, as with a whaling [harpoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harpoon), or to draw the launcher to the target, as a [grappling hook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grappling_hook) does.
