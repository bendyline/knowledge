The **Church of England** (**C of E**) is the [established](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_religion#State_churches) [Christian church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Christian_denominations) in [England](9316.md) and the [Crown Dependencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_Dependencies). It was the initial church of the [Anglican tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglicanism). The church traces its history to the Christian hierarchy recorded as existing in the [Roman province of Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Britain) by the 3rd century and to the 6th-century [Gregorian mission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorian_mission) to [Kent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kent) led by [Augustine of Canterbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Canterbury). Its members are called *Anglicans*.

In 1534, the Church of England renounced the authority of the [Papacy](23056.md) under the direction of King [Henry VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_VIII) pursuant to laws passed by the [Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Reformation_Parliament), beginning the [English Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Reformation). The guiding theologian that shaped Anglican doctrine was the reforming Archbishop [Thomas Cranmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Cranmer), who developed the Church of England's liturgical text, the *[Book of Common Prayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Common_Prayer)*.[^cite_note-Samuel2020-4] Papal authority was [briefly restored](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Statute_of_Repeal) under [Mary I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_I_of_England), before her successor [Elizabeth I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_I) [renewed the breach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Act_of_Supremacy_1558). The [Elizabethan Settlement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabethan_Settlement) (implemented 1559–1563) concluded the English Reformation, charting a course for the English church to describe itself as a *[via media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Via_media)* between two branches of Protestantism—[Lutheranism](23371382.md) and [Calvinism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvinism)—and later, a denomination that is both [Reformed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reformed_Christianity) and [Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_\(term\)).[^cite_note-HSEC2003-5]

In the earlier phase of the [English Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Reformation) there were both [Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Catholic_martyrs_of_the_English_Reformation) and [Protestant martyrs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Protestant_martyrs_of_the_English_Reformation). This continued into the later phases, which saw the [Penal Laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Penal_Laws) punish Catholics and [nonconforming Protestants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonconformist_\(Protestantism\)). Various factions continued to challenge the leadership and doctrine of the church into the 17th century, which under [Charles I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_I_of_England) veered towards a more Catholic interpretation of the Elizabethan Settlement, especially under Archbishop [Laud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Laud). Following the victory of the [Roundheads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roundhead) in the [English Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Civil_War), the [Puritan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puritan) faction dominated and the *Book of Common Prayer* and episcopacy were abolished. Both were restored under the [Stuart Restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restoration_\(1660\)) in 1660.

Since the English Reformation, the Church of England has used the [English language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language) in the [liturgy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liturgy). The Church authorized English translations of the Bible, including the influential [King James Version](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_James_Version). As a [broad church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broad_church), the Church of England contains several doctrinal strands: the main traditions are known as [Anglo-Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Catholic#Practices_and_beliefs), [high church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_church), [central church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_churchmanship), and [low church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_church), the last producing a growing [evangelical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelical_Anglicanism) wing that includes [Reformed Anglicanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reformed_Anglicanism), with a smaller number of [Arminian Anglicans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Arminianism).[^cite_note-The_Gospel_Coalition-6] Tensions between theological [conservatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Christianity) and [liberals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Christianity) find expression in debates over the [ordination of women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordination_of_women_in_the_Anglican_Communion) and [same-sex marriage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homosexuality_and_the_Anglican_Communion). The [British monarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_monarch) (currently [Charles III](125248.md)) is the [supreme governor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Governor_of_the_Church_of_England), and the [archbishop of Canterbury](2345.md) ([Sarah Mullally](4356626.md) since 28 January 2026) is the most senior [cleric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleric). The governing structure of the Church is based on [dioceses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Church_of_England_dioceses), each presided over by a bishop. Within each diocese are local parishes. The [General Synod of the Church of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Synod_of_the_Church_of_England) is the legislative body for the church and comprises bishops, other clergy and [laity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laity). Its [measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_England_measure) must be approved by the [Parliament of the United Kingdom](13964.md).

## History

Main article: [History of the Church of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Church_of_England)

### Middle Ages



[Hereford Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hereford_Cathedral) is one of the church's 43 cathedrals; many have histories stretching back centuries.

Further information: [Religion in Medieval England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_Medieval_England) and [Anglo-Saxon Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_Christianity)

There is evidence for [Christianity in Roman Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_Roman_Britain) as early as the 3rd century. After the fall of the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire), England was conquered by the [Anglo-Saxons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Saxons), who were [pagans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_paganism), and the [Celtic church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_church) was confined to Cornwall and Wales.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMoorman19733–4,_9-7] In 597, [Pope Gregory I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Gregory_I) sent missionaries to England to [Christianise the Anglo-Saxons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianisation_of_Anglo-Saxon_England). This mission was led by [Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Canterbury), who became the first [archbishop of Canterbury](2345.md). The Church of England considers 597 the start of its formal history.[^cite_note-DetailedHistory-8][^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10]

In [Northumbria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northumbria), Celtic missionaries competed with their Roman counterparts. The Celtic and Roman churches disagreed over the [date of Easter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_controversy), baptismal customs, and the style of [tonsure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonsure) worn by monks.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMoorman197319-11] King [Oswiu of Northumbria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswiu_of_Northumbria) summoned the [Synod of Whitby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synod_of_Whitby) in 664. The king decided Northumbria would follow the Roman tradition because [Saint Peter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Peter) and his successors, the [bishops](4092.md) of Rome, hold the [keys of the kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keys_of_the_kingdom) of heaven.[^cite_note-12]

By the [late Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Middle_Ages), [Catholicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholicism) was an essential part of English life and culture. The 9,000 [parishes](23623.md) covering all of England were overseen by a hierarchy of [deaneries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deanery), [archdeaconries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archdeaconries), [dioceses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese) led by bishops, the primate of All England, the archbishop of Canterbury, and ultimately the pope who presided over the Catholic Church from Rome.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMarshall2017a11-13] Catholicism taught that the [contrite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contrite) person could cooperate with [God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Christianity) towards their [salvation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvation_in_Christianity) by performing [good works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merit_\(Christianity\)) (see [synergism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synergism)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMacCulloch1996210-14] God's [grace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grace_in_Christianity) was given through the [seven sacraments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacraments_of_the_Catholic_Church).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMarshall2017a7-15] In the [Mass](5684685.md), a [priest](8655005.md) consecrated bread and wine to become the [body](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_of_Christ) and [blood of Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_of_Christ) through [transubstantiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transubstantiation). The church taught that, in the name of the congregation, the priest offered to God the same [sacrifice of Christ on the cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crucifixion_of_Jesus) that provided [atonement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atonement_in_Christianity) for the [sins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_views_on_sin) of humanity.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMarshall2017a8–9-16][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHefling202197–98-17] The Mass was also an offering of prayer by which the living could help [souls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul) in [purgatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purgatory).[^cite_note-18] While [penance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrament_of_Penance) removed the guilt attached to sin, Catholicism taught that a penalty still remained. It was believed that most people would end their lives with these penalties unsatisfied and would have to spend time in purgatory. Time in purgatory could be lessened through [indulgences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indulgence) and [prayers for the dead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prayers_for_the_dead), which were made possible by the [communion of saints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communion_of_saints).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMarshall2017a16–17-19]

### Reformation

Main article: [English Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Reformation)

In 1527, [Henry VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_VIII) was desperate for a male heir and asked [Pope Clement VII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Clement_VII) to annul his marriage to [Catherine of Aragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catherine_of_Aragon). When the Pope refused, Henry used [Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_England) to assert royal authority over the English church. In 1533, Parliament passed the [Act in Restraint of Appeals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute_in_Restraint_of_Appeals), barring legal cases from being appealed outside England. This allowed the Archbishop of Canterbury to annul the marriage without reference to Rome. In November 1534, the [Act of Supremacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Act_of_Supremacy_1534) formally abolished papal authority and declared Henry [Supreme Head of the Church of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Head_of_the_Church_of_England).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEShagan201729–31-20]

Henry's religious beliefs remained aligned to traditional Catholicism throughout his reign, albeit with reformist aspects in the tradition of [Erasmus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erasmus) and firm commitment to royal supremacy. In order to secure royal supremacy over the church, however, Henry allied himself with Protestants, who until that time had been treated as [heretics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heretics).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEShagan201732-21] The main doctrine of the [Protestant Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant_Reformation) was [justification by faith alone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sola_fide) rather than by good works.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHefling202196-22] The logical outcome of this belief is that the Mass, sacraments, charitable acts, [prayers to saints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prayer_to_saints), prayers for the dead, pilgrimage, and the [veneration of relics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veneration_of_relics) do not mediate divine favour. To believe they can, would be [superstition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superstition) at best and [idolatry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idolatry) at worst.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHefling202197-23][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMarshall2017a126-24]

Between 1536 and 1540, Henry engaged in the [dissolution of the monasteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_monasteries), which controlled much of the richest land. He disbanded religious houses, appropriated their income, disposed of their assets, and provided pensions for the former residents. The properties were sold to pay for the wars. Historian [George W. Bernard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Bernard) argues:

> The dissolution of the monasteries in the late 1530s was one of the most revolutionary events in English history. There were nearly 900 religious houses in England, around 260 for monks, 300 for regular canons, 142 nunneries and 183 friaries; some 12,000 people in total, 4,000 monks, 3,000 canons, 3,000 friars and 2,000 nuns....one adult man in fifty was in religious orders.[^cite_note-25]



Thomas Cranmer, a [Reformer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant_Reformers), was the first Protestant Archbishop of Canterbury and principal compiler of the *[Book of Common Prayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Common_Prayer)*, *[Thirty-nine Articles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirty-nine_Articles)*, and *[Books of Homilies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Books_of_Homilies)*.

In the reign of [Edward VI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_VI) (1547–1553), the Church of England underwent an extensive theological reformation. Justification by faith was made a central teaching.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMarshall2017a308-26] Government-sanctioned [iconoclasm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iconoclasm) led to the destruction of images and relics. Stained glass, shrines, statues, and [roods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rood) were defaced or destroyed. Church walls were [whitewashed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitewash) and covered with biblical texts condemning idolatry.[^cite_note-27] The most significant reform in Edward's reign was the adoption of an English liturgy to replace the old Latin rites.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEShagan201741-28] Written by the [Protestant reformer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant_Reformers) archbishop [Thomas Cranmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Cranmer), the [1549 *Book of Common Prayer*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1549_Book_of_Common_Prayer) implicitly taught justification by faith,[^cite_note-29] and rejected the Catholic doctrines of transubstantiation and the sacrifice of the Mass.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMacCulloch1996412,_414-30] This was followed by a greatly revised [1552 *Book of Common Prayer*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1552_Book_of_Common_Prayer), which propounded a [Reformed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvinism) view of the Lord's Supper (cf. *[Lord's Supper in Reformed theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord's_Supper_in_Reformed_theology)*).[^cite_note-Strout2024-31] Along with *The Book of Common Prayer*, *[The Thirty-nine Articles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirty-nine_Articles)* and *[The Books of Homilies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Books_of_Homilies)*, assembled through the efforts of the reformer [Thomas Cranmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Cranmer), became the basis of Anglican doctrine after the English Reformation.[^cite_note-Samuel2020-4]

During the reign of [Mary I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_I_of_England) (1553–1558), England was briefly reunited with the Catholic Church. Mary died childless, so it was left to the new regime of her half-sister [Queen Elizabeth I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Elizabeth_I) to resolve the direction of the Church. The [Elizabethan Religious Settlement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabethan_Religious_Settlement) returned the Church to where it stood in 1553 before Edward's death. The [Act of Supremacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Act_of_Supremacy_1558) made the monarch the Church's [supreme governor of the Church of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Governor_of_the_Church_of_England). The [Act of Uniformity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Act_of_Uniformity_1558) restored a slightly altered 1552 *Book of Common Prayer*. In 1571, the [Thirty-nine Articles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirty-nine_Articles) received parliamentary approval as a doctrinal statement for the Church. The settlement ensured the Church of England was Protestant, but it was unclear what kind of Protestantism was being adopted.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMarshall2017b49–51-32] Anglicanism was said to be a *[via media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Via_media)* between two forms of Protestantism, [Lutheranism](23371382.md) and [Reformed Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvinism), though more aligned with the latter than the former.[^cite_note-HSEC2003-5] The prayer book's Reformed [eucharistic theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eucharistic_theology) posited a [real spiritual presence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord's_Supper_in_Reformed_theology) (pneumatic presence), since Article 28 of the Thirty-nine Articles taught that the body of Christ was eaten "only after an heavenly and spiritual manner".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMarshall2017b50–51-33][^cite_note-Strout2024-31] Nevertheless, there was enough ambiguity to allow later theologians to articulate various versions of [Anglican eucharistic theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_eucharistic_theology).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMarshall2017b51-34]

The Church of England was the [established church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Established_church) (constitutionally established by the state with the head of state as its supreme governor). The exact nature of the relationship between church and state would be developed over the next century.[^cite_note-Eberle-35][^cite_note-Fox-36][^cite_note-Ferrante-37] Notably, the [Act of Settlement 1701](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Act_of_Settlement_1701), which remains in force today, stipulates that the monarch (who serves as the supreme governor of the Church of England) be a Protestant, maintain the Protestant succession, and "join in communion with the Church of England as by law established."[^cite_note-Doe2024-38] The [Coronation Oath Act 1688](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronation_Oath_Act_1688) (reiterated in the Act of Settlement 1701) requires the rising Sovereign to take an oath to maintain "the true Profession of the Gospel and the Protestant Reformed Religion Established by Law" in the United Kingdom.[^cite_note-Doe2024-38]

### Stuart period

Further information: [Stuart period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_period)

Struggle for control of the church persisted throughout the reigns of [James I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_VI_and_I) and his son [Charles I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_I_of_England), culminating in the outbreak of the [First English Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_English_Civil_War) in 1642. The two opposing factions consisted of [Puritans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puritans), who sought to "purify" the church and enact more far-reaching Protestant reforms, and those who wanted to retain traditional beliefs and practices. In a period when many believed "true religion" and "good government" were the same thing, religious disputes often included a political element, one example being the struggle over bishops. In addition to their religious function, bishops acted as state censors, able to ban sermons and writings considered objectionable, while lay people could be tried by [church courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arches_Court) for crimes including [blasphemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blasphemy), [heresy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heresy), [fornication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fornication) and other 'sins of the flesh', as well as matrimonial or inheritance disputes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHelmholz2003102-39] They also sat in the [House of Lords](13658.md) and often blocked legislation opposed by the Crown; their ousting from Parliament by the [1640 Clergy Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clergy_Act_1640) was a major step on the road to war.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWedgwood198331-40]



Major repairs were done to Canterbury Cathedral after the Restoration in 1660.

Following [Royalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavalier) defeat in 1646, the episcopacy was formally abolished.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKing1968523–537-41] In 1649, the [Commonwealth of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_of_England) outlawed a number of former practices and [Presbyterian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyterian_polity) structures replaced the episcopate. The Thirty-nine Articles were replaced by the [Westminster Confession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Confession). Worship according to the *Book of Common Prayer* was outlawed and replaced by the *[Directory of Public Worship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directory_of_Public_Worship)*. Despite this, about one quarter of English clergy refused to conform to this form of state [presbyterianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyterianism).[^cite_note-42] It was also opposed by the other Puritan Nonconformists who rejected the very idea of state-mandated religion, and included the [Congregationalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congregational_church) like [Oliver Cromwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Cromwell), and the [Baptists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptists), who were especially well represented in the [New Model Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Model_Army).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESpurr199811–12-43]

After the [Stuart Restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_Restoration) in 1660, Parliament restored the Church of England to a form not far removed from the Elizabethan version. Until [James II of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_II_of_England) was ousted by the [Glorious Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glorious_Revolution) in November 1688, many [Nonconformists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonconformist_\(Protestantism\)) still sought to negotiate terms that would allow them to re-enter the Church.[^cite_note-44] In order to secure his political position, [William III of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_III_of_England) ended these discussions and the Tudor ideal of encompassing all the people of England in one religious organisation was abandoned. The religious landscape of England assumed its present form, with the Anglican established church occupying the middle ground and Nonconformists continuing their existence outside. One result of the Restoration was the ousting of 2,000 parish ministers who had not been ordained by bishops in the apostolic succession or who had been ordained by ministers in presbyter's orders. Official suspicion and legal restrictions continued well into the 19th century. Roman Catholics, perhaps 5% of the English population (down from 20% in 1600) were grudgingly tolerated, having had little or no official representation after the Pope's excommunication of Queen Elizabeth in 1570, though the Stuarts were sympathetic to them. By the end of 18th century they had dwindled to 1% of the population, mostly amongst upper middle-class gentry, their tenants, and extended families.

### Union with the Church of Ireland

By the Fifth Article of the [Union with Ireland 1800](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_of_Union_1800), the Church of England and [Church of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Ireland) were united into "one Protestant Episcopal church, to be called, the United Church of England and Ireland".[^cite_note-45] Although "the continuance and preservation of the said united church ... \[was] deemed and taken to be an essential and fundamental part of the union",[^cite_note-46] the [Irish Church Act 1869](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Church_Act_1869) separated the Irish part of the church again and disestablished it, the Act coming into effect on 1 January 1871.

### Victorian era: 1840s to 1890s

Main article: [Religion in Victorian England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_Victorian_England)

Religion was politically controversial during this era, with Nonconformists pushing for the [disestablishment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disestablishmentarianism) of the Church of England.[^cite_note-Owen_Chadwick2-47] Nonconformists comprised about half of church attendees in England in 1851. They were a clear majority in Wales. Scotland and Ireland had separate religious cultures.[^cite_note-48] and gradually the legal discrimination that had been established against them outside of Scotland was removed.[^cite_note-49][^cite_note-50][^cite_note-51][^cite_note-52] Legal restrictions on [Roman Catholics](606848.md) were also largely [removed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Relief_Act_1829). The number of Catholics grew in Great Britain due to [conversions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_England_and_Wales#Converts) and [immigration from Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_England_and_Wales#Nineteenth_century_and_Irish_immigration).[^cite_note-Owen_Chadwick2-47] Secularism and doubts about the accuracy of the [Old Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Testament) grew among people with higher levels of education.[^cite_note-53] Northern English and Scottish academics tended to be more religiously conservative, whilst agnosticism and even atheism (though its promotion was illegal)[^cite_note-54] gained appeal among academics in the south.[^cite_note-Lewis-200732-55] Historians refer to a 'Victorian Crisis of Faith', a period when religious views had to readjust to accommodate new scientific knowledge and criticism of the Bible.[^cite_note-56]

The strong [evangelical movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelicalism) inside the Church of England placed great emphasis on a respectable and moral code of behaviour. This included features such as charity, personal responsibility, controlled habits,[^cite_note-57] [child discipline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_discipline) and self-criticism.[^cite_note-58][^cite_note-59] As well as personal improvement, importance was given to social reform.[^cite_note-60] [Utilitarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utilitarianism) was another philosophy that saw itself as based on science rather than on morality, but also emphasised social progress.[^cite_note-61][^cite_note-62] An alliance formed between these two ideological strands.[^cite_note-63] The reformers emphasised causes such as improving the conditions of women and children, giving police reform priority over harsh punishment to prevent crime, religious equality, and political reform in order to establish a democracy.[^cite_note-64] The political legacy of the reform movement was to link the [nonconformists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonconformist_\(Protestantism\)) (part of the evangelical movement outside the Church of England )—especially Methodists—in England and Wales with the [Liberal Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Party_\(UK\)).[^cite_note-65] This continued until the [First World War](4764461.md).[^cite_note-66] The [Presbyterians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyterianism) played a similar role as a religious voice for reform in Scotland.[^cite_note-67]

### Overseas developments



Captain [John Smith's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Smith_of_Jamestown) 1624 map of Bermuda, showing St Peter's at centre, left

Main article: [Anglican Communion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Communion)

Further information: [Historical development of Church of England dioceses § Colonial dioceses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_development_of_Church_of_England_dioceses#Colonial_dioceses)

As the English Empire (after the 1707 [union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_of_Union_1707) of the [Kingdom of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_England) with the [Kingdom of Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Scotland) to form the [Kingdom of Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Great_Britain), the [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire)) expanded, English (after 1707, *British*) colonists and colonial administrators took the established church doctrines and practices together with ordained ministry and formed overseas branches of the Church of England.

The [Diocese of Nova Scotia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_Nova_Scotia_and_Prince_Edward_Island) was created on 11 August 1787 by [Letters Patent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letters_Patent) of [George III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_III_of_the_United_Kingdom) which "erected the Province of [Nova Scotia](21184.md) into a bishop's see" and these also named [Charles Inglis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Inglis_\(bishop\)) as first bishop of the see.[^cite_note-68] The diocese was the first Church of England see created outside England and Wales (i.e. the first colonial diocese). At this point, the see covered present-day New Brunswick, Newfoundland, Nova Scotia, Prince Edward Island and Quebec.[^cite_note-69] From 1825 to 1839, it included the [nine parishes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Church_of_Bermuda) of [Bermuda](3460.md), subsequently transferred to the [Diocese of Newfoundland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_Newfoundland).[^cite_note-70]

As they developed, beginning with the United States of America, or became sovereign or independent states, many of their churches became separate organisationally, but remained linked to the Church of England through the [Anglican Communion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Communion). In the provinces that made up Canada, the church operated as the "Church of England in Canada" until 1955 when it became the [Anglican Church of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Church_of_Canada).[^cite_note-x25-71]

In Bermuda, the oldest remaining British overseas possession, the first Church of England services were performed by the Reverend Richard Buck, one of the survivors of the 1609 wreck of the *[Sea Venture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_Venture)* which initiated Bermuda's permanent settlement. The nine parishes of the Church of England in [Bermuda](3460.md), each with its own church and [glebe land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glebe), rarely had more than a pair of ordained ministers to share between them until the 19th century. From 1825 to 1839, Bermuda's parishes were attached to the [See of Nova Scotia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_Nova_Scotia_and_Prince_Edward_Island). Bermuda was then grouped into the new Diocese of Newfoundland and Bermuda from 1839. In 1879, the Synod of the Church of England in Bermuda was formed. At the same time, a Diocese of Bermuda became separate from the [Diocese of Newfoundland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_Newfoundland), but both continued to be grouped under the *Bishop of Newfoundland and Bermuda* until 1919, when Newfoundland and Bermuda each received its own bishop. The Church of England in Bermuda was renamed in 1978 as the [Anglican Church of Bermuda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Church_of_Bermuda), which is an [extra-provincial diocese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extra-provincial_Anglican_churches),[^cite_note-72] with both [metropolitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_bishop) and [primatial](24672.md) authority coming directly from the Archbishop of Canterbury. Among its parish churches is [St Peter's Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Peter's_Church,_St._George's) in the [UNESCO World Heritage Site](44940.md) of [St George's Town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._George's,_Bermuda), which is the oldest Anglican church outside of the British Isles, and the oldest Protestant church in the New World.[^cite_note-73]

The [Church of India, Burma and Ceylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_India,_Burma_and_Ceylon) was established in [Colonial India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonial_India), with its first diocese being erected in 1813, the [Diocese of Calcutta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_Calcutta_of_the_Church_of_North_India). Indian bishops were present at the first [Lambeth Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambeth_Conference).[^cite_note-Tovey2017-74]

The first Anglican missionaries arrived in Nigeria in 1842 and the first Anglican Nigerian was consecrated a bishop in 1864. However, the arrival of a rival group of Anglican missionaries in 1887 led to infighting that slowed the Church's growth. In this large African colony, by 1900 there were only 35,000 Anglicans, about 0.2% of the population. However, by the late 20th century the [Church of Nigeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Nigeria) was the fastest growing of all Anglican churches, reaching about 18 per cent of the local population by 2000.[^cite_note-x25-71]

The church established its presence in Hong Kong and Macau in 1843. In 1951, the [Diocese of Hong Kong and Macao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong_Sheng_Kung_Hui) became an extra-provincial diocese, and in 1998 it became a province of the Anglican Communion, under the name [Hong Kong Sheng Kung Hui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong_Sheng_Kung_Hui).

From 1796 to 1818 the Church began operating in [Sri Lanka](26750.md) (formerly [Ceylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceylon)), following the 1796 start of British colonisation, when the first services were held for the British civil and military personnel. In 1799, the first Colonial Chaplain was appointed, following which CMS and SPG missionaries began their work, in 1818 and 1844 respectively. Subsequently, the [Church of Ceylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Ceylon) was established: in 1845 the diocese of Colombo was inaugurated, with the appointment of [James Chapman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Chapman_\(Bishop_of_Colombo\)) as Bishop of Colombo. It served as an extra-provincial jurisdiction of the [archbishop of Canterbury](2345.md), who served as its [metropolitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_bishop).

### Early 21st century

#### Deposition from holy orders overturned

Under the guidance of [Rowan Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rowan_Williams) and with significant pressure from clergy union representatives, the ecclesiastical penalty for convicted felons to be defrocked was set aside from the [Clergy Discipline Measure 2003](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Church_of_England_measures). The clergy union argued that the penalty was unfair to victims of hypothetical miscarriages of criminal justice, because the ecclesiastical penalty is considered irreversible. Although clerics can still be banned for life from ministry, they remain ordained as priests.[^cite_note-75]

#### Continued decline in attendance and church response



One of the now "redundant" buildings, [Holy Trinity Church, Wensley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Trinity_Church,_Wensley), in North Yorkshire; much of the current structure was built in the 14th and 15th centuries.

Bishop [Sarah Mullally](4356626.md) has insisted that declining numbers at services should not necessarily be a cause of despair for churches, because people may still encounter God without [attending a service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_attendance) in a church; for example hearing the Christian message through social media sites or in a café run as a community project.[^cite_note-76] The Church of England estimates that 35 – 50 million people visit its churches as tourists annually.[^cite_note-77][^cite_note-78] Additionally, 9.7 million people visit at least one of its churches every year and 1 million students are educated at Church of England schools (which number 4,700).[^cite_note-79] In 2019, an estimated 10 million people visited a cathedral and an additional "1.3 million people visited [Westminster Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Abbey), where 99% of visitors paid / donated for entry".[^cite_note-80] In 2022, the church reported that an estimated 5.7 million people visited a cathedral and 6.8 million visited Westminster Abbey.[^cite_note-81] In 2024, 9.87 million people visited a cathedral, up from 9.7 million in 2019.[^cite_note-82] Nevertheless, the archbishops of [Canterbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justin_Welby) and [York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Sentamu) warned in January 2015 that the Church of England would no longer be able to carry on in its current form unless the downward spiral in membership were somehow to be reversed, as typical Sunday attendance had halved to 800,000 in the previous 40 years:[^cite_note-83]

> The urgency of the challenge facing us is not in doubt. Attendance at Church of England services has declined at an average of one per cent per annum over recent decades and, in addition, the age profile of our membership has become significantly older than that of the population... Renewing and reforming aspects of our institutional life is a necessary but far from sufficient response to the challenges facing the Church of England. ... The age profile of our clergy has also been increasing. Around 40 per cent of parish clergy are due to retire over the next decade or so.

Between 1969 and 2010, almost 1,800 church buildings, roughly 11% of the stock, were closed (so-called "[redundant churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redundant_churches)"); the majority (70%) in the first half of the period; only 514 being closed between 1990 and 2010.[^cite_note-84] Some active use was being made of about half of the closed churches.[^cite_note-85] By 2019 the rate of closure had steadied at around 20 to 25 per year (0.2%); some being replaced by new places of worship.[^cite_note-86] Additionally, in 2018 the church announced a £27 million growth programme to create 100 new churches.[^cite_note-87]

#### Low salaries

In 2015 the Church of England admitted that it was embarrassed to be paying staff under the living wage. The Church of England had previously campaigned for all employers to pay this minimum amount. The archbishop of Canterbury acknowledged it was not the only area where the church "fell short of its standards".[^cite_note-88]

#### Sexual abuse

The 2020 report from the [Independent Inquiry into Child Sexual Abuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_Inquiry_into_Child_Sexual_Abuse) found several cases of sexual abuse within the Church of England, and concluded that the Church did not protect children from sexual abuse, and allowed abusers to hide.[^cite_note-IICSA_2020-89][^cite_note-religionmediacentre-1-90][^cite_note-religionmediacentre-2-91] The Church spent more effort defending alleged abusers than supporting victims or protecting children and young people.[^cite_note-IICSA_2020-89] Allegations were not taken seriously, and in some cases clergymen were ordained even with a history of child sex abuse.[^cite_note-92] Bishop [Peter Ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Ball_\(bishop\)) was convicted in October 2015 on several charges of [indecent assault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indecent_assault) against young adult men.[^cite_note-religionmediacentre-1-90][^cite_note-religionmediacentre-2-91][^cite_note-sentence2015-93]

In June 2023, the [Archbishops' Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishops'_Council) dismissed the three board members of the Independent Safeguarding Board, which was set up in 2021 "to hold the Church to account, publicly if needs be, for any failings which are preventing good safeguarding from happening". A statement issued by the archbishops of Canterbury and York referred to there being "no prospect of resolving the disagreement and that it is getting in the way of the vital work of serving victims and survivors". [Jasvinder Sanghera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jasvinder_Sanghera) and Steve Reeves, the two independent members of the board, had complained about interference with their work by the Church.[^cite_note-94] The bishop of Birkenhead, [Julie Conalty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julie_Conalty), speaking to [BBC Radio 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_4) in connection with the dismissals, said: "I think culturally we are resistant as a church to accountability, to criticism. And therefore I don't entirely trust the church, even though I'm a key part of it and a leader within it, because I see the way the wind blows is always in a particular direction."[^cite_note-95] On 20 July 2023, it was announced that the archbishops of Canterbury and York had appointed [Alexis Jay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexis_Jay) to provide proposals for an independent system of safeguarding for the Church of England.[^cite_note-96] In February 2025, the General Synod voted not to make safeguarding the responsibiity of an independent body, to the dismay of many.[^cite_note-97] In February 2025, the Church of England announced it would take forward the clergy disciplinary measure process against 10 clergy, following the Makin Review of the serial abuser [John Smyth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Smyth_\(barrister\)).[^cite_note-98]

### Impact of COVID-19 pandemic

The [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_the_United_Kingdom) had a sizeable effect on church attendance, with attendance in 2020 and 2021 well below that of 2019. By 2022, the first full year without substantial restrictions related to the pandemic, numbers were still notably down on pre-pandemic participation. According to the 2022 release of "Statistics for Mission" by the church, the median size of each church's worshipping community (those who attend in person or online at least once a month) stood at 37 people, with average weekly attendance having declined from 34 to 25; while Easter and Christmas services had seen falls from 51 to 38 and 80 to 56 individuals respectively.[^cite_note-\:5-99] The reports for 2023 and 2024 showed a mixed picture: compared to pre-pandemic trends, the Church's average weekly attendance was 8% below and 4% below what was expected, respectively. The numbers attending particular services such as baptisms (up 20% and 29%) and marriages (up 24% and 42%) had increased compared to the projected pre-pandemic trend but this was attributed to pandemic-related postponements rather than a natural increase.[^cite_note-\:6-100][^cite_note-\:9-101]

## Doctrine and practice

See also: [Anglicanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglicanism) and [Anglican doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_doctrine)



[Richard Hooker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Hooker_\(theologian\)) (1554–1600), one of the most influential figures in shaping Anglican theology and self-identity



[Canterbury Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canterbury_Cathedral) houses the *[cathedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedra)* or episcopal chair of the [Archbishop of Canterbury](2345.md), and is the cathedral of the [Diocese of Canterbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_Canterbury) and the [mother church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mother_church) of the Church of England as well as a focus for the [Anglican Communion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Communion).

[Thomas Cranmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Cranmer), the guiding [Protestant Reformer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant_Reformer) who shaped Anglican doctrine after the English Reformation, was instrumental in the compilation of the *[Thirty-Nine Articles of Religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirty-nine_Articles)*, *[Book of Common Prayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Common_Prayer)* and *[Books of Homilies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Books_of_Homilies)*.[^cite_note-Samuel2020-4][^cite_note-Jensen2015-102] The [canon law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_law) of the Church of England identifies the [Christian scriptures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible) as the source of its doctrine. In addition, doctrine is also derived from the teachings of the [Church Fathers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_Fathers) and [ecumenical councils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecumenical_councils) (as well as the [ecumenical creeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecumenical_creeds)) in so far as these agree with scripture. This doctrine is expressed in the *Thirty-Nine Articles of Religion*, the *Book of Common Prayer*, and the Ordinal containing the rites for the ordination of [deacons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deacon#Anglicanism), [priests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_priest), and the consecration of bishops.[^cite_note-CanonA5-103] [Richard Hooker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Hooker)'s appeal to scripture as the primary source of Christian doctrine, informed by [church tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_tradition), and [reason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reason), has been influential in hermeneutics.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEShepherd,_Jr.Martin2005349–350-104]

The Church of England's doctrinal character today is largely the result of the Elizabethan Settlement. The historical development of Anglicanism saw itself as navigating a *[via media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Via_media)* between two forms of Protestantism—[Lutheranism](23371382.md) and [Reformed Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvinism)—though leaning closer to the latter than the former.[^cite_note-HSEC2003-5][^cite_note-Robinson2012-105] The Church of England affirms the protestant reformation principle that scripture contains all things necessary to salvation and is the final arbiter in doctrinal matters. The Thirty-nine Articles are the church's only official confessional statement. The Church of England did retain [three orders of ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_ministry) and the [apostolic succession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostolic_succession) of bishops, as with the Scandinavian Lutheran Churches (such as the [Church of Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Sweden)) and Roman Catholicism. Its identity has thus been described as [Reformed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reformed_Christianity) and [Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_\(term\)).[^cite_note-HSEC2003-5] There are differences of opinion within the Church of England over the necessity of episcopacy. Some consider it essential, while others feel it is needed for the proper ordering of the church.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEShepherd,_Jr.Martin2005349–350-104] The Bible, the Creeds, Apostolic Order, and the administration of the Sacraments are sufficient to establish catholicity. The Protestant Reformation in England was initially much concerned about doctrine but the Elizabethan Settlement tried to put a stop to doctrinal contentions. The proponents of further changes, nonetheless, tried to get their way by making changes in Church Order (abolition of bishops), governance (Canon Law) and liturgy. They did not succeed because the monarchy and the Church resisted and the majority of the population were indifferent. Moreover, "despite all the assumptions of the Reformation founders of that Church, it had retained a catholic character." The existence of cathedrals "without substantial alteration" and "where the "old devotional world cast its longest shadow for the future of the ethos that would become Anglicanism,"[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMacCulloch199079-106] This is "One of the great mysteries of the English Reformation,"[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMacCulloch199079-106] that there was no complete break with the past but a muddle that was per force turned into a virtue.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMacCulloch1990142-107]

The Church of England has, as one of its distinguishing marks, a breadth of opinion from [liberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Christianity) to conservative clergy and members.[^cite_note-108] This tolerance has allowed Anglicans who emphasise the catholic tradition and others who emphasise the reformed tradition to coexist. The [three schools of thought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churchmanship) (or parties) in the Church of England are sometimes called [high church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_church) (or [Anglo-Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Catholic)), [low church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_church) (or [evangelical Anglican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelical_Anglican)) and [broad church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broad_church) (or [liberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Christianity)). The high church party places importance on the Church of England's continuity with the pre-Reformation Catholic Church, adherence to ancient liturgical usages and the sacerdotal nature of the priesthood. As their name suggests, Anglo-Catholics maintain many traditional catholic practices and liturgical forms.[^cite_note-HighChurch-109] The Catholic tradition, strengthened and reshaped from the 1830s by the Oxford movement, has stressed the importance of the visible Church and its sacraments and the belief that the ministry of bishops, priests and deacons is a sign and instrument of the Church of England's Catholic and apostolic identity.[^cite_note-\:1-110] The low church party is more Protestant in both ceremony and theology.[^cite_note-LowChurch-111] It has emphasized the significance of the Protestant aspects of the Church of England's identity, stressing the importance of the authority of Scripture, preaching, justification by faith and personal conversion.[^cite_note-\:1-110] The theological perspectives within the Church of England have included the [Reformed Anglican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reformed_Anglican) perspective, as well as a minority [Arminian Anglican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Arminianism) view.[^cite_note-The_Gospel_Coalition-6] Historically, the term 'broad church' has been used to describe those of middle-of-the-road ceremonial preferences who lean theologically towards liberal Protestantism.[^cite_note-BroadChurch-112] The liberal broad church tradition has emphasized the importance of the use of reason in theological exploration. It has stressed the need to develop Christian belief and practice in order to respond creatively to wider advances in human knowledge and understanding and the importance of social and political action in forwarding God's kingdom.[^cite_note-\:1-110] The balance between these strands of churchmanship is not static: in 2013, 40% of Church of England worshippers attended [evangelical Anglican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelical_Anglicanism) churches (compared with 26% in 1989), and 83% of very large congregations were evangelical. Such churches were also reported to attract higher numbers of men and young adults than others.[^cite_note-113]

### Worship and liturgy



Stained glass window in [Rochester Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rochester_Cathedral), Kent

In 1604, [James I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_VI_and_I) ordered an English language translation of the Bible known as the [King James Version](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_James_Version), which was published in 1611 and authorised for use in parishes, although it was not an "official" version per se.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECowartKnappen2007?-114] The Church of England's official book of liturgy as established in English Law is the [1662 version of the *Book of Common Prayer*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Common_Prayer_\(1662\)) (BCP). In the year 2000, the General Synod approved a modern [liturgical book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liturgical_book), *[Common Worship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Worship)*, which can be used as an alternative to the BCP. Like its predecessor, the 1980 *[Alternative Service Book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternative_Service_Book)*, it differs from the *Book of Common Prayer* in providing a range of alternative services, mostly in modern language, although it does include some BCP-based forms as well, for example Order Two for Holy Communion. (This is a revision of the BCP service, altering some words and allowing the insertion of some other liturgical texts such as the *[Agnus Dei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agnus_Dei_\(liturgy\))* before communion.) The Order One rite follows the pattern of more modern liturgical scholarship.

The liturgies are organised according to the traditional [liturgical year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liturgical_year) and the [calendar of saints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_\(Church_of_England\)). The [sacraments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_sacraments) of [baptism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptism) and the [eucharist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eucharist) are generally thought necessary to salvation. [Infant baptism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infant_baptism) is practised. At a later age, individuals baptised as infants receive [confirmation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confirmation) by a bishop, at which time they reaffirm the baptismal promises made by their parents or sponsors. The eucharist, consecrated by a thanksgiving prayer including Christ's [Words of Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Words_of_Institution), is believed to be "a memorial of Christ's once-for-all redemptive acts in which Christ is objectively present and effectually received in faith".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEShepherd,_Jr.Martin2005350-115]

The use of hymns and [music in the Church of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_church_music) has changed dramatically over the centuries. Traditional [Choral evensong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evening_Prayer_\(Anglican\)) is a staple of most cathedrals. The style of [psalm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psalm) chanting harks back to the Church of England's pre-reformation roots. During the 18th century, clergy such as [Charles Wesley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Wesley) introduced their own styles of worship with poetic hymns.[^cite_note-116]

In the latter half of the 20th century, the influence of the [Charismatic Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charismatic_Movement) significantly altered the worship traditions of numerous Church of England parishes, primarily affecting those of [evangelical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelical) persuasion. These churches now adopt a [contemporary worship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contemporary_worship) form of service, with minimal liturgical or ritual elements, and incorporating [contemporary worship music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contemporary_worship_music).[^cite_note-117]

Just as the Church of England has a large conservative or "traditionalist" wing, it also has many [liberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Christianity) members and clergy. Approximately one third of clergy "doubt or disbelieve in the physical resurrection".[^cite_note-118] Others, such as [Giles Fraser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giles_Fraser), a contributor to *The Guardian*, have argued for an allegorical interpretation of the [virgin birth of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgin_birth_of_Jesus).[^cite_note-119] *The Independent* reported in 2014 that, according to a YouGov survey of Church of England clergy, "as many as 16 per cent are unclear about God and two per cent think it is no more than a human construct."[^cite_note-120][^cite_note-121] Moreover, many congregations are seeker-friendly environments. For example, one report from the [Church Mission Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_Mission_Society) suggested that the church open up "a pagan church where Christianity \[is] very much in the centre" to reach out to spiritual people.[^cite_note-122]

The Church of England is launching a project on "gendered language" in Spring 2023 in efforts to "study the ways in which God is referred to and addressed in liturgy and worship".[^cite_note-123]

### Women's ministry

Women were appointed as deaconesses from 1861, but they could not function fully as deacons and were not considered ordained clergy. Women have historically been able to serve as [lay readers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lay_reader). During the First World War, some women were appointed as lay readers, known as "[bishop's messengers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop's_messenger)", who also led missions and ran churches in the absence of men. After the war, no women were appointed as lay readers until 1969.[^cite_note-124]

Legislation authorising the ordination of women as deacons was passed in 1986 and they were first ordained in 1987. The [ordination of women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordination_of_women) as priests was approved by the [General Synod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Synod_of_the_Church_of_England) in 1992 and [began in 1994](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_the_first_32_women_ordained_as_Church_of_England_priests). In 2010, for the first time in the history of the Church of England, more women than men were ordained as priests (290 women and 273 men),[^cite_note-125] but in the next two years, ordinations of men again exceeded those of women.[^cite_note-126]

In July 2005, the synod voted to "set in train" the process of allowing the consecration of women as bishops. In February 2006, the synod voted overwhelmingly for the "further exploration" of possible arrangements for parishes that did not want to be directly under the authority of a bishop who is a woman.[^cite_note-127] On 7 July 2008, the synod voted to approve the ordination of women as bishops and rejected moves for alternative episcopal oversight for those who do not accept the ministry of bishops who are women.[^cite_note-128] Actual ordinations of women to the episcopate required further legislation, which was narrowly rejected in a General Synod vote in November 2012.[^cite_note-129][^cite_note-130] On 20 November 2013, the General Synod voted overwhelmingly in support of a plan to allow the ordination of women as bishops, with 378 in favour, 8 against and 25 abstentions.[^cite_note-131]

On 14 July 2014, the General Synod approved the ordination of women as bishops. The House of Bishops recorded 37 votes in favour, two against with one abstention. The House of Clergy had 162 in favour, 25 against and four abstentions. The House of Laity voted 152 for, 45 against with five abstentions.[^cite_note-132] This legislation had to be approved by the Ecclesiastical Committee of the Parliament before it could be finally implemented at the November 2014 synod. In December 2014, [Libby Lane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libby_Lane) was announced as the first woman to become a bishop in the Church of England. She was consecrated as a bishop in January 2015.[^cite_note-133] In July 2015, [Rachel Treweek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rachel_Treweek) was the first woman to become a diocesan bishop in the Church of England when she became the [Bishop of Gloucester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Gloucester).[^cite_note-anglicannews1-134] She and [Sarah Mullally](4356626.md), Bishop of Crediton, were the first women to be ordained as bishops at [Canterbury Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canterbury_Cathedral).[^cite_note-anglicannews1-134] Treweek later made headlines by calling for gender-inclusive language, saying that "God is not to be seen as male. God is God."[^cite_note-135]

In May 2018, the Diocese of London consecrated [Dame Sarah Mullally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dame_Sarah_Mullally) as the first woman to serve as the [Bishop of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_London),[^cite_note-136] the third most senior position in the Church of England.[^cite_note-137] Mullally has described herself as a feminist and said she would ordain both men and women to the priesthood.[^cite_note-138] She is also considered by some to be a theological liberal.[^cite_note-139] On women's [reproductive rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reproductive_rights), Mullally describes herself as [pro-choice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abortion-rights_movements) while also being personally [pro-life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-abortion_movements).[^cite_note-140] On marriage, she supports the current stance of the Church of England that marriage is between a man and a woman, but also said that: "It is a time for us to reflect on our tradition and scripture, and together say how we can offer a response that is about it being inclusive love."[^cite_note-141]

### Same-sex unions and LGBT clergy

See also: [Homosexuality and the Anglican Communion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homosexuality_and_the_Anglican_Communion)

The Church of England has been discussing [same-sex marriages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Same-sex_marriage_in_the_United_Kingdom) and LGBT clergy.[^cite_note-142][^cite_note-143] The church holds that marriage is a union of one man with one woman.[^cite_note-144][^cite_note-145] The church does not allow clergy to perform same-sex marriages, but in February 2023 approved of blessings for same-sex couples following a civil marriage or civil partnership.[^cite_note-146][^cite_note-147] The church teaches "Same-sex relationships often embody genuine mutuality and fidelity."[^cite_note-x75-148][^cite_note-149] In January 2023, the Bishops approved "prayers of thanksgiving, dedication and for God's blessing for same-sex couples."[^cite_note-150][^cite_note-151][^cite_note-152] The commended prayers of blessing for same-sex couples, known as "**Prayers of Love and Faith**," may be used during ordinary church services, and in November 2023 General Synod voted to authorise "standalone" blessings for same-sex couples on a trial basis, while permanent authorisation will require additional steps.[^cite_note-153][^cite_note-154] The church also officially supports [celibate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celibacy) civil partnerships; "We believe that Civil Partnerships still have a place, including for some Christian [LGBTI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intersex_and_LGBT) couples who see them as a way of gaining legal recognition of their relationship."[^cite_note-155]

[Civil partnerships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_partnership_in_the_United_Kingdom) for clergy have been allowed since 2005, so long as they remain sexually abstinent,[^cite_note-156][^cite_note-157][^cite_note-\:0-158] and the church extends pensions to clergy in same-sex civil partnerships.[^cite_note-159] In a missive to clergy, the church communicated that "there was a need for committed same-sex couples to be given recognition and 'compassionate attention' from the Church, including special prayers."[^cite_note-160] "There is no prohibition on prayers being said in church or there being a 'service'" after a civil partnership.[^cite_note-161] After same-sex marriage was legalised, the church sought continued availability of civil partnerships, saying "The Church of England recognises that same-sex relationships often embody fidelity and mutuality. Civil partnerships enable these Christian virtues to be recognised socially and legally in a proper framework."[^cite_note-162] In 2024, the General Synod voted in support of eventually permitting clergy to enter into civil same-sex marriages.[^cite_note-163][^cite_note-164]

In 2014, the bishops released guidelines that permit "more informal kind of prayer" for couples.[^cite_note-165] In the guidelines, "gay couples who get married will be able to ask for special prayers in the Church of England after their wedding, the bishops have agreed."[^cite_note-x75-148] In 2016, the bishop of Grantham, [Nicholas Chamberlain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Chamberlain), announced that he is gay, in a same-sex relationship and celibate, becoming the first bishop to do so in the church.[^cite_note-166] The church had decided in 2013 that gay clergy in civil partnerships so long as they remain sexually abstinent could become bishops.[^cite_note-\:0-158][^cite_note-167] "The House \[of Bishops] has confirmed that clergy in civil partnerships, and living in accordance with the teaching of the church on human sexuality, can be considered as candidates for the episcopate."[^cite_note-168]

In 2017, the House of Clergy voted against the motion to "take note" of the bishops' report defining marriage as between a man and a woman.[^cite_note-169] Due to passage in all three houses being required, the motion was rejected.[^cite_note-170] After General Synod rejected the motion, the archbishops of Canterbury and York called for "radical new Christian inclusion" that is "based on good, healthy, flourishing relationships, and in a proper 21st century understanding of being human and of being sexual."[^cite_note-171] The church officially opposes "[conversion therapy](26410.md)", a practice which attempts to change a gay or lesbian person's sexual orientation, calling it unethical and supports the [banning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversion_therapy_ban) of "conversion therapy" in the UK.[^cite_note-172][^cite_note-173] The [Diocese of Hereford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_Hereford) approved a motion calling for the church "to create a set of formal services and prayers to bless those who have had a same-sex marriage or civil partnership."[^cite_note-174] In 2022, "The House \[of Bishops] also agreed to the formation of a Pastoral Consultative Group to support and advise dioceses on pastoral responses to circumstances that arise concerning LGBTI+ clergy, ordinands, lay leaders and the lay people in their care."[^cite_note-175]

Regarding [transgender issues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_and_transgender_people), the 2017 General Synod voted in favour of a motion saying that transgender people should be "welcomed and affirmed in their parish church".[^cite_note-176][^cite_note-177] The motion also asked the bishops "to look into special services for transgender people."[^cite_note-178][^cite_note-179] The bishops initially said "the House notes that the Affirmation of Baptismal Faith, found in *Common Worship*, is an ideal liturgical rite which trans people can use to mark this moment of personal renewal."[^cite_note-180] The Bishops also authorised services of celebration to mark a gender transition that will be included in formal liturgy.[^cite_note-181][^cite_note-182] Transgender people may marry in the Church of England after legally making a transition.[^cite_note-183] "Since the [Gender Recognition Act 2004](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_Recognition_Act_2004), trans people legally confirmed in their gender identity under its provisions are able to marry someone of the opposite sex in their parish church."[^cite_note-184] The church further decided that same-gender couples may remain married when one spouse experiences [gender transition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transitioning_\(transgender\)) provided that the spouses identified as opposite genders at the time of the marriage.[^cite_note-185][^cite_note-186] Since 2000, the church has allowed priests to undergo gender transition and remain in office.[^cite_note-187] The church has ordained openly transgender clergy since 2005.[^cite_note-188] The Church of England ordained the church's first openly [non-binary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-binary_gender) priest in 2022.[^cite_note-189][^cite_note-190] In 2023, the Church of England appointed [Rachel Mann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rachel_Mann) as the church's first openly trans [Archdeacon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archdeacon).[^cite_note-191][^cite_note-192]

In January 2023, a meeting of the bishops of the Church of England rejected demands for clergy to conduct same-sex marriages. However, proposals would be put to the General Synod that clergy should be able to hold church blessings for same-sex civil marriages, albeit on a voluntary basis for individual clergy. This comes as the Church continued to be split on same-sex marriages.[^cite_note-193]

In February 2023, ten archbishops of the [Global South Fellowship of Anglican Churches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_South_Fellowship_of_Anglican_Churches) released a statement stating that they had broken communion and no longer recognised [Justin Welby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justin_Welby) as "the first among equals" or "[primus inter pares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primus_inter_pares)" in the Anglican Communion in response to the General Synod's decision to approve the blessing of same-sex couples following a civil marriage or partnership, leading to questions as to the status of the Church of England as the [mother church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mother_church) of the international [Anglican Communion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Communion).[^cite_note-\:2-194][^cite_note-\:3-195][^cite_note-\:4-196]

In November 2023, the General Synod narrowly voted to allow church blessings for same-sex couples on a trial basis.[^cite_note-197] In December 2023, the first blessings of same-sex couples began in the Church of England.[^cite_note-198][^cite_note-199] In 2024, the General Synod voted to support moving forward with "stand-alone" services of blessing for same-sex couples after a civil marriage or civil partnership.[^cite_note-200][^cite_note-201][^cite_note-202] In 2025, the House of Bishops announced the blessings of same-sex couples may continue during the regularly scheduled services; however, they also announced that authorising stand-alone blessings would require a two-thirds majority of General Synod, and that allowing clergy to enter into same-sex marriages would require a simple majority vote of the General Synod.[^cite_note-203][^cite_note-204][^cite_note-205][^cite_note-206] Individual clergy of the Church of England have declared their intent to unofficially offer stand-alone blessings for same-sex couples.[^cite_note-207][^cite_note-208]

In February 2026, the General Synod formally abandoned proposals to deliver stand-alone blessing ceremonies for same-sex couples in churches. General Synod did vote to continue to look into the issue in the future, but bishops had already decided there were theological and legal barriers to having such ceremonies now. However, the Prayers of Love and Faith approved in 2023 for use within ordinary services are still in place.[^cite_note-209]

### Bioethics issues

The Church of England is generally opposed to abortion but believes "there can be strictly limited conditions under which abortion may be morally preferable to any available alternative".[^cite_note-210] The church also opposes euthanasia. Its official stance is that "While acknowledging the complexity of the issues involved in assisted dying/suicide and voluntary euthanasia, the Church of England is opposed to any change in the law or in medical practice that would make assisted dying/suicide or voluntary euthanasia permissible in law or acceptable in practice." It also states that "Equally, the Church shares the desire to alleviate physical and psychological suffering, but believes that assisted dying/suicide and voluntary euthanasia are not acceptable means of achieving these laudable goals."[^cite_note-211] In 2014, George Carey, a former archbishop of Canterbury, announced that he had changed his stance on euthanasia and now advocated legalising "assisted dying".[^cite_note-212] On embryonic stem-cell research, the church has announced "cautious acceptance to the proposal to produce cytoplasmic hybrid embryos for research".[^cite_note-213]

In the 19th century, English law required the burial of people who had died by suicide to occur only [between the hours of 9 p.m. and midnight and without religious rites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interments_\(felo_de_se\)_Act_1882).[^cite_note-214] The Church of England permitted the use of alternative burial services for people who had died by suicide. In 2017, the Church of England changed its rules to permit the full, standard Christian burial service regardless of whether a person had died by suicide.[^cite_note-215]

## Social work

### Church Urban Fund

The Church of England set up the [Church Urban Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_Urban_Fund) in the 1980s to tackle poverty and [deprivation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_deprivation). It sees poverty as trapping individuals and communities with some people in urgent need, leading to [dependency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dependant), [homelessness](19394651.md), [hunger](149422.md), [isolation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_isolation), [low income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_income), [mental health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_health) problems, [social exclusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_exclusion) and violence. The organisation believes that poverty reduces confidence and [life expectancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_expectancy) and that people born in poor conditions have difficulty escaping their disadvantaged circumstances.[^cite_note-216]

#### Child poverty

In parts of [Liverpool](18081.md), Manchester and [Newcastle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newcastle_upon_Tyne) two-thirds of babies are born to poverty and have poorer life chances, also a life expectancy 15 years lower than babies born in the more affluent communities.[^cite_note-217]

> The deep-rooted unfairness in our society is highlighted by these stark statistics. Children being born in this country, just a few miles apart, couldn't witness a more wildly differing start to life. In child poverty terms, we live in one of the most unequal countries in the western world. We want people to understand where their own community sits alongside neighbouring communities. The disparity is often shocking but it's crucial that, through greater awareness, people from all backgrounds come together to think about what could be done to support those born into poverty. \[Paul Hackwood, the Chair of Trustees at Church Urban Fund][^cite_note-218]

### Action on hunger

Many prominent people in the Church of England have spoken out against poverty and welfare cuts in the United Kingdom. Twenty-seven bishops are among 43 Christian leaders who signed a letter which urged [David Cameron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Cameron) to make sure people [have enough to eat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poverty#Hunger).[^cite_note-219]

> We often hear talk of hard choices. Surely few can be harder than that faced by the tens of thousands of older people who must 'heat or eat' each winter, harder than those faced by families whose wages have stayed flat while [food prices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_prices) have gone up 30% in just five years. Yet beyond even this we must, as a society, face up to the fact that over half of people using food banks have been put in that situation by cutbacks to and failures in the benefit system, whether it be payment delays or punitive sanctions.[^cite_note-BBC20/02/14-220]

Thousands of UK citizens use [food banks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_bank). The church's campaign to end hunger considers this "truly shocking" and called for a national day of [fasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fasting) on 4 April 2014.[^cite_note-BBC20/02/14-220]

## Membership

Between 1890 and 2001, [churchgoing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_attendance) in the United Kingdom declined steadily.[^cite_note-221] In the years 1968 to 1999, Anglican Sunday church attendances almost halved, from 3.5 per cent of the population to 1.9 per cent.[^cite_note-222] By 2014, Sunday church attendances had declined further to 1.4 per cent of the population.[^cite_note-223] In 2008, 13.4 million Britons identified themselves as members of the Church of England.[^cite_note-224][^cite_note-225]

In 2026, the *[Encyclopedia Britannica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica)* claimed there are approximately 22 million baptised members of the Church of England.[^cite_note-226] In 2015, research, published in the *[Journal of Anglican Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_Anglican_Studies),* by Daniel Muñoz stated that the church claimed 26 million baptised members with approximately 1.7 million of these members being active members.[^cite_note-227][^cite_note-228] In 2016, research by [St. Mary's University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Mary's_University,_Twickenham), London, found that 19.8% of the population of England and Wales identified themselves as Anglican; and that half of those who had been raised Anglican reported 'no religion' in the survey.[^cite_note-229][^cite_note-230] In 2017, *Growth and Decline in the Anglican Communion: 1980 to the Present*, collected research concluding that there were 25 million baptised members of the Church of England, 1 to 4 million monthly individual attenders, and 1.1 million adults on the [electoral rolls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parochial_church_council#Powers_and_duties).[^cite_note-\:8-231]

In 2018, the [British Social Attitudes Survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Social_Attitudes_Survey) (BSA) found that 14% of Britons, or about 10 million people, identified themselves as members of the Church of England; this was called an "unrelenting decline" by a spokesperson for the survey.[^cite_note-232] 2018 research conducted by [YouGov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/YouGov) found that 56% of Christians in England and Wales described themselves as members of the Church of England or the Church in Wales.[^cite_note-233]

In 2023, 53% of Christians in the UK, or about 16.7 million people, identified themselves as Anglican, most in the Church of England.[^cite_note-234] The Church of England Yearbook, in 2024, reported 26 million members.[^cite_note-235] A 2025 YouGov survey claimed 21.5% of British respondents identified themselves as Anglican and that 25% of Britons had a favourable view of the Church of England.[^cite_note-236] However, a 2024 BSA survey found that only 11% of respondents, or approximately 7.6 million people, actively self-identified as Anglicans.[^cite_note-237]

Due to the Church of England's status as the [established](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_religion) church, in general, anyone may be married, have their children baptised or their funeral held in their local [parish church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_England_parish_church), regardless of whether they are baptised or regular churchgoers.[^cite_note-238] In 2017, research found that 20% of children continue to be baptised in the Church of England.[^cite_note-\:8-231]

Research published in 2022 showed that the large number of people baptised in the Church of England is due to the state church's "quasi-ethnic dimension, whereby the large numbers of the English population have been baptized (sic) Anglican, even though they otherwise engage minimally with the church or Christian faith. The C of E’s nominal membership is 24 million, but its...Sunday attendance was a small fraction of that figure."[^cite_note-239]

## Personnel

As of August 2026, the church reported that there were 18,075 active clergy. This was composed of 6,753 stipendiary (paid) ministry staff, as well as 2,500 SSM (self-supporting ministers), and 9,629 others (including retired but who could still officiate, and chaplains).[^cite_note-245] This was an increase from June 2025, when the church reported there were 17,885 active clergy serving. This was made up of 6,695 stipendiary ministry staff, 2,511 SSM, and 8,679 others.[^cite_note-246]

At the turn of the 20th century, in 1901, there were 23,670 stipendiary clergy.[^cite_note-\:7-247] By 1961, this had reduced to 15,391 individuals.[^cite_note-\:7-247] In 2020, there were almost 20,000 active clergy serving in the Church of England, including 7,200 retired clergy who continued to serve. In that year, 580 were ordained (330 in stipendiary posts and 250 in self-supporting parochial posts) and a further 580 ordinands began their training.[^cite_note-Ministry2020-248] In that year, 33% of those in ordained ministry were female, an increase from the 26% reported in 2016.[^cite_note-Ministry2020-248]

A decline in ministry numbers is expected to continue as nearly 50% of stipendiary clergy are aged 55 or older, as well as not enough new clergy being ordained to replace the large numbers retiring or leaving. In 2020, 591 were recommended for training, which was the highest for 13 years.[^cite_note-249] To maintain current levels, around 600 individuals would need to be ordained annually.[^cite_note-250] However, by 2025, the numbers had dropped back to 417 people recommended for training.[^cite_note-251] At current trends, the number of active stipendariary clergy will fall to about 5,400 in 2033.[^cite_note-252]

## Structure

See also: [Anglican ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_ministry) and [List of Church of England dioceses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Church_of_England_dioceses)



Dioceses of the Church of England&#x20;

&#x20; [Province of Canterbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Canterbury)

&#x20; [Province of York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_York)

Article XIX ('Of the Church') of the Thirty-nine Articles defines the church as follows:

> The [visible Church of Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_visible) is a congregation of faithful men, in which [the pure Word of God is preached, and the sacraments be duly ministered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marks_of_the_Church_\(Protestantism\)) according to Christ's ordinance in all those things that of necessity are requisite to the same.[^cite_note-253]

[The British monarch](47063.md) has the constitutional title of [Supreme Governor of the Church of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Governor_of_the_Church_of_England). The canon law of the Church of England states, "We acknowledge that the King's most excellent Majesty, acting according to the laws of the realm, is the highest power under God in this kingdom, and has supreme authority over all persons in all causes, as well ecclesiastical as civil."[^cite_note-254] In practice this power is often exercised through [Parliament](13964.md) and on the advice of the [Prime Minister](24150.md).

The [Church of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Ireland) and the [Church in Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_in_Wales) separated from the Church of England in 1869[^cite_note-irishchurchact-255] and 1920[^cite_note-wales-heritage-256] respectively and are autonomous churches in the Anglican Communion; Scotland's national church, the [Church of Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Scotland), is [Presbyterian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyterian), but the [Scottish Episcopal Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Episcopal_Church) is part of the Anglican Communion.[^cite_note-episcopal-history-257]

In addition to England, the jurisdiction of the Church of England extends to the [Isle of Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isle_of_Man), the [Channel Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_Islands) and a few parishes in [Flintshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flintshire), [Monmouthshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monmouthshire) and [Powys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powys) in Wales [which voted to remain with the Church of England rather than joining the Church in Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_England_border_polls_1915%E2%80%931916).[^cite_note-258] Expatriate congregations on the continent of Europe have become the [Diocese of Gibraltar in Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_Gibraltar_in_Europe).

The church is structured as follows (from the lowest level upwards):



The parish church of St Lawrence in [Toot Baldon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toot_Baldon) is typical of many small English village churches.

- [Parish](23623.md) is the most local level, often consisting of one church building (a [parish church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_England_parish_church)) and community, although many parishes are joining forces in a variety of ways for financial reasons. The parish is looked after by a parish [priest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_priest) who for historical or legal reasons may be called by one of the following offices: [vicar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vicar), [rector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rector_\(ecclesiastical\)), [priest in charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priest_in_charge), team rector, team vicar. The first, second, fourth and fifth of these may also be known as the 'incumbent'. The running of the parish is the joint responsibility of the incumbent and the [parochial church council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parochial_church_council) (PCC), which consists of the parish clergy and elected representatives from the congregation. The Diocese of Gibraltar in Europe is not formally divided into parishes.

- There are a number of local churches that do not have a parish. In urban areas there are a number of [proprietary chapels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proprietary_chapel) (mostly built in the 19th century to cope with urbanisation and growth in population). Also in more recent years there are increasingly [church plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_plant) and [fresh expressions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresh_expression) of church, whereby new congregations are planted in locations such as schools or pubs to spread the Gospel of Christ in non-traditional ways.



Map showing the [Diocese of Gibraltar in Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_Gibraltar_in_Europe) with the archdeaconries colour-coded

- [Deanery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deanery), *e.g.*, Lewisham or Runnymede. This is the area for which a [rural dean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rural_Dean) (or area dean) is responsible. It consists of a number of parishes in a particular district. The rural dean is usually the incumbent of one of the constituent parishes. The parishes each elect lay (non-ordained) representatives to the deanery [synod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synod). Deanery synod members each have a vote in the election of representatives to the diocesan synod.

- Archdeaconry, *e.g.*, the seven in the [Diocese of Gibraltar in Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_Gibraltar_in_Europe). This is the area under the jurisdiction of an [archdeacon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archdeacon). It consists of a number of deaneries.

- [Diocese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese), *e.g.*, [Diocese of Durham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_Durham), [Diocese of Guildford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_Guildford), [Diocese of St Albans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_St_Albans). This is the area under the jurisdiction of a diocesan bishop, *e.g.*, the bishops of Durham, Guildford and St Albans, and will have a cathedral. There may be one or more [suffragan bishops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suffragan_bishop) within the diocese who assist the diocesan bishop in his ministry, *e.g.*, in Guildford diocese, the Bishop of Dorking. In some very large dioceses a legal measure has been enacted to create "episcopal areas", where the diocesan bishop runs one such area himself and appoints "area bishops" to run the other areas as mini-dioceses, legally delegating many of his powers to the area bishops. Dioceses with episcopal areas include [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_London), [Chelmsford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_Chelmsford), [Leeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Diocese_of_Leeds), [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_Oxford) and [Southwark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Diocese_of_Southwark). The bishops work with an elected body of [lay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laity) and [ordained](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordained) representatives, known as the [Diocesan Synod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocesan_Synod), to run the diocese. A diocese is subdivided into a number of archdeaconries.

- [Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesiastical_province), *i.e.*, Canterbury or York. This is the area under the jurisdiction of an [archbishop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop), *i.e.* the archbishops of Canterbury and York. Decision-making within the province is the responsibility of the General Synod (see also above). A province is subdivided into dioceses.

- [Primacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primacy_of_Canterbury), *i.e.*, Church of England. The [archbishop of York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop_of_York)'s title of "Primate of England" is essentially honorific and carries with it no powers beyond those inherent in being [Archbishop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop) and [Metropolitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_bishop) of the [Province of York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_York).

  [^cite_note-EGM-259]

  &#x20;The [archbishop of Canterbury](2345.md), on the other hand, the "Primate of All England", has powers that extend over the whole of England, and also [Wales](69894.md)—for example, through the [Faculty Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_Faculties), the archbishop may grant a "special marriage licence" permitting the parties to marry otherwise than in a church: for example, in a school, college or university chapel;

  [^cite_note-260]

  &#x20;or anywhere, if one of the parties to the intended marriage is in danger of imminent death.

  [^cite_note-261]

  [^cite_note-262]

- [Royal peculiar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_peculiar), a small number of churches which are more closely associated with [the Crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Crown), for example [Westminster Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Abbey), and a very few more closely associated with the law which although conforming to the rites of the Church, are outside episcopal jurisdiction.

All rectors and vicars are [appointed by patrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advowson), who may be private individuals, corporate bodies such as cathedrals, colleges or trusts, or by the bishop or directly by the Crown. No clergy can be instituted and inducted into a parish without swearing the Oath of Allegiance to His Majesty, and taking the Oath of Canonical Obedience "in all things lawful and honest" to the bishop. Usually they are instituted to the benefice by the bishop and then inducted by the archdeacon into the possession of the benefice property—church and parsonage. [Curates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curate) (assistant clergy) are appointed by rectors and vicars, or if priests-in-charge by the bishop after consultation with the patron. Cathedral clergy (normally a [dean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_deans_in_the_Church_of_England) and a varying number of residentiary canons who constitute the cathedral chapter) are appointed either by the Crown, the bishop, or by the dean and chapter themselves. Clergy officiate in a diocese either because they hold office as beneficed clergy or are licensed by the bishop when appointed, or simply with permission.

### Primates



[Sarah Mullally](4356626.md), [Archbishop of Canterbury](2345.md) since January 2026

The most senior bishop of the Church of England is the [archbishop of Canterbury](2345.md), who is the [metropolitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_bishop) of the southern province of England, the Province of Canterbury. She has the status of Primate of All England, and is the focus of unity for the worldwide Anglican Communion of independent national or regional churches. [Dame Sarah Mullally](4356626.md) currently serves as Archbishop, succeeding [Justin Welby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justin_Welby), who resigned effective 6 January 2025.[^cite_note-263] Mullally is the first woman to hold this office.[^cite_note-264][^cite_note-265][^cite_note-266]

The second most senior bishop is the [archbishop of York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop_of_York), who is the metropolitan of the northern province of England, the Province of York. For historical reasons (relating to the time of York's control by the [Danes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danelaw))[^cite_note-267] he is referred to as the primate of England. [Stephen Cottrell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Cottrell) became Archbishop of York in 2020.[^cite_note-268] The [bishop of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_London), the [bishop of Durham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Durham) and the [bishop of Winchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Winchester) are ranked in the next three positions, insofar as the holders of those sees automatically become members of the [House of Lords](13658.md).[^cite_note-269][^cite_note-270]

### Diocesan bishops

The process of appointing diocesan bishops is complex, due to historical reasons balancing hierarchy against democracy, and is handled by the [Crown Nominations Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appointment_of_Church_of_England_bishops) which submits names to the prime minister (acting on behalf of the Crown) for consideration.[^cite_note-271]

### Representative bodies

Main article: [General Synod of the Church of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Synod_of_the_Church_of_England)

The Church of England has a legislative body, General Synod. This can create two types of legislation, [measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Church_of_England_measures) and [canons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_law). Measures have to be approved but cannot be amended by the [British Parliament](13964.md) before receiving [royal assent](153067.md) and becoming part of the law of England.[^cite_note-272] Although it is the [established](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_religion) church in England only, its measures must be approved by both Houses of Parliament including the non-English members. Canons require royal licence and royal assent, but form the law of the church, rather than the law of the land.[^cite_note-273]

Another assembly is the [Convocation of the English Clergy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convocations_of_Canterbury_and_York), which is older than the General Synod and its predecessor the Church Assembly. By the [Synodical Government Measure 1969](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synodical_Government_Measure_1969) almost all of the convocations' functions were transferred to the General Synod. Additionally, there are [Diocesan Synods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocesan_Synod) and [deanery synods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deanery#Anglican_usage), which are the governing bodies of the divisions of the Church.[^cite_note-274]

### House of Lords

Main article: [Lords Spiritual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lords_Spiritual)

Of the 42 diocesan [archbishops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop) and bishops in the Church of England, 26 are permitted to sit in the [House of Lords](13658.md). The archbishops of Canterbury and York automatically have seats, as do the bishops of [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_London), [Durham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Durham) and [Winchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Winchester). The remaining 21 seats are filled in order of seniority by date of [consecration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consecration). It may take a diocesan bishop a number of years to reach the House of Lords, at which point he or she becomes a [lord spiritual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lords_Spiritual). The [bishop of Sodor and Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Sodor_and_Man) and the [bishop of Gibraltar in Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Gibraltar_in_Europe) are not eligible to sit in the House of Lords as their dioceses lie outside the United Kingdom.[^cite_note-275]

### Crown Dependencies

Although they are not part of England or the United Kingdom, the Church of England is also the established church in the [Crown Dependencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_Dependencies) of the [Isle of Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isle_of_Man), the [Bailiwick of Jersey](15693.md) and the [Bailiwick of Guernsey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bailiwick_of_Guernsey). The Isle of Man has its own diocese of [Sodor and Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodor_and_Man), and the [bishop of Sodor and Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Sodor_and_Man) is an ex officio member of the [legislative council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislative_Council_of_the_Isle_of_Man) of the [Tynwald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tynwald) on the island.[^cite_note-276] Historically the [Channel Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_Islands) have been under the authority of the [bishop of Winchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Winchester), but this authority has temporarily been delegated to the [bishop of Dover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Dover) since 2015. In [Jersey](15693.md) the [dean of Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dean_of_Jersey) is a non-voting member of the [States of Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_of_Jersey). In [Guernsey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guernsey) the Church of England is the [established church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_religion), although the [dean of Guernsey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dean_of_Guernsey) is not a member of the [States of Guernsey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_of_Guernsey).[^cite_note-277]

## Funding and finances

Main article: [Properties and finances of the Church of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Properties_and_finances_of_the_Church_of_England)

Although an established church, the Church of England does not receive any direct government support for its operations. However, it does receive significant governement funding for building work, amounting to £23.6 million per year. Donations comprise its largest source of income, and it also relies heavily on the income from its various historic endowments. In 2005, the Church of England had estimated total outgoings of around £900 million.[^cite_note-278]

The Church of England manages an [investment portfolio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Investment_portfolio) which is worth more than £8 billion.[^cite_note-279]

## Online church directories

The Church of England runs *A Church Near You*, an online directory of churches. A user-edited resource, it currently lists more than 16,000 churches and has 20,000 editors in 42 dioceses.[^cite_note-280] The directory enables parishes to maintain accurate location, contact and event information, which is shared with other websites and [mobile apps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_app). The site allows the public to find their local worshipping community, and offers churches free resources,[^cite_note-A_Church_Near_You-281] such as hymns, videos and social media graphics.

The *Church Heritage Record* includes information on over 16,000 church buildings, including architectural history, archaeology, art history, and the surrounding natural environment.[^cite_note-282] It can be searched by elements including church name, diocese, date of construction, footprint size, [listing grade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Listed_building), and church type. The types of church identified include:

- Major Parish Church: "some of the most special, significant and well-loved places of worship in England", having "most of all" of the characteristics of being large (over 1,000msq), [listed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Listed_building) (generally grade I or II\*), having "exceptional significance and/or issues necessitating a conservation management plan" and having a local role beyond that of an average parish church. As of December 2021 there are 312 such churches in the database.
