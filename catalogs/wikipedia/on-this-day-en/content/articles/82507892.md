# International sporting sanctions during the Russian invasion of Ukraine

&#x20;The exclusion of [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sport_in_Russia) and [Belarusian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Belarus#Sport) athletes and teams from most international sports competitions, widely referred to as the **sanctions** or **boycott** on [Russian sport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sport_in_Russia), represents one of the most comprehensive responses by global sporting bodies to a [Russian invasion of Ukraine](79400268.md), which started on 24 February 2022. These measures began with a swift recommendation from the [International Olympic Committee](15147.md) (IOC) and were implemented by numerous federations within days.

Over the subsequent four years, the situation evolved from blanket exclusions to carefully conditioned [neutral participation for individuals](54764622.md), while team bans largely remained in place. The IOC lifted its ban in July 2026.

## Background

Since 1993, the [United Nations General Assembly](31957.md) has supported the IOC and the development of the [Olympic](22576.md) and [Paralympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paralympic_Games) by adopting a resolution every two years that calls on member states to observe the [Olympic Truce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Truce).[^cite_note-1]



UN General Assembly resolution A-RES-76-13

On 2 December 2021, at the 76th session of the UN General Assembly in New York, a resolution titled "Building a peaceful and better world through sport and the Olympic ideal" was adopted by consensus, with 173 of the 193 member states voting in favor.[^cite_note-\:6-2] The resolution called for the observance of the Olympic Truce for the [Beijing 2022 Winter Olympic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Winter_Olympics) and [Paralympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Winter_Paralympics), covering the period from seven days before the start of the Olympic Games until seven days after the conclusion of the Paralympic Games.[^cite_note-\:6-2]

Given that the Beijing Winter Olympics began on 4 February 2022, Russia initially observed the start of the truce. The truce period, however, was set to last until seven days after the end of the Winter Paralympics, which, according to the official schedule, concluded on 13 March 2022. Therefore, the Olympic Truce was in effect until 20 March 2022.

On 24 February 2022, Russia launched a military [invasion of Ukraine](79400268.md). On the same day, the IOC strongly condemned this violation of the Olympic Truce in an official statement.[^cite_note-3] The following day, the IOC Executive Board urged all international sports federations to cancel or relocate any sports events planned for Russia or Belarus due to the breach of the truce.[^cite_note-\:0-4][^cite_note-5] Furthermore, the IOC stipulated that no Russian or Belarusian flags or national anthems should be displayed at any international sports event.[^cite_note-\:0-4]

## Chronology of key suspensions

### 24 February



Saint Petersburg Arena after a [UEFA Champions League](44220.md) match in 2021

The [UEFA](32332.md) Executive Committee decided to relocate the final of the men's [Champions League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_UEFA_Champions_League_final) from the [Gazprom Arena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krestovsky_Stadium) in [Saint Petersburg](24320051.md) to the [Stade de France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stade_de_France).[^cite_note-6] On 28 February, UEFA decided to end its partnership with Gazprom across all competitions.[^cite_note-7]

The [ATP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_of_Tennis_Professionals) chose to move the [St. Petersburg Open](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Petersburg_Open) tournament.[^cite_note-St_Petersburg_Open-8]



The starting grid at the [2014 Russian Grand Prix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Russian_Grand_Prix)

[Formula 1](10854.md) suspended the [Russian Grand Prix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Grand_Prix), scheduled to take place in [Sochi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sochi) in September 2022.[^cite_note-9] World Champions [Sebastian Vettel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sebastian_Vettel) and [Max Verstappen](41758713.md) had previously called for the cancellation of the race.[^cite_note-10] On 3 March, the contract with the promoter was terminated, and it was stated that Russia would "not have a race in the foreseeable future".[^cite_note-11] Later, Formula 1 stopped official broadcasts in Russia[^cite_note-12] and blocked access to its website from Russian territory.[^cite_note-13]

### 25 February

The [International Federation of Sport Climbing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Federation_of_Sport_Climbing) (IFSC) announced the suspension of the [Boulder and Speed World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_IFSC_Climbing_World_Cup) in Moscow, 1–3 April, with intent to relocate and reschedule the event.[^cite_note-sus-14]

The [International Ski Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Ski_Federation) (FIS) announced that all remaining events in Russia were cancelled.[^cite_note-ISF-2022-02-25-15]

The [World Curling Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Curling_Federation) announced that the 2022 [European Curling Championship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Curling_Championship), scheduled for 19–26 November, will no longer be held in [Perm](389777.md).[^cite_note-16]

### 26 February

The owner of the English football club [Chelsea](7473.md), Russian national [Roman Abramovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Abramovich), announced he would hand "stewardship" of the club to the trustees of the team's charitable foundation in an attempt to shield it from future repercussions.[^cite_note-17]

Similarly, the major international boxing organizations ([IBF](241038.md), [WBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Boxing_Council), [WBA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Boxing_Association), [WBO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Boxing_Organization)) announced the suspension of championship events in Russia until further notice.

The [volleyball](32558.md) federations of France and Poland, the reigning Olympic and World champions respectively, declared they would withdraw from the men's [World Championship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_FIVB_Men's_Volleyball_World_Championship) if it were to be held in Russia.

### 27 February

The [International Swimming Federation](317029.md) (FINA) cancelled the [World Junior Championships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_FINA_World_Junior_Swimming_Championships) scheduled to be held in Russia and confirmed that no other international competitions would be held in the country until further notice.[^cite_note-18] Subsequently, the championship was moved to [Lima](85423.md), Peru.[^cite_note-Koos14Apr2022-19]



Vladimir Putin in [judo uniform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judogi)

The [International Judo Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Judo_Federation) (IJF) suspended Russian President [Vladimir Putin](32817.md) from his positions as Honorary President and Ambassador of the organization.[^cite_note-20]

### 28 February

The IOC issued a resolution in which it:[^cite_note-\:0-4]

1. Recommended that international sports federations not invite or permit the participation of Russian and Belarusian athletes and officials in international competitions.

2. Stated that where participation is unavoidable for logistical reasons, Russian or Belarusian individuals or teams should only be allowed as neutrals, without any national symbols, colors, flags, or anthems being displayed.

3. Reiterated its recommendation not to organize sports events in Russia and Belarus.

4. Withdrew the Olympic Order from key Russian government officials, including President Vladimir Putin

   [^cite_note-21]

   &#x20;(who had received the [Olympic Order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Order) in 2001).

The IOC said that the recommendation was made "in order to protect the integrity of global sports competitions and for the safety of all the participants", adding that it had acted with a "heavy heart".[^cite_note-22]

On the same day, [FIFA](11049.md) suspended Russian national teams from participating in the [2022 World Cup](17742072.md).[^cite_note-\:3-23][^cite_note-\:9-24] [UEFA](32332.md) concurrently announced the disqualification of [Russian clubs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Premier_League) from its international competitions.[^cite_note-\:3-23]

The [International Ice Hockey Federation](16760449.md) (IIHF) suspended all Russian and Belarusian national teams and clubs from its competitions across all categories until further notice.[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26]

### 1 March

[World Athletics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Athletics) announced the immediate exclusion of athletes and officials from [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Athletics_Federation) and [Belarus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belarus_Athletic_Federation) from all World Athletics Series events "for the foreseeable future".[^cite_note-27]

The [International Volleyball Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F%C3%A9d%C3%A9ration_Internationale_de_Volleyball) (FIVB) withdrew Russia's hosting rights of the 2022 Men's World Championship scheduled for August and September.[^cite_note-28]

The ski federations of the [Scandinavian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scandinavia) countries, [Norway](21241.md) and [Sweden](5058739.md), announced that Russian and Belarusian athletes would not be allowed to enter their territories to participate in competitions scheduled for the following month.

The [British government](25318118.md) declared that national sports teams from Russia and Belarus were considered "[persona non grata](521241.md)".

### 3 March

The [International Paralympic Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Paralympic_Committee) on 3 March banned Russian athletes from competing at the [2022 Winter Paralympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Winter_Paralympics).[^cite_note-\:7-29][^cite_note-\:8-30]

### 4–5 March

Following a 1 March meeting of the [World Motor Sport Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FIA_World_Motor_Sport_Council), the [Fédération Internationale de l'Automobile](248019.md) announced three days later the introduction of "circular-emergency measures". Such policies barred Russian and Belarusian drivers from competing in FIA-sanctioned races unless they agreed to conditions such as condemning the invasion and to not use their respective countries' emblems.[^cite_note-31] Those with [multiple citizenships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_citizenship) were not affected by the rule and could compete under their other nationality.[^cite_note-32]

Unlike its [auto racing](1022.md) counterpart, the [Fédération Internationale de Motocyclisme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F%C3%A9d%C3%A9ration_Internationale_de_Motocyclisme) imposed a full ban on Russian and Belarusian riders from international [motorcycle racing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motorcycle_racing) events. The decision came after the FIM Board of Directors met on 5 March.[^cite_note-33]

## National responses to the suspensions

Several National Olympic Committees (NOCs) publicly endorsed the IOC's recommendation to exclude Russian and Belarusian athletes from international competitions, including those of [Australia](4689264.md), [Croatia](5573.md), [Denmark](76972.md), [Ecuador](9334.md), [Netherlands](21148.md), [Portugal](23033.md), [Spain](26667.md), [Switzerland](26748.md) and the [United Kingdom](31717.md). In most cases, these endorsements were accompanied by practical measures restricting sporting relations with Russia and Belarus and relocating events previously scheduled in those countries.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColucciCottrell20228–10-34]

In Australia, the [NOC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Olympic_Committee) urged member federations not to invite Russian or Belarusian athletes and to withdraw from events involving them. Denmark's NOC and Sports Confederation publicly supported a comprehensive ban and called on clubs to sever sporting relations. Ecuador prohibited Russian and Belarusian athletes from competing in tournaments held on its territory.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColucciCottrell20228–10-34]

The [Netherlands' Olympic Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netherlands_Olympic_Committee) urged international federations to relocate events from Russia and Belarus and to exclude their athletes from competitions abroad. Spain introduced a ban on Russian national teams and athletes competing under the Russian flag in events organized on Spanish territory and requested Spanish federations to avoid participation in events held in Russia. Portugal's federations facilitated the temporary registration and support of Ukrainian athletes displaced by the war.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColucciCottrell20228–10-34]

Poland combined sporting and humanitarian measures: the [Polish Football Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_Football_Association) refused to play Russia in the [2022 FIFA World Cup qualification playoffs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_FIFA_World_Cup_qualification_\(UEFA\)) and supported regulatory changes allowing simplified registration of Ukrainian players in Polish leagues. Croatia provided training facilities for Ukrainian athletes and supported charity football matches involving Ukrainian clubs.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColucciCottrell20228–10-34]

In Sweden, the national sports confederation requested government funding to support Ukrainian refugees through sports programmes, while some federations recommended limiting contractual relations with Russian players. In the United Kingdom, government sanctions against Russian individuals had direct consequences for sport, most notably in the forced sale of Chelsea FC following sanctions imposed on Roman Abramovich; [Wimbledon](91195.md) also imposed a unilateral ban on Russian and Belarusian players in 2022.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColucciCottrell20228–10-34]

## Impact on sponsorship agreements

The suspension of Russian sport was accompanied in several countries by the termination or reassessment of sponsorship agreements involving Russian companies. While some states had limited exposure to Russian or Belarusian sponsors, others saw high-profile commercial relationships suspended.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColucciCottrell202212–13-35]

In the United Kingdom, [Manchester United](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_United_F.C.) terminated its sponsorship agreement with [Aeroflot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeroflot), and [Everton](91155.md) suspended partnerships with Russian companies linked to sanctioned individuals. In Italy, [AC Milan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AC_Milan) ended its partnership with the Russian betting company [FonBet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fonbet).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColucciCottrell202212–13-35]

Spanish clubs responded differently: [Real Madrid](26413.md) cancelled a regional sponsorship agreement with Fonbet and announced humanitarian donations related to the war, whereas [FC Barcelona](68187.md) maintained its sponsorship arrangement with the Russian betting company [1xBET](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1xBet). In Switzerland, the ice hockey club [EV Zug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EV_Zug) suspended its sponsorship agreement with [Nord Stream AG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nord_Stream_AG). At the European level, the [EHF Champions League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EHF_Champions_League) released clubs from certain obligations connected to sponsorship arrangements involving Nord Stream 2 AG.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColucciCottrell202212–13-35]

[Adidas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adidas) suspended its long-term kit partnership with the [Russian Football Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Football_Union), which first began in 2008. Adidas had provided all Russian teams with kits and had expanded the federation's replica kit sales in the retail market.[^cite_note-auto-36]

## Russian football suspension

Further information: [Russian Football Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Football_Union) and [Russia national football team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia_national_football_team)



[Aleksandr Golovin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleksandr_Golovin_\(footballer\)) (in red) during a [2022 FIFA World Cup qualifying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_FIFA_World_Cup_qualification) match between [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia_national_football_team) and [Slovenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slovenia_national_football_team), in Sochi, 27 March 2021

On 24 February 2022, the three teams in Russia's [2022 World Cup qualifying playoffs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_FIFA_World_Cup_qualification_%E2%80%93_UEFA_second_round#Path_B), namely the [Czech Republic](5321.md), [Poland](22936.md), and [Sweden](5058739.md), immediately declared their unwillingness to play any matches on Russian territory.[^cite_note-37] Within days, this stance hardened into a total boycott: Poland and Sweden extended their refusal to all qualifying games on 26 February, with the Czech Republic following suit the next day.[^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40] This collective decision effectively eliminated Russia from contention, as it prevented them from playing their playoff match against Poland, as well as any potential final against the Czech Republic or Sweden.

On 27 February, [FIFA](11049.md) announced a number of sanctions impacting Russia's participation in international football. Russia was prohibited from hosting international competitions, and the national team was ordered to play all home matches [behind closed doors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behind_closed_doors_\(sport\)) in neutral countries. Under these sanctions, Russia would not be allowed to compete under the country's name, flag, or national anthem; similarly to the Russian athletes' participation in events such as the Olympics,[^cite_note-41] the team would compete under the abbreviation of their national federation, the Russian Football Union (RFU), rather than "Russia".[^cite_note-42]

The next day, on 28 February, FIFA decided to suspend Russia from international competitions "until further notice", including its participation in the [2022 World Cup](17742072.md) in [Qatar](8238258.md).[^cite_note-\:3-23][^cite_note-\:9-24] [UEFA](32332.md) concurrently announced the disqualification of [Russian clubs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Premier_League) from its international competitions.[^cite_note-\:3-23]

By May, UEFA published clarifications on how the qualification cycle would continue without Russia — which included annulling results and recalculating groups in national and youth team tournaments.[^cite_note-43]

On 20 September, UEFA officially confirmed that Russia was not included in the draw for qualification for [Euro 2024](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UEFA_Euro_2024) precisely because all Russian teams remained suspended "until further notice". At the same time, it was publicly affirmed that Russia was excluded from the qualification process.[^cite_note-44]

The RFU unsuccessfully appealed the FIFA ban to the [Court of Arbitration for Sport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_Arbitration_for_Sport), which upheld the ban.[^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46] The final texts of the rulings and press releases acknowledge the situation as "extraordinary," as well as the high threshold for interfering in the autonomy of sports organizations when their measures are motivated by the conduct of competitions and safety, rather than a "punitive" purpose.[^cite_note-\:4-47][^cite_note-\:11-48] In the commission's view, it is regrettable that the current military operations in Ukraine, for which Russian football teams, clubs, and players bear no responsibility, have, as a result of the decisions by FIFA and UEFA, had such a negative impact on them and on Russian football as a whole. However, in the commission's opinion, these consequences were outweighed by the need to ensure the safe and orderly conduct of football events for the rest of the world.[^cite_note-\:4-47][^cite_note-\:11-48]

During the ban, talk circulated that the RFU were considering seeking to change confederations to the [Asian Football Confederation](715249.md), but this ultimately never happened.[^cite_note-49]

On 11 November 2024, a circular distributed by UEFA stated 192 group stage matches would take place, confirming Russia's exclusion from participating in the [2026 FIFA World Cup qualification](49459509.md).[^cite_note-50]

### U-17 return attempt

In October 2023, FIFA and UEFA lifted their ban on Russian U-17 teams, permitting them to rejoin international competitions.[^cite_note-51] In a statement, UEFA emphasized that "It is particularly aggrieving that, due to the enduring conflict, a generation of minors is deprived of its right to compete in international football". While UEFA President [Aleksander Čeferin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleksander_%C4%8Ceferin) ruled out any further softening of the ban regarding Russian adult teams, he stressed the organization's stance on youth: "Uefa is determined that this position will continue until the war is over and peace restored. But by banning children from our competitions, we not only fail to recognise and uphold a fundamental right for their holistic development but we directly discriminate against them".

This decision drew opposition from Ukraine and several other UEFA member associations.[^cite_note-52] The vice presidents from [England](9316.md), [Poland](22936.md) and [Wales](69894.md) refused to support the proposal and at least 12 of the 55 member stated that their teams would continue to refuse to play matches against Russia.[^cite_note-\:10-53] Two weeks later, UEFA abandoned the plan, stating that "the agenda point was withdrawn as no technical solution to allow Russian teams to play could be found".[^cite_note-\:10-53]

### Reactions

Some observers, while approving of the boycott of Russia, have pointed out that FIFA did not boycott [Saddam Hussein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saddam_Hussein)'s [Iraq](26215470.md) as an aggressor during the [Iran–Iraq War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Iraq_War),[^cite_note-54] Saudi Arabia for its [military intervention in Yemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saudi_Arabian%E2%80%93led_intervention_in_Yemen),[^cite_note-55] Qatar for its [human rights violations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights_in_Qatar),[^cite_note-56] or the [United States](3434750.md) for the actions of the US military during the [Iraq War](5043324.md).[^cite_note-57]

In September 2025, in an interview with Politico,[^cite_note-58] Aleksander Čeferin stated that the ban on Russian teams had not contributed to ending the war in Ukraine: "Now, the ban for Russian teams is, I think, three and a half years. Did the \[Ukraine] war stop? It didn't". Čeferin noted that "the situation in Russia and Ukraine, there was a super strong political pressure. Now it's more a pressure of the civil society than politicians, because politicians are obviously, when it comes to wars and victims, very pragmatic. I cannot say what will happen. There are talks about everything, but me personally, I'm against kicking the athletes out". Čeferin also supported the idea of reinstating Russian youth teams under 17 in European competitions: "I still think that the children should be treated differently because, you know, they are raised in fear and hatred".[^cite_note-59][^cite_note-60]

In February 2026, in an interview with [Sky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky_UK), FIFA President [Gianni Infantino](9286117.md) stated the need to consider the issue of Russia's return to participation in international football tournaments, and that the current ban had achieved nothing. "It has only generated more disappointment and hatred", Infantino stated.[^cite_note-61]

## Russian Olympic Committee suspension

Main article: [Russian Olympic Committee](25045856.md)

On 12 October 2023, the IOC issued a statement noting that after Russia began its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, the Russian Olympic Committee (ROC) unilaterally transferred four regions that were originally under the jurisdiction of the [National Olympic Committee of Ukraine](13688336.md): [Donetsk Oblast](494889.md), [Luhansk Oblast](494904.md), [Kherson Oblast](1273370.md), and [Zaporizhzhia Oblast](494936.md) to the ROC; at the time, its president said "I don't see any difficulties here".[^cite_note-\:1-62][^cite_note-63] The IOC stated that the ROC's unilateral action constituted a breach of the [Olympic Charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Charter) because it violated the territorial integrity of the [NOC of Ukraine](13688336.md), and further announced the immediate suspension of the membership of the ROC.[^cite_note-\:1-62] The IOC stated that as a result the ROC was no longer entitled to operate as a National Olympic Committee, and could not receive any funding from the Olympic Movement, and that the IOC reserved the right to decide about the participation of [individual neutral athletes](54764622.md) with a Russian passport in the [Olympic Games Paris 2024](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Summer_Olympics) and the [Olympic Winter Games Milano Cortina 2026](38416091.md).[^cite_note-\:1-62]

In February 2024, the arbitration confirmed the legality of this decision.[^cite_note-\:5-64]

On 7 May 2026, the IOC lifted its recommendations on restrictions against [Belarus Olympic Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belarus_Olympic_Committee), while maintaining the suspension on ROC.[^cite_note-65] In turn, several sports federations, including World Athletics and [International Tennis Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Tennis_Federation), stated that the suspension of Belarusian athletes would remain in force despite the IOC's recommendations.[^cite_note-66][^cite_note-67] On 7 July 2026, the IOC lifted its recommendations on restrictions against the ROC, after ensuring that the ROC does not conduct activities in the [Russian-occupied territories of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian-occupied_territories_of_Ukraine) or include regional sports organizations from there as its members.[^cite_note-68] Russian sports minister Mikhail Delyagin said that this will allow sporting federations to lift bans on Russian athletes in time for them to participate in the qualifying events for the [2028 Los Angeles Olympics](12695048.md).[^cite_note-69]

## Russian Paralympic Committee suspension

Main article: [Russian Paralympic Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Paralympic_Committee)

On 2 March 2022, the [International Paralympic Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Paralympic_Committee) (IPC) announced that the Russian Paralympic Committee (RPC) team designation would be banned and that Russian athletes could only compete at the [2022 Winter Paralympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Winter_Paralympics) under a fully neutral designation as in [2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Winter_Paralympics).[^cite_note-70] However, the very next day, after boycott threats from other nations, the IPC banned Russian athletes from competing entirely.[^cite_note-\:7-29][^cite_note-\:8-30] IPC President [Andrew Parsons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Parsons_\(sports_administrator\)) stated that the organisation had initially attempted to preserve the participation of athletes while protecting the integrity of the Games. However, after multiple national delegations threatened to boycott the Paralympics and raised concerns about the viability of the competitions, the IPC concluded that excluding Russian and Belarusian athletes had become the only workable solution to ensure the Games could proceed safely.[^cite_note-\:8-30][^cite_note-71]

On 16 November, the IPC again suspended the RPC at an extraordinary meeting of the IPC General Assembly.[^cite_note-72]

In September 2023 there was again partial suspension of RPC.[^cite_note-73]

### Membership reinstatement

On 27 September 2025, the IPC General Assembly fully reinstated the membership of the RPC, with the country returning to the Paralympic Games in [2026](41045653.md).[^cite_note-74] Despite mounting international pressure and boycott threats by several national Paralympic committees (such as Ukraine and the Czech Republic), the IPC confirmed it will uphold its decision to allow Russian and Belarusian athletes to compete under their national flags and anthems at the 2026 Winter Paralympics, with IPC President Andrew Parsons stating the decision is final and irreversible.[^cite_note-75] In addition, Andrew Parsons stated that injured Russian soldiers would be allowed to compete at a future Paralympic Games.[^cite_note-76]

## Recommendation of neutral athlete status

Further information: [Authorised Neutral Athletes](54764622.md) and [Independent Olympians at the Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_Olympians_at_the_Olympic_Games)



The final version of the AIN flag assigned by the IOC in March 2024

In 2023, the discussion about a complete ban began to shift into a discussion about a "neutral return". On 28 March 2023, the Olympic movement, citing a request from the Olympic Summit and four months of consultations, issued a set of recommendations for federations on the possible admission of athletes with Russian or Belarusian passports solely as Individual Neutral Athletes (AIN), but emphasized that the question of participation in Paris 2024 was "not under consideration".[^cite_note-\:2-77]

Admission to AIN is possible only on an individual basis, with teams being excluded; athletes and support personnel who "actively support the war" are cut off, as are those who have contracts with the military or national security bodies. The mandatory requirement for anti-doping compliance is also emphasized separately.[^cite_note-\:2-77]

In December 2023, the Olympic movement moved from general principles to "Olympic admission": it was announced that Russian and Belarusian athletes could participate in [Olympic Games Paris 2024](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Summer_Olympics) as neutrals, without flags, emblems, or anthems, with team sports being excluded.[^cite_note-78]

Russian officials publicly labeled the conditions as "discriminatory", while the Ukrainian side and a number of its allies repeatedly warned of the possibility of boycotting the Olympics if Russians and Belarusians were allowed to compete — even as neutrals.[^cite_note-79][^cite_note-80]

### 2024 Summer Olympics

Main article: [Individual Neutral Athletes at the 2024 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Individual_Neutral_Athletes_at_the_2024_Summer_Olympics)



[Mirra Andreeva](71915009.md) and [Diana Shnaider](68209798.md) on [2024 Summer Olympics women's doubles tennis tournament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tennis_at_the_2024_Summer_Olympics_%E2%80%93_Women's_doubles)

The scale of participation at [Paris 2024](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Summer_Olympics) turned out to be minimal compared to "pre-war" delegations: 32 neutral athletes (15 from Russia and 17 from Belarus) competed in the Games across 10 sports.[^cite_note-81]

Russian sports structures employed a "boycott" and "forced self-restrictions" (stemming from disagreement with the vetting process and criteria, as well as due to invitations not being extended to many leading athletes). For instance, the Russian Judo Federation sent no one to Paris after only four out of 17 contenders were approved to compete in neutral status, deeming such conditions humiliating and politically motivated.[^cite_note-82] A similar situation emerged in wrestling: in the summer of 2024, it was reported that a group of Russian wrestlers refused invitations to the Olympics amid disputes over who was allowed/not allowed to compete as neutrals and, consequently, over the "quality" of the Olympic tournament without the top athletes.[^cite_note-83]

At Paris 2024, the neutral athletes did not participate in the opening ceremony as a delegation. However, the Olympic authorities allowed them to take part in the closing ceremony, explaining this by stating that the closing was organized "as an athletes' event", not a parade of national teams, and that the flag of the neutral athletes would not be used.[^cite_note-84]

### 2026 Winter Olympics

Main article: [Individual Neutral Athletes at the 2026 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Individual_Neutral_Athletes_at_the_2026_Winter_Olympics)

At the [Milan-Cortina 2026 Winter Games](38416091.md), 13 Russian and 7 Belarusian athletes competed as Individual Neutral Athletes across sports including figure skating, cross-country skiing, and speed skating.[^cite_note-85] The same neutral conditions applied and Russians remained absent from the official medal table. Ukrainian officials boycotted related Paralympic ceremonies in protest at any Russian presence.[^cite_note-86]

## Courts and arbitration

The reaction of Russian sporting organisations, clubs and athletes to the invasion of Ukraine varied significantly; while some publicly distanced themselves from the political context, many Russian federations, clubs and individual athletes chose to challenge the measures imposed by international sports bodies, filing multiple appeals before the [Court of Arbitration for Sport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_Arbitration_for_Sport) (CAS) and the internal judicial bodies of international federations.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColucciCottrell202211-87] Russia's legal strategy developed along two principal lines: contesting specific federation decisions, particularly in football and certain individual sports, and disputing broader measures adopted within the Olympic movement concerning the status of the Russian Olympic Committee.

In football, the key body of decisions from 2022 illustrates the position of the CAS: the suspension was viewed as an administrative measure by the competition organizer, rather than a disciplinary punishment for violating "football" rules; the war and its consequences were described as a force majeure environment for holding competitions (sanctions, travel bans, refusal of opponents to play), while it was emphasized that an association's right to participate in tournaments is not absolute and may yield to the broader interests of the competition system. For instance, the text of the arbitration ruling in the case of the [Russian Football Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Football_Union) versus football authorities emphasizes the "high threshold" for interfering in the autonomy of sport and the legitimacy of exceptional measures under "the rarest of circumstances".[^cite_note-\:4-47]

Along the "Olympic" line, the case of 2023–2024 is indicative: the decision to suspend the Russian Olympic Committee for including regional organizations from occupied territories was challenged, but the arbitration upheld it. This demonstrates that even where the Olympic movement declares its intention not to punish individual athletes "for the actions of the state", institutional violations (in the terminology of the Olympic Charter—the territorial integrity of the NOC) are interpreted as an independent basis for sanctions against the NOC.[^cite_note-\:5-64][^cite_note-88]

In October 2025, the CAS issued a ruling that allowed Russian luge athletes to participate in qualifying competitions as neutral participants, after the [International Luge Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Luge_Federation) extended the existing exclusion of Russian athletes from its competitions in July.[^cite_note-89][^cite_note-90]

On 2 December, the CAS announced that it had partially upheld appeals against the decision of the [International Ski and Snowboard Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Ski_and_Snowboard_Federation) (FIS), which was a "blanket" exclusion of athletes based on nationality. The arbitrators indicated that the FIS statutes protect individuals from [discrimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrimination) and require political neutrality, and therefore a "blanket exclusion" (total non-admission "by passport", without assessing whether an individual meets the criteria for neutral status) is unlawful.[^cite_note-91][^cite_note-92][^cite_note-reuters_2025-12-02-93]

The practical effect of this ruling was not a "return under the flag", but rather a legal compulsion for the federation to open its qualification events and the Olympic pathway to neutral athletes, provided they meet the neutrality criteria (essentially, imposing the Individual Neutral Athlete model where the federation had attempted to maintain a complete suspension). This represents one of the few situations between 2022 and 2026 where the Russian side achieved a noticeable legal breakthrough in favor of participation, albeit in a "reduced" format.[^cite_note-reuters_2025-12-02-93]

Subsequent individual appeals, including one by cross-country skier [Alexander Bolshunov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Bolshunov) ahead of the 2026 Winter Olympics, also failed to overturn exclusions where evidence of war support existed.[^cite_note-94]

## Attempts at reinstatement

In boxing, the IBA decided as early as October 2022 to return Russians and Belarusians to competitions under their own flags, with national anthems played for gold medals, justifying this by citing political neutrality and equal treatment.[^cite_note-95][^cite_note-96]

In judo, the IJF announced at the end of 2025 its decision to again allow Russians to compete under their national flag, with the anthem and symbols at IJF tournaments, thus ending the period of the neutral regime in this sport.[^cite_note-97][^cite_note-98]

In chess, [FIDE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FIDE) in December 2025 reinstated Russian and Belarusian teams in official competitions and restored the rights of young players; however, for adult teams, restrictions on national symbols remain in place pending consultations with Olympic structures.[^cite_note-\:12-99]

On 11 December 2025 the IOC Olympic Summit supported its executive board's recommendation that youth athletes from Russia and Belarus no longer face access restrictions in individual or team youth competitions, with national flags and anthems permitted. Implementation was left to individual federations and was slated for consideration ahead of the [2026 Dakar Youth Olympic Games](64557687.md).[^cite_note-100]

## Legal analysis

According to Miguel Ángel Dávila, a lawyer and specialist in international and sports law, the sanctions can be seen as linked to Russia's breach of the [Olympic Truce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Truce) resolution adopted by the United Nations General Assembly in December 2021, which called on member states to refrain from hostilities during the Olympic and Paralympic Winter Games and thus provided a normative basis for the IOC to act after the invasion. Dávila argues that while some sanctions, particularly those adopted by the IOC, find legal support in the [Olympic Charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Charter), which empowers the IOC Executive Board and disciplinary bodies to impose measures in cases of violations of applicable rules, other federation actions (such as those by FIFA and UEFA) depend on broad interpretations of their governing statutes allowing urgent decisions by executive councils. Dávila contends that, although the sanctions clearly carry a [political dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_spectrum), they are not entirely devoid of legal grounding because they relate to the violation of prior international commitments and existing regulatory frameworks within the sport system.[^cite_note-146]

## Historical precedents

The exclusion of Russian sport following the 2022 invasion of Ukraine has drawn comparisons with earlier instances in which political conflicts affected international competition. The Olympic Games have previously been shaped by geopolitical crises: the 1916, 1940 and 1944 Games were cancelled due to the World Wars, while [Germany](11867.md) and [Japan](15573.md) were excluded from the [1948 Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1948_Summer_Olympics) because of their roles in [World War II](32927.md). [South Africa](17416221.md) was barred from Olympic participation during the [apartheid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apartheid) era, and in 2020 Russia was sanctioned over a state-sponsored [doping scheme](55980489.md), although individual athletes were allowed to compete under a neutral status.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColucciCottrell202212-147]

The Olympic Games have also been subject to formal boycotts, most notably in [1980](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980_Summer_Olympics) and [1984](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1984_Summer_Olympics), though these involved state-led refusals to participate rather than institutional suspensions imposed by governing bodies.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColucciCottrell202212-147]

Beyond the Olympic context, sporting sanctions have occasionally followed international political decisions. In 1992, following [United Nations Security Council Resolution 757](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Security_Council_Resolution_757), sports organizations implemented a boycott of the [Federal Republic of Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Republic_of_Yugoslavia) during the [Yugoslav wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yugoslav_Wars), affecting participation in European football competitions. In another politically sensitive case, [Malaysia](3607937.md) was stripped in 2019 of the right to host the [World Para Swimming Championships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Para_Swimming_Championships) after refusing to grant entry to [Israeli](9282173.md) athletes, and in 2021 the cancellation of the [World Men's Team Squash Championship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Squash_Team_Championships) in [Kuala Lumpur](16854.md) prompted the IOC to warn that host nations must guarantee non-discriminatory access for athletes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEColucciCottrell202212-147]

Another example often cited in discussions of the [Olympic Truce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Truce) occurred in 2026, when airstrikes carried out by the [United States and Israel against Iran](82533582.md) on 28 February took place during the period covered by the United Nations–endorsed Olympic Truce for the [Milan–Cortina Winter Olympic](38416091.md) and [Paralympic Games](41045653.md). The incident drew attention because [Russia's invasion of Ukraine](79400268.md) in February 2022 had likewise occurred during the Olympic–Paralympic period and was cited by the IOC as one of the factors behind the unprecedented sporting sanctions imposed on Russian and Belarusian athletes. Despite the 2026 strikes being widely described as a breach of the symbolic truce, the IOC did not consider punitive measures against the countries involved and reiterated that the Olympic Truce is a non-binding resolution and that the Olympic movement generally avoids direct involvement in geopolitical conflicts. Observers noted that the contrasting reactions highlighted the largely symbolic nature of the Olympic Truce and the discretionary character of sanctions within international sport governance.[^cite_note-148][^cite_note-149][^cite_note-150]
