**Malaria** is a [mosquito-borne infectious disease](21054623.md) that is transmitted by the bite of *[Anopheles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anopheles)* [mosquitoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosquito).[^cite_note-WHO-Vector-2023-5][^cite_note-WHO-Factsheet-2025-3] The [symptoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signs_and_symptoms) of human malaria typically include [fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fever), [fatigue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatigue_\(medical\)), [vomiting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vomiting) and [headaches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Headache).[^cite_note-Caraballo-2014-1][^cite_note-6] In severe cases, the disease can cause [jaundice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaundice), [seizures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epileptic_seizure), [coma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coma), or [death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death).[^cite_note-Caraballo-2014-1][^cite_note-7] Symptoms usually begin 10 to 15 days after being bitten by an infected [*Anopheles* mosquito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anopheles_mosquito).[^cite_note-WHO-Factsheet-2025-3][^cite_note-CDC-FAQ-2023-8] If not properly treated, people may have recurrences of the disease months later.[^cite_note-WHO-Factsheet-2025-3] Those who survive an infection develop partial [immunity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunity_\(medicine\)), being susceptible to reinfection although with milder symptoms.[^cite_note-Caraballo-2014-1] This partial [resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunity_\(medical\)) disappears over months to years if the person has no continuing exposure to malaria.[^cite_note-Caraballo-2014-1]

Malaria is caused by single-celled [parasites](43937.md) of the genus *[Plasmodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium),*[^cite_note-WHO-Factsheet-2025-3] generally spread through the bites of an infected female *Anopheles* mosquito.[^cite_note-WHO-Factsheet-2025-3][^cite_note-9] The mosquito bite introduces the parasites from the mosquito's [saliva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saliva) into the blood.[^cite_note-WHO-Factsheet-2025-3] The parasites initially reproduce and mature in the [liver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liver) without causing symptoms.[^cite_note-10] After a few days the mature parasites spread into the bloodstream, where they infect and destroy [red blood cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_blood_cell), causing the symptoms of infection.[^cite_note-11] Five species of *Plasmodium* commonly infect humans.[^cite_note-WHO-Factsheet-2025-3] The three species associated with more severe cases are *[P. falciparum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium_falciparum)* (which is responsible for the vast majority of malaria deaths), *[P. vivax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium_vivax)* and *[P. knowlesi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium_knowlesi)* (a [simian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simian) malaria that spills over into thousands of people a year).[^cite_note-WHO-Factsheet-2025-3][^cite_note-12] *[P. ovale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium_ovale)* and *[P. malariae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium_malariae)* generally cause a milder form of malaria.[^cite_note-Caraballo-2014-1][^cite_note-WHO-Factsheet-2025-3] Malaria is typically diagnosed by the [microscopic examination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Histology) of blood using [blood films](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_film), or with [antigen-based](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria_antigen_detection_tests) [rapid diagnostic tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapid_diagnostic_test).[^cite_note-Caraballo-2014-1] Methods that use the [polymerase chain reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymerase_chain_reaction) to detect the parasite's [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA) have been developed, but they are not widely used in areas where malaria is common, due to their cost and complexity.[^cite_note-Nadjm-2012-13]

The risk of disease can be reduced by preventing mosquito bites through the use of [mosquito nets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosquito_net) and [insect repellents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insect_repellent) or with [mosquito-control measures](2887318.md) such as spraying [insecticides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insecticide) and draining [standing water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standing_water).[^cite_note-Caraballo-2014-1] Several [prophylactic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preventive_healthcare) medications are available to [prevent malaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria_prophylaxis) in areas where the disease is common.[^cite_note-WHO-Factsheet-2025-3] As of 2023, two [malaria vaccines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria_vaccine) have been endorsed by the [World Health Organization](33583.md).[^cite_note-WHO_recommends_R21-2023-14] The parasites have evolved resistance to several [antimalarial medications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimalarial_medication); for example, [chloroquine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroquine)-resistant *P. falciparum* has spread to most malaria-prone areas and resistance to artemisinin has become a problem in some parts of [Southeast Asia](28741.md).[^cite_note-WHO-Factsheet-2025-3] Because of this, drug treatment for malaria infection should be tailored to best fit the [*Plasmodium* species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Plasmodium_species) involved and the geographical location where the infection was acquired.[^cite_note-15]

The disease is widespread in the [tropical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropics) and [subtropical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtropical) regions that exist in a broad band around the [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator).[^cite_note-16][^cite_note-Caraballo-2014-1] This includes much of [sub-Saharan Africa](27067.md), [Asia](689.md) and [Latin America](18524.md).[^cite_note-WHO-Factsheet-2025-3] In 2024, some 282 million cases of malaria worldwide resulted in an estimated 610,000 deaths.[^cite_note-WHO-Report-2025a-4] Around 95% of the cases and deaths occurred in sub-Saharan Africa.[^cite_note-WHO-Report-2025a-4][^cite_note-WHO-Briefing-2024-17] Malaria is commonly associated with [poverty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poverty) and has a significant negative effect on economic development;[^cite_note-Gollin-2007-18][^cite_note-Worrall-2005-19] in 2005 it was estimated that [Africa](5334607.md) loses US\$12 billion a year due to increased healthcare costs, lost ability to work and adverse effects on [tourism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism).[^cite_note-Greenwood-2005-20]

## Etymology

The word *malaria* originates from [Medieval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) [Italian](14708.md): *mala aria*, 'bad air', a part of [miasma theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miasma_theory); the disease was formerly called *ague, paludism* or *marsh fever* due to its association with swamps and marshland.[^cite_note-21] The word appeared in English at least as early as 1768.[^cite_note-22] The scientific study of malaria is malariology.[^cite_note-23]

## Signs and symptoms



Main symptoms of malaria

[^cite_note-Fairhurst-2015-24]

Symptoms during the early stages of malaria infection are fever, chills, headache, nausea and vomiting and diarrhoea; more serious cases may show enlarged [spleen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Splenomegaly) or [liver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepatomegaly) and mild [jaundice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaundice).[^cite_note-Crutcher-1996-25][^cite_note-26] These symptoms can resemble other conditions such as [sepsis](158400.md), [gastroenteritis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastroenteritis), [flu](19572217.md) and other [viral diseases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viral_disease).[^cite_note-Nadjm-2012-13][^cite_note-Bartoloni-2012-27] Without treatment, symptoms - particularly the fever - can settle into a regular pattern, recurring every two or three days ([paroxysmal attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paroxysmal_attack)).[^cite_note-ECDC-Information-2024-28][^cite_note-Crutcher-1996-25]

Symptoms typically begin 10–15 days after the initial mosquito bite, but can occur as late as several months after infection. Travellers taking preventative malaria medications may develop symptoms once they stop taking the drugs.[^cite_note-CDC-Features-2024-29]

Severe malaria occurs when the Plasmodium infection causes damage to vital organs such as the kidney, liver, lungs or brain. Symptoms include severe [anaemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaemia), jaundice, [convulsions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convulsion), confusion, [coma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coma), [kidney failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kidney_failure) and eventually death.[^cite_note-CDC-Features-2024-29][^cite_note-ECDC-Information-2024-28] Severe malaria is usually caused by *[P. falciparum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium_falciparum)*;[^cite_note-Crutcher-1996-25] it should be treated as a medical emergency.[^cite_note-30]

### Complications

A unique feature of *P. falciparum* is its ability to generate adhesive proteins on the surface of infected red blood cells (RBC). Infected RBCs obstruct capillaries (causing [hypoxia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypoxia_\(medicine\))) and accumulate in vital organs, interfering with their function.[^cite_note-31] *P. falciparum* infection underlies most severe complications of malaria.[^cite_note-WHO-Factsheet-2025-3]

Cerebral malaria is a form of severe malaria affecting the brain. Infected RBCs blocking capillaries in the brain trigger an immune reaction, which in turn damages the [blood-brain barrier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood%E2%80%93brain_barrier).[^cite_note-32] Individuals with cerebral malaria exhibit [neurological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurological_disorder) symptoms, such as confusion, [seizures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seizure), or [coma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coma).[^cite_note-Bartoloni-2012-27] Cerebral malaria, if untreated, can lead to death within forty-eight hours of the first symptoms; survivors may have long-term neurological damage.[^cite_note-Bartoloni-2012-27][^cite_note-ECDC-Information-2024-28]

Severe [anaemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaemia) is caused by a combination of the destruction of RBCs (both infected and uninfected) together with reduced [RBC production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erythropoiesis) in the bone marrow; it is a major cause of deaths in children under 5.[^cite_note-33]

Malaria can lead to [acute respiratory distress syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_respiratory_distress_syndrome) in up to 25% of cases. It is caused by damage to the capillary endothelium, in turn damaging the [alveoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alveolus) of the lung.[^cite_note-Greenwood-2005-20][^cite_note-Taylor-2012-34] Symptoms are extreme shortness of breath and a bluish tinge to the lips ([cyanosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanosis)) indicating lack of oxygen.[^cite_note-Taylor-2012-34] It is a leading killer of adults, with around 40% mortality once symptoms begin.[^cite_note-Taylor-2012-34][^cite_note-35]

[Coinfection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coinfection) of [HIV](14170.md) with malaria increases mortality.[^cite_note-Korenromp-2005-36]

Other complications include an [enlarged spleen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Splenomegaly), [enlarged liver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepatomegaly) or [both of these](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepatosplenomegaly).[^cite_note-Bartoloni-2012-27] So-called [blackwater fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackwater_fever) occurs when haemoglobin from [lysed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lysis) red blood cells leaks into and discolours the urine; this often precedes [kidney failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kidney_failure).[^cite_note-Bartoloni-2012-27]

[Malaria during pregnancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pregnancy-associated_malaria) can cause [stillbirths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stillbirth), [infant mortality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infant_mortality), [miscarriage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miscarriage) and [low birth weight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_birth_weight),[^cite_note-Hartman-2010-37] particularly in *P. falciparum* infection, but also with *P. vivax*.[^cite_note-Rijken-2012-38]

## Cause



The life cycle of malaria parasites: Sporozoites are introduced by a mosquito bite. When they reach the liver, they multiply into thousands of merozoites. The merozoites infect red blood cells and replicate, infecting more and more red blood cells. Some parasites form gametocytes, which are taken up by a mosquito, continuing the life cycle.

Malaria is caused by infection with [parasites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasite) in the genus *[Plasmodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium)*, which are transmitted between the human hosts by mosquitoes in the genus *[Anopheles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anopheles)*.[^cite_note-CDC-About-2022-39]

### Life cycle

The plasmodium parasite has a complex life cycle involving human and mosquito hosts, taking a different form at each stage of the cycle.[^cite_note-Arrow-2004-40]

- The *Anopheles* mosquitoes initially get infected by *Plasmodium* by taking a blood meal from a previously infected person. The next time the mosquito feeds, its bite introduces *Plasmodium*—in a mobile form called [sporozoites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sporozoite)—into a new human host.

  [^cite_note-Arrow-2004-40]

- Within the human host, the sporozoites enter the bloodstream and travel to the [liver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liver), where they invade liver cells ([hepatocytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepatocyte)).

  [^cite_note-Cowman-2016-41]

- They grow and divide in the liver, with each infected hepatocyte eventually harboring up to 40,000 parasites.

  [^cite_note-Cowman-2016-41]

  &#x20;After 5 to 25 days the infected hepatocytes break down, releasing *Plasmodium*—in a smaller form called [merozoites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merozoite), into the bloodstream.

  [^cite_note-Arrow-2004-40]

- In the blood, the merozoites rapidly invade individual [red blood cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_blood_cell), with each replicating over 24–72 hours to form 16–32 new merozoites.

  [^cite_note-Cowman-2016-41]

  &#x20;The infected red blood cell bursts, releasing new merozoites which again infect red blood cells, resulting in a cycle that continuously amplifies the number of parasites in an infected person.

  [^cite_note-Cowman-2016-41]

- A small portion of parasites do not replicate, but instead develop into early sexual stage parasites called male and female [gametocytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gametocyte). These gametocytes develop in the [bone marrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bone_marrow) for 11 days, then return to the blood circulation to await uptake by the bite of another mosquito.

  [^cite_note-Cowman-2016-41]

- Once inside a mosquito, the gametocytes undergo sexual reproduction, and eventually form daughter sporozoites that migrate to the mosquito's [salivary glands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salivary_gland) to be injected into a new host when the mosquito next bites.

  [^cite_note-Cowman-2016-41]

  [^cite_note-Arrow-2004-40]

The liver infection causes no symptoms; all symptoms of malaria result from the infection of red blood cells.[^cite_note-Ashley-2018-42] Symptoms develop once there are more than around 100,000 parasites per [milliliter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milliliter) of blood.[^cite_note-Ashley-2018-42] Many of the symptoms associated with severe malaria are caused by the tendency of *P. falciparum* to bind to [blood vessel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_vessel) walls, resulting in damage to the affected vessels and surrounding tissue. Parasites sequestered in the blood vessels of the lung contribute to [respiratory failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Respiratory_failure). In the brain, they contribute to [coma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coma). During pregnancy they accumulate in the [intervillous space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intervillous_space) and hinder the function of the placenta, contributing to low birthweight and preterm labor, and increasing the risk of abortion and stillbirth.[^cite_note-Ashley-2018-42] The destruction of red blood cells during infection often results in anaemia, exacerbated by reduced production of new red blood cells during infection.[^cite_note-Ashley-2018-42]

Only female mosquitoes feed on blood; male mosquitoes feed on plant nectar and do not transmit the disease. Females of the mosquito genus *Anopheles* prefer to feed at night. They usually start searching for a meal at dusk, and continue through the night until they succeed.[^cite_note-Arrow-2004-40] However, they may be adapting to the extensive use of bed nets and beginning to bite earlier, before bed-nets are deployed.[^cite_note-43] Malaria parasites can also be transmitted by [blood transfusions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_transfusion), although this is rare.[^cite_note-Owusu-Ofori-2010-44]

### *Plasmodium* species

In humans, malaria is caused by six *Plasmodium* species: *[P. falciparum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium_falciparum)*, *[P. malariae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium_malariae)*, *[P. ovale curtisi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium_ovale_curtisi)*, *[P. ovale wallikeri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium_ovale_wallikeri)*, *[P. vivax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium_vivax)* and *[P. knowlesi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium_knowlesi)*.[^cite_note-Ashley-2018-42] Among those infected, *P. falciparum* is the most common species identified (\~75%) followed by *P. vivax* (\~20%).[^cite_note-Nadjm-2012-13] *P. falciparum* is prevalent in Africa and accounts for the majority of deaths,[^cite_note-Sarkar-2009-45] while *P. vivax* is dominant outside Africa.[^cite_note-Arnott-2012-46][^cite_note-WHO-Factsheet-2025-3] Some cases have been documented of human infections with several species of *Plasmodium* from [higher apes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simian), but except for *P. knowlesi*—a [zoonotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoonosis) species that causes malaria in [macaques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macaques)[^cite_note-Collins-2012-47]—these are mostly of limited public health importance.[^cite_note-Collins-2009-48]

Two species—*P. vivax* and *P. ovale*—form a dormant stage called a [hypnozoite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apicomplexan_life_cycle) which can persist in the liver, even after drug treatment has eliminated the infection from the blood. These can reactivate after weeks or months and cause relapse of the disease.[^cite_note-Arrow-2004-40]

### Recurrent malaria

Symptoms of malaria can recur after varying symptom-free periods. Depending upon the cause, recurrence can be classified as either [recrudescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recrudescence), [relapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relapse), or reinfection. Recrudescence is when symptoms return after a symptom-free period due to failure to remove blood-stage parasites by adequate treatment.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWHO20154-49] Relapse is when symptoms reappear after the parasites have been eliminated from the blood but have persisted as dormant [hypnozoites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium#Life_cycle)[^cite_note-50] in liver cells. Reinfection means that parasites were eliminated from the entire body but a new infection has established. Recurrence of infection within two weeks of treatment ending is typically attributed to treatment failure.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWHO201541-51]

## Pathophysiology

Further information: [Plasmodium falciparum § Pathogenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium_falciparum#Pathogenesis)



Electron micrograph of a *Plasmodium falciparum*-infected red blood cell (center), illustrating adhesion protein "knobs"

Malaria infection develops via two phases: one that involves the [liver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liver) (exoerythrocytic phase), and one that involves red blood cells, or [erythrocytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erythrocyte) (erythrocytic phase). When an infected mosquito pierces a person's skin to take a blood meal, sporozoites in the mosquito's saliva enter the bloodstream and migrate to the liver where they infect hepatocytes, multiplying asexually and asymptomatically for a period of 8–30 days.[^cite_note-Bledsoe-2005-52] During this time, these organisms [differentiate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_differentiation) to yield thousands of merozoites, which, following rupture of their host cells, escape into the blood and infect red blood cells to begin the erythrocytic stage of the life cycle.[^cite_note-Bledsoe-2005-52]

The first phase of infection is asymptomatic; the clinical symptoms of malaria are all associated with the merozoite stage of the life cycle.[^cite_note-CDC-Features-2024-29] In this, the parasites multiply asexually within red blood cells, periodically breaking out to infect new ones. Within each infected erythrocyte, the parasite multiplies, consuming the cytoplasm as it does. After a period of 2 or 3 days, the erythrocyte bursts, releasing a number (between 16 and 32) of new merozoites.[^cite_note-53] This release of merozoites into the bloodstream, together with their waste products and fragments of erythrocyte, triggers fever and other symptoms which can be periodic and intense.[^cite_note-Bledsoe-2005-52][^cite_note-CDC-Features-2024-29]

In some species of *Plasmodium*, some sporozoites do not immediately develop into exoerythrocytic-phase merozoites, but instead, produce [hypnozoites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apicomplexan_life_cycle) that remain dormant for periods ranging from several months (7–10 months is typical) to several years.[^cite_note-White-2011-54] After a period of dormancy, they reactivate and produce merozoites. Hypnozoites are responsible for long incubation and late relapses in *P. vivax* and *P. ovale* infections.[^cite_note-White-2011-54][^cite_note-55]

### Immune system evasion

Immune evasion is a key feature of Plasmodium, underlying its success and persistence as a parasite.[^cite_note-56] Approximately 10% of the *Plasmodium* genome is dedicated to mechanisms which avoid or subvert the immune system.[^cite_note-57]

#### Liver



Illustration of a merosome budding off from an infected hepatocyte

Specialised [macrophages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macrophage) called [Kupffer cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kupffer_cell) defend the liver; they identify alien material in the bloodstream and destroy it. Sporozoites attack Kupffer cells and neutralise them.[^cite_note-Pradel-2001-58] They transit through these impaired cells (which die after a few hours) to infect [hepatocytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepatocyte).[^cite_note-Pradel-2001-58] Within the hepatocyte, they generate thousands of merozoites within a [vacuole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuole) which protects them from [cellular defence mechanisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Innate_immune_system).[^cite_note-Ezema-2023-59] After a few days, the infected hepatocyte releases merozoites in batches called merosomes, which bud off from the hepatocyte's membrane. This membrane cloaks the merozoites, enabling them to sneak past the remaining Kupffer cells to exit the liver.[^cite_note-60][^cite_note-Ezema-2023-59][^cite_note-Vaughan-2008-61]

#### Circulation



An illustration of infected erythrocytes (IE) clustering in the intervillous space around placental villi.

Free merozoites in the blood circulation system are vulnerable to attack by leucocytes which target their surface antigens. The parasite defeats this defence by means of [antigenic polymorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymorphism_\(biology\));[^cite_note-62] at each stage of the life cycle it expresses a different variant of surface antigen, effectively a moving target which outpaces the [adaptive immune system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaptive_immune_system).[^cite_note-Ezema-2023-59] Once they penetrate a red blood cell (RBC) merozoites have a safe haven, masked from leucocytes and protected within a vacuole. Infected RBCs can nevertheless be detected and destroyed, either by the [spleen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spleen) or by [phagocytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phagocyte).[^cite_note-Lee-2022-63] To avoid this fate, the merozoite generates adhesive proteins which appear as knobs on the surface of infected RBCs.[^cite_note-Tilley-2011-64] These work in two ways. They bind to uninfected RBCs forming clumps - nicknamed "rosettes" - in which the infected cell at the centre is shielded by the uninfected cells surrounding it; rosettes interfere with normal blood flow in capillaries.[^cite_note-Lee-2022-63] Alternatively infected RBCs can avoid passage through the spleen by adhering (sequestering) to the walls of blood vessels in tissues such as the brain, lungs, and [intervillous spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intervillous_space) (in [pregnancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pregnancy-associated_malaria)).[^cite_note-65] Both sequestered and rosetted types interfere with the normal organ functions, leading to complications such as cerebral malaria and pregnancy-associated malaria.[^cite_note-Ezema-2023-59]

### Genetic resistance

Main article: [Human genetic resistance to malaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_genetic_resistance_to_malaria)

Due to the high levels of [mortality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death) and [morbidity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morbidity) caused by malaria—especially the *P. falciparum* species—it has placed the greatest [selective pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selection_\(biology\)) on the [human genome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_genome) in recent history. Several genetic factors provide some resistance to it including [sickle cell trait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sickle_cell_trait), [thalassaemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thalassaemia) traits, [glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucose-6-phosphate_dehydrogenase_deficiency), and the absence of [Duffy antigens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duffy_antigen) on red blood cells.[^cite_note-66][^cite_note-Kwiatkowski-2005-67][^cite_note-Hedrick-2011-68]

The effect of sickle cell trait on malaria immunity illustrates some evolutionary trade-offs that have occurred because of endemic malaria. Sickle cell trait causes a change in the haemoglobin molecule in the blood. Normally, red blood cells have a very flexible, biconcave shape that allows them to move through narrow [capillaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capillaries); however, when the modified [haemoglobin S](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemoglobin_S) molecules are exposed to low amounts of oxygen, or crowd together due to dehydration, they can stick together forming strands that cause the cell to distort into a curved sickle shape. In these strands, the molecule is not as effective in taking or releasing oxygen, and the cell is not flexible enough to circulate freely. In the early stages of malaria, the parasite can cause infected red cells to sickle, and so they are removed from circulation sooner. This reduces the frequency with which malaria parasites complete their life cycle in the cell. Individuals who are [homozygous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homozygous) (with two copies of the abnormal haemoglobin beta [allele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allele)) have [sickle-cell anaemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sickle-cell_anaemia), while those who are heterozygous (with one abnormal allele and one normal allele) experience resistance to malaria without severe anaemia. Although the shorter life expectancy for those with the homozygous condition would tend to disfavour the trait's survival, the trait is preserved in malaria-prone regions because of the [benefits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterozygote_advantage) provided by the heterozygous form.[^cite_note-Hedrick-2011-68][^cite_note-Weatherall-2008-69]

## Diagnosis

Main article: [Diagnosis of malaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagnosis_of_malaria)



The blood film is the [gold standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_standard_\(test\)) for malaria diagnosis.



Ring-forms and [gametocytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gametocyte) of *Plasmodium falciparum* in human blood

Due to the non-specific nature of malaria symptoms, diagnosis is typically suspected based on symptoms and travel history, then confirmed with a laboratory test to detect the presence of the parasite in the blood (parasitological test). In areas where malaria is common, the [World Health Organization](33583.md) (WHO) recommends clinicians suspect malaria in any person who reports having fevers, or who has a current temperature above 37.5 °C without any other obvious cause.[^cite_note-WHO-2021a-70] Malaria should be suspected in children with signs of [anaemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaemia): [pale palms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palmar_pallor) or a laboratory test showing [haemoglobin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haemoglobin) levels below 8 [grams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gram) per [deciliter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deciliter) of blood.[^cite_note-WHO-2021a-70] In areas of the world with little to no malaria, testing is only recommended for people with possible exposure to malaria (typically travel to a malaria-endemic area) and unexplained fever.[^cite_note-WHO-2021a-70][^cite_note-CDC-Diagnosis-2024c-71]

Malaria is usually confirmed by the microscopic examination of [blood films](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_film) or by [antigen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antigen)-based [rapid diagnostic tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria_antigen_detection_tests) (RDT). Microscopy—i.e. examining [Giemsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giemsa)-stained blood with a [light microscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_microscope)—is the [gold standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_standard_\(test\)) for malaria diagnosis.[^cite_note-Ashley-2018-42] Microscopists typically examine both a "thick film" of blood, allowing them to scan many blood cells in a short time, and a "thin film" of blood, allowing them to clearly see individual parasites and identify the infecting *Plasmodium* species.[^cite_note-Ashley-2018-42] Under typical field laboratory conditions, a microscopist can detect parasites when there are at least 100 parasites per [microliter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microliter) of blood, which is around the lower range of symptomatic infection.[^cite_note-WHO-2021a-70] Microscopic diagnosis is relatively resource intensive, requiring trained personnel, specific equipment and a consistent supply of [microscopy slides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microscope_slide) and stains.[^cite_note-WHO-2021a-70]

[Rapid diagnostic tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapid_diagnostic_test) (RDTs) can be used to confirm a microscopic diagnosis;[^cite_note-CDC-Diagnosis-2024c-71] they are also used in places where microscopy is unavailable. RDTs are fast and easily deployed to places without full diagnostic laboratories.[^cite_note-WHO-2021a-70] The test detects parasite proteins in a [fingerstick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fingerstick) blood sample.[^cite_note-WHO-2021a-70] A variety of RDTs are available, targeting the parasite proteins [lactate dehydrogenase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactate_dehydrogenase), [aldolase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldolase), or histidine rich protein 2 (HRP2, which is specific to *P. falciparum* only).[^cite_note-WHO-2021a-70] The HRP2 test is widely used in Africa, where *P. falciparum* predominates.[^cite_note-Ashley-2018-42] However, since HRP2 persists in the blood for up to five weeks after an infection is treated, an HRP2 test sometimes cannot distinguish whether someone currently has malaria or previously had it.[^cite_note-WHO-2021a-70] Additionally, some *P. falciparum* parasites lack the *HRP2* gene, complicating detection.[^cite_note-72] Rapid tests also cannot quantify the parasite burden in a person, nor the species of *Plasmodium* involved.[^cite_note-Daily_2025-73][^cite_note-CDC-Diagnosis-2024c-71]

A [polymerase chain reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymerase_chain_reaction) (PCR) test is the most sensitive method for diagnosing malaria. The test amplifies parasite DNA in blood; it can detect *Plasmodium* and identify the species, even at very low levels in the blood.[^cite_note-Fisher-2022-74] It requires specialised laboratory equipment so is rarely available in developing countries; it is generally used in developed world to confirm diagnosis in returning travellers.[^cite_note-Fisher-2022-74][^cite_note-CDC-Diagnosis-2024c-71]

In April 2026, WHO [prequalified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-qualification) three rapid diagnostic tests designed to detect P. falciparum strains with pfhrp2 deletions, which can evade commonly used HRP2-based tests and cause false-negative results. The new tests target the parasite protein pf-LDH and are recommended where more than 5% of malaria cases are missed because of pfhrp2 deletions.[^cite_note-prefir-75]

## Treatment

Advertisement entitled "The Mosquito Danger". Includes 6 panel cartoon:#1 breadwinner has malaria, family starving; #2 wife selling ornaments; #3 doctor administers quinine; #4 patient recovers; #5 doctor indicating that quinine can be obtained from post office if needed again; #6 man who refused quinine, dead on stretcher.

An advertisement for [quinine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinine) as a malaria treatment from 1927



*Artemisia annua*, also known as sweet wormwood, the original source of artemisinin

Malaria is treated with [antimalarial medications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimalarial_medication). To ensure a complete cure and prevent the parasite from developing [drug resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drug_resistance), treatment guidelines since 2001 generally require [two drugs in combination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combination_therapy), with one of them being a derivative of [artemisinin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemisinin) and the other a complementary drug.[^cite_note-76][^cite_note-77] The exact combination of drugs depends on the *Plasmodium* species involved, the probability of drug resistance, relevant facts from the patient's medical and travel history and any previous use of antimalarials.[^cite_note-78][^cite_note-CDC-Treatment-2024-79] Treatment regimens in national [formularies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formulary_\(pharmacy\))[^cite_note-82] are generally based on guidelines issued by WHO which are updated regularly.[^cite_note-WHO-Guidelines-9.1-2025-83][^cite_note-84]

Several classes of drugs can be used as components of combination therapies. Artemisinin-based drugs include [artemether](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemether) and [artesunate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artesunate).[^cite_note-Arrow-2004a-85] [Quinoline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinoline) derivatives include [chloroquine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroquine), [quinine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinine) and [mefloquine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mefloquine). [Antifolate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antifolate) compounds include [pyrimethamine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrimethamine), [proguanil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proguanil) and [sulfadoxine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfadoxine).[^cite_note-Arrow-2004a-85] Other drugs include [lumefantrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumefantrine), [atovaquone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atovaquone), [doxycycline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doxycycline) and [clindamycin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clindamycin).[^cite_note-Evans-2025-86][^cite_note-CDC-Treatment-2025-87]

Malaria is generally classified as either "severe" or "uncomplicated".[^cite_note-Nadjm-2012-13][^cite_note-CDC-Treatment-2024-79] Symptoms which indicate severe malaria include the following (not a complete list):[^cite_note-CDC-Treatment-2024-79][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWHO201573-88]

- Decreased consciousness, coma, convulsions

- Severe anaemia

- [Acute respiratory distress syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_respiratory_distress_syndrome)

- [Kidney failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kidney_failure) or [haemoglobin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haemoglobin) in the urine

- [Jaundice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaundice)

- [Acidosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acidosis) or [lactate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactic_acid) levels of greater than 5 mmol/L

- A high level of parasites visible in a blood smear.

### Uncomplicated malaria

Uncomplicated malaria can be treated using oral medication taken for between 3 and 7 days.[^cite_note-CDC-Treatment-2025-87] The drug regimen should be selected according to *Plasmodium* species, location and patient's history. As an example, the most common first-line treatment for uncomplicated *P. falciparum* malaria is [Artemether-lumefantrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemether/lumefantrine) taken orally over three days.[^cite_note-Lalloo-2016-89][^cite_note-Evans-2025-86] In 2026, WHO [prequalified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-qualification) the first artemether-lumefantrine formulation specifically developed for newborns and young infants weighing between 2 and 5 kg.[^cite_note-prefir-75]

This treatment is not always suitable, so other drug combinations are recommended.[^cite_note-CDC-Treatment-2025-87] Treatment should start as soon as possible after diagnosis, in order to prevent severe symptoms from developing.[^cite_note-90] In case of infection by *P. vivax or P. ovale* (which form dormant hypnozoites in the liver) treatment should continue for a further 7 to 14 days.[^cite_note-CDC-Treatment-2025-87]

### Severe and complicated malaria

Severe malaria is generally caused by *P. falciparum*; it is almost always fatal if untreated.[^cite_note-91] Even with treatment, the fatality rate is estimated between 13% and 20%, with survivors often facing long term [after-effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequela).[^cite_note-92] The standard treatment is intravenous artesunate, switching to oral medication once the patient is stable.[^cite_note-Evans-2025-86][^cite_note-93] Patients may deteriorate rapidly so close monitoring, preferably in a [high dependency unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-dependency_unit), is vital.[^cite_note-94]

### Pregnancy

[Malaria in pregnancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pregnancy-associated_malaria) is more likely to be serious, possibly fatal, for both mother and child; pregnant women are three times more likely to develop severe malaria.[^cite_note-CDC-Pregnant-2024-95] Some drugs could injure the developing embryo, especially during the first [trimester](1771587.md). Special treatment regimens are recommended, which vary according to the trimester and pose minimal risk.[^cite_note-CDC-Pregnant-2024-95][^cite_note-96]

### Drug resistance

[Drug resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drug_resistance) poses a growing problem in malaria treatment; *Plasmodium* populations have a high level of genetic diversity and a rapid reproduction rate which enable them to adapt and evade challenges from antimalarials.[^cite_note-Sinha-2014-97][^cite_note-98] *P. falciparum* parasites began to develop resistance to the first synthetic antimalarial, chloroquine, in the 1950s;[^cite_note-MMV-History-99] since then chloroquine resistance has spread to almost the entire range of this species.[^cite_note-100] Resistance to proguanil developed even more rapidly; the drug was introduced in 1948 and resistance began to be noted the next year, in 1949.[^cite_note-MMV-History-99] In 2001, malaria with partial resistance to artemisinin emerged in Southeast Asia; resistance has subsequently spread to parts of Africa.[^cite_note-MMV-History-99][^cite_note-101] In order to overcome resistance, drugs may be given in combination, in higher doses, or for longer periods; there is an urgent need for new drugs to be brought on line.[^cite_note-MMV-History-99][^cite_note-102]

## Prognosis

When properly treated, people with uncomplicated malaria can usually expect a complete recovery.[^cite_note-CDC-FAQ-2010a-103] However, severe malaria can progress extremely rapidly and cause death within hours or days.[^cite_note-Trampuz-2003-104] In the most severe cases of the disease, [fatality rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatality_rate) can reach 20%, even with intensive care and treatment.[^cite_note-Nadjm-2012-13]

Malaria has the potential to cause permanent damage to many organs, especially the brain, kidney, liver and spleen.[^cite_note-105] During childhood, malaria causes anaemia during a period of rapid brain development, and also direct brain damage resulting from cerebral malaria.[^cite_note-Fernando-2010-106] Survivors of cerebral malaria have an increased risk of neurological and cognitive deficits, [behavioural disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotional_and_behavioral_disorders) and [epilepsy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epilepsy).[^cite_note-Idro-2010-107]

## Prevention



An *[Anopheles stephensi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anopheles_stephensi)* mosquito shortly after obtaining blood from a human (the droplet of blood is expelled as a surplus). This mosquito is a [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disease_vector) of malaria, and mosquito control is an effective way of reducing its incidence.

Methods used to prevent malaria include vaccination, prophylactic medication, mosquito elimination and the prevention of bites.[^cite_note-WHO-Factsheet-2025-3]

### Vaccines

Main article: [Malaria vaccine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria_vaccine)

It has proved very difficult to develop an effective malaria vaccine. The parasite has evolved several strategies to evade the human immune response.[^cite_note-108] There are five species of *plasmodium*; each of these has three life stages in the human host—sporozoite, merozoite and gametocyte. Each stage has different antigens on its surface, meaning that an immune response against one stage is not effective against the others.[^cite_note-Good-2021-109] In addition, genetic variation in the parasites means that the antigens themselves can vary even within a single life stage.[^cite_note-Good-2021-109][^cite_note-110] As a consequence, natural immunity seems to develop slowly—acquired through multiple infections—is only partial, and is not long lasting.[^cite_note-Crutcher-1996-25][^cite_note-CDC-Vaccines-2024-111] The Malaria Vaccine Technology Roadmap has set a target for new malaria vaccines to have a protective efficacy of at least 75% against clinical malaria.[^cite_note-CDC-Vaccines-2024-111]

The first promising vaccine studies into a malaria vaccine were performed in 1967 by immunising mice with live, radiation-[attenuated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attenuator_\(genetics\)) *Plasmodium* [sporozoites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apicomplexan_life_cycle), which provided significant protection to the mice upon subsequent injection with normal, viable sporozoites.[^cite_note-112][^cite_note-Vanderberg-2009-113]

In 2013, the WHO and the malaria vaccine funders group set a goal to develop vaccines designed to interrupt malaria transmission with the long-term goal of malaria eradication.[^cite_note-114] As of February 2026, two [malaria vaccines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria_vaccine) have been licensed for use.[^cite_note-WHO_recommends_R21-2023-14]

The first approved vaccine targeting *P. falciparum* is [RTS,S](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RTS,S), known by the brand name Mosquirix,[^cite_note-EMA_Mosquirix-115] which completed [clinical trials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinical_trial) in 2014. The WHO adopted a cautious approach to awarding it [prequalification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-qualification) and, as part of the Malaria Vaccine Implementation Programme (MVIP) approved pilot programs in three sub-Saharan African countries—Ghana, Kenya and Malawi—starting in 2019. These programs targeted children under 5, who are particularly at risk of severe infection and death.[^cite_note-116][^cite_note-117] It was approved by the World Health Organization in 2021.[^cite_note-118] Up to 2023, three million children had received the vaccine,[^cite_note-119] showing a significantly reduced incidence of malaria as well as a reduction in childhood mortality (from all causes) of 13%.[^cite_note-WHO-Factsheet-2025-3][^cite_note-120]

The second vaccine is R21/Matrix-M, with a 77% efficacy rate shown in initial trials and significantly higher antibody levels than with the RTS,S vaccine.[^cite_note-Roxby-2021-121][^cite_note-EurekAlert-2021-122] The R-21/Matrix M malaria vaccine was found to reduce cases of malaria by 75% in areas with seasonal spread and by 68% in areas of year-round spread in children in sub-Saharan Africa.[^cite_note-123] The R-21/Matrix M malaria vaccine was endorsed by the WHO for the prevention of malaria in children in 2023.[^cite_note-WHO_recommends_R21-2023-14]

### Mosquito control



Man spraying kerosene oil in standing water, [Panama Canal Zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panama_Canal_Zone), 1912

Main article: [Mosquito control](2887318.md)

The objective of mosquito control is to stop mosquitoes from biting humans - either by using some forms of barrier, or by reducing mosquito numbers. They can broadly be classified as personal protection, environmental controls outside buildings, indoor measures within houses and buildings, and those incorporated into the fabric of buildings.[^cite_note-EPA-Control-2016-124]

#### Personal protection

Insect repellent, such as [DEET](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DEET) or [picaridin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picaridin), is recommended for travellers. Loose clothing that covers most of the body is also recommended. Clothing may be treated with [permethrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permethrin) as an additional safeguard.[^cite_note-mosquitobiteavoidance-125][^cite_note-126]

#### Environmental control

Since many mosquitoes breed in [standing water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standing_water), source reduction can be as simple as emptying water from containers around the home, by filling or draining puddles, swampy areas and tree stumps. Eliminating such mosquito breeding areas can be an extremely effective and permanent way to reduce mosquito populations without resorting to insecticides.[^cite_note-127] It is also possible to use [larvicides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larvicide) to kill mosquito larvae in pools or puddles that cannot be drained.[^cite_note-Mississippi-2025-128]

#### Indoors

[Insecticide-treated nets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insecticide-treated_net) (ITNs) and [indoor residual spraying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indoor_residual_spraying) (IRS) are effective, have been widely used to prevent malaria, and their use has contributed significantly to reducing the prevalence of malaria in the 21st century.[^cite_note-Fox-2022-129][^cite_note-mosquitobiteavoidance-125][^cite_note-Pluess-2010-130] ITNs and IRS may not be sufficient to eliminate the disease, as these interventions depend on how many people use nets, how many gaps in insecticide there are (low coverage areas), if people are not protected when outside of the home, and an increase in mosquitoes that are resistant to insecticides.[^cite_note-Fox-2022-129]

##### Insecticide-treated nets



A mosquito net in use

[Mosquito nets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosquito_net) help keep mosquitoes away from people and reduce infection rates and transmission of malaria. Nets are not a perfect barrier and are often treated with an [insecticide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insecticide) designed to kill the mosquito before it has time to find a way past the net. ITNs are estimated to be twice as effective as untreated nets and offer greater than 70% protection compared with no net.[^cite_note-Raghavendra-2011-131]

Most nets are impregnated with [pyrethroids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrethroid), a class of insecticides with low [toxicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toxicity); they are most effective when used from dusk to dawn.[^cite_note-132] In areas where mosquitoes are resistant to pyrethroids, other ingredients are being combined with pyrethroids in mosquito netting; these include [piperonyl butoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piperonyl_butoxide), [chlorfenapyr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorfenapyr) and [pyriproxyfen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyriproxyfen).[^cite_note-Gleave-2021-133][^cite_note-134]

UNICEF notes that the use of insecticide-treated nets has been increased since 2000 through accelerated production, procurement and delivery, stating that "Almost 2.5 billion ITNs have been distributed globally since 2004, with 2.2 billion (87 per cent) distributed in sub-Saharan Africa".[^cite_note-UNICEF-2023-135] By 2023, 52% of children in sub-Saharan Africa were sleeping under ITNs; however there were large regional differences in coverage.[^cite_note-UNICEF-2023-135]

A 2025 [Malaria Atlas Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria_Atlas_Project) analysis estimated that malaria interventions in Africa prevented 1.57 billion cases from 2000 to 2024, with ITNs accounting for 72% of cases averted.[^cite_note-Malaria-Atlas-2025-136] The report warned that progress has slowed due to plateauing ITN coverage and emphasized that expanding access to ITNs remains essential.[^cite_note-Malaria-Atlas-2025-136]

##### Indoor residual spraying



Walls where indoor residual spraying of DDT has been applied. The dead mosquitoes remain on the wall, eventually falling to the floor.

Indoor residual spraying (IRS) is the spraying of insecticides on the walls inside a home. After a blood meal (during which it may ingest *Plasmodium* parasites), the female mosquito rests on a nearby surface while digesting the blood and developing eggs. If the walls of houses have been coated with insecticides, the resting mosquitoes can be killed before they can bite another person. Spraying indoor surfaces does not prevent the mosquito from its first feeding; however, it protects the community as a whole by preventing a second blood meal in which they may pass the infection to a human host.[^cite_note-CDC-Spraying-2024-137][^cite_note-mosquitobiology-138] Chemicals recommended by WHO for IRS fall into the following classes:[^cite_note-Pryce-2022a-139][^cite_note-WHO-Spraying-2024-140]

- Pyrethroids such as [Alpha-cypermethrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cypermethrin), [Bifenthrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bifenthrin)

- Organophosphates such as [malathion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malathion)

- Carbamates: [Bendiocarb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bendiocarb), [Propoxur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propoxur)

- Organochlorides: [DDT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DDT) (very restricted use)

  [^cite_note-mosquitobiology-138]

In order to be effective, IRS should be applied to a minimum of 80% of households in a community. It is estimated that IRS has contributed to 10% of the malaria cases averted in parts of Africa.[^cite_note-Pryce-2022a-139]

#### Structures

To keep mosquitoes from entering the home, screens should be installed on windows and doors and eaves, as well as netting to cover any other sources of ventilation.[^cite_note-EPA-Control-2016-124][^cite_note-141] In 2021, the World Health Organization's (WHO) Guideline Development Group conditionally recommended screening houses in this manner to reduce malaria transmission.[^cite_note-WHO-Guidelines-9.1-2025-83] Several studies have suggested that screening houses can have a significant effect on malaria transmission. Beyond the protective barrier screening provides, it also does not call for daily behavioral changes in the household.[^cite_note-Nalinya-2022-142] Screening eaves can also have a community-level protective effect, ultimately reducing mosquito-biting densities in neighboring houses that do not have this intervention in place.[^cite_note-Nalinya-2022-142]

#### Limitations in vector control

As insecticides are used more widely, mosquito populations can evolve resistance, reducing the effectiveness of both long-lasting [insecticide-treated nets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insecticide_treated_nets) (LLINs) and [indoor residual spraying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indoor_residual_spraying) (IRS).[^cite_note-143][^cite_note-WHO-Report-2025a-4] This resistance can occur through physiological mechanisms, in which genetic changes allow mosquitoes to survive insecticide contact.[^cite_note-Abbasi-2026-144][^cite_note-Fouet-2018-145] Mosquitoes with these advantageous traits can pass them on to their offspring, increasing the proportion of resistant mosquitoes in the population over time.[^cite_note-Abbasi-2026-144][^cite_note-Fouet-2018-145]

Beyond physiological resistance, malaria [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disease_vector) have demonstrated behavioral adaptations that further reduce the effectiveness of current vector control methods. These changes are termed "behavioral resistance", where mosquito behavior shifts in ways that reduce exposure to insecticides.[^cite_note-Gatton-2013-146] *[Anopheles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anopheles)* mosquitoes have traditionally exhibited endophagy, meaning they prefer to bite humans indoors, and endophily, meaning they rest indoors after feeding.[^cite_note-Huho-2013-147] They also typically bite at night, when individuals are protected by [bed nets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bed_nets).[^cite_note-Huho-2013-147]

However, some *Anopheles* species have increasingly been observed biting earlier in the evening or outdoors, exhibiting [exophagy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exophagy), when people are less likely to be protected by bed nets or indoor spraying.[^cite_note-Gatton-2013-146][^cite_note-Carrasco-2019-148] They may also rest outdoors rather than on indoor walls that have been sprayed, exhibiting exophily.[^cite_note-Gatton-2013-146][^cite_note-Carrasco-2019-148] These behavioral changes contribute to what is known as residual transmission, defined as malaria transmission that persists even after the implementation of core vector control interventions.[^cite_note-Carrasco-2019-148] As a result, malaria transmission can continue even in areas with widespread use of LLINs and IRS.&#x20;

These adaptations indicate a key limitation of current strategies, which primarily rely on interrupting the human mosquito contact during nighttime indoor feeding.[^cite_note-WHO-Report-2025a-4] Therefore, incorporating vector behavioral changes into malaria control strategies is increasingly important, as the protective effects of their interventions are being reduced by such adaptations.

### Preventive medication

Main article: [Malaria prophylaxis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria_prophylaxis)

There are a number of medications that can help prevent or interrupt malaria in travellers to places where infection is common; many of these medications are also used in treatment. In places where *Plasmodium* is resistant to one or more medications, three medications—[mefloquine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mefloquine), [doxycycline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doxycycline), or the combination of [atovaquone/proguanil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atovaquone/proguanil) (*Malarone*)—are frequently used for prevention.[^cite_note-Tickell-Painter-2017-149]

The protective effect does not begin immediately, and people visiting areas where malaria exists are recommended to start taking the drugs one to two weeks before they arrive, and continue taking them for four weeks after leaving (except for atovaquone/proguanil, which only needs to be started two days before and continued for seven days afterward).[^cite_note-Freedman-2008-150] Malaria preventative medications are recommended for those vulnerable to the disease. Children under 5-years old, pregnant women, and some experts recommend school age children, in malaria endemic areas are recommended to receive malaria preventative medications on a seasonal basis or intermittent basis.[^cite_note-Daily_2025-73][^cite_note-Littmann_2024-151] Combining malarial preventative medications with anti-malarial vaccines has a greater effect in reducing malaria case-loads than medications alone.[^cite_note-Daily_2025-73]

[Intermittent preventive therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermittent_preventive_therapy) is an intervention that has been used successfully to control malaria in pregnant women and infants,[^cite_note-Bardají-2012-152] and in preschool children where transmission is seasonal.[^cite_note-Meremikwu-2012a-153] During pregnancy, medication to prevent malaria has been found to improve the weight of the baby at birth and decrease the risk of [anaemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaemia) in the mother.[^cite_note-154] Giving antimalarial drugs to infants through intermittent preventive therapy can reduce the risk of having malaria infection, hospital admission and anaemia.[^cite_note-155]

Antimalarial [mass drug administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_drug_administration) to an entire population at the same time may reduce the risk of contracting malaria in the population.[^cite_note-Shah-2021-156] In the 1950s, the WHO included mass drug administration (MDA) of antimalarial drugs as a tool for malaria eradication in exceptional conditions when conventional control techniques have failed.[^cite_note-WHO-1951-157] In 1971, the WHO expert committee on malaria still recommended MDA in special circumstances.[^cite_note-WHO-1951-157] Subsequently, MDA was linked to the emergence of [drug resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drug_resistance) and its overall benefit was questioned.[^cite_note-Wernsdorfer-1992-158][^cite_note-Verdrager-1995-159][^cite_note-Verdrager-1986-160][^cite_note-Payne-1988-161]

### Others

Community participation and [health education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_education) strategies promoting awareness of malaria and the importance of control measures have been successfully used to reduce the incidence of malaria in some areas of the developing world.[^cite_note-Lalloo-2006-162] Recognising the disease in the early stages can prevent it from becoming fatal. Education can also inform people to cover over areas of stagnant, still water, such as water tanks that are ideal breeding grounds for the parasite and mosquito, thus cutting down the risk of the transmission between people. This is generally used in urban areas where there are large centers of population in a confined space and transmission would be most likely in these areas.[^cite_note-Mehlhorn-2016-163]

## Epidemiology

According to the World Health Organization's 2025 World Malaria Report,[^cite_note-WHO-Report-2025a-4] there were an estimated 282 million new malaria cases globally in 2024, in 80 endemic countries. The number of deaths attributed to malaria stood at 610,000 in 2024. Children under five years old were the most affected, accounting for 75% of malaria deaths in Africa during 2024.[^cite_note-WHO-Report-2025a-4]

The malaria parasite depends on mosquitoes of the *Anopheles* genus to transmit between [hosts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Host_\(biology\)). It is [endemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endemic_\(epidemiology\)) in areas where conditions are favourable for these species of mosquitoes.[^cite_note-WHO-Report-2025a-4] This includes almost all tropical and subtropical areas of the world, where either regular rainfall creates essential breeding habitats all year, or seasonally during and after rainy seasons when standing water is abundant.[^cite_note-vectorsofmalaria-164] The optimum temperature for the parasite is 27 °C (81 °F) but it can develop in temperatures between 20 °C (68 °F) and 40 °C (104 °F).[^cite_note-165] Malaria is uncommon at altitudes above 1,500 meters, where temperatures tend to be lower,[^cite_note-166] and in urban environments where good drainage eliminates pools of water.[^cite_note-167]

Malaria is presently endemic in a broad band around the equator, in areas of the Americas, many parts of Asia and much of Africa.[^cite_note-WHO-Report-2025a-4] Malaria was once common in parts of Europe and North America, where it is no longer endemic.[^cite_note-ECDC-Information-2024-28][^cite_note-CDC-where-2024a-168] *Anopheles* mosquitoes are still present in these areas, so there is a risk of the disease returning.[^cite_note-CDC-where-2024a-168][^cite_note-169]

### Climate change

Further information: [Climate change and infectious diseases § Malaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_and_infectious_diseases#Malaria)

[Climate change](5042951.md) is likely to affect malaria transmission, but the degree of effect and the areas affected is uncertain.[^cite_note-WHO-Climate-2016-174] Greater rainfall in certain areas of India, and following an [El Niño](317311.md) event is associated with increased mosquito numbers.[^cite_note-175]

Since 1900 there has been substantial change in temperature and rainfall over Africa.[^cite_note-176] However, factors that contribute to how rainfall results in water for mosquito breeding are complex, incorporating the extent to which it is absorbed into soil and vegetation for example, or rates of runoff and evaporation.[^cite_note-Smith-2020-177] Recent research has provided a more in-depth picture of conditions across Africa, combining a malaria climatic suitability model with a continental-scale model representing real-world hydrological processes.[^cite_note-Smith-2020-177]

#### Changes in geographic distribution

Climate change has led to shifts in malaria-endemic regions, with the disease expanding into higher altitudes and previously malaria-free areas.[^cite_note-Caminade-2014-178] Rising temperatures allow mosquitoes to survive in regions that were once too cold for them, including highland areas in Africa, South America and parts of Asia.[^cite_note-Caminade-2014-178] A study analyzing malaria cases in Ethiopian and Colombian highlands found a strong correlation between increased temperatures and malaria incidence, demonstrating that climate change has made previously inhospitable areas suitable for transmission.[^cite_note-Siraj-2014-179]

#### Increased transmission season

Malaria transmission is highly sensitive to temperature and rainfall patterns.[^cite_note-Siraj-2014-179] Climate change has led to longer transmission seasons in tropical regions, where mosquitoes can breed year-round due to prolonged periods of high humidity and warm temperatures.[^cite_note-Colon-Gonzalez-2021-180] Research suggests that in parts of sub-Saharan Africa, the malaria transmission season has lengthened by several months, particularly in regions where warming has pushed temperatures into the optimal range for *Plasmodium falciparum* development.[^cite_note-Colon-Gonzalez-2021-180] In regions such as West Africa and parts of India, increasing temperatures and prolonged rainy seasons have contributed to a rise in malaria cases.[^cite_note-Colon-Gonzalez-2021-180] Some studies predict that by 2050, many malaria-endemic areas will experience a 20–30% increase in transmission duration due to warming trends.[^cite_note-Caminade-2014-178]

#### Effects of extreme weather events

Extreme weather events, such as heavy rainfall, flooding and droughts, are increasing in frequency and intensity due to climate change, creating favorable conditions for malaria outbreaks.[^cite_note-Bouma-1996-181] Flooding provides ideal breeding grounds for mosquitoes by forming stagnant water pools, while droughts can also exacerbate malaria by forcing human populations to store water in open containers, which serve as mosquito habitats.[^cite_note-Bouma-1996-181] This effect has been observed in parts of sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, where prolonged drought periods were followed by spikes in malaria cases.[^cite_note-Bouma-1996-181] A review of malaria outbreaks linked to climate variability found that El Niño events, which increase rainfall and temperatures in malaria-endemic regions, have been associated with significant surges in cases.[^cite_note-182]

#### Resistance and adaptation of vectors

Higher temperatures accelerate the development of *Plasmodium* parasites within mosquitoes, potentially leading to increased transmission efficiency.[^cite_note-Shapiro-2017-183] Additionally, rising temperatures and changing environmental conditions have been linked to the spread of insecticide resistance in mosquito populations, complicating malaria control efforts.[^cite_note-Shapiro-2017-183] A global survey found that *Anopheles* mosquitoes in Africa, Asia and South America have developed increased resistance to commonly used insecticides such as pyrethroids.[^cite_note-184]

## History

Main articles: [History of malaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_malaria) and [Mosquito-malaria theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosquito-malaria_theory)



Ancient malaria oocysts preserved in [Dominican amber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominican_amber)

Although the parasite responsible for *P. falciparum* malaria has been in existence for 50,000–100,000 years, the population size of the parasite did not increase until about 10,000 years ago, concurrently with advances in agriculture[^cite_note-Harper-2010-185] and the development of human settlements. Close relatives of the human malaria parasites remain common in chimpanzees. Some evidence suggests that the *P. falciparum* malaria may have originated in gorillas.[^cite_note-Prugnolle-2011-186]

References to the unique periodic fevers of malaria are found throughout history.[^cite_note-Cox-2002-187] The Roman [Columella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucius_Columella) associated the disease with insects from swamps.[^cite_note-Strong-1944-188] Hippocrates described periodic fevers, labelling them tertian, quartan, subtertian and quotidian.[^cite_note-Strong-1944-188] Malaria may have contributed to the [decline of the Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decline_of_the_Roman_Empire),[^cite_note-BBC_News-2001-189] and was so pervasive in Rome that it was known as the "[Roman fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Fever_\(disease\))".[^cite_note-Sallares-2002-190] Several regions in ancient Rome were considered at-risk for the disease because of the favourable conditions present for malaria vectors. This included areas such as [southern Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Italy), the island of [Sardinia](29376.md), the [Pontine Marshes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontine_Marshes), the lower regions of coastal [Etruria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etruria) and the city of [Rome](25458.md) along the [Tiber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiber). The presence of stagnant water in these places was preferred by mosquitoes for breeding grounds. Irrigated gardens, swamp-like grounds, run-off from agriculture and drainage problems from road construction led to the increase of standing water.[^cite_note-Hays-2005-191]



British doctor [Ronald Ross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Ross) received the [Nobel Prize for Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_for_Physiology_or_Medicine) in 1902 for his work on malaria.

The few surviving medical [records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aztec_codex) of [Mesoamerican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerica) civilizations do not show any record of malaria. European settlers and the West Africans they [enslaved](49005.md) likely brought malaria to the Americas starting in the 16th century.[^cite_note-192][^cite_note-193]

Scientific studies on malaria made their first significant advance in 1880, when [Charles Louis Alphonse Laveran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Louis_Alphonse_Laveran)—a French army doctor working in the military hospital of [Constantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine,_Algeria) in [Algeria](358.md)—observed parasites inside the red blood cells of infected people for the first time.[^cite_note-194] He, therefore, proposed that malaria is caused by this organism, the first time a [protist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protist) was identified as causing disease.[^cite_note-Nobel-Medicine-1907-195] For this and later discoveries, he was awarded the 1907 [Nobel Prize for Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_for_Physiology_or_Medicine).[^cite_note-nobel-196] A year later, [Carlos Finlay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlos_Finlay), a Cuban doctor treating people with [yellow fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_fever) in [Havana](49719.md), provided strong evidence that mosquitoes were transmitting disease to and from humans.[^cite_note-Tan-2008-197] This work followed earlier suggestions by [Josiah C. Nott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josiah_C._Nott),[^cite_note-Chernin-1983-198] and work by [Sir Patrick Manson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Patrick_Manson), the "father of tropical medicine", on the transmission of [filariasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filariasis).[^cite_note-Chernin-1977-199]



Chinese medical researcher [Tu Youyou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tu_Youyou) received the [Nobel Prize for Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_for_Physiology_or_Medicine) in 2015 for her work on the antimalarial drug [artemisinin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemisinin).

In April 1894, a Scottish physician, [Sir Ronald Ross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Ross), visited Sir Patrick Manson at his house on Queen Anne Street, London. This visit was the start of four years of collaboration and fervent research that culminated in 1897 when Ross, who was working in the [Presidency General Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidency_General_Hospital) in [Calcutta](47905.md), proved the complete life-cycle of the malaria parasite in mosquitoes.[^cite_note-Cox-2010-200] He thus proved that the mosquito was the vector for malaria in humans by showing that certain mosquito species transmit malaria to birds. He isolated malaria parasites from the salivary glands of mosquitoes that had fed on infected birds.[^cite_note-Cox-2010-200] Ross received the 1902 Nobel Prize for Physiology or Medicine "for his work on [malaria](20423.md), by which he has shown how it enters the organism and thereby has laid the foundation for successful research on this disease and methods of combating it".[^cite_note-nobel-1902-201] After resigning from the [Indian Medical Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Medical_Service), Ross worked at the newly established [Liverpool School of Tropical Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liverpool_School_of_Tropical_Medicine) and directed malaria-control efforts in [Egypt](8087628.md), [Panama](22997.md), [Greece](12108.md) and [Mauritius](19201.md).[^cite_note-CDC-History-Ross-202] The findings of Finlay and Ross were later confirmed by a medical board headed by [Walter Reed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Reed) in 1900. Its recommendations were implemented by [William C. Gorgas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_C._Gorgas) in [the health measures undertaken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_measures_during_the_construction_of_the_Panama_Canal) during construction of the [Panama Canal](24849.md). This public-health work saved the lives of thousands of workers and helped develop the methods used in future public-health campaigns against the disease.[^cite_note-Simmons-1979-203]

In 1896, [Amico Bignami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amico_Bignami) discussed the role of mosquitoes in malaria.[^cite_note-204] In 1898, Bignami, [Giovanni Battista Grassi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Battista_Grassi) and [Giuseppe Bastianelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giuseppe_Bastianelli) succeeded in showing experimentally the transmission of malaria in humans, using infected mosquitoes to contract malaria themselves which they presented in November 1898 to the [Accademia dei Lincei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accademia_dei_Lincei).[^cite_note-Cox-2010-200]



*Artemisia annua*, source of the antimalarial drug [artemisinin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemisinin)

The first effective treatment for malaria came from the bark of [cinchona tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinchona), which contains [quinine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinine). This tree grows on the slopes of the [Andes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andes), mainly in [Peru](170691.md). The [indigenous peoples of Peru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_Peru) made a [tincture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tincture) of cinchona to control fever. Its effectiveness against malaria was found and the [Jesuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesuit) introduced the treatment to Europe around 1640; by 1677, it was included in the [London Pharmacopoeia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Pharmacopoeia) as an antimalarial treatment.[^cite_note-Kaufman-2005-205] It was not until 1820 that the active ingredient, quinine, was extracted from the bark, isolated and named by the French chemists [Pierre Joseph Pelletier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Joseph_Pelletier) and [Joseph Bienaimé Caventou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Bienaim%C3%A9_Caventou).[^cite_note-Pelletier-1820-206][^cite_note-Kyle-1974-207]

Quinine was the predominant malarial medication until the 1920s when other medications began to appear. In the 1940s, chloroquine replaced quinine as the treatment of both uncomplicated and severe malaria until resistance supervened, first in Southeast Asia and South America in the 1950s and then globally in the 1980s.[^cite_note-Achan-2011-208]

The medicinal value of *[Artemisia annua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemisia_annua)* has been used by Chinese herbalists in [traditional Chinese medicines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_Chinese_medicines) for 2,000 years.[^cite_note-209][^cite_note-210] In 1596, Li Shizhen recommended tea made from qinghao specifically to treat malaria symptoms in his "[Compendium of Materia Medica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compendium_of_Materia_Medica)", however the efficacy of tea, made with *A. annua*, for the treatment of malaria is dubious, and is discouraged by the [World Health Organization](33583.md) (WHO).[^cite_note-211][^cite_note-212] Artemisinins, discovered by Chinese scientist [Tu Youyou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tu_Youyou) and colleagues in the 1970s from the plant *Artemisia annua*, became the recommended treatment for *P. falciparum* malaria, administered in severe cases in combination with other antimalarials.[^cite_note-Hsu-2006-213] Tu says she was influenced by a [traditional Chinese herbal medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_herbology) source, *The Handbook of Prescriptions for Emergency Treatments*, written in 340 by [Ge Hong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ge_Hong).[^cite_note-214] For her work on malaria, Tu Youyou received the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.[^cite_note-Nobel-Medicine-2015-215]

*Plasmodium vivax* was used between 1917 and the 1940s for [malariotherapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malariotherapy)—deliberate injection of malaria parasites to induce a fever to combat certain diseases such as tertiary [syphilis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syphilis). In 1927, the inventor of this technique, [Julius Wagner-Jauregg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Wagner-Jauregg), received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for his discoveries. The technique was dangerous, killing about 15% of patients, so it is no longer in use.[^cite_note-Vogel-2013-216]



U.S. Marines with malaria in a field hospital on [Guadalcanal](113645.md), October 1942

The first pesticide used for indoor residual spraying was [DDT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DDT).[^cite_note-CDC-Eradication-2010-217] Although it was initially used exclusively to combat malaria, its use quickly spread to [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture). In time, pest control, rather than disease control, came to dominate DDT use, and this large-scale agricultural use led to the evolution of [pesticide-resistant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pesticide_resistance) mosquitoes in many regions. The DDT resistance shown by *Anopheles* mosquitoes can be compared to [antibiotic resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibiotic_resistance) shown by bacteria. During the 1960s, awareness of the negative consequences of its indiscriminate use increased, ultimately leading to bans on agricultural applications of DDT in many countries in the 1970s.[^cite_note-van_den_Berg-2009-218] Before DDT, malaria was successfully eliminated or controlled in tropical areas like Brazil and Egypt by removing or poisoning the breeding grounds of the mosquitoes or the aquatic habitats of the larval stages, for example by applying the highly toxic arsenic compound [Paris Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Green) to places with standing water.[^cite_note-Killeen-2002-219]

## Eradication efforts

Main article: [Eradication of malaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eradication_of_malaria)

There have been two major global malaria eradication efforts: the first, led by the [World Health Organization](33583.md) between 1955 and 1969, and the second, initiated by the United Nations in the 21st century through the [Millennium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennium_Development_Goals) and [Sustainable Development Goals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_Development_Goals). As of 2025, malaria has been eliminated or significantly reduced in many regions of the world, but remains widespread in others. Most of [Europe](9239.md), [North America](21139.md), [Australia](4689264.md), [North Africa](21714.md) and the [Caribbean](18956035.md), along with parts of [South America](26769.md) and [Asia](689.md) are now free from malaria, while much of the central part of [Africa](5334607.md) continues to experience high levels of transmission.[^cite_note-Malaria-free-2025-220]

### Initial WHO program (1955–1969)



1962 Pakistani postage stamp promoting malaria eradication program

In 1955 the WHO launched the Global Malaria Eradication Program (GMEP).[^cite_note-Duintjer-2009-221] The program relied largely on DDT for mosquito control and rapid diagnosis and treatment to break the transmission cycle.[^cite_note-222] The program eliminated the disease in "North America, Europe, the former [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union)",[^cite_note-Sadasivaiah-2007-223] and in "[Taiwan](25734.md), much of the [Caribbean](18956035.md), the [Balkans](4829.md), parts of northern Africa, the northern region of Australia, and a large swath of the South Pacific"[^cite_note-Gladwell-2001-224] and dramatically reduced mortality in [Sri Lanka](26750.md) and India.[^cite_note-Harrison-1978-225]

However, failure to sustain the program, increasing mosquito tolerance to DDT and increasing drug resistance (e.g. to chloroquine) led to a resurgence.[^cite_note-van_den_Berg-2009-218][^cite_note-Feachem-2007-226] In many areas early successes partially or completely reversed, and in some cases rates of transmission increased. Experts tie malarial resurgence to multiple factors, including poor leadership, management and funding of malaria control programs; poverty; civil unrest; and increased [irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrigation).[^cite_note-Chapin-1981-227] Besides, the program barely managed to be implemented in sub-Saharan Africa, the region of the planet with the highest malaria burden.[^cite_note-Nájera-2011-228]

WHO suspended the program in 1969[^cite_note-Duintjer-2009-221][^cite_note-Nájera-2011-228] and attention instead focused on controlling and treating the disease. Efforts shifted from spraying to the use of [bednets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosquito_net) impregnated with insecticides and other interventions.[^cite_note-Sadasivaiah-2007-223][^cite_note-Rogan-2005-229]

### The Roll Back Malaria Partnership

In 1998, a new major global initiative was launched: The Roll Back Malaria Partnership. However, it failed to secure adequate funding and reverse the upward trend in malaria deaths, which peaked in 2004.[^cite_note-230]

### 21st century



Global incidence of new cases of malaria from 2015 to 2023, expressed as cases per thousand in vulnerable populations. Source: WHO



Global malaria deaths from 2015 to 2023 (in thousands). Source: WHO

At the start of the 21st century, several global initiatives renewed efforts to control and eventually [eradicate malaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eradication_of_malaria). In 2000, malaria control became a key objective of the United Nations [Millennium Development Goals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennium_Development_Goals),[^cite_note-231] followed in 2015 by the [Sustainable Development Goals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_Development_Goals), which aim to end the malaria epidemic by 2030. From 2005 to 2014, global financing for malaria programmes rose sharply from about US \$960 million to US \$2.5 billion, largely driven by international donors and focused on the WHO African Region.[^cite_note-Cibulskis-2016-232] This surge in funding, channelled through programmes such as the [Global Fund to Fight AIDS, Tuberculosis and Malaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Fund_to_Fight_AIDS,_Tuberculosis_and_Malaria)[^cite_note-233] and [Malaria No More](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria_No_More)[^cite_note-234] supported an extensive scale-up of malaria control tools such as insecticide-treated nets, indoor spraying, rapid diagnostic tests and artemisinin-based combination therapies, though overall resources remained below the estimated US \$5 billion needed annually to meet global targets.[^cite_note-Cibulskis-2016-232]

Between 2004 and 2015, the global scale-up of malaria control led to a dramatic rise in [insecticide-treated net](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insecticide-treated_net) deliveries to endemic countries, from 6 million to nearly 190 million per year, with more than a billion distributed overall. As a result, household access to such nets in sub-Saharan Africa increased from 7 percent in 2005 to about two-thirds by 2015, though universal coverage targets remained unmet.[^cite_note-Cibulskis-2016-232] By 2014, coverage of preventive malaria treatment during pregnancy had increased, with more than half of eligible women receiving at least one dose. However, many opportunities to deliver treatment were missed, and preventive drug programmes for children and infants were adopted in only a few countries.[^cite_note-Cibulskis-2016-232]

Between 2005 and 2014, diagnostic testing for malaria increased sharply, particularly in the [WHO African Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_WHO_regions#African_Region_\(AFR\)), where testing of suspected cases in the public sector rose from 36 to 65 percent, largely due to the spread of rapid diagnostic tests. Over the same period, treatment practices improved, with the share of children under five receiving [artemisinin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemisinin)-based combination therapies increasing from under 1 percent in 2005 to about 16 percent in 2015, while use of older antimalarial drugs declined.[^cite_note-Cibulskis-2016-232] From 2000 to 2015, global malaria cases declined from about 262 million to 214 million, a reduction of roughly 18 percent, with the WHO African Region accounting for nearly nine out of ten infections. During the same period, malaria deaths fell by almost half, from an estimated 839 000 to 438 000, reflecting major progress toward international targets to reduce disease incidence and mortality.[^cite_note-Cibulskis-2016-232]

By 2015, a growing number of countries had moved closer to eliminating malaria. The number of nations with fewer than 1,000 cases rose from 13 in 2000 to 33, and 16 countries reported no locally transmitted cases. That same year, the [WHO European Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_WHO_regions#European_Region_\(EUR\)) recorded zero indigenous infections for the first time, meeting the target set in the [Tashkent Declaration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tashkent_Declaration).[^cite_note-Cibulskis-2016-232]

The progress made until 2015 stalled thereafter. From 2015 to 2022, the rate of new cases remained broadly stable, followed by a slight rise in 2023 (see illustration), with sharp increases reported in Ethiopia, Madagascar and Pakistan. Malaria deaths fell steadily until 2019, rose sharply in 2020 as a result of COVID-19 disruptions, and then declined again in the following years. While malaria remained most deadly for young children, their share of overall deaths had fallen. [Sub-Saharan Africa](27067.md) continues to account for 95% of global cases, with Nigeria, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Uganda, Ethiopia and Mozambique together responsible for more than half.[^cite_note-235][^cite_note-WHO-Briefing-2024-17]

This stalling from 2015 was due to a combination of environmental, humanitarian and biological challenges. Climate change created conditions that favored mosquito breeding and survival, with rising temperatures, shifting rainfall and frequent floods expanding transmission zones. In Pakistan, for example, the 2022 floods led to an eightfold increase in malaria cases within two years.[^cite_note-Health_Policy_Watch-2024-236] Conflicts and resulting humanitarian crises have also disrupted health services and displaced millions of people into areas with little access to prevention or treatment. At the same time, growing resistance to both insecticides and artemisinin-based therapies has made malaria harder to control and treat. These overlapping pressures—climate change, conflict, displacement and resistance—have together undermined the progress achieved in earlier decades.[^cite_note-Health_Policy_Watch-2024-236]

In 2021, the WHO Global Technical Strategy for Malaria was updated, proposing three consecutive priorities to be addressed as progress is made in reducing disease transmission.[^cite_note-237] The first priority focuses on reducing morbidity and mortality by decreasing transmission, the second pursues malaria elimination through epidemiological surveillance of clinical cases and active case-finding, and the third seeks to consolidate achievements and prevent the resurgence of malaria once local transmission has been eliminated. This renewed global commitment aims to reach a world largely free of malaria by 2050.[^cite_note-238]

## Society and culture

### Economic consequences



Malaria clinic in Tanzania

Malaria is not just a disease commonly associated with [poverty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poverty); some evidence suggests that it is also a cause of poverty and a major hindrance to [economic development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_development).[^cite_note-Gollin-2007-18][^cite_note-Worrall-2005-19]

A comparison of average per capita [GDP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GDP) in 1995, adjusted for [parity of purchasing power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purchasing_power_parity), between countries with malaria and countries without malaria gives a fivefold difference (US\$1,526 versus US\$8,268). In the period 1965 to 1990, countries where malaria was common had an average per capita GDP that increased only 0.4% per year, compared to 2.4% per year in other countries.[^cite_note-Sachs-2002-239]

Poverty can increase the risk of malaria since those in poverty do not have the financial capacities to prevent or treat the disease. In its entirety, the economic consequences of malaria has been estimated to cost Africa US\$12 billion every year. This includes costs of health care, working days lost due to sickness, days lost in education, decreased productivity due to brain damage from cerebral malaria and loss of investment and tourism.[^cite_note-Greenwood-2005-20] The disease has a heavy burden in some countries, where it may be responsible for 30–50% of hospital admissions, up to 50% of [outpatient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outpatient) visits and up to 40% of public health spending.[^cite_note-WHO-2003-240]

Cerebral malaria is one of the leading causes of neurological disabilities in African children.[^cite_note-Idro-2010-107] Studies comparing cognitive functions before and after treatment for severe malarial illness continued to show significantly impaired school performance and cognitive abilities even after recovery.[^cite_note-Fernando-2010-106] Consequently, severe and cerebral malaria have far-reaching [socioeconomic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socioeconomic) consequences that extend beyond the immediate effects of the disease.[^cite_note-Ricci-2012-241]

Sophisticated [counterfeits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterfeit_drugs) have been found in several Asian countries such as [Cambodia](334751.md),[^cite_note-Lon-2006-242] [China](5405.md),[^cite_note-Newton-2008-243] [Indonesia](14579.md), [Laos](17752.md), [Thailand](30128.md) and [Vietnam](202354.md), and are a major cause of avoidable death in those countries.[^cite_note-Newton-2006-244] The WHO said that studies indicate that up to 40% of artesunate-based malaria medications are counterfeit, especially in the Greater [Mekong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mekong) region. They have established a rapid alert system to rapidly report information about counterfeit drugs to relevant authorities in participating countries.[^cite_note-Parry-2005-245] There is no reliable way for doctors or lay people to detect counterfeit drugs without help from a laboratory. Companies are attempting to combat the persistence of counterfeit drugs by using new technology to provide security from source to distribution.[^cite_note-Gautam-2009-246]

Another clinical and public health concern is the proliferation of substandard antimalarial medicines resulting from inappropriate concentration of ingredients, contamination with other drugs or toxic impurities, poor quality ingredients, poor stability and inadequate packaging.[^cite_note-Caudron-2008-247] A 2012 study found that roughly one-third of antimalarial medications in Southeast Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa failed chemical analysis, packaging analysis, or were falsified.[^cite_note-Nayyar-2012-248] A 2024 systematic review of African studies conducted between 2014 and 2024 found a lower but still substantial prevalence, with approximately 15.6% of sampled antimalarial medicines found to be substandard or falsified.[^cite_note-Asrade-Mekonnen-2024-249]

In February 2023 a United Nations report estimated that 267,000 deaths per year are linked to counterfeit or substandard medication in Africa alone.[^cite_note-250]

### War



World War II poster

Throughout history, the contraction of malaria has played a prominent role in the fates of government rulers, nation-states, military personnel and military actions.[^cite_note-Russell-2009-251] In 1910, Sir Ronald Ross (himself a malaria survivor), published *The Prevention of Malaria* that included a chapter titled "The Prevention of Malaria in War". The chapter's author, Colonel C. H. Melville, Professor of Hygiene at [Royal Army Medical College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Army_Medical_College) in London, addressed the prominent role that malaria has historically played during wars: "The history of malaria in war might almost be taken to be the history of war itself, certainly the history of war in the Christian era. ... It is probably the case that many of the so-called camp fevers, and probably also a considerable proportion of the camp dysentery, of the wars of the sixteenth, seventeenth and eighteenth centuries were malarial in origin."[^cite_note-Melville-1910-252] In British-occupied India the cocktail [gin and tonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gin_and_tonic#History) may have come about as a way of taking quinine, known for its antimalarial properties.[^cite_note-253]

Malaria was the most significant health hazard encountered by U.S. troops in the South Pacific during [World War II](32927.md), where about 500,000 men were infected.[^cite_note-Bray-2004-254] According to Joseph Patrick Byrne, "Sixty thousand American soldiers died of malaria during the African and South Pacific campaigns."[^cite_note-Byrne-2008-255] Malaria was a contributing factor to the U.S. surrender at [Bataan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Bataan) in 1942.[^cite_note-256]

During [World War II](32927.md), both Germany and the [Axis powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_powers) suffered troop losses caused by malaria and committed resources to malaria prevention. In Germany, concentration camp inmates in [Dachau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dachau_concentration_camp) and [Buchenwald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buchenwald_concentration_camp) were used as guinea pigs for sometimes lethal experimental drug treatments.[^cite_note-257] Early in 1942, the U.S. established a program called Malaria Control in War Areas (MCWA), "established to control malaria around military training bases in the southern United States and its territories, where malaria was still problematic". This organisation evolved into the present day [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention](6811.md).[^cite_note-CDC-History-2010b-258]

## Research directions

### Global coordination

The Malaria Eradication Research Agenda (malERA) was a project carried out in 2007 by the global scientific community to identify the steps and future research that must be done in order to eradicate malaria. It was created after the Malaria Forum in 2007, and resulted in a research and development agenda intended to complement ongoing research into malaria control by identifying knowledge gaps and tools needed for full eradications.[^cite_note-259] In 2015, it was reviewed and updated under 6 headings: "basic science and enabling technologies, insecticide and drug resistance, characterizing the reservoir and measuring transmission, tools for elimination, combination interventions and modeling, health systems and policy research."[^cite_note-260]

### Vaccines

The search for a malaria vaccine started in the 1960s and is ongoing.[^cite_note-El-Moamly-2023-261][^cite_note-262] As of February 2026, two malaria vaccines have completed clinical trials and are in use; they are [RTS,S](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RTS,S) (Mosquirix) and R21/Matrix-MTM,[^cite_note-WHO_recommends_R21-20232-263] both of which target the *P. falciparum* sporozoite;[^cite_note-264] a further 25 clinical trials are in progress.[^cite_note-265]

There are three main types of vaccine candidate. Pre-erythrocytic vaccines aim to block infection completely by incapacitating the sporozoite before it reaches the liver.[^cite_note-El-Moamly-2023-261] Erythrocytic vaccines (blood-stage vaccines) aim to target the merozoite stage in the bloodstream; these have largely proved ineffective due to antigenic polymorphism of this stage of the parasite.[^cite_note-El-Moamly-2023-261] Transmission-blocking vaccines aim to generate antibodies against the sexual stages of the parasite - gametes, zygotes and ookinetes. These either prevent transmission of the parasite from human to mosquito, or hinder the sexual stage of its life cycle within the infected mosquito.[^cite_note-El-Moamly-2023-261]

### Drugs

There are two aspects to drug research. As the parasite evolves to resist to existing drugs, there is an urgent need for new drugs.[^cite_note-Siqueira-Neto-2023-266] There is also ongoing research into existing drugs, to improve their effectiveness by testing drug combinations and dosage,[^cite_note-267] and by improving diagnosis, availability and compliance in people who need them.[^cite_note-268]

An element of the search for new drugs is finding novel mechanisms which can be targeted. There are many candidates - some of these include the parasite's [proteasome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proteasome), [proteases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protease) and [kinases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinase).[^cite_note-269][^cite_note-Siqueira-Neto-2023-266]

### Vector biology and control

This field of research looks at the mosquito vector, identifying and investigating the species which serve as vectors, as well as ways in which they can either be controlled, or prevented from harboring the parasite e.g. by genetic modification.[^cite_note-270][^cite_note-271][^cite_note-272]

### Socioeconomic research

Malaria is strongly linked to poverty.[^cite_note-273] This field of research aims to investigate social and economic factors which obstruct eradication efforts. It includes studies on improving access to diagnostic tools and appropriate treatment in under-served regions.[^cite_note-274] It is also important to understand and address community perceptions of malaria to improve the adoption of preventative measures like bed nets.[^cite_note-275][^cite_note-276]

### Climate-informed malaria surveillance and early warning systems

Recent research has focused on integrating climate data into malaria surveillance systems to improve prediction and prevention of outbreaks. Climate-informed early warning systems use environmental data such as temperature, rainfall and humidity, combined with epidemiological and satellite data, to forecast malaria risk in specific regions.[^cite_note-worldmalariareport-277] These systems allow public health authorities to implement targeted interventions, such as indoor residual spraying and distribution of insecticide-treated bed nets, before outbreaks occur.[^cite_note-278]

Advances in modeling and artificial intelligence have further improved the accuracy of malaria predictions by incorporating real-time climate and environmental data.[^cite_note-279] These approaches are increasingly used in malaria-endemic regions, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, to support proactive disease control strategies. Ongoing research aims to refine these tools and integrate them into national health systems to enhance preparedness and reduce malaria burden.[^cite_note-worldmalariareport-277]

### Emerging technologies

Recent advances in genetic technologies offer new approaches to malaria control. One potential strategy is the genetic modification of mosquitoes using [gene drive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_drive) technologies, such as [CRISPR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRISPR)-[Cas9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cas9).[^cite_note-Naidoo-2025-280][^cite_note-Phillips-2017-281] Through CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing, scientists can introduce genes into mosquito populations that either make them resistant to *[Plasmodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium)* parasites or enable population suppression, where mosquitoes are modified so that any progeny are sterile.[^cite_note-Naidoo-2025-280][^cite_note-Phillips-2017-281]

Gene drive technologies increase the probability that a modified gene will be inherited by offspring, allowing traits such as malaria resistance or sterility to spread rapidly through mosquito populations over generations.[^cite_note-Naidoo-2025-280][^cite_note-Phillips-2017-281] This rapid spread is driven by the short life cycle and high reproductive rate of *[Anopheles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anopheles)* mosquitoes.[^cite_note-CDC-2024-282] They can complete development from egg to adult in approximately 10 to 14 days, with female mosquitoes laying 50–200 eggs at a time.[^cite_note-CDC-2024-282] While these approaches remain in early stages of development and raise ecological and ethical considerations, they present a promising strategy in addition to current interventions.&#x20;

## Other animals

While none of the main four species of malaria parasite that cause human infections are known to have [animal reservoirs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoonosis),[^cite_note-283] *[P. knowlesi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium_knowlesi)* is known to infect both humans and non-human primates.[^cite_note-Collins-2012-47] Other non-human primate malarias (particularly *P. cynomolgi* and *P. simium*) have also been found to have spilled over into humans.[^cite_note-284] Nearly 200 *Plasmodium* species have been identified that infect [birds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium_species_infecting_birds), [reptiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium_species_infecting_reptiles) and [other mammals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium_species_infecting_mammals_other_than_primates),[^cite_note-Rich-2006-285] and about 30 of them naturally infect non-human primates.[^cite_note-Baird-2009-286] Some malaria parasites of non-human primates (NHP) serve as [model organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_organism) for human malarial parasites, such as *[P. coatneyi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium_coatneyi)* (a model for *P. falciparum*) and *[P. cynomolgi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium_cynomolgi)* (a model for *P. vivax*). Diagnostic techniques used to detect parasites in NHP are similar to those employed for humans.[^cite_note-Ameri-2010-287] Malaria parasites that infect rodents are widely used as models in research, such as *[P. berghei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium_berghei)*.[^cite_note-Mlambo-2008-288] [Avian malaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avian_malaria) primarily affects species of the order [Passeriformes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passeriformes), and poses a substantial threat to birds of [Hawaii](13270.md), the [Galapagos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galapagos) and other [archipelagoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archipelago). The parasite *[P. relictum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasmodium_relictum)* is known to play a role in limiting the distribution and abundance of [endemic Hawaiian birds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endemic_birds_of_Hawaii). [Global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming) is expected to increase the prevalence and global distribution of avian malaria, as elevated temperatures provide optimal conditions for parasite reproduction.[^cite_note-Lapointe-2012-289]
