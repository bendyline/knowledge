[Far-right politics](221220.md) are a recurring phenomenon in the [United Kingdom](31717.md) since the early 20th century, with the formation of [fascist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fascism), [antisemitic](1078.md), or [Islamophobic](162173.md) movements. The far-right is an [umbrella term](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umbrella_term) that can encompass a diversity of ideologies ranging from [populist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Populist) to [neo-Nazi](54361.md). Within political science, the far-right is commonly distinguished between the [radical right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_right_\(Europe\)) and [fascism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_fascism).[^cite_note-1] In the UK, the radical right generally accepts [electoral democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representative_democracy) while challenging aspects of [liberal democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberalism), whereas the extreme right rejects democratic principles and includes fascist and Neo-Nazi movements.[^cite_note-2] However, some scholarship identifies an historical and ideological link between the two groups,[^cite_note-3] including Britain.[^cite_note-4]

Early examples of far right politics in Britain was the formation of [British Fascisti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Fascisti) in 1923, followed by [Oswald Mosley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_Mosley)'s [British Union of Fascists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Union_of_Fascists) (BUF) in 1932. The best known episode involving the BUF occurred in 1936 when [antifascists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antifascists) prevented the group from marching through the [East End](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_End_of_London) of [London](17867.md), as part of the [Battle of Cable Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Cable_Street). By the mid to late 20th century, the far right subsequently became dominated by [white nationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_nationalism) organisations that opposed [Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_British) and [South Asian](21566765.md) immigration. Since the 21st century, [far-right terrorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Far-right_terrorism) from the British extreme right has become a growing threat which includes [cultural nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_nationalism), white nationalism and [white supremacist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_supremacist) ideologies. As of the 2020s, scholars have categorised [radical right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_right_\(Europe\)) parties in the UK as belonging to a subset of the far-right, distinguished from the extreme right.

In the 1950s, [A. K. Chesterton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._K._Chesterton) founded the [League of Empire Loyalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Empire_Loyalists). The league would merge into the [National Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_National_Front) (NF) in the 1960s, experiencing a rise in popularity and influence during the 1970s. The NF became the largest post-war far-right party, boosted by the [Rivers of Blood speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rivers_of_Blood_speech) given by [Enoch Powell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enoch_Powell) in 1968. In the 1980s, [John Tyndall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Tyndall_\(politician\)) launched the [British National Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_National_Party) (BNP), with both NF and the BNP maintaining strong opposition to non-white [immigration](4599312.md). As the NF began its decline in the 1990s, the BNP found electoral success in [local elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_local_elections). In 1992 the BNP formed [Combat 18](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combat_18), a neo-Nazi militant wing of the party, and by the end of the decade, [Nick Griffin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_Griffin) replaced Tyndall as BNP leader. During the 2000s, the BNP grew in popularity under Griffin. In 2009, the party won its first seat on the [Greater London Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_London_Assembly) and gained two [MEPs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MEPs). Meanwhile, the NF continued its decline. The BNP reached an electoral peak in the [2010 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_United_Kingdom_general_election) with 1.9% of the vote.

The 2010s saw the rise of the [English Defence League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Defence_League) (EDL), [National Action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Action_\(UK\)), and [Britain First](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Britain_First). With the EDL seeing rapid growth, the [UK Independence Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_Independence_Party) (UKIP) started to gain popularity soon after, while the NF continued declining and the BNP vote share collapsed in the [2015 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_United_Kingdom_general_election). Two years prior, UKIP leader [Nigel Farage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Farage) said that his party absorbed much of the BNP's former voters, with support peaking for the party in 2015. Former BNP members also joined the newly formed [English Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Democrats) and [British Democratic Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Democratic_Party_\(2013\)). In [2017](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_United_Kingdom_general_election), UKIP lost their final MP with Farage departing the party the following year. In 2019 he launched the Brexit Party, which renamed to [Reform UK](59757202.md) in 2021, and won five seats in the [2024 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_Kingdom_general_election).

In 2020, [Europol](525329.md) reported that the UK had the highest number of [far-right terrorist attacks](5210960.md) and plots in Europe during the previous year. That same year, National Action became the first far-right group proscribed under the [Terrorism Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrorism_Act_2000), and the first banned since the Second World War, and the white nationalist group [Patriotic Alternative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriotic_Alternative) (PA) was established, later splitting to form the [Homeland Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeland_Party_\(UK\)) in 2023. The following year, [far-rights riots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_Kingdom_riots) occurred in England and Northern Ireland, the worst disorder in the UK in over a decade, involving [racist attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racist_attack), arson, and looting. 2025 then saw [anti-immigration protests](80681466.md) and a [flag-raising campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Raise_the_Colours), supported by far-right groups and individuals including Britain First and EDL founder [Tommy Robinson](32537879.md), among new political parties [Advance UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advance_UK) and [Restore Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restore_Britain) splitting from Reform UK.

## Terminology

According to the [Centre for Research and Evidence on Security Threats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre_for_Research_and_Evidence_on_Security_Threats?action=edit\&redlink=1) (CREST), in the UK, far-right is an umbrella term for various groups located to the right of mainstream politics. CREST wrote that the term can encompass a diversity of ideologies, and often in a politicised way, is used to label groups ranging from [populist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Populist) political parties to [neo-Nazi](54361.md) cells.[^cite_note-Centre_for_Research_and_Evidence_on_Security_Threats-2017-5] While the term far-right is often associated with fascism, not all far-right groups are fascist, instead some have adopted liberal arguments.[^cite_note-\:8-6]

The radical right has been categorised by scholars as belonging to a subset of far-right politics, distinguished from the extreme right.[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-Bennie-2025-8] CREST reported that 'radical' and 'extreme' are regularly used to distinguish between far-right groups: the radical right-wing groups, those working within democratic processes that share a common ideology with the far-right, and the extreme right, who oppose parliamentary democracy.[^cite_note-Centre_for_Research_and_Evidence_on_Security_Threats-2017-5] Far-right has also been described as the range between the violent extremism of the extreme right and the [illiberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illiberal) populism of the radical right.[^cite_note-\:02-9]

Since 2008, the term 'far right' has been increasingly invoked by scholars, as an umbrella term, based on [nativist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nativism_\(politics\)) and [authoritarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authoritarian) politics between the extreme right and radical right. As of 2022, this usage has also been reflected within the public sphere, the media, and among governing leaders. Concerned with normalising illiberalism by reducing the label to only applying to violent "old-school" white supremacists, professor Nigel Copsey wrote that at its core "all those who comprise the far right share a common [chauvinistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chauvinistic) and exclusionary nationalism, whether [cultural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_nationalism), [ethnic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_nationalism) or [racial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_nationalism)". In 2026, Copsey attributed the ambiguity of the term 'far right' partly to the fragmented nature of organisations in the UK, along with the rejection of the term by far-right figures.[^cite_note-\:02-9]

In the *[British Journal of Politics & International Relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Journal_of_Politics_&_International_Relations)*, Alice Silbey categorises the post-war fascist movement as splitting into three: the "revolutionary [ultranationalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultranationalists)" (otherwise referred to as fascists), the [ethnopluralists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnopluralism), and the less extreme groups that are "compatible with liberal democracy". According to Silbey, the far-right are focused on identity, race, and immigration; they accept nativism, inequality, and authoritarianism; and are made up of "both democratic and anti-democratic extremists".[^cite_note-\:8-6]

According to CREST, with formally organised groups, the origins of such organisations are sometimes used to interpret conclusions, while manifestos and affiliations with other political parties can help to clarify political positions. Often there is limited information available about such groups and the information is contested, examples include the attempted transition of the [BNP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_National_Party) from racism to [cultural nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_nationalism) and populism. With less formalised groups, a greater emphasis can be placed on statements and activities of its members, in absence of further information. The terminology far-right is also used in popular debate to make political arguments, with the politicisation of the term making analysis more difficult.[^cite_note-Centre_for_Research_and_Evidence_on_Security_Threats-2017-5]

### New far-right

"New far-right" redirects here. For far-right politics, see [far-right politics](221220.md).

In 2023, Silbey described a "New Far-Right", based on an anti-Islam (or [counter-jihad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counter-jihad)) framework and distinguished from the "traditional right" by means of opposing fascism, as being defined by "an aggressive opposition to Islam, support of democracy, use of social media, ultra-patriotism, critique of mainstream politics, and the adoption of some [liberal values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_values)".[^cite_note-\:8-6]

The EDL has been described as being part of the European 'new far-right', with scholars identifying the groups ideology as being "a new iteration of traditional far right politics", within a broader ideology of [Islamophobia](162173.md). In 2011, Dr Paul Jackson noted different fractions of the EDL blurring the lines regarding [moderate Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moderate_Muslims) and [radical Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_Islam), while the official EDL narrative was to at minimum try and distinguish between the two, rather than to characterise the religion in its entirety.[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11]

### Radical right

Further information: [Radical right (Europe) § United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_right_\(Europe\)#United_Kingdom)

In the 2020s, UKIP and Reform UK (formerly the Brexit Party) were labelled as radical right, while the categorisation of the BNP remains disputed.[^cite_note-LeCras-2020-12][^cite_note-13] In *[West European Politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_European_Politics)*, Martin, Scott, & Kappe, based the classification of the three parties on their "traditional, authoritarian, and nationalist" characteristics.[^cite_note-Martin-2025-14]

In the *[Australian Journal of Politics and History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Journal_of_Politics_and_History),* LeCras argued that, prior to 2016 and the [EU referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_United_Kingdom_European_Union_membership_referendum), the emergence of the radical right in the UK can be ideologically traced back to the rise of UKIP, originating from the "radical traditions of English conservatism", as opposed to the fascist origins of the extreme right. Additionally, that "the ideological interests of conservative populists and right‐wing extremists in the United Kingdom have frequently intersected — and that this blurring of the boundaries is likely to continue in future manifestations of the country's populist radical right". LeCras further wrote that prior to [Brexit](41688778.md) there was "some uncertainty among political scientists as to what British parties could be included within the populist radical right‐wing family", with [Cas Mudde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cas_Mudde) in 2016 predicting that UKIP would "gravitate further to the radical right" after the result of the EU referendum. In 2020, LeCras said that UKIP had been "broadly accepted as the exemplar of radical right‐wing populism in Britain", whereas BNP remains contested (or "borderline" according to Mudde), based on "its historical connection with neo‐fascism and the extreme right".[^cite_note-LeCras-2020-12] According to CREST, with the BNP serving as an example, there are concerns that "distinguishing between radical and extreme groups serves to gloss over the extreme roots of many of the groups now considered radical, potentially legitimising them."[^cite_note-Centre_for_Research_and_Evidence_on_Security_Threats-2017-5]

### 1930s to 1960s

See also: [British fascism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_fascism)

#### British Union of Fascists



A flowchart showing the history of the early British fascist movement

The British far right rose out of the [fascist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fascism) movement. In 1932, [Oswald Mosley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_Mosley) founded the [British Union of Fascists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Union_of_Fascists), which was banned during the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War).[^cite_note-pb-15][^cite_note-Stephen-17] The party peaked in popularity in mid-1934 with approximately 50,000 members, only to plummet to 5,000 members by late 1935. By the outbreak of the war, the party had recovered to 22,500 members, but remained an underachieving fascist group.[^cite_note-cullen1994-18]

The most famous episode involving the BUF was the [Battle of Cable Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Cable_Street), which occurred in October 1936, after the party announced a march to celebrate the fourth anniversary of the organisation. The increasingly antisemitic BUF chose [East London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_London) as the location, a decision regarded as deliberately provocative as the area was then home to Britain's largest Jewish community. The Home Secretary declined appeals for the march to be banned, leading to approximately 100,000 to 300,000[^cite_note-Britannica-19] people to take to the streets in an attempt to block the route.[^cite_note-The_Battle_for_the_East_End-20]

The fascists assembled at [Tower Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_Hill), where there were clashes between the BUF and counter-protesters. The Police separated the two sides and attempted to clear a route through either [Aldgate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldgate) or [Cable Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cable_Street) for the marchers to take. They failed in this, frustrated by a both passive and direct resistance. The BUF got no further than Tower Hill, and the Police, wishing avoid further disorder, instructed Mosley to leave the [East End](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_End_of_London), and march to the [West End](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_End_of_London) instead.[^cite_note-The_East_End,_Than_and_Now-21]

The Battle was a heavy psychological blow to the BUF and undermined Mosley's authority among senior party figures, leading to resignations, sackings and splits in the months that followed. The BUF also lost prestige with Mussolini and Italian funding began to dry up, leading the BUF to identify more closely with Hitler.[^cite_note-Hurrah_for_the_Blackshirts-22][^cite_note-The_Battle_for_the_East_End-20]

Following the wartime ban, Mosley founded the [Union Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_Movement) in 1948, the year after [India achieved independence from Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_independence_movement). The movement aimed to curb [decolonisation](302084.md), concerned with the diminishing powers of European imperialism, such as [in Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonisation_of_Africa). It was following this that far-right groups became more prevalent.[^cite_note-pb-15][^cite_note-Stephen-17] Mosley argued that fascism was the only possible way to save Britain from socio-economic ruin and a communist takeover.[^cite_note-Matthew-23]

During the 1950s and 1960s, the landscape of extreme right groups continued to evolve with the emergence of organisations such as the [League of Empire Loyalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Empire_Loyalists) (LEL) and the [National Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Front_\(UK\)) (NF). The rise of far-right ideologies during this period can be attributed, in part, to the dismantling of the British Empire, due to a sense of national decline.[^cite_note-pb-15]

#### League of Empire Loyalists

Founded in 1954 by [A. K. Chesterton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._K._Chesterton), the [League of Empire Loyalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Empire_Loyalists) (LEL) became the main British far right group at the time. The LEL believed that the British empire brought around a sense of "pride and joy" and so aimed to "force upon existing parties favourable to national and imperial survival," with the hope to see a return of the "British world... at home and abroad."[^cite_note-pb-15] The LEL was a [pressure group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_group) rather than a political party, and did not contest elections. Most of its members were part of the [Conservative Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Party_\(UK\)), and they were known for politically embarrassing stunts at party conferences. Additionally, throughout its time, LEL was involved in many non-violent protests, which involved heckling speakers.[^cite_note-pb-15][^cite_note-24]

Along with the Union Movement, a [global Jewish conspiracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Jewish_conspiracy) behind the dismantling of the British empire was central to the beliefs of the group. However in contrast to the Union Movement that promoted [new imperialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_imperialism) with the formation of a collaborative European colonial power, LEL cherished the traditional British empire, instead desiring Britain to re-establish imperial control. Despite this diversity of views, both groups failed to mobilise a [mass movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_movement_\(politics\)) of political support.[^cite_note-pb-15]

In 1958, with power transferred from Mosley to the LEL, the [White Defence League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Defence_League) (WDL) and the [National Labour Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Labour_Party_\(UK_1950s\)) (NLP) emerged. Both groups would then merge into the neo-fascist [British National Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_National_Party_\(1960s\)) (BNP) two years later, led by former leaders of the WDL and NLP; [Andrew Fountaine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Fountaine), [Colin Jordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_Jordan), and [John Bean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Bean_\(politician\)).[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-BBC-26] The BNP, which attracted up to 200 supporters,[^cite_note-BBC-26] campaigned against what they described as "the Black invasion of Britain", before being absorbed into the NF by 1967. According to Hope not Hate, "despite antisemitism remaining at its core, anti-immigrant and [anti-black racism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Black_racism) began to take centre stage".[^cite_note-27]

### 1970s to 1990s

#### National Front

Main article: [History of the National Front (UK)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_National_Front_\(UK\))



A National Front march in Yorkshire during the 1970s

The [National Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Front_\(UK\)) (NF) quickly became Britain's largest far-right party post-war,[^cite_note-Taylor-2018-28] boosted in popularity by the [Rivers of Blood speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rivers_of_Blood_speech) given by [Enoch Powell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enoch_Powell) in 1968.[^cite_note-29][^cite_note-30] Founded by [A. K. Chesterton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._K._Chesterton), the NF was formed from the merging of the LEL, the [Bean's BNP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_National_Party_\(1960\)), the [Racial Preservation Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_Preservation_Society), and the [Greater Britain Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Britain_Movement), that was at the time led by Tyndall.[^cite_note-Taylor-2018-28] The NF opposed the mass-migration of non-white migrants throughout the 1970s and was able to boost its popularity through the opposition to the immigration rules introduced by [Edward Heath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Heath), leader of the [Conservative and Unionist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Party_\(UK\)) and prime minister, which saw a boost to their membership, to an estimated 17,500 by 1972.[^cite_note-31]

Throughout the 1970s, the NF saw a rise in popularity and influence, mainly at a local level; for example, in the [1973 West Bromwich by-election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_West_Bromwich_by-election) the NF won 16 per cent.[^cite_note-fr-32] They finished third in three [by-elections](155434.md), although these results were atypical of the country as a whole. The party supported extreme [loyalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_loyalism) in [Northern Ireland](21265.md), and attracted Conservative Party members who had become disillusioned after [Harold Macmillan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Macmillan) had recognised the right to independence of the [African](5334607.md) colonies, and had criticised [Apartheid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Africa_under_apartheid) in South Africa.[^cite_note-33] During the 1970s, the NF's rallies became a regular feature of British politics. Election results remained strong in a few [working class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Working_class) urban areas, with a number of local council seats won, but the party never came anywhere near winning representation in parliament. Throughout its active years, the NF were involved in several violent incidences, notably the 1974 [Red Lion Square disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_of_Kevin_Gately),[^cite_note-BBC-1974-34] over the amnesty of illegal immigrants and the 1977 '[Battle of Lewisham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Lewisham)', which aimed to intimidate local minority residents.[^cite_note-35]

In 1983, the [political soldiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_Soldier), supported by [Nick Griffin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_Griffin), known as the radicals, gradually gained control of the party and pursued the politics of the [Third Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Way), a specific strain of neofascist ideology that embraced radicals such as [Louis Farrakhan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Farrakhan). Integrated with the European neofascist movement, the political soldiers advocated for ethnonationalism and "the death of Europe". On the other hand, the older generation split to form the National Front Support Group, also known as the [Flag Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_Group).[^cite_note-36]

#### British National Party

Nick Griffin led the BNP from 1999 to 2014.

[John Tyndall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Tyndall_\(politician\)) formed the [New National Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_National_Front) in 1980, and changed its name to the [British National Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_National_Party) (BNP) in 1982. They, alongside the Conservative Monday Club, campaigned against the increasing integration of the UK into the [European Union](9317.md). However, with Thatcher in her prime and Tyndall's reputation of a 'brutal, street fighting background' and his admiration for [Hitler](2731583.md) and the [Nazis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Party) prevented the party from gaining any respectability.

The BNP have had a number of local councillors in some inner-city areas of [East London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_London), and towns in [Yorkshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yorkshire) and [Lancashire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lancashire), such as [Burnley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burnley) and [Keighley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keighley). East London has been the bedrock of far-right support in the UK since the 1930s, whereas BNP success in the [north of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_of_England) was a newer phenomenon. The only other part of the country to provide any significant level of support for such views is the [West Midlands](351076.md).

There was some success in 1993, BNP scored its first electoral success when [Derek Beackon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derek_Beackon) won a [council vote seat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1993_Millwall_by-election) on the Isle of Dogs with 34 per cent vote.[^cite_note-fr-32] They developed a policy of eschewing the traditional far right methods of extra-parliamentary movements, and concentrated instead on the ballot box. [Nick Griffin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_Griffin) replaced Tyndall as BNP leader in 1999 and introduced several policies to make the party more electable. Repatriation of ethnic minorities was made voluntary and several other policies were moderated.

#### Combat 18

In 1992, with assistance from US neo-Nazi [Harold Covington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Covington),[^cite_note-Institute_for_Jewish_Policy_Research-1998-37] the BNP formed the neo-Nazi [Combat 18](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combat_18) (C18) group intended as stewards for events,[^cite_note-38][^cite_note-Ryan_1998-39] while operating as a militant wing.[^cite_note-40] The "18" in its name is derived from the initials of the [Nazi Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Party) leader [Adolf Hitler](2731583.md): A and H are the first and eighth letters of the [Latin alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_alphabet).[^cite_note-BBC_20012-41] Adopting features of the [far-right in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Far_right_in_the_United_States), the group promotes violence and antisemitism.[^cite_note-Institute_for_Jewish_Policy_Research-1998-37] In 1998, after the imprisonment of the groups leader for killing a C18 supporter, *[The Independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Independent)* described the group as "the UK's most notorious neo-Nazi group".[^cite_note-Ryan_1998-39]

### 2000s

The National Front continued to decline, whilst Nick Griffin and the BNP grew in popularity and was one of the most successful and fastest-growing far-right parties in the twenty-first century.[^cite_note-cp-42] It promoted ethnic nationalism and believed that "being British is more than merely a passport," as such, it is noted by some, such as [Matthew Goodwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matt_Goodwin_\(author\)), that this is what separates the BNP from other parties in British politics.[^cite_note-43] However, throughout the twentieth and twenty-first century the BNP were able to established itself as an alternative for working-class voters who were angry at the "political establishment" for its ignorance towards their concerns over immigration, which allowed the BNP to have representation at a local council level throughout the mid-2000s.[^cite_note-cp-42][^cite_note-44]

The [2006 local elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_United_Kingdom_local_elections) brought the BNP a considerable breakthrough. They gained 33 council seats,[^cite_note-\:2-45] the second highest gain of any party at the elections;[^cite_note-46] in [Barking and Dagenham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Borough_of_Barking_and_Dagenham), they gained 12 councillor seats.[^cite_note-\:2-45] In the [2008 local elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_United_Kingdom_local_elections), the party won a seat on the [Greater London Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_London_Assembly) for the first time.[^cite_note-47] In the [2009 European Parliament Election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_European_Parliament_election_in_the_United_Kingdom), the BNP gained two Members of the European Parliament for [Yorkshire and the Humber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yorkshire_and_the_Humber_\(European_Parliament_constituency\)) and [North West England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_West_England_\(European_Parliament_constituency\)).[^cite_note-48] Analysing the EU elections, Ford, Goodwin, & Cutts, concluded that "aggregate research provides evidence of high correlations between support for UKIP and the extreme right BNP", due to the perception of both parties "adopt\[ing] similar discourses on issues of immigration and national identity.[^cite_note-49]

In the [2009 Doncaster mayoral election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_Doncaster_mayoral_election), [English Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Democrats) candidate and former UKIP campaigner, [Peter Davis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Davies_\(politician\)), was elected, becoming the party's only electoral success.[^cite_note-\:7-50][^cite_note-51] In October 2009, BNP leader Nick Griffin was invited on the BBC topical debate show *[Question Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Question_Time_\(TV_series\))*. [His appearance caused much controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Question_Time_British_National_Party_controversy) and the show was watched by almost 8 million people.[^cite_note-52]

### 2010s



British National Party (BNP) vote share in the 2010 UK general election

The 2010s saw the rise of the English Defence League (EDL), National Action (NA) and Britain First (BF). At the beginning of this decade, it was determined that domestic terrorism, such as ERW terrorism, was not a threat to the UK.[^cite_note-rw-53] Throughout the 2010s, there was a continuing trend of the far-right being more intimidating towards minority groups.[^cite_note-54]

At the [2010 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_United_Kingdom_general_election), the BNP fielded a record 338 candidates, more than 200 candidates compared to 2005,[^cite_note-\:1-55] and won 563,743 votes (1.9% of total) but no seats.[^cite_note-56] The election was the largest push for success at the ballot box by an extreme right party in British history. Considered a blow to the party despite arguably being their best performance by vote count, the party also failed to retain any of the 12 councillors in [Barking and Dagenham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barking_and_Dagenham) in the concurrent [local election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_United_Kingdom_local_elections), with only 2 councillors re-elected out of a total of 28. In Barking, 6,620 votes were cast for party leader Griffin, becoming the highest number of votes for a BNP candidate in a general election; although in percentage terms, Griffin's vote share declined by 2.3 percent to 14.6.[^cite_note-\:1-55]

Since 2010, a number of former BNP members joined the [English Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Democrats).[^cite_note-57] In 2013, [Peter Davies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Davies_\(politician\)), the incumbent [Mayor of Doncaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor_of_Doncaster), quit the party due to the concern over the influx of BNP member joining the party.[^cite_note-\:7-50] Party leader [Robin Tilbrook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robin_Tilbrook) stated that approximately 200–300 out of the party's membership of 3,000 were former BNP members.[^cite_note-58] The BNP and [English Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Democrats) shared views including opposing the [Barnett formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnett_formula) and the creation of an [English Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_parliament), along with rejecting [political correctness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_correctness), multicultural [Britishness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Britishness), and the perceived threat of [Islamification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamification) in Britain or England. While UKIP felt the politics of [ethnic nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_nationalism) threatened the [British Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Union), the EDL shared common views with the BNP on immigration and Islam, along with the English Democrats' version of English preservation. In *[The British Journal of Politics & International Relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_British_Journal_of_Politics_&_International_Relations)*, Hayton & Mycock wrote that "it is clear that [Englishness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Englishness) is implicitly ethnicised", in reference to the English Democrats stating "public culture of England should be that of the indigenous English", similar to the BNP's conceptions based on the 'white working class'.[^cite_note-59]

In 2010, [Nick Griffin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_Griffin) announced that he would resign as BNP leader in 2013 after 15 years steering the party.[^cite_note-60] Having stepped down in 2014 he was subsequently expelled later that year, accused of factionalism and destabilising the party. According to Griffin, divisions were based on the decline in electoral support since 2009.[^cite_note-61] The following year, BNP membership was estimated at 500,[^cite_note-62] and at the [2015 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_United_Kingdom_general_election), the party's vote share declined 99.7% from 2010, having fielded only eight candidates.[^cite_note-63]

[Nigel Farage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Farage), who had departed from UKIP in early December 2018,[^cite_note-64][^cite_note-65] launched the [Brexit Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brexit_Party) in April 2019,[^cite_note-66] that would later change its name to [Reform UK](59757202.md).[^cite_note-BBC_News-2021-67] Farage stated that there was "no difference between the Brexit party and UKIP in terms of policy, \[but] in terms of personnel, there's a vast difference", criticising UKIP's connections to the far right.[^cite_note-guardian-68]

Professor Copsey wrote that the British far-right in the post-2010 era had "lost its organisational anchor" after the collapse of the BNP, with what developed described as "unpredictable and fluid, ranging from Islamophobic street protest on the one hand, fronted by online/street influencers such as ‘[Tommy Robinson](32537879.md)’, to radicalising right-wing populism on the other, associated with Nigel Farage, and his many party-political incarnations", including UKIP and the Brexit Party.[^cite_note-\:0-69]

#### English Defence League



[Street protest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demonstration_\(people\)) organised by the EDL in Newcastle, England, in 2010

The racist and Islamophobic group, the [English Defence League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Defence_League), oversaw early "rapid and unprecedented" growth,[^cite_note-dl-70] appealing to nationalist sentiments on a cultural rather than explicitly racial basis. Originally formed in [Luton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luton) in 2009,[^cite_note-\:3-71] by former BNP member [Tommy Robinson](32537879.md),[^cite_note-\:4-72] it protested against what it considered the [Islamification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamification) of Britain.[^cite_note-\:3-71] Its ideology was driven by opposition to Islam, which it deemed a "threat" to "our way of life, our customs, and our rule of law."[^cite_note-dl-70] This opposition led the EDL to organise demonstrations in towns and cities across [England](9316.md), the largest of which occurred in Luton in February 2011.[^cite_note-73]

In November 2011, the EDL came to an agreement with the [British Freedom Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Freedom_Party), in order to contest local elections. Robinson shared a vice-chair role in the party, with [Paul Weston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Weston_\(politician\)), a former [UK Independence Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_Independence_Party) candidate, as chairman.[^cite_note-indy-74][^cite_note-\:4-72] The party was founded as a BNP breakaway founded by [Eddy Butler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eddy_Butler), who had previously made an unsuccessful attempt to oust the BNP leader Griffin.[^cite_note-\:4-72]

#### Britain First

In 2011, the far-right, anti-Islam, and fascist party [Britain First](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Britain_First) shared views similar to that of the EDL.[^cite_note-dl-70] This movement was formed by former members of the BNP[^cite_note-ibt-75] and campaigns primarily against [immigration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration_to_the_United_Kingdom_since_Irish_independence), [multiculturalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiculturalism) and what it sees as the [Islamisation of the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_the_United_Kingdom), and aims to protect with the intention of "protecting British and Christian morality."[^cite_note-dl-70] The group is inspired by [Ulster loyalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_loyalism) and has a [vigilante](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vigilante) wing called the "Britain First Defence Force". Throughout its time, BF was largely digital. In response to videos of young Muslims intimidating women, gay men and those drinking alcohol, BF became more confrontational in its resistance against Muslims and Islam.[^cite_note-dl-70] Additionally, It attracted attention by taking [direct action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_action) such as protests outside homes of alleged Islamists, and what it describes as "[Christian patrols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharia_patrols_\(London\)#Responses)" and "invasions" of British mosques,[^cite_note-ibt-75][^cite_note-76] and has been noted for its [online activism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_activism).[^cite_note-77] Its leader [Paul Golding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Golding) stood as a candidate in the [2016 London mayoral election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_London_mayoral_election), receiving 31,372 or 1.2% of the vote, coming eighth of twelve candidates.[^cite_note-78] Golding was jailed for eight weeks in December 2016 for breaking a court order banning him from entering mosques or encouraging others to do so.[^cite_note-79] In 2018, Golding was convicted and imprisoned again, this time for harassment.[^cite_note-\:5-80]

#### British Democratic Party

In February 2013, the [British Democratic Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Democratic_Party_\(2013\)) (BDP) was launched by former [Member of the European Parliament](258909.md) (MEP) and [National Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Front_\(UK\)) chairman [Andrew Brons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Brons), who had resigned from the BNP in October 2012 after narrowly failing in his campaign to unseat [Nick Griffin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_Griffin) as BNP leader in [2011](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_British_National_Party_leadership_election).[^cite_note-Colin_Liddell-81] Brons remains the BDP's inaugural president, and the chairman is James Lewthwaite.[^cite_note-82] The BDP has attracted former members of the [British National Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_National_Party) (BNP), Democratic Nationalists, Freedom Party, [UK Independence Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_Independence_Party) (UKIP),[^cite_note-83][^cite_note-Colin_Liddell-81] [For Britain Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/For_Britain_Movement), and [Civil Liberty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Liberty_\(UK\)), including long-standing far-right political leader [John Bean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Bean_\(politician\)). In 2013, Nick Lowles of [Hope not Hate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hope_not_Hate) believed the party would be a serious threat to the BNP, commenting "The BDP brings together all of the hardcore [Holocaust deniers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocaust_denial) and racists that have walked away from the BNP over the last two to three years, plus those previously, who could not stomach the party's image changes".[^cite_note-Collins-84]

#### National Action



A National Action protest in 2014 at the [statue of Nelson Mandela in Parliament Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statue_of_Nelson_Mandela,_Parliament_Square), with the use of a [Nazi salute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_salute)

Founded in 2013 by Christopher Lythgoe, [National Action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Action_\(UK\)) is a terrorist organisation and the first extreme right-wing group banned by the UK government since the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War).[^cite_note-85] National Action is described as a Neo-Nazi organisation that stirs up "hatred, glorifies violence and promotes vile ideology."[^cite_note-na-86] It focused mainly on attracting young people through targeted propaganda.[^cite_note-na-86] In 2016, 22 members were arrested and charged for being members,[^cite_note-87] with by the end of the year the group being proscribed under the [Terrorism Act 2000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrorism_Act_2000).[^cite_note-88] In the years that followed, many figures were linked to the group and arrested on suspicion of plots to commit extremist acts, such as plotting to kill [Rosie Cooper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosie_Cooper), in a bid to "replicate" the [murder of Jo Cox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murder_of_Jo_Cox),[^cite_note-89] and the possession of terrorist manifestos.[^cite_note-90]

With origins rooted in the BNP, National Action was originally part of the [intellectual tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intellectual_tradition) within the far-right, before moving towards direct action and becoming increasingly confrontational with other far-right groups, such as the EDL. In contrast to the tradition within the British far-right since the 2000s, the organisation publicly identified as part of the [national socialist movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Socialist_movement) and embraced a more traditional Nazi ideology, while distancing itself from populism such as that of UKIP and its supporters. In 2017, Hope not Hate suggested that the group had become the most dominant influence within the British far-right. Its ideology includes ultranationalism, racism, and antisemitism.[^cite_note-91]

#### UK Independence Party

In 2015, the [UK Independence Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_Independence_Party) (UKIP) was not universally considered far-right.[^cite_note-92][^cite_note-93] With the recent collapse of the BNP and EDL, professor [Steven Fielding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Fielding_\(political_historian\)) attributed the rise of the UKIP to having gained BNP voters and "extreme [Eurosceptics](10037.md)". Political scientist, [Tim Bale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tim_Bale), commented "you could say that UKIP is populist radical right".[^cite_note-94]

The [British National Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_National_Party) (BNP) blamed UKIP for its decline, accusing the latter of stealing BNP policies and slogans.[^cite_note-95] UKIP leader [Nigel Farage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Farage) claimed that his party absorbed much of the BNP's former voters during their electoral peak in the early 2010s.[^cite_note-\:6-96] UKIP's vote and support peaked at the [2015 United Kingdom general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_United_Kingdom_general_election) with nearly 4 million votes representing 12.6% of the vote, up from 3.1% in the [2010 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_United_Kingdom_general_election), consequently surpassing the [Liberal Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Democrats_\(UK\)) as the third most voted for party.[^cite_note-97] UKIP support steadily declined thereafter, polling only 1.8% percent in the [2017](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_United_Kingdom_general_election) and losing their final seat,[^cite_note-98] followed by 0.1% in [2019](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_United_Kingdom_general_election).[^cite_note-99]

From 2018 to 2019, under the leadership of [Gerard Batten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerard_Batten), [UKIP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UKIP) was widely described as moving into [far-right](221220.md) territory, at which point many longstanding members – including former leaders [Nigel Farage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Farage) and [Paul Nuttall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Nuttall) – left. As the new permanent leader, Batten focused the party more on opposing Islam and sought closer relations with the far-right activist Stephen Yaxley-Lennon, otherwise known as [Tommy Robinson](32537879.md), and his followers.[^cite_note-100] Batten would leave the leadership of UKIP in 2019.[^cite_note-101]

#### For Britain Movement

In October 2017, former [UKIP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UKIP) [leadership candidate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_UK_Independence_Party_leadership_election) and [anti-Islam](162173.md) activist [Anne Marie Waters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_Marie_Waters) launched the [For Britain Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/For_Britain_Movement).[^cite_note-102][^cite_note-103][^cite_note-104] Unlike most far-right parties that came before them, For Britain were [zionist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zionist), opposed to [antisemitism](1078.md), and held more moderate views on social issues like [LGBT rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGBT_rights). Former [English Defence League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Defence_League) leader [Tommy Robinson](32537879.md) and singer-songwriter [Morrissey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morrissey) announced their support for the party,[^cite_note-105] and fellow far-right and [counter-jihad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counter-jihad) political party [Liberty GB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberty_GB) merged with For Britain.[^cite_note-106] The party received support from several former members of the BNP, [Generation Identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generation_Identity), and National Action. For Britain had some limited success in local council elections, but failed to make any significant breakthroughs in the parliamentary by-elections they contested.[^cite_note-107]

#### Patriotic Alternative

In September 2019,[^cite_note-Hope_not_Hate-108] the former director of publicity of the BNP, neo-Nazi[^cite_note-109] and [antisemitic conspiracy theorist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antisemitic_canard)[^cite_note-Gdn-110][^cite_note-111] [Mark Collett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Collett) established the far-right group [Patriotic Alternative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriotic_Alternative) (PA). The group promotes a [white nationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_nationalism) ideology;[^cite_note-Hope_not_Hate-108] its stance has been variously described as [Islamophobic](162173.md), fascist and racist.[^cite_note-112][^cite_note-113][^cite_note-114][^cite_note-115][^cite_note-116] According to Hope not Hate, members of PA have supported [political violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_violence), the [white genocide conspiracy theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_genocide_conspiracy_theory), and [Holocaust denial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocaust_denial).[^cite_note-Hope_not_Hate2-117][^cite_note-118] The group opposes [Black Lives Matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Lives_Matter), has displayed [White Lives Matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Lives_Matter) banners around the UK,[^cite_note-Report_2021-119] and campaigned against what they call "white genocide".[^cite_note-120]

### 2020s–present

In July 2022, Waters announced that [For Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/For_Britain) was ceasing all operations with immediate effect,[^cite_note-121] with their elected councillor subsequently joining the [British Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Democratic_Party_\(2013\)). The BDP experienced a sharp increase in membership that year, with several former members of [Britain First](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Britain_First) and the [BNP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_National_Party).[^cite_note-HOPE_not_hate-2-122] In [the local elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_United_Kingdom_local_elections) the following year, UKIP lost its remaining district and county councillors, with professor [Tim Bale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tim_Bale) declaring the party as having been wiped out.[^cite_note-123] The BDP also performed poorly losing its sole councillor amid standing five candidates,[^cite_note-HOPE_not_hate-2-122] with PA supporting the BDP in the elections, according to [*Searchlight*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Searchlight_\(magazine\)).[^cite_note-124]

In November 2023, *The Times* described PA as "Britain's largest far-right [white supremacist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_supremacist) movement".[^cite_note-Mitib_Ledwith_Parker_2023_b658-125] According to Hope not Hate, splits, especially the 2023 creation of the [Homeland Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeland_Party_\(United_Kingdom\)), which has eclipsed it, then pushed PA into decline.[^cite_note-HnH20250424-126]

In August 2024, a prominent member of PA was reported to have taken part in the [Southport riot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Southport_riot), while another member helped to promote the event,[^cite_note-Kennedy-20242-127][^cite_note-Wallis-2024-128] amid the EDL being considered defunct as an organisation.[^cite_note-129] As of April 2025, PA has around 500 members,[^cite_note-HnH20250424-126] and in May, Collett urged PA supporters to [infiltrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entryism) the [Reform UK](59757202.md) party, seeking to move the party further to the right.[^cite_note-130] Supporters of the group also organised and participated in [anti-immigration protests](80681466.md).[^cite_note-131] The following month, after disagreement with [Nigel Farage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Farage), former [Deputy Leader of Reform UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deputy_Leader_of_Reform_UK), [Ben Habib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Habib), launched [Advance UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advance_UK).[^cite_note-Self-20252-132] Having received endorsement from [Tommy Robinson](32537879.md),[^cite_note-133][^cite_note-Grierson-2025-134] the party sponsored the [Unite the Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unite_the_Kingdom) rally in September.[^cite_note-The_Observer-2025-135][^cite_note-Grierson-20252-136] It announced de-registering as a political party the following June,[^cite_note-137][^cite_note-138] with Habib endorsing [Restore Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restore_Britain) due to being "very, very similar".[^cite_note-139]

After the [2024 riots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_UK_riots), what developed was based on the same "underlying grievances" according to professor Copsey, this includes the [Operation Raise the Colours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Raise_the_Colours) campaign the following year. As of 2025, the UK far-right currently thrives on social media platforms, having adapted after the riots towards flag raising that lacks an openly extremist appearance. In 2026, Copsey described the shift as transitioning in recent years "from violent, extra-parliamentary protest to decentralised, non-violent cultural-symbolic activism that weaponised [national identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_identity) through flags, heritage and territorial marking."[^cite_note-\:0-69] In April 2026, [Jonathan Portes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Portes), writing for [UK in a Changing Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_in_a_Changing_Europe), argued that claims the UK was close to civil war and could encourage narratives in which democratic processes are seen as fundamentally compromised.[^cite_note-140]

#### Reform UK



[Reform UK](59757202.md) leader [Nigel Farage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Farage), described by professor [Tim Bale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tim_Bale) as the "British representative of the [populist radical right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Populist_radical_right) in Europe"

[^cite_note-141]

The Brexit Party officially changed its name to [Reform UK](59757202.md) in January 2021.[^cite_note-BBC_News-2021-67] Farage subsequently stepped down as leader and was replaced by the party chairman, [Richard Tice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Tice),[^cite_note-142] before Farage replaced Tice as leader again in June 2024.[^cite_note-guardian-u-turn-143]

Since 2020,[^cite_note-LeCras-2020-12] [Reform UK](59757202.md) has been described by political scientists as [radical right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_right_\(Europe\)), as a subset of far-right politics,[^cite_note-Bennie-2025-8][^cite_note-Martin-2025-14] and the party has been categorised as far-right in various peer-reviewed academic journals.[^cite_note-144] Reform UK rejects the descriptor of far-right, and has threatened legal action against media using it.[^cite_note-Naughtie_2024_h130-145] After winning 14% of the vote and winning five seats in the [2024 UK general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_Kingdom_general_election), political scientist [Tim Bale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tim_Bale) described Reform UK party leader Farage as the "British representative of the populist radical right in Europe".[^cite_note-146] According to Bale in 2025: "most political scientists" would say that Reform is a far-right party, "if you accept that as the umbrella term and then say they're populist radical right".[^cite_note-147]

In the *[Parliamentary Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_Affairs)* journal, Bennie & Widfeldt categorised Reform UK as far-right by fitting "into a broader European family of radical right parties working within democratic structures".[^cite_note-Bennie-2025-8] In [*British Politics*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Politics_\(journal\)), Hayton placed this as "outside of the mainstream right and as part of the far right, but distinguishes it from the extreme right elements of the far right."[^cite_note-148] In the *[Journal of Contemporary European Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_Contemporary_European_Studies)*, Shuttleworth, Brown & Mondon stated that the categorisation of Reform UK as far-right had been regularly left out from academic and public discourse, despite its "clear credentials".[^cite_note-149]

#### Homeland Party

The [Homeland Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeland_Party_\(UK\)) was founded as a splinter of [Patriotic Alternative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriotic_Alternative) (PA) in April 2023 by the white nationalist [Kenny Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenny_Smith_\(far-right_activist\)),[^cite_note-MS-150][^cite_note-WN-151] and registered as a party in January 2024.[^cite_note-152] The party was formed predominately by Scottish members who had left PA. It is ideologically similar to PA and ideological differences were not a reason for the split.[^cite_note-BF-153][^cite_note-FR-154][^cite_note-SF-155] Homeland espouses the [white genocide conspiracy theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_genocide_conspiracy_theory) and has been described as the largest [fascist group in the UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_fascism) by [Hope not Hate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hope_not_Hate).[^cite_note-156][^cite_note-HOPE_not_hate-157]

Internationally, Homeland is associated with the far-right European parties [Alternative for Germany](38708229.md) (AfD) and the Polish [Confederation Liberty and Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederation_Liberty_and_Independence).[^cite_note-HOPE_not_hate-157] The party also merged with a local branch of the European [Identitarian movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identitarian_movement).[^cite_note-158] In April 2025 *[The Telegraph](23797577.md)* described the party as "[nationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalism) and [anti-immigration](6491626.md)", highlighting one of its policies of "the re-migration, or encouraged mass emigration, of unintegrated and [illegal migrants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illegal_immigration_to_the_United_Kingdom)."[^cite_note-159]

#### 2024 riots

Main article: [2024 United Kingdom riots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_United_Kingdom_riots)



A police van on fire during the 30 July 2024 riots in [Southport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southport)

In July and August 2024, far-right riots occurred in England and Northern Ireland, after [a mass stabbing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Southport_stabbing) in [Southport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southport) on 29 July.[^cite_note-Far-Right-160] The first riot started in Southport and later many protests and riots spread across the country. The riots involved [racist attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racist_attack), [arson](184695.md) and [looting](209811.md), and were the worst disorder in the [United Kingdom](31717.md) since the [2011 England riots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_England_riots).[^cite_note-161][^cite_note-CNN-Picheta-05Aug24-162] The riots were fuelled by underlying [Islamophobia](162173.md),[^cite_note-163][^cite_note-Al-Jazeera-2024-164][^cite_note-BBC-Church-20Aug24-165] racist,[^cite_note-White-2024-166][^cite_note-AlJazeera-4Aug24-167] anti-immigrant and anti-mass immigration sentiments,[^cite_note-AlJazeera-03Aug24-168][^cite_note-169][^cite_note-Independent-02Aug24-170] and disinformation about the identity of the Southport stabber. Far-right groups spread misinformation online.[^cite_note-Casciani-2024-171][^cite_note-l154-172][^cite_note-Bintliff-2024-173]

The riots had limited formal organisation;[^cite_note-McDonald-2024-174][^cite_note-Financial_Times-2024-175] instead, rioters assembled around individual far-right social media personalities[^cite_note-McDonald-2024-174][^cite_note-Financial_Times-2024-175] with the aid of far-right [Telegram](41501305.md) group chats[^cite_note-Financial_Times-2024-175] affiliated with [Active Club England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_Club_England), the [terrorgram](72327036.md) network, and [football hooliganism firms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Football_hooliganism_in_the_United_Kingdom).[^cite_note-Georgia_Lambert-2024-176] Groups involved in the riots included supporters of the EDL, including its former leader Tommy Robinson,[^cite_note-Toff-2024-177] members of Patriotic Alternative,[^cite_note-Kennedy-2024-178] and Britain First.[^cite_note-Evening_Standard-2024-179] The riots were also supported by the British Movement and National Front.[^cite_note-Bintliff-2024-173][^cite_note-Casciani-20242-180] Rioters clashed with local Muslims and [counter-protesters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counter-protest),[^cite_note-181][^cite_note-Parker-2024-182] who were mobilised by [Stand Up to Racism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stand_Up_to_Racism)[^cite_note-Murray-2024-183] and other anti-fascist and anti-racist groups.[^cite_note-Cobham-2024-184][^cite_note-BBC_News-2024b-185][^cite_note-Cheshire-2024-186]

#### 2025 anti-immigration disorder

Main article: [2025 British anti-immigration protests](80681466.md)



Anti-immigration protest in [Aberdeen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aberdeen) on 6 September 2025

Since early 2025, [protests](266137.md) against [immigration](1247786.md) have taken place in parts of the [United Kingdom](31717.md).[^cite_note-187][^cite_note-188][^cite_note-189][^cite_note-190][^cite_note-191][^cite_note-192] The protests have led to officers being attacked and multiple arrests.[^cite_note-BBC25Hotel-193][^cite_note-194][^cite_note-195] Some have been focused around hotels being used by the [government of the United Kingdom](25318118.md) to provide accommodation for [asylum seekers](276005.md), while others are against immigration more broadly. Many were organised or attended by [far-right](221220.md) groups including [Britain First](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Britain_First), [UKIP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UKIP), [Homeland Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeland_Party_\(United_Kingdom\)), and [Patriotic Alternative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriotic_Alternative), who have also been accused of spreading misinformation online.[^cite_note-196][^cite_note-197][^cite_note-198]

The wave of far-right anti-immigration protests began in the aftermath of a series starting in [Epping](26609107.md) in July, later spreading to other cities.[^cite_note-199][^cite_note-independent-200][^cite_note-wapo-201] Protests occurred across the country, with disorder in cities including [Bristol](36741.md), [Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glasgow), [Liverpool](18081.md), [Manchester](20206.md) and [Newcastle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newcastle_upon_Tyne); the largest protest came on 13 September when up to 150,000 people took part in a [London](17867.md) march organised by far-right activist [Tommy Robinson](32537879.md), where 26 police officers were injured.[^cite_note-202][^cite_note-wapo-201]

UKIP organised some of the protests under their "Mass Deportations Tour", which has seen them met with sometimes large counter-protests organised by groups such as [Stand Up To Racism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stand_Up_To_Racism).[^cite_note-203][^cite_note-204] The protests have seen an increase in far-right efforts to use fears of violence against women to lend their views respectability.[^cite_note-independent-200][^cite_note-205][^cite_note-206] Multiple experts consulted by [The Independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Independent) – including Andrea Simon, director of the [End Violence Against Women Coalition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End_Violence_Against_Women_Coalition); Lois Shearing, author of *Pink Pilled*; and a researcher for [Hope not Hate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hope_not_Hate) – said that the far-right was exploiting fears to make the far-right seem more "palatable", and that this is to appeal to a broader audience and gain more of a foothold in mainstream opinion. According to Simon, "these narratives are promoted by those who exploit genuine public concerns about sexual violence to intentionally fuel racism in our communities."[^cite_note-independent-200]

#### Restore Britain

In June 2025, [Rupert Lowe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rupert_Lowe) launched [Restore Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restore_Britain).[^cite_note-207] The party has been described as [far-right](221220.md)[^cite_note-208][^cite_note-Quinn-2026-209][^cite_note-Politico2-210][^cite_note-211] to the right of [Reform](59757202.md),[^cite_note-Mulhall-2026-212][^cite_note-213][^cite_note-214] as well as part of the [radical right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_right_\(Europe\)).[^cite_note-Politico2-210] Lowe has stated indifference to Restore Britain being described as far-right or [racist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racism).[^cite_note-215][^cite_note-Mulla-216] [Hope not Hate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hope_not_Hate) categorised Restore as "part of a broader re-racialisation of the British far right" with openly [racial politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_politics), while assembling a coalition of figures located "to the right of Reform, all the way through to open [fascists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fascists)".[^cite_note-Mulhall-2026-212]

In April 2026, *[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times)* reported that "prominent neo-fascist leaders" had backed the party. Former official of the [British National Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_National_Party), Simon Birkett, described Restore Britain as the last hope for the far-right to achieve political power, stating that there was a "a world of difference" between the party and Reform UK. According to [Steve Laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Laws), the party can be trusted due to being "well to the right" of other far-right parties including [Britain First](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Britain_First), the [British Democratic Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Democratic_Party_\(2013\)), and the [Homeland Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeland_Party_\(UK\)).[^cite_note-217] A month prior, Britain First had agreed to defer to Restore Britain in order to avoid fielding candidates in the same areas.[^cite_note-\:4b-218]

#### 2025 flag raising



A [Union Flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_Flag) flying from a lamppost, September 2025

Main article: [Operation Raise the Colours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Raise_the_Colours)

"Operation Raise the Colours" is a 2025 [political campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_campaign) in the [United Kingdom](31717.md) that consists of displaying the [Union Flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_Flag) and the [Saint George's Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_England) in public places, as well as the flags of the other [constituent countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countries_of_the_United_Kingdom) of the UK. The campaign began in August 2025, shortly after the beginning of the [2025 British anti-immigration protests](80681466.md), and has particularly aroused controversy around the [Flag of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_England) due to its history of use by anti-immigration [nationalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_nationalism). It has involved tying flags to [lamp-posts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_light) and painting them onto [mini-roundabouts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mini-roundabout). Supporters of the campaign say that this is with the aim of promoting [patriotism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriotism) and it is [non-partisan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonpartisanship), although it has been supported by several figures and organisations associated with the far-right, including the [fascist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fascist) political party [Britain First](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Britain_First) and the [anti-Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counter-jihad) campaigner [Tommy Robinson](32537879.md).[^cite_note-Guardian0925-219][^cite_note-220][^cite_note-221]

Anti-racist organisation Stand Up to Racism expressed its opposition to the campaign and organised counter-protests against Raise the Colours campaigners putting up flags.[^cite_note-Guardian0925-219] The group, citing the campaign's support by Robinson and Britain First, said that it is an intentional attempt by the far-right to spread their ideas, inflame tensions and intimidate asylum seekers, immigrants, Muslims and other minority groups amid rising anti-immigration sentiment in the United Kingdom.[^cite_note-Guardian0925-219] Anti-racist group Hope not Hate have said that some of the campaign's organising had been implemented by far-right activists.[^cite_note-222]

## Right-wing terrorism

See also: [Right-wing terrorism](5210960.md)

Between 2007 and 2022, seventy people associated with the far-right have been convicted of more than two hundred terrorist offences, based on eight differenent offences from the [Terrorism Act 2000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrorism_Act_2000) and [2006](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrorism_Act_2006). In 2013, the UK government assessed that right-wing extremism was a "very low risk to national security"; this changed the following year after the emergence of the neo-Nazi group [National Action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Action_\(UK\)). Since 2014, there has been a significant increase in the threat from **extreme right-wing terrorism[^cite_note-224]** (ERWT) in the UK. In response, experts in security and intelligence have increased monitoring of threats from groups and individuals holding extreme right-wing views. [Counter Terrorism Policing](54350607.md) reported that between March 2017 and December 2021, 12 of 32 imminent terrorism plots that were thwarted were ERWT. The [Independent Reviewer of Terrorism Legislation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_Reviewer_of_Terrorism_Legislation) reported in 2022 that one in five counter-terrorism investigations and 40% of terrorism-related arrests were related to far-right perspectives. As of 2025, ERWT includes [cultural nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_nationalism), [white nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_nationalism) and [white supremacist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_supremacist) ideologies.[^cite_note-Jupp-2025-223]

### Origins

In the UK, ERWT can be traced back to [Sir Oswald Mosley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Oswald_Mosley)’s [British Union of Fascists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Union_of_Fascists), an [ultranationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultranationalism) group that was subsequently banned in 1940. In *[Studies in Conflict & Terrorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Studies_in_Conflict_&_Terrorism)*, Jupp wrote that "Politically motivated violence and terrorism have been persistent features of this movement, which comprises a complex assortment of political parties, street movements, extremist organizations and atomized lone actors advocating a diverse range of ideologies and narratives." This includes individuals associated with the NF and BNP, with David Copeland's [London bombings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1999_London_nail_bombings) serving as a notable example.[^cite_note-Jupp-2025-223]

Over three successive weekends between 17 and 30 April 1999, David Copeland set off homemade nail bombs in [Brixton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brixton) in south London; at [Brick Lane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brick_Lane), Spitalfields, in the East End; and at the [Admiral Duncan pub](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Admiral_Duncan_pub) in Soho in the West End. Copeland, who became known as the "London nail bomber", was a neo-Nazi militant and a former member of the BNP and the [National Socialist Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Socialist_Movement_\(UK,_1997\)). The bombings were aimed at London's [Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_British_people), [Bengali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Bangladeshis), and [LGBT communities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGBTQ_communities). Copeland was convicted of murder in 2000 and given [six life sentences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Back-to-back_life_sentences).[^cite_note-Buncombe-225][^cite_note-226][^cite_note-Jupp-2025-223] Copeland killed 3 people and injured 140 others.[^cite_note-Jupp-2025-223]

### 2008–2019

In June 2008, a [Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi) sympathiser was convicted of three terrorism charges, having kept four home-made and other weapons in his home. He was convicted for preparing, possessing articles, and collecting information, for terrorist purposes; and also admitted to thousands of images of [child pornography](25211885.md).[^cite_note-227] In December 2008, a [neo-Nazi](54361.md) was sentenced to more than seven years for racially aggravated harassment and possession of material for terrorist purposes.[^cite_note-228] In June 2009, a neo-Nazi who wanted to target 'non-British' people was jailed for more than seven years.[^cite_note-229]

In February 2012, a [Home Affairs Select Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_Affairs_Select_Committee) reported "persuasive evidence" regarding the possible threat from the far-right, referring to the criticism of the government's [Prevent strategy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prevent_strategy). The committee also referenced the growth of such groups as well as connections with European organisations. Chairman of the committee, [Keith Vaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keith_Vaz) MP, stated that "more resources need to be directed to these threats and to preventing radicalisation through the internet and in private spaces". The [Home Office](225695.md) responded stating that the strategy primarily "tackles the threat from home-grown terrorism on and off line".[^cite_note-230]

In June 2016, [member of parliament, Jo Cox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murder_of_Jo_Cox), was murdered by Thomas Alexander Mair who had links to British and American far-right political groups including the NF, the U.S. neo-Nazi organisation [National Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Alliance_\(United_States\)), and the EDL.[^cite_note-Guardian332-231][^cite_note-Stelloh2-232][^cite_note-Potok2-233][^cite_note-SPLC2-234] Writers at *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)* suggested that he targeted Cox, because he viewed her as "one of 'the collaborators' \[and] a traitor" to [white people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_people).[^cite_note-Guardian332-231] In December, [Home Secretary](149104.md) [Amber Rudd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amber_Rudd) designated NA as a terrorist organisation.[^cite_note-235]

In June 2017, multiple terrorist attacks occurred. Darren Osbourne [used a van to attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_Finsbury_Park_van_attack) Muslim worshippers at Finsbury Park mosque, killing 1 and injuring 11.[^cite_note-236] Ethan Stables planned an attack with a machete on a Gay Pride event in [Cumbria](73021.md), and was later sentenced to [life in prison](148681.md).[^cite_note-237] Jack Renshaw, a neo-Nazi from [Lancashire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lancashire), was charged with the [attempted murder](906044.md) of Labour MP [Rosie Cooper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosie_Cooper), and also subsequently jailed for life.[^cite_note-238] The following month, CREST stated that: "Although the vast majority of far-right activists are non-violent, far-right activism has security implications in the UK and globally. Risks associated with the far-right include large-scale acts of terrorism, [hate crime](13547.md), and [public order issues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_order_crime). Far-right narratives also undermine [community cohesion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community_cohesion) and can support narratives offered by opposing [extremist groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extremist_groups)."[^cite_note-Centre_for_Research_and_Evidence_on_Security_Threats-2017-5]

In 2018 February, [Mark Rowley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Rowley_\(police_officer\)), the outgoing head of UK counter-terror policing, revealed that four far-right terror plots had been foiled since the [Westminster attack in March 2017](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_Westminster_attack).[^cite_note-239] The following month, the government reported that the threat from ERWT had been growing. The government added that in the previous five years there had been four lone-actor terrorist attacks, including the murder of Jo Cox, motivated to varying degrees by extreme right-wing ideologies.[^cite_note-sf-240] The government also said that prior to 2014, far-right activity was confined to a small, established group that promoted anti-immigration and [white supremacist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_supremacist) views, and that these groups were assessed present a very low risk to national security.[^cite_note-sf-240] In June, [Jack Renshaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Renshaw_\(far-right_activist\)), an alleged former member of NA, admitted planning to murder [Rosie Cooper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosie_Cooper) MP as an act of terrorism.[^cite_note-241][^cite_note-242]

In May 2019, the [Royal United Services Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_United_Services_Institute) criticised the [Home Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_Office_\(UK\)) for its "apparent unwillingness" to engage with the threats from ERWP. The institute anticipated further increase in right-wing extremist activity in the UK as well as Europe, describing a "[leaderless vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaderless_resistance) in which these groups can flourish".[^cite_note-243] Four months later, the [Metropolitan Police](192450.md) assistant commissioner, [Neil Basu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neil_Basu), declared that ERWT was his "fastest-growing problem" and that there was the clear correlation between far-right activity and the rise of [hate crimes](13547.md); while being unsure if the link was "causal", given the history of the threat from the far-right in the country.[^cite_note-244]

### 2020–present

In June 2020, [Europol](525329.md) reported that the UK had the highest number of far-right terrorist attacks and plots in Europe during the previous year, with one attack and three foiled.[^cite_note-Ind19Europol-245] The attack was a 50-year-old in [Stanwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanwell) attacking cars with non-white drivers before stabbing a Bulgarian teenager. The man was targeting Muslims, and shouted: "All Muslims should die, white supremacists rule. I’m going to murder a Muslim." *[The Independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Independent)* reported that a plot referred to in the report was believed to be a teenage neo-Nazi who had planned attacks on synagogues and other targets in [Durham, England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durham,_England).[^cite_note-Ind19Europol-245]

In September 2021, a neo-Nazi member of "The British Hand" was convicted for attempting to acquire a gun to shoot his friend as part of a terrorist plot.[^cite_note-246] The group, which consisted of a small cell of up to 15 young members,[^cite_note-247] was founded by a 14 year old teenager in August 2020 who was also convicted.[^cite_note-248]

In 2022, the [Intelligence and Security Committee of Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence_and_Security_Committee_of_Parliament) published a report on ERWT which said that key views of various far-right groups include white supremacy, cultural nationalism, [opposition to immigration](6491626.md) and the [Identitarian movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identitarian_movement). Far-right groups and individuals disproportionately target ethnic minority and religious groups, LGBT+ groups, politicians, and public figures.[^cite_note-rw-53] The threat by the extreme right has moved from being a political movement to being a greater threat to national security.[^cite_note-House_of_Commons_Library-2022-249] On 31 March 2022, of the 233 prisoners in custody for terrorism-connected offences, 57 were categorised as extreme-right.[^cite_note-House_of_Commons_Library-2022-249]

In April 2024, the [Terrorgram Collective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrorgram_Collective) was added to the list of proscribed organisations,[^cite_note-250] becoming the sixth extreme right-wing group proscribed. The [National Centre for Social Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Centre_for_Social_Research) described the collective as "a network of fascist, neo-Nazi and white supremacist channels on Telegram that label terrorists as ‘Saints’ and praise their attacks, including the [1999 London nail bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1999_London_nail_bombings), the [2011 Norway attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Norway_attacks) and the [2019 Christchurch attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christchurch_mosque_shootings)."[^cite_note-251] Home Secretary [James Cleverly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Cleverly) labelled the international group "an online network of [neo-fascists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-fascists)" and stated that the collective "spreads vile propaganda and aims to radicalise young people to conduct heinous terrorist acts".[^cite_note-252] The UK was the first country to proscribe the group, with the United States and Canada following suit.[^cite_note-253]
