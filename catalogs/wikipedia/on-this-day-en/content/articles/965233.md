The **culture of Iran** ([Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language): فرهنگ ایران) or **culture of Persia**[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-2][^cite_note-yarshater1-3] is one of the oldest and among the most influential in the world. [Iran](14653.md) ([Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Name_of_Iran)) is widely regarded as one of the [cradles of civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cradle_of_civilization).[^cite_note-HI-4][^cite_note-CC-5][^cite_note-IC-6][^cite_note-AC-7] Because of its dominant [geopolitical position](163225.md) in the world, it has heavily influenced peoples and cultures situated in [Southern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Europe) and [Eastern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Europe) to the west; [Central Asia](6742.md) to the north; and [South Asia](21566765.md), [East Asia](19605700.md), and [Southeast Asia](28741.md) to the east.[^cite_note-HI-4][^cite_note-CC-5][^cite_note-8] [Iranian history](64213.md) has significantly influenced the world through [art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_art), [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_architecture), [poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_literature), [science and technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_and_technology_in_Iran), [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Iranian_medicine), [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_philosophy), and [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_engineering).

An "eclectic cultural elasticity" has been said to be one of the key defining characteristics of the Iranian identity and a clue to its historical longevity.[^cite_note-9]

## History

The history of Iran's culture is marked by the influence of ancient civilizations such as the [Elamites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elamite_dynasty) and [Persians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persians), as well as the [Achaemenid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire) and [Sassanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_Empire) empires.[^cite_note-10]

The [Arab conquest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_conquest) in the 7th century introduced Islamic traditions, which merged with pre-Islamic customs. This fusion resulted in a distinct cultural identity characterized by significant contributions to [literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_literature), [art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_art), and philosophy.[^cite_note-11] Iran has historically served as a center of culture and learning, impacting the surrounding region.[^cite_note-HI-4][^cite_note-CC-5][^cite_note-IC-6][^cite_note-AC-7] The [South Caucasus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Caucasus) and [Central Asia](6742.md) "occupy an important place in the historical geography of Persian civilization." Much of the region was included in the pre-Islamic [Persian empires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_empire), and many of its ancient peoples either belonged to the Iranian branch of the Indo-European peoples (e.g. [Medes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medes) and [Soghdians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soghdians)), or were in close cultural contact with them (e.g. the [Armenians](387816.md)).[^cite_note-12] In the words of Iranologist [Richard Nelson Frye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Nelson_Frye):

> Many times I have emphasized that the present peoples of central Asia, whether Iranian or Turkic speaking, have one culture, one religion, one set of social values and traditions with only language separating them.

The culture of Persia has thus developed over several thousand years. But historically, the peoples of what are now [Iran](14653.md), [Kurdistan Region](3949386.md),[^cite_note-13] [Armenia](10918072.md), [Azerbaijan](746.md), [Turkey](11125639.md), [Uzbekistan](31853.md), [Tajikistan](30108.md), [Turkmenistan](198149.md),[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15] Eastern [Georgia](48768.md), and [Afghanistan](737.md) are related to one another as part of the larger group of peoples of the [Greater Iranian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Iran) cultural and historical sphere. The [Northern Caucasus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Caucasus) is well within the sphere of influence of Persian culture as well, as can be seen from the many remaining relics, ruins, and works of literature from that region.[^cite_note-16][^cite_note-17]

## Art

Main article: [Persian art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_art)



Intricate stone art of [Persepolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persepolis)

Iran has one of the oldest, richest, and most influential art heritages in the world,[^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19] which encompasses many disciplines including [literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literature), [music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music), [dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dance), [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture), [painting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Painting), [weaving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weaving), [pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pottery), [calligraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calligraphy), [metalworking](266443.md), [embroidery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embroidery), and [stonemasonry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stonemasonry). Iranian art has gone through numerous phases, which is evident from the unique aesthetics of Iran. From the [Elamite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elam) [Chogha Zanbil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chogha_Zanbil) to the [Median](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medes) and Achaemenid reliefs of [Persepolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persepolis) to the [mosaics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosaic) of [Bishapur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishapur).&#x20;

The [Islamic Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age) brought changes to the styles and practice of the arts. However, each Iranian dynasty had its own particular foci, building upon the previous dynasty's, all of which during their times were heavily influential in shaping the cultures of the world then and today.

### Language

Several [languages are spoken throughout Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Iran), including languages from the [Iranian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_languages), [Turkic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkic_languages), and [Semitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitic_languages) language families. According to the [CIA World Factbook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_World_Factbook), 78% of Iranians speak an Iranian language as their [native tongue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native-language_identification), 18% speak a Turkic language as their native tongue, and 2% speak a Semitic language as their native tongue, while the remaining 2% speak languages from various other groups.[^cite_note-21] Although the [Azerbaijanis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azerbaijanis) speak a Turkic language, their culture, history, and genetics are often associated with the [Iranian people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_peoples).[^cite_note-22]

The predominant language and national language of Iran is [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language), which is spoken fluently across the country. [Azerbaijani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azerbaijani_language) is spoken primarily and widely in the northwest, [Kurdish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurdish_language) and [Luri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luri_language) are spoken primarily in the west, [Mazandarani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mazandarani_language) and [Gilaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilaki_language) are spoken in the regions along the [Caspian Sea](19653787.md), [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language) primarily in the Persian Gulf coastal regions, [Balochi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balochi_language) primarily in the southeast, and [Turkmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkmen_people) primarily in northern border regions. Smaller languages spread in other regions notably include [Talysh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talysh_language), [Georgian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_language), [Armenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_language), [Assyrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_Neo-Aramaic), and [Circassian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circassian_language).[^cite_note-23][^cite_note-24][^cite_note-25] According to recent scientific papers, the following languages have the highest number of speakers in Iran: Persian, 63.3%; Azeri Turkic (and its dialects), 13%; Kurdish, 7%; Gilaki, 3.6%; Mazandarani (or Tabari), 3%; Arabic, 1.8%; other languages (including Lorish and Balochi), 8.3%.[^cite_note-26]

### Literature



[Behistun inscription reliefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behistun_Inscription)



[Tomb of Hafez in Shiraz, Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomb_of_Hafez)

See also: [Persian literature in Western culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_literature_in_Western_culture)

The [literature of Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literature_in_Iran) is one of the world's oldest and most celebrated, spanning over 2,500 years from the many Achaemenid inscriptions, such as the [Behistun inscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behistun_Inscription), to the celebrated Iranian poets of the Islamic Golden Age and modern Iran.[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28][^cite_note-29] [Iranian literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_literature) has been described as one of the great literature of humanity and one of the four main pillars of world literature.[^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31] [L.P. Elwell-Sutton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L.P._Elwell-Sutton) describes the literature of the Persian language as "one of the richest poetic literature of the world".[^cite_note-32]

Very few literary works of [pre-Islamic Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Islamic_Iran) have survived following the destruction of the libraries of [Persepolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persepolis) by [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great) and the subsequent [invasion of Iran by the Arabs in 641](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_conquest_of_Persia), who sought to eradicate all non-[Quranic texts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quran).[^cite_note-EL-33] This resulted in all Iranian libraries being destroyed and books either being burnt or thrown into rivers. The only way that Iranians could protect these books was to bury them, but many of the texts were forgotten over time.[^cite_note-EL-33] As soon as circumstances permitted, the Iranians wrote books and assembled libraries.[^cite_note-EL-33]



[The Tomb of Ferdowsi in Tus, Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomb_of_Ferdowsi)&#x20;

Iranian literature encompasses a variety of literature in the languages used in Iran. Modern Iranian literature includes [Persian literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_literature), [Azerbaijani literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azerbaijani_literature), [Kurdish literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurdish_literature), and the literature of the remaining minority languages. The Persian language has been often dubbed as the most worthy language of the world to serve as a conduit for poetry.[^cite_note-34] Azerbaijani literature was developed highly after Iran's first reunification in 800 years under the [Safavid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_dynasty), whose rulers themselves wrote poetry.[^cite_note-35] There remain a few literary works of the extinct Iranian language of [Old Azeri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Azeri_language) that was used in [Azerbaijan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azerbaijan_\(Iran\)) prior to the linguistic Turkification of the people of the region.[^cite_note-36] Kurdish literature has incorporated the various Kurdish dialects that are spoken throughout the [Middle East](19323.md). The earliest works of Kurdish literature are those of the 16th-century poet [Malaye Jaziri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaye_Jaziri).[^cite_note-37]

Some notable poets include [Ferdowsi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdowsi), [Saadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saadi_\(poet\)), [Hafiz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafiz_Shirazi), [Attar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attar_of_Nishapur), [Nezami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nezami_Ganjavi), [Jami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jami), [Rumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jalal_ad-Din_Muhammad_Rumi), [Omar Khayyam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omar_Khayy%C3%A1m), [Taleb Amoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taleb_Amoli), [Ubayd Zakani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ubayd_Zakani), [Shams Tabrizi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shams_Tabrizi), [Rudaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudaki) and [Vahshi Bafqi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vahshi_Bafqi).[^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39] These poets have inspired [Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goethe), [Ralph Waldo Emerson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Waldo_Emerson), and many others. Contemporary literature has been influenced by classical Persian poetry, but also reflects the particularities of modern-day Iran, through writers such as [Houshang Moradi-Kermani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houshang_Moradi-Kermani), the most translated modern Iranian author, and poet [Ahmad Shamlou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmad_Shamlou).[^cite_note-40]

### Music

See also: [List of Iranian musicians and singers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Iranian_musicians_and_singers)



Music, dance, poetry and wine-drinking at the court of 17th century ruler [Shah Abbas II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shah_Abbas_II)

[Iranian music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Iran) has directly influenced the cultures of [West Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Asia), [Central Asia](6742.md), [Europe](9239.md), and [South Asia](21566765.md).[^cite_note-XI-41] It has mainly influenced and built up much of the musical terminology of the neighboring Turkic and Arabic cultures and reached India through the 16th-century [Persianate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persianate_society) [Mughal Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire), whose court promoted new musical forms by bringing Iranian musicians.[^cite_note-XI-41]

Iran is the place of origin of complex instruments, with the instruments dating back to the 3rd millennium BC.[^cite_note-MH-42] Several [trumpets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trumpet) made of silver, gold, and copper were found in eastern Iran that are attributed to the [Oxus civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bactria%E2%80%93Margiana_Archaeological_Complex) and date back between 2200 and 1750 BC. The use of both vertical and horizontal [angular harps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_harp) have been documented at the archaeological sites of Madaktu (650 BC) and [Kul-e Fara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kul-e_Farah) (900–600 BC), with the largest collection of Elamite instruments documented at Kul-e Fara. Multiple depictions of horizontal harps were also sculpted in [Assyrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyria) palaces, dating back between 865 and 650 BC.[^cite_note-MH-42]

The reign of Sassanian ruler [Khosrow II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khosrow_II) is regarded as a "golden age" for Iranian music. Sassanid music is where many the many music cultures of the world trace their distant origins. The court of Khosrow II hosted several prominent musicians, including Azad, [Bamshad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bamshad), [Barbad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbad), [Nagisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagisa_\(harpist\)), Ramtin, and [Sarkash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarkash). Among these attested names, Barbad is remembered in many documents and has been named as remarkably highly skilled. He was a poet-musician who developed [modal music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mode_\(music\)) and may have invented the [lute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lute) and the musical tradition that was to transform into the forms of [dastgah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dastgah_music) and [maqam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_maqam).[^cite_note-MH-42][^cite_note-EI-43][^cite_note-44] He has been credited to have organized a musical system consisting of seven "royal modes" (*xosrovāni*), 30 derived modes (*navā*), and 360 melodies (*dāstān*).[^cite_note-MH-42][^cite_note-EI-43]

The academic [classical music of Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_traditional_music), in addition to preserving [melody types](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melody_type) that are often attributed to Sassanian musicians, is based on the theories of sonic aesthetics as expounded by the likes of Iranian musical theorists in the early centuries after the [Muslim conquest of the Sasanian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_conquest_of_Persia), most notably [Avicenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avicenna), [Farabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Farabi), [Qotb-ed-Din Shirazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qutb_al-Din_al-Shirazi), and [Safi-ed-Din Urmawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safi_al-Din_al-Urmawi).[^cite_note-XI-41]

### Dance

See also: [Persian dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_dance) and [Mithraism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mithraism)

Iran has a rich and ancient dance culture that extends from the 6th millennium BC. Dances from ancient artifacts, excavated at the archaeological pre-historic sites of Iran, portray a vibrant culture that mixes different forms of dances for all occasions. In conjunction with music, the artifacts depicted actors, dancers, and ordinary people dancing in plays, dramas, celebrations, mourning, and religious rituals with equipment such as costumes of animals or plants, masks, and surrounding objects. As time progressed, this culture of dance began to develop and flourish.[^cite_note-45]

Although the cultures of its ethnic groups are very similar and in most areas nearly identical, each has its own distinct and specific dance style. Iran possesses four categories of dance with these being: group dances, solo improvisational dances, war or combat dances, and spiritual dances. Typically, the group dances are often unique and named after the region or the ethnic groups with which they are associated. These dances can be chain dances involving a group or the more common group dances mainly performed at festive occasions like weddings and [Noruz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noruz) celebrations which focus less on communal line or circle dances and more on solo improvisational forms, with each dancer interpreting the music in their special way but within a specific range of dance vocabulary sometimes blending other dance styles or elements.[^cite_note-PD-46] [Solo dances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solo_dance) are usually reconstructions of the [historical and court dances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_dance) of the various Iranian dynasties throughout history, with the most common types being that of the [Safavid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_dynasty) and [Qajar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qajar_dynasty) dynasties due to them being relatively newer.[^cite_note-PD-46] These often are improvisational dances and utilize delicate, graceful movements of the hands and arms, such as wrist circles.[^cite_note-PD-46]

[War or combat dances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_dance) imitate combat or serve as training for warriors. For instance, the ritualized wrestling movements of the [Zurkhaneh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pahlevani_and_zoorkhaneh_rituals) ("House of Strength") are considered a form of dance called "Raghs-e-Pa" or "Pay-Bazi." These movements also resemble [martial arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martial_arts) and are part of the cultural heritage of warrior training.[^cite_note-PD-46][^cite_note-47] Spiritual dances are known as "sama". These dances serve spiritual purposes, such as removing ill omens or exorcising evil spirits. These dances involve trance, music, and complex movements. One example is the [Balochi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baloch_people) dance "le'b gowati", performed to rid a possessed person of the offending spirit. In the [Balochi language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balochi_language), the term "gowati" refers to psychologically ill patients who have recovered through music and dance.[^cite_note-48][^cite_note-49]

The earliest researched dance from Iran is a dance worshiping [Mithra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mithra), the [Zoroastrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism) angelic divinity of covenant, light, and oath, which was used commonly by the [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire) [cult of Mithra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mithraism).[^cite_note-FH-50] One of the cult's ceremonies involved the sacrifice of a bull followed by a dance that promoted vigor in life.[^cite_note-FH-50]

### Architecture

[Iranian architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_architecture) dates back to at least 5000 BC with characteristic examples distributed over a vast area from Turkey and Iraq to Uzbekistan and Tajikistan to the South Caucasus and Zanzibar. Currently, there are 19 [UNESCO](21786641.md)-designated [World Heritage Sites](44940.md) that were designed and constructed by Iranians, with 11 of them being located outside of Iran. Iranian architecture displays a great variety of both structure and aesthetics and despite the repeated trauma of destructive invasions and cultural shocks, the Iranian zeal and identity has always triumphed and flourished. In turn, it has greatly influenced the architecture of its invaders from the [Greeks](42056.md) to the [Arabs](2185.md) to the [Turks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkic_peoples).[^cite_note-AP-51][^cite_note-AU-52]



[Sassanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sassanian) fortress in [Derbent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derbent), Dagestan. Inscribed on Russia's [UNESCO](21786641.md) world heritage list since 2003.

The traditional theme of Iranian architecture is cosmic symbolism, which depicts the communication and participation of man with the powers of heaven. This theme has given continuity and longevity to the architecture and has been a primary source of the emotional character of the nation. Architecture ranges from simple structures to "some of the most majestic structures the world has ever seen".[^cite_note-AU-52][^cite_note-NA-53] Architectural style is the combination of intensity and simplicity to form immediacy, while ornament and, often, subtle proportions reward sustained observation. Iranian architecture makes use of abundant symbolic geometry, using pure forms such as the circle and square, and plans are based on often symmetrical layouts featuring rectangular courtyards and halls. The paramount virtues of Iranian architecture are: "a marked feeling for form and scale; structural inventiveness, especially in [vault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vault_\(architecture\)) and [dome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_domes) construction; a genius for decoration with a freedom and success not rivaled in any other architecture".[^cite_note-54]

The traditional architecture is categorized into two families and six following classes or styles. The two categories are Zoroastrian and Islamic, which reference the eras of pre-Islamic and post-Islamic Iran, and the six styles, in order of their era, are Parsian, Parthian Khorasani, Razi, Azari, Esfahani. The pre-Islamic styles draw on 3,000 to 4,000 years of architectural development from the various civilizations of the Iranian plateau. The post-Islamic architecture draws ideas from its pre-Islamic predecessor and has geometrical and repetitive forms, as well as surfaces that are richly decorated with glazed tiles, carved [stucco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stucco), patterned brickwork, floral [motifs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motif_\(art\)), and [calligraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calligraphy).[^cite_note-55]

In addition to historic gates, palaces, bridges, buildings, and religious sites that highlight the highly developed supremacy of the Iranian art of architecture, gardens are also an example of cosmic symbolism and unique style of combining intensity and simplicity for form immediacy.[^cite_note-AP-51][^cite_note-NA-53] There are currently 14 Iranian gardens that are listed as UNESCO World Heritage Sites, with 5 of them being located outside of Iran.[^cite_note-56] The traditional style of Iranian gardens is to represent an earthly paradise or a heaven on Earth. From the time of the Achaemenid Empire, the idea of an earthly paradise spread through Iranian literature to other cultures, with the word for paradise in the Iranian languages of Avestan, Old Persian, and Median, spreading to languages across the world.[^cite_note-MF-57] The style and design of the Iranian garden greatly influenced the garden styles of countries from Spain to Italy and Greece to India, with some notable examples of such gardens being the gardens of the [Alhambra](30543.md) in Spain, [Humayun's Tomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humayun's_Tomb), and the [Taj Mahal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taj_Mahal) in India, the [Hellenistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period) gardens of the [Seleucid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucid_Empire) and the [Ptolemies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_Kingdom#List_of_Ptolemaic_rulers) in [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria).[^cite_note-MF-57]

## Cuisine

[Iranian cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_cuisine) refers to the culinary practices of Iran. Due to the historically common usage of the term "[Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Name_of_Iran)" to refer to Iran in the [Western world](21208200.md),[^cite_note-58][^cite_note-59][^cite_note-yarshater1-3] it is alternatively known as Persian cuisine, despite [Persians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persians) being only one of a multitude of [Iranian ethnic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnicities_in_Iran) who have contributed to Iran's culinary traditions.[^cite_note-61] Iran has a rich variety of traditional dishes, and has influenced many other cuisines over the ages, among them the [cuisine from the Caucasus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_dishes_from_the_Caucasus), [Central Asian cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Asian_cuisine), [Greek cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_cuisine), [Levantine cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levantine_cuisine), [Mesopotamian cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_cuisine), [Russian cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_cuisine) and [Turkish cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_cuisine).[^cite_note-62][^cite_note-63][^cite_note-64][^cite_note-65] Aspects of Iranian cuisine have also been significantly adopted by [Indian cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_cuisine) and [Pakistani cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistani_cuisine) through various historical [Persianate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persianate_society) sultanates that flourished during [Muslim rule on the Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_rulers_in_the_Indian_subcontinent), with the most notable and influential of these polities being the [Mughal Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire).[^cite_note-66][^cite_note-67][^cite_note-Holland2014-68]

Typical Iranian main dishes are combinations of rice with meat, vegetables and nuts. Herbs are frequently used, along with fruits such as [plums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plum), [pomegranates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pomegranate), [quince](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quince), [prunes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prune), [apricots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apricot) and [raisins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raisin). Characteristic Iranian spices and flavourings such as [saffron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saffron), [cardamom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardamom), and [dried lime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dried_lime) and other sources of sour flavoring, [cinnamon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinnamon), [turmeric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turmeric) and [parsley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parsley) are mixed and used in various dishes. Outside of Iran, a strong presence of Iranian cuisine can be found in cities with significant [Iranian diaspora](9573713.md) populations, namely the [San Francisco Bay Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_Bay_Area), [Toronto](64646.md),[^cite_note-london-69][^cite_note-LA-70][^cite_note-71][^cite_note-72] [Houston](13774.md) and especially [Los Angeles and its environs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tehrangeles).[^cite_note-london-69][^cite_note-LA-70][^cite_note-73]

Bread an important food, with a large variety, some of the most popular of which include: [nan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naan) which is baked in large clay ovens (also called "tenurs"). There are many dishes that are made from dairy products. One of the most popular of which includes yoghurt ("mast")—which has a specific fermentation process that is widely put to use amongst most Iranians. In addition, [mast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strained_yogurt) is used to make soup and is vital in the production of oil. In addition to these dairy products, Iranian cuisine involves a lot of dishes cooked from rice. Some popular rice dishes include boiled rice with a variety of ingredients such as meats, vegetables, and seasonings ("plov") including dishes like chelo-horesh, shish kebab with rice, chelo-kebab, rice with lamb, meatballs with rice, and kofte (plain boiled rice). In addition, Iranian cuisine is famous for its sweets. One of the most famous of which includes "baklava" with almonds, cardamom, and egg yolks. Iranian sweets typically involve the use of honey, cinnamon, lime juice, and sprouted wheat grain. One very popular dessert drink in Iran, "sherbet sharbat-portagal", is made from a mixture of orange peel and orange juice boiled in thin sugar syrup and diluted with rose water. The preferred drink of the people of Iran is tea (without milk).[^cite_note-Cuisine2-74]

## Religion

Main article: [Religion in Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_Iran)

See also: [Religion and culture in ancient Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_and_culture_in_ancient_Iran)



[Shah Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shah_Mosque_\(Isfahan\)), [Isfahan](223149.md)

[Zoroastrianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism), [the ancient religion of Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_religions), was the faith of Iran's people for more than a millennium before the Rashidun conquest. It has had an immense influence on Iranian philosophy, culture and art after the people of Iran adopting Islam.[^cite_note-SS-75] The Persian [Samanid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samanid_Empire) dynasty made great attempts to spread the Islamic faith in the 9th and 10th century while promoting a Persian cultural revival. Until the 16th century, Iran ushered a golden age of the arts and sciences.[^cite_note-SS-75] In 1501 the [Safavid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_Iran) dynasty took control of Iran and made [Shia Islam the state religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_conversion_of_Iran_to_Shia_Islam), with this being one of the most important events in Islamic history.[^cite_note-SS-75]

Of Iran’s Muslim population, about 89% are Shi’a and about 9% are Sunni.[^cite_note-76] Iran has 37–40% of the global Shia population.[^cite_note-77]

Followers of the [Baháʼí Faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bah%C3%A1%CA%BC%C3%AD_Faith) form the largest non-Muslim minority in Iran. Baháʼís are scattered throughout small communities in Iran, although there seems to be a large population of Baháʼís in [Tehran](57654.md). The Iranian government actively pursues the [persecution of Baháʼís](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persecution_of_Bah%C3%A1%CA%BC%C3%ADs).

Followers of the [Christian](5211.md) faith consist of around 250,000 Armenians, around 32,000 [Assyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_people), and a small number of Roman Catholic, Anglican, and Protestant Iranians that have been converted by missionaries in earlier centuries. Thus, Christians that live in Iran are primarily descendants of indigenous Christians that were converted during the 19th and 20th centuries. Judaism is an officially recognized faith in Iran, and in spite of the hostilities between Iran and Israel over the Palestinian issue, the millennia-old Jewish community in Iran enjoys the right to practise their religion freely as well as a dedicated seat in parliament to a representative member of their faith. In addition to Christianity and Judaism, Zoroastrianism is another officially recognized religion in Iran, although followers of this faith do not hold a large population in Iran. In addition, although there have been instances of prejudice against Zoroastrians, most followers of this faith have not faced widespread persecution.[^cite_note-Religion-78]

## Philosophy

Main articles: [Ancient philosophy § Ancient Iranian philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_philosophy#Ancient_Iranian_philosophy), and [Iranian philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_philosophy)



[Scholars Pavilion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholars_Pavilion) is a monument donated by Iran to the [United Nations Office in Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Office_at_Vienna), with statues of Iranian mediaeval scholars.

Iranian philosophy can be traced back as far as Old Iranian philosophical traditions and thoughts which originated in ancient [Indo-Iranian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Iranians) roots and were influenced by [Zarathustra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zarathustra)'s teachings. Throughout Iranian history and due to remarkable political and social changes such as the Arab and Mongol invasions, a wide spectrum of schools of thoughts showed a variety of views on philosophical questions, extending from Old Iranian and mainly Zoroastrianism-related traditions, to schools appearing in the late pre-Islamic era such as [Manichaeism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manichaeism) and [Mazdakism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mazdakism) as well as post-Islamic schools.

The [Cyrus Cylinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_Cylinder) is seen as a reflection of the questions and thoughts expressed by [Zoroaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroaster) and developed in Zoroastrian schools of the Achaemenid era.[^cite_note-79] Post-Islam Iranian philosophy is characterised by different interactions with the [Old Iranian philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_philosophy#Ancient_Iranian_philosophy), the [Greek philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_philosophy) and with the development of [Islamic philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_philosophy). The [Illumination School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illumination_School) and the [Transcendent Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendent_Philosophy) are regarded as two of the main philosophical traditions of that era in Iran. Contemporary Iranian philosophy has been limited in its scope by [intellectual repression](80259436.md).[^cite_note-80]

## Mythology and folklore



Statue of [Rostam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rostam), with his son [Sohrab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sohrab), in [Sabzevar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabzevar)

[Iranian mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_mythology) consists of ancient [Iranian folklore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_folklore) and stories of extraordinary beings reflecting on [good and evil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_and_evil) ([Ahura Mazda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahura_Mazda) and [Ahriman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahriman)), actions of the gods, and the exploits of heroes and creatures. The tenth-century Persian poet, [Ferdowsi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdowsi), is the author of the [national epic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_epic) known as the *[Shahnameh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shahnameh)* ("Book of Kings"), which is for the most part based on *Xwadāynāmag*, a [Middle Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Persian) compilation of the history of Iranian kings and heroes,[^cite_note-81] as well as the stories and characters of the [Zoroastrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism) tradition, from the texts of the [Avesta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avesta), the [Denkard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denkard), the [Vendidad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vendidad) and the [Bundahishn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundahishn). Modern scholars study the myths to shed light on the religious and political institutions of not only Iran but of the [Greater Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Iran), which includes regions of West Asia, Central Asia, South Asia, and Transcaucasia where the culture of Iran has had significant influence.

Storytelling has a significant presence in Iranian folklore and culture.[^cite_note-82] In classical Iran, minstrels performed for their audiences at royal courts and in public theatres.[^cite_note-83] A minstrel was referred to by the [Parthians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthian_Empire) as [gōsān](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gusans), and by the [Sasanians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_Empire) as huniyāgar.[^cite_note-84] Since the Safavid Empire, storytellers and poetry readers appeared at coffeehouses.[^cite_note-auto6-85][^cite_note-86] After the Iranian Revolution, it took until 1985 to found the [Ministry of Cultural Heritage, Tourism and Handicrafts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Cultural_Heritage,_Tourism_and_Handicrafts),[^cite_note-87] a heavily centralised organisation supervising all kinds of cultural activities. It held the first scientific meeting on anthropology and folklore in 1990.[^cite_note-88]

## Wedding ceremonies

See also: [Persian wedding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_wedding)

text

Iranian wedding ceremony

There are two stages in a typical wedding ritual in Iran. Sometimes, both phases take place in one day. The first stage is known as "Aghd", which is basically the legal component of marriage in Iran. In this process, the bride and groom, as well as their respective guardians, sign a marriage contract. This phase usually takes place in the bride's home. After this legal process is over, the second phase, "Jashn-e Aroosi" lasts 3 to 7 days and includes feasts and celebrations. The ceremony takes place in a decorated room with flowers and a beautifully decorated spread on the floor.&#x20;

This spread is typically passed down from mother to daughter and is composed of very nice fabric such as "[Termeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Termeh)" (cashmere), "Atlas" (gold embroidered [satin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satin)), or "[Abrisham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrisham)" (silk). Items are placed on the spread include a mirror (of fate), two candelabras (representing the bride and groom and their bright future), a tray of seven multi-colored herbs and spices (including poppy seeds, wild rice, angelica, salt, nigella seeds, black tea, and frankincense). These herbs and spices play specific roles ranging from breaking spells and witchcraft, to blinding the evil eye, to burning evil spirits. In addition to these herbs/spices, a special baked and decorated flatbread, a basket of decorated eggs, decorated almonds, walnuts and hazelnuts (in their shell to represent fertility), a basket of pomegranates/apples (for a joyous future as these fruits are considered divine), a cup of rose water (from special Persian roses)—which helps perfume the air, a bowl made out of sugar (apparently to sweeten life for the newlywed couple), and a brazier holding burning coals and sprinkled with wild rue (as a way to keep the evil eye away and to purify the wedding ritual) are placed on the spread as well. Additional items include a bowl of gold coins (to represent wealth and prosperity), a scarf/shawl made of silk/fine fabric (to be held over the bride and groom's head at certain points in the ceremony), two sugar cones (which are ground above the bride and groom's head, thus symbolizing sweetness/happiness), a cup of honey (to sweeten life), a needle and seven strands of colored thread (the shawl that is held above the bride and groom's head is sewn together with the string throughout the ceremony), and a copy of the couple's [Holy Book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Book) (other religions require different texts); but all of these books symbolize God's blessing for the couple.[^cite_note-Wedding-117]

An early age in marriage—especially for brides—is a long documented feature of marriage in Iran. While the people of Iran have been trying to legally change this practice by implementing a higher minimum in marriage, there have been countless blocks to such attempts. Although the average age of women being married has increased by about five years in the past couple decades, young girls being married is still common feature of marriage in Iran—even though there is an article in the Iranian Civil Code that forbid the marriage of women younger than 15 years of age and males younger than 18 years of age.[^cite_note-Weddings_2-118]

## Persian rugs

Antique Persian Mashad rug

In Iran, [Persian rugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_carpet) have always been a vital part of the Persian culture. Iranians were some of the first people in history to weave carpets. First deriving from the notion of basic need, the Persian rug started out as a simple/pure weave of [fabric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textile) that helped nomadic people living in ancient Iran stay warm from the cold, damp ground. As time progressed, the complexity and beauty of rugs increased to a point where rugs are now bought as decorative pieces.[^cite_note-Rugs_1-119] Because of the long history of fine silk and wool rug weaving in Iran, Persian rugs are world-renowned as some of the most beautiful, intricately designed rugs available. Around various places in Iran, rugs seem to be some of the most prized possessions of the local people. Iran currently produces more rugs and carpets than all other countries in the world put together.[^cite_note-Rugs_2-120]

### Fashion and clothing

Main article: [Fashion in Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fashion_in_Iran)

The date of the emergence of weaving in Iran is not known, but it is likely to coincide with the emergence of civilisation. [Ferdowsi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdowsi) and many historians have considered [Keyumars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keyumars) to be first to use animals' skin and hair as clothing, while others propose [Hushang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hushang).[^cite_note-HU-121] Ferdowsi considers [Tahmuras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tahmuras) to be a kind of textile initiator in Iran. The clothing of ancient Iran took an advanced form, and the fabric and colour of clothing became very important. Depending on the social status, eminence, climate of the region and the season, [Persian clothing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_clothing) during the Achaemenian period took various forms. This clothing, in addition to being functional, had an aesthetic role.[^cite_note-HU-121]

### Cinema, animation, and theatre

Main articles: [Cinema of Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinema_of_Iran), [Iranian animation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_animation), and [Persian theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_theatre)



Reproduction of a 3rd millennium BC goblet from [Shahr-e Sukhteh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shahr-e_Sukhteh), possibly the world's oldest animation, at the [National Museum of Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Iran)

[^cite_note-122]

A 3rd-millennium BC earthen goblet discovered at the [Burnt City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shahr-e_Sukhteh) in southeast Iran depicts what could be the world's oldest example of animation.[^cite_note-123] The earliest attested Iranian examples of visual representations, however, are traced back to the bas-reliefs of Persepolis, the ritual centre of the Achaemenid Empire.[^cite_note-124]

The first Iranian filmmaker was probably [Mirza Ebrahim (Akkas Bashi)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirza_Ebrahim_Khan_Akkas_Bashi), the court photographer of [Mozaffar-ed-Din](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mozaffar_ad-Din_Shah_Qajar) of the Qajar Empire. Mirza Ebrahim obtained a camera and filmed the Qajar ruler's visit to Europe. In 1904, [Mirza Ebrahim (Sahhaf Bashi)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirza_Ebrahim_Khan_Sahhafbashi) opened the first public cinema in Tehran.[^cite_note-massoudmehrabi1-125] The first Iranian feature film, *[Abi and Rabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abi_and_Rabi)*, was a silent comedy directed by [Ovanes Ohanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ovanes_Ohanian) in 1930. The first sound one, *[Lor Girl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lor_Girl)*, was produced by [Ardeshir Irani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ardeshir_Irani) and [Abd-ol-Hosein Sepanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdolhossein_Sepanta) in 1932. Iran's animation industry began by the 1950s and was followed by the establishment of the influential [Institute for the Intellectual Development of Children and Young Adults](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_the_Intellectual_Development_of_Children_and_Young_Adults) in 1965.[^cite_note-126][^cite_note-127]

With the screening of the films *[Qeysar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qeysar_\(film\))* and *[The Cow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cow_\(1969_film\))*, directed by [Masoud Kimiai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masoud_Kimiai) and [Dariush Mehrjui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dariush_Mehrjui) respectively in 1969, alternative films set out to establish their status in the film industry and [Bahram Beyzai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahram_Beyzai)'s *[Downpour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downpour_\(film\))* and [Nasser Taghvai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasser_Taghvai)'s *Tranquility in the Presence of Others* followed. Attempts to organise a film festival, which had begun in 1954 within the Golrizan Festival, resulted in the festival of Sepas in 1969. It also resulted in the formation of Tehran's World Film Festival in 1973.[^cite_note-Esfandiary2012-128]



[Asghar Farhadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asghar_Farhadi), two-time [Academy Award](324.md) winner and a prominent filmmaker in the 21st century

[^cite_note-129]

Following the Cultural Revolution, a new age emerged in Iranian cinema, starting with *Long Live!* by [Khosrow Sinai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khosrow_Sinai) and followed by other directors, such as [Abbas Kiarostami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbas_Kiarostami) and [Jafar Panahi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jafar_Panahi). Kiarostami, an acclaimed director, planted Iran firmly on the map of world cinema when he won the [Palme d'Or](612946.md) for *[Taste of Cherry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taste_of_Cherry)* in 1997.[^cite_note-Dabashi2007-130] The presence of Iranian films in prestigious international festivals, such as [Cannes](101460.md), [Venice](32497.md) and [Berlin](18948039.md), attracted attention to Iranian films.[^cite_note-DecherneyAtwood2014-131] In 2006, 6 films represented Iranian cinema at Berlin; critics considered this a remarkable event in Iranian cinema.[^cite_note-132][^cite_note-133] [Asghar Farhadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asghar_Farhadi), an Iranian director, has received a [Golden Globe Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Globe_Award) and two [Academy Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Award), representing Iran for Best Foreign Language Film in 2012 and 2017, with *[A Separation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Separation)* and *[The Salesman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Salesman_\(2016_film\))*.[^cite_note-134][^cite_note-135][^cite_note-136] In 2020, [Ashkan Rahgozar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashkan_Rahgozar)'s "[The Last Fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Last_Fiction)" became the first representative of Iranian animated cinema in the competition section, in [Best Animated Feature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Animated_Feature) and [Best Picture](61702.md) categories at the [Academy Awards](324.md).[^cite_note-137][^cite_note-138][^cite_note-139][^cite_note-140]

The oldest Iranian initiation of theatre can be traced to ancient epic ceremonial theatres such as *Sug-e Siāvuڑ* ("mourning of [Siāvaڑ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siy%C3%A2vash)"), as well as dances and theatre narrations of Iranian mythological tales reported by [Herodotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotus) and [Xenophon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenophon). Iran's traditional theatrical genres include Baqqāl-bāzi ("grocer play", a form of slapstick comedy), Ruhowzi (or *Taxt-howzi*, comedy performed over a courtyard pool covered with boards), Siāh-bāzi (the central comedian appears in blackface), Sāye-bāzi ([shadow play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadow_play)), Xeyme-ڑab-bāzi ([marionette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marionette)), and Arusak-bāzi ([puppetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puppetry)), and [Ta'zie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ta'zieh) (religious tragedy plays).[^cite_note-141]

The [Roudaki Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roudaki_Hall) is home to the Tehran Symphony Orchestra, the Tehran Opera Orchestra, and the [Iranian National Ballet Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_National_Ballet_Company), and was officially renamed *[Vahdat Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vahdat_Hall)* after the revolution.

## Sports

The game of [polo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polo) originated with Iranian tribes in ancient times and was regularly seen throughout the country until the revolution of 1979 when it became associated with the monarchy. It continues to be played, but only in rural areas and discreetly. Recently, as of 2005, it has been gaining more attention. In March 2006, there was a highly publicized tournament and all significant matches are televised.

## Women in Persian culture

Main article: [Iranian women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_women)

Since the 1979 Revolution, Iranian women have lost nearly all the rights they had gained in 1962 under the rule of [Mohammad Reza Pahlavi](20065598.md). Many restrictive and legal measures have been imposed since 1979, including mandatory Hijab. By law, women's testimony in court is worth half of that of men under the rule of the Islamic Regime, the [age of consent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_consent) for women is 9, and [marital rape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marital_rape) is legal. Women cannot leave the country without the formal permission of their guardians (father or husband). They cannot run for presidency or become a judge. This is a striking change from the full and equal rights that women enjoyed during the Pahlavi era. The Iranian women who had gained confidence and higher education during the Pahlavi era participated in demonstrations against the Shah to topple the monarchy. The culture of education for women was very strongly established by the time of revolution so that even after the revolution, large numbers of women entered civil service and higher education.[^cite_note-142] Even though the Islamic Republic pushed back on women's rights, Iranian women have been at the forefront of progress, education and battle for freedom. In 1996, 14 women were elected to the [Islamic Consultative Assembly](383276.md). In 2003, Iran's first female judge during the Pahlavi era, [Shirin Ebadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shirin_Ebadi), won the Nobel Peace Prize for her efforts in promoting human rights.

According to a UNESCO world survey, at the primary level of enrolment, Iran has the highest female to male ratio among all other sovereign nations, with a female to male ratio of 1.22 : 1.00.[^cite_note-143] By 1999, Iran had 140 female publishers, enough to hold an exhibition of books and magazines published by women.[^cite_note-144] As of 2005, 65% of Iran's university students and 43% of its salaried workers were women.[^cite_note-145] and as of early 2007 nearly 70% of Iran's science and engineering students are women.[^cite_note-146] This has led to many female school and university graduates being under-utilized. This has started to have an effect on Iranian society and was a contributing factor in protests by Iranian youth in over the past decades.

In recent decades, Iranian women have had a significant presence in Iran's [scientific movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_in_Iran), [art movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_modern_and_contemporary_art), [literary new wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_literature) and contemporary [Iranian cinema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_cinema). Women account for 60% of all students in the natural sciences, including one in five PhD students.[^cite_note-147]

## Contributions to humanity in ancient history



Figurine holding a stringed instrument (early [tanbur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanbur) or lute). First half of second millennium BC. Susa. Kept at the National Museum of Iran.

From the [brick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brick) to the [windmill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windmill), Persians were creative with art and offered the world significant contributions.[^cite_note-148][^cite_note-149] What follows is a few examples of the cultural contributions of [Greater Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Iran).

- (10,000 BC) – Earliest known domestication of the [goat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goat).

  &#x20;Some of the oldest bricks found to date are Persian, from c. 6000 BC.

- (5000 BC) – Invention of [wine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wine). Discovery made by [University of Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania) excavations at [Hajji Firuz Tepe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hajji_Firuz_Tepe) in northwestern Iran.

  [^cite_note-155]

- (5000 BC) – Invention of the [Tar (lute)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tar_\(lute\)), which led to the development of the [guitar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guitar).

  [^cite_note-156]

The [Sialk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sialk) ziggurat, according to the [Cultural Heritage Organization of Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_Heritage_Organization_of_Iran), predates that of [Ur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ziggurat_of_Ur) or any other of Mesopotamia's 34 ziggurats.

The word of \[Bonchaq, or Bonchagh] in modern Persian language is new version of old Avestan and Pahlavi language "check". In Persian, it means a document which resembles money value for gold, silver and property. By law, people were able to buy and sell these documents or exchange them.

- (500 BC) – World's oldest [staple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staple_\(fastener\)).

- (500 BC) – The first [taxation system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax) (under the [Achaemenid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire)).

- (500 BC) – "[Royal Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Road)" – the first courier post.

  [^cite_note-167]

- (980 AD – 1037 AD) – [Avicenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avicenna), a physician, writes *[The Canon of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Canon_of_Medicine)*, one of the foundational manuals in the history of modern medicine.

- (1048 AD – 1131 AD) – [Khayyam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khayyam), one of the greatest [polymaths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymath) of all time, presents a theory of [heliocentricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliocentricity) to his peers. His contributions to laying the foundations of [algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra) are also noteworthy.
