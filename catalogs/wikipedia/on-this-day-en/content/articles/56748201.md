[Presidential elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_presidential_elections) were held in Russia from 15 to 17 March 2024.[^cite_note-ElectionDay-1] It was the eighth presidential election in the country. The incumbent president [Vladimir Putin](32817.md) won with 88% of the vote, the highest percentage in a presidential election in post-[Soviet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) Russia, gaining a fifth term in what was widely viewed as a foregone conclusion. He was [inaugurated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth_inauguration_of_Vladimir_Putin) on 7 May 2024.

In November 2023, [Boris Nadezhdin](71749458.md), a former member of the [State Duma](1010187.md), became the first person backed by a registered political party to announce his candidacy, running on an [anti-war](70159281.md) platform. He was followed by incumbent and independent candidate Vladimir Putin in December 2023, who was eligible to seek re-election as a result of the [2020 constitutional amendments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Russian_constitutional_amendments). Later the same month, [Leonid Slutsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonid_Slutsky_\(politician\)) of the [Liberal Democratic Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Democratic_Party_of_Russia), [Nikolay Kharitonov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolay_Kharitonov) of the [Communist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_Party_of_the_Russian_Federation) and [Vladislav Davankov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladislav_Davankov) of [New People](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_People_\(political_party\)) announced their candidacies.

Other candidates also declared their candidacy but were barred for various reasons by the [Central Election Commission](9345768.md) (CEC). As was the case in the [2018 presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Russian_presidential_election), the most prominent [opposition](38212364.md) leader, [Alexei Navalny](29174356.md), was barred from running due to a prior criminal conviction seen as politically motivated. Navalny [died in prison in February 2024](76110861.md), weeks before the election, under suspicious circumstances. Nadezhdin, despite passing the initial stages of the process, on 8 February 2024, was also barred from running. The decision was announced at a special CEC session, citing alleged irregularities in the signatures of voters supporting his candidacy. Nadezhdin's status as the only explicitly [anti-war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protests_against_the_Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine) candidate was widely regarded as the real reason for his disqualification, although Davankov promised "peace and negotiations on our own terms". As a result, Putin faced no credible opposition. Anti-Putin activists [called on voters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noon_Against_Putin) to spoil their ballot. The elections saw 1.4 million invalid or blank ballots cast, around 1.6% of all votes cast, a 45 percent increase compared to the 2018 elections.

Most international observers did not expect the election to be either [free or fair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_and_fair_election), with Putin having increased political repressions after launching his [full-scale war with Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine) in 2022. The elections were also held in the [Russian-occupied territories of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian-occupied_territories_of_Ukraine), as well as in [Moldova](19260.md)'s Russian-occupied region of [Transnistria](376581.md). In the election, there were reports of [irregularities](382165.md), including [ballot stuffing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballot_stuffing) and coercion, with statistical analysis suggesting unprecedented levels of fraud.

## Eligibility

Main article: [2020 amendments to the Constitution of Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_amendments_to_the_Constitution_of_Russia)

According to clause 3 of article 81 of the [Constitution of Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Russia), prior to the 2020 constitutional revision, the same person could not hold the position of [President of the Russian Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_Russian_Federation) for more than two consecutive terms, which allowed Vladimir Putin to become president in 2012 for a third term not consecutive with his prior terms.[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7] Until the reform, the prospect of these term limits forcing Putin from office was widely discussed as the [2024 problem](#2024_problem). The constitutional reform established a hard limit of two terms overall. However, terms served before the constitutional revision do not count, which gives Putin eligibility for two more presidential terms until 2036.[^cite_note-8]

According to the new version of the Constitution, presidential candidates must:[^cite_note-9]

- Be at least 35 years old (the requirement has not changed);

- Be a resident in Russia for at least 25 years (previously 10 years);

- Not have foreign citizenship or residence permit in a foreign country, neither at the time of the election nor at any time before (new requirement).

### Rejected candidates

Individuals in this section have had their document submissions accepted by the CEC to register their participation, and later gathered the necessary signatures from voters. The deadline to submit documents was 27 December 2023 for independents and 1 January 2024 for party-based nominations, with the commission already announcing the rejection of some candidates based on alleged issues with their paperwork.[^cite_note-Ведомости-16]

Towards the deadline to submit documents, the CEC stated that 33 potential candidates were intending to be registered as candidates (24 independents and 9 party-based nominations). The commission accepted the documents of 15 candidates.[^cite_note-18]

The next step was to collect signatures by 31 January 2024. Independents had to gather 300,000 signatures from the public in at least 40 of Russia's [federal subjects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Subjects_of_Russia) to support their participation and thereby be included on the ballot, while potential candidates nominated by political parties that are not represented in the [State Duma](1010187.md) or in at least a third of the country's [regional parliaments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_parliaments_of_Russia) had to gather 100,000 signatures.[^cite_note-19]

Vladimir Putin was the first to achieve this, having gathered more than half a million signatures by 30 December; by 17 January he had gathered 2.5 million signatures.[^cite_note-tass.ru-20][^cite_note-21] He was followed by Davankov, Kharitonov, Slutsky, Nadezhdin and Malinkovich (in no particular order). Others either failed to achieve this or withdrew from the process.

The CEC accepted the signatures of Putin, while rejecting Nadezhdin and Malinkovich on the basis of what it described to be irregularities. Davankov, Kharitonov and Slutsky were not required to collect signatures as they were nominated by political parties represented in the State Duma. This confirmed the final number of candidates at four.

### Other parties

At [Yabloko](429579.md)'s congress, which took place on 9 December 2023, somewhat unconventionally, the party decided that [Grigory Yavlinsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grigory_Yavlinsky) would run for president as its nominee if he obtains 10 million signatures from potential voters,[^cite_note-43] which is higher than the total number of votes Yavlinsky obtained [during his most successful run for president](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_Russian_presidential_election) (5.55 million).[^cite_note-44] Yabloko later stated that it would not be nominating any candidate.[^cite_note-vedomosti.ru-45] Furthermore, Yavlinsky only managed to gather around a million signatures.[^cite_note-46]

The [Left Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_Front_\(Russia\)) stated that it would run a primary election between 22 candidates, but later announced it would not be holding the primary due to threats received from the police.[^cite_note-47] Instead, the party called on their "comrades in the Communist Party" to vote for one of the following to be nominated at the party congress: [Pavel Grudinin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pavel_Grudinin), [Nikolai Bondarenko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Bondarenko), [Valentin Konovalov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valentin_Konovalov), [Andrey Klychkov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrey_Klychkov), [Sergey Levchenko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergey_Levchenko), [Nina Ostanina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nina_Ostanina), [Igor Girkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igor_Girkin).

## Preparation of public opinion

See also: [Propaganda in Russia](45060755.md) and [Media freedom in Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_freedom_in_Russia)

In June 2023, a few posters advocating for [Yevgeny Prigozhin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yevgeny_Prigozhin), leader of the [Wagner Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wagner_Group) private military company, were noticed[^cite_note-times-48] in [Krasnodar](474128.md), with a [QR code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QR_code) to a website hinting at the 2024 election.[^cite_note-yuga-49] Prigozhin himself denied any relation to such posters and "any political activity in the internet",[^cite_note-yuga-49] but fuelled speculation that he was about to go into politics after holding press conferences across Russia[^cite_note-times-48] and leading the [Wagner Group rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wagner_Group_rebellion). He [died in a plane crash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_Wagner_Group_plane_crash) later that year.&#x20;

According to an investigation published in February 2024 by a coalition of journals including [VSquare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VSquare?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Delfi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delfi_\(web_portal\)), [Expressen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expressen) and [Paper Trail Media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper_Trail_Media), Putin ordered Decree Number 2016, titled "On deputy heads responsible for social and political work of federal government agencies", on 17 February 2023. The decree stated its aim of coordination between the [Ministry of Education and Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Education_and_Science_\(Russia\)) and other state agencies to "increase the number of voters and the support of the main candidates" in the 2024 presidential election and other elections. Documents from a governmental "non-profit organisation", ANO Integration, highlighted the emphasis on increasing the number of voters and the support of the main candidates, with turnout being used to indicate the scale of support and opposition to Putin.[^cite_note-VSquare_KremlinLeaks-50][^cite_note-meduza-election-51][^cite_note-Ebel-52]

The ANO Integration documents presented a plan to create lists of all employees and sub-lists of opinion leaders in institutions within the ministry's responsibility, and to monitor political attitudes and voting preferences and "increas\[e] \[the employee's] level of socio-political literacy". The documents planned for the preparation of secret instructions for social events in which selected opinion leaders and "experts" would meet with students and teachers in preparation for the election. [Martin Kragh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Kragh?action=edit\&redlink=1) of the [Center for East European Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_for_East_European_Studies?action=edit\&redlink=1) in Stockholm described the documents by stating, "All these documents show how little the Kremlin believes that people might just spontaneously support the ruling party". [Mark Galeotti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Galeotti), a British historian, lecturer and writer, described the process as "pre-rigging" the election in order to minimise the amount of manipulation needed in the numbers of votes cast for Putin in the election. He stated, "The Kremlin cannot even trust what mayors and governors tell them about the \[political] situation in their region."[^cite_note-VSquare_KremlinLeaks-50][^cite_note-53][^cite_note-54]

The election was seen as a foregone conclusion.[^cite_note-55][^cite_note-56] When asked by a BBC journalist about his electoral campaign, [Nikolay Kharitonov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolay_Kharitonov) refused to answer why he thought he would be a better candidate than Putin, before proceeding to praise the latter for "trying to solve a lot of the problems of the 1990s" and consolidating the country for "victory in all areas".[^cite_note-57] Shortly after filing his candidacy in December 2023, [Leonid Slutsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonid_Slutsky_\(politician\)) said he did not "dream of beating Putin" and predicted that the latter would achieve "a huge victory".[^cite_note-58] [Vladislav Davankov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladislav_Davankov) said he would not criticize his political opponents.[^cite_note-59] He also promised "peace and negotiations on our own terms".[^cite_note-60][^cite_note-61]

## Conduct

### Russian-occupied territories of Ukraine

See also: [Russo-Ukrainian War](42085878.md) and [Russian-occupied territories of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian-occupied_territories_of_Ukraine)

[Early voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_voting) opened on 26 February and lasted until 14 March to allow certain residents in remote areas in 37 federal subjects of Russia as well as in the [regions of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian-occupied_territories_of_Ukraine) that it annexed following its invasion in 2022 to vote.[^cite_note-62][^cite_note-63][^cite_note-EdwardsStageManage-64][^cite_note-65] In the latter areas, a campaign called InformUIK was set up to encourage participation in the election, with its representatives going door-to-door escorted by armed men to compile voter lists and collect ballots from residences. A resident of [Kherson Oblast](1273370.md) described the elections in his area as a "comedy show", noting that households were being visited by "two locals – one holding a list of voters and the other a ballot box – and a military man with a machine gun".[^cite_note-bbcukraine-66]

Russian officials also used home visits by the mobile polling stations to monitor the population and find those participating in resistance activities or refusing to obtain Russian government documents.[^cite_note-67] Reports also emerged of Russian-installed authorities coercing people to vote by withholding social benefits and healthcare treatment, while human rights activists said at least 27 Ukrainians were arrested for refusing to vote in Kherson and [Zaporizhzhia Oblasts](494936.md).[^cite_note-coercion-68] Despite Russian electoral laws prohibiting those without Russian passports from voting, voters in occupied Ukraine were allowed to present any valid identification documents, including a [Ukrainian passport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_passport) or driving license.[^cite_note-69] In [Melitopol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melitopol), Zaporizhzhia Oblast, soldiers armed with machine guns sealed off apartments being visited by mobile polling teams.[^cite_note-70] In one instance, a man who fled his village near [Mariupol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariupol), [Donetsk Oblast](494889.md), to Ukrainian-controlled territory following the Russian invasion alleged that his name appeared in Russian-produced voter lists and was listed as having voted for Putin by election officials.[^cite_note-71]

### Efforts to promote turnout



Vladimir Putin at the [United Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Russia) congress

On the eve of the first round of regular voting on 14 March, Putin called on citizens to vote in order to show their unity behind his leadership, saying in a video message that "We have already shown that we can be together, defending the freedom, sovereignty and security of Russia," and urged them "not to stray from this path".[^cite_note-f24vote-72]

Latvia-based Russian news outlet *[Meduza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meduza_\(website\))* reported that Kremlin officials asked Russia's regional governments to secure 70% voter turnout and more than 80% support for Putin.[^cite_note-meduza-election-51][^cite_note-73]

Civil servants and employees at state-run companies were ordered to vote.[^cite_note-74] In [Omsk](62593.md), officials issued 50,000 free tickets at polling stations to first-time voters aged between 18 and 24 years of age for one Ferris wheel ride at an amusement park. In [Altai Krai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altai_Krai), voters were to be given a chance to win sanctioned goods and appliances such as an [iPhone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPhone) in a raffle, provided that they upload pictures on [VKontakte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VKontakte) showing them at polling stations. In [Strezhevoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strezhevoy), [Tomsk Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomsk_Oblast), the mayor promised free bread rolls and porridge to voters. In [Sverdlovsk Oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sverdlovsk_Oblast), authorities set up an election day trivia quiz about the region's history with and offered 2,000 smartphones, 45 apartments, 20 motorcycles, and 100 [Moskvich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moskvich) cars as prizes, but said that correct answers would not guarantee a win. In [Tatarstan](67135.md), officials set a music festival in [Kazan](202295.md) on 17 March that would be open to visitors upon presentation of a bracelet obtained at polling stations that would also guarantee free and unlimited access to public transportation, along with a chance to win in a raffle with three [Lada Vesta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lada_Vesta) cars at stake.[^cite_note-75]

Reports also emerged of pressure being exerted by authorities on students and young people to vote. Students at a construction college in [Perm Krai](1765550.md) were ordered to vote inside the campus, with the school administration pledging to monitor turnout using video surveillance cameras. At [Tula State Pedagogical University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tula_State_Pedagogical_University?action=edit\&redlink=1), students were required to submit a photo of their ballot to prove that they voted. Its rector had also publicly endorsed Putin. At [Voronezh State Pedagogical University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voronezh_State_Pedagogical_University), students said they were required to inform authorities about who they were voting for.[^cite_note-76]

### Regular voting



Putin voted in the Russian presidential election online.

On the regular election days, polls opened at 08\:00 local time in [Kamchatka Krai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamchatka_Krai) on 15 March and are expected to close at 20\:00 local time in [Kaliningrad Oblast](17311.md) on 17 March.[^cite_note-f24vote-72][^cite_note-77][^cite_note-78] Independent watchdogs were prevented from observing the conduct of the election, as only registered candidates and state-backed advisory bodies were allowed to send observers to polling stations. The independent election monitor [Golos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golos_\(election_monitor\)) described the election as the "most vapid" since the [2000 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2000_Russian_presidential_election), noting that campaigning was "practically unnoticeable" and that authorities were "doing everything" to prevent people noticing that an election was taking place while state media provided less airtime to the election compared to [2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Russian_presidential_election). It also described Putin's campaign as disguised by his activities as president, while his registered opponents were "demonstrably passive".[^cite_note-79]

On 15 March, the Kremlin published images of Putin casting his vote online using a computer in his office.[^cite_note-80] On the morning of the same day, the online voting system went down temporarily, with Golos and other independent electoral observers attributing the outage to the traffic generated by votes coming from workplaces.[^cite_note-81]

## Allegations of fraud

### Statistical analyses

According to a *[Novaya Gazeta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novaya_Gazeta)* investigation using a method proposed by mathematician [Sergey Shpilkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergey_Shpilkin?action=edit\&redlink=1), around 22 million of the non-online (polling booth) votes for Putin were [falsified](382165.md), out of 64.7 million non-online votes for Putin in total. *Novaya Gazeta* described the analysis showing "record levels of fraud even for a Russian presidential election".[^cite_note-NovayaGazeta_at_least_22M-82][^cite_note-novayagazeta-83]

*[Meduza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meduza_\(website\))* carried out statistical analyses on the official results released by the CEC. Based on [scatter plots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scatter_plot) of the vote percentage for Putin compared to turnout, a tail in which [voter turnout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voter_turnout) visually correlates to Putin support, which appeared weakly in earlier Russian presidential elections, was found by *Meduza* to completely dominate in 2024. "[Churov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Churov)'s saw", a statistical effect interpreted as fraudulent in which sharp peaks at round numbers appear in voter turnout and percentage votes for Putin, was found by *Meduza* to have strengthened in the 2024 election, with *Meduza* arguing that the number of polling stations with likely fraud became the majority in 2024, while earlier the fraction of fraudulent polling stations had been a minority. Overall, *Meduza* stated that the 2024 presidential election "was almost certainly the most fraudulent" in "modern Russian history", and that the "sheer magnitude of fraud eclipses that of [2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Russian_presidential_election)".[^cite_note-Meduza_breaks_down_evidence-84]

### Other incidents of fraud

On 16 March, Golos released a video on social media appearing to show staff at a polling station in [Krasnodar](474128.md) doing [ballot stuffing](382165.md#Ballot_stuffing).[^cite_note-podolsk-85] It also said that it had received reports of attempts to inspect filled-out ballots before they were cast, and one instance in which police demanded a ballot box be opened to remove a ballot.[^cite_note-APvictory-86] [Thermochromic ink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermochromic_ink) that disappears when heated was also allegedly used in [Kursk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kursk) and [Rostov-on-Don](317562.md) on 15 March.[^cite_note-87] The usage of such ink was previously reported during the [2021 regional elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Russian_regional_elections) in [Khimki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khimki).[^cite_note-88] Overall, Golos described the 2024 election as an "imitation", adding that it had not previously observed "a presidential campaign that fell so short of constitutional standards".[^cite_note-89]

## Incidents

### Attacks by Ukrainian and other armed groups

Attacks have been launched against Russian electoral institutions in occupied areas of Ukraine. On the first day of early voting on 27 February 2024, two bombs were detonated at the local offices of the United Russia party and near a polling station in [Nova Kakhovka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nova_Kakhovka), Kherson Oblast.[^cite_note-bbcukraine-66] On 6 March, a local official of the Russian Central Election Commission in [Berdiansk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berdiansk), [Zaporizhzhia Oblast](494936.md), was killed by a car bomb, according to Ukrainian officials.[^cite_note-90] When asked about the killing, the Ukrainian-appointed governor of the oblast, [Ivan Fedorov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Fedorov_\(politician\)), attributed the attack to "[our resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_resistance_in_Russian-occupied_Ukraine)", adding that they were linked to Ukrainian secret services and that "it is abnormal when our citizens collaborate with Russians".[^cite_note-bbcukraine-66] On 15 March, an improvised explosive device was [detonated inside a trash can](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skadovsk_polling_center_bombing) in front of a polling station in [Skadovsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skadovsk), Kherson Oblast,[^cite_note-bbcarrests-91] injuring five Russian soldiers.[^cite_note-92] On 16 March, the Russian-installed governor of Kherson Oblast, [Vladimir Saldo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Saldo) claimed that one person was killed and four others were injured in a Ukrainian drone strike in [Kakhovka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kakhovka), which he claimed was an attempt to disrupt voting, while TASS reported that a Ukrainian drone struck a polling station in Zaporizhzhia Oblast.[^cite_note-ap-ballots-93]

During an [incursion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_2024_Western_Russia_incursion) into [Kursk](480916.md) and [Belgorod Oblasts](478857.md) on 12 March, the [Sibir Battalion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sibir_Battalion), an armed Russian opposition group based in Ukraine, published a video condemning the elections, saying that "Ballots and polling stations in this case are fiction."[^cite_note-94] A member of the [Freedom of Russia Legion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_Russia_Legion), which also participated in the attacks, acknowledged that they were "timed with the so-called elections" and referred to it as a "voting method".[^cite_note-95] Putin also described the incursion as an attempt to "disrupt" the election and "interfere with the normal process of expressing the will of citizens".[^cite_note-96] Throughout the election, the border city of [Belgorod](478861.md) was subjected to shelling and rocket attacks by Ukraine, killing two people in what most analysts believed to be an attempt to disrupt the vote and incite discontent against Putin by convincing Russians of his responsibility in bringing the war on Ukraine to Russian soil by launching the invasion in the first place, although the high turnout of 78 percent in [Belgorod Oblast](478857.md) suggested that the strategy had led to increased support for Putin.[^cite_note-97]

On 25 March, the independent news outlet [Mediazona](52026388.md) reported that the [Federal Security Service](385990.md) had arrested three people on suspicion of plotting an arson attack against a Putin campaign office in [Barnaul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnaul), [Altai Krai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altai_Krai), prior to the election. One of the suspects was said to have been in contact with an "unidentified terrorist organization".[^cite_note-98]

### Incidents involving civilians

During regular polling, several election-related incidents were reported across the country, resulting in at least 13 arrests, seven of which were for pouring [liquid substances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zelyonka_attack) on ballot boxes and four for committing acts of arson in polling stations,[^cite_note-99] one of which involved a woman in [Saint Petersburg](24320051.md) who was arrested for throwing a [molotov cocktail](20922.md) at a school hosting two polling stations after having allegedly been promised a financial incentive by a "Ukrainian Telegram channel".[^cite_note-100] A voting booth was also set on fire in [Moscow](19004.md).[^cite_note-apsuspense-101] In [Podolsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Podolsk), [Moscow Oblast](372315.md), a voter was charged with "discrediting the Russian army" and fined 30,000 rubles (\$342) after spoiling her ballot by writing an unspecified message.[^cite_note-podolsk-85] A voter in Saint Petersburg was ordered arrested on similar charges after writing the words "No to War" on her ballot.[^cite_note-102] Some voters uploaded images of them spoiling their ballots by writing messages such as "killer and thief" and "waiting for you in [The Hague](30269.md)", a reference to the [arrest warrant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Criminal_Court_arrest_warrants_for_Russian_figures) issued by the [International Criminal Court](14880.md) against Putin over [war crimes in Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_abductions_in_the_Russo-Ukrainian_War).[^cite_note-apcrowd-103]

On 17 March, a [Moldovan](19260.md) national was arrested after throwing two molotov cocktails at the grounds of the [Russian embassy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embassy_of_Russia,_Chi%C8%99in%C4%83u) in [Chișinău](56635.md), which was being used as a polling station for [Russian nationals in Moldova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russians_in_Moldova). Moldovan police said that the man, who also claimed to be carrying Russian citizenship, "justified his action by some dissatisfaction he has with the actions of the Russian authorities".[^cite_note-104]

In response to the attacks on polling stations, former president and deputy chair of the [Security Council of Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Security_Council_of_Russia) [Dmitry Medvedev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dmitry_Medvedev) called for charges of treason to be filed against those who vandalize polling stations for attempting to derail the vote amid the fighting in Ukraine.[^cite_note-apcrowd-103]

### Cyberattacks

On 16 March, the United Russia party said its website was targeted by a cyberattack.[^cite_note-105]

### Exit polling

Exit polls on 17 March released by the [Russian Public Opinion Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Public_Opinion_Research_Center) showed Vladimir Putin with 87% of the vote, 10% more than in 2018, Nikolai Kharitonov with 4.6%, Vladislav Davankov with 4.2% and Leonid Slutsky with 3%. Invalid ballots accounted for 1.2% of votes cast.[^cite_note-107]

Official results abroad

The CEC said that 388,791 Russians cast their ballots from abroad.[^cite_note-official-108]

In contrast to the official exit polls and results of the election both inside and outside of Russia, unofficial exit polls of the votes cast abroad showed a much poorer performance for Putin. According to the Vote Abroad project,[^cite_note-voteabroad-109] Davankov won a plurality of the votes at most of the voting stations abroad, earning his best result in [Trabzon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trabzon). However, Putin secured pluralities in [Genoa](47332321.md), [Rome](25458.md), [Nicosia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicosia), [Chișinău](56635.md), [Ankara](802.md), [Samarkand](51491.md) and [Bonn](3295.md). He also won 59% in [Athens](1216.md), making it his best performance abroad.[^cite_note-110] Conversely, he got his worst showing in [The Hague](30269.md), with just 2% of the vote.[^cite_note-111] Putin won 3% in [Poland](22936.md), [Serbia](29265.md) and [Montenegro](20760.md), and 8% in [Almaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almaty), Kazakhstan.[^cite_note-112]

According to the Vote Abroad project, Putin was also voted for by 4% of Russians living in Lithuania and the Czech Republic, 5% in [Istanbul](3391396.md), Turkey, 6% in Argentina and the United Kingdom, 7% in Austria, Ireland and Slovakia, 8% in Estonia, Denmark and [Yerevan](34352.md), Armenia, 9% in Portugal, 10% in Thailand, Finland and [Berlin](3354.md), Germany, 11% in [Madrid](41188263.md), Spain and [Paris](22989.md), France, 13% in Norway, 14% in Sweden and Hungary, 15% in Vietnam and the United States, 16% in [Tel Aviv](31453.md), Israel and [Bern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bern), Switzerland, 17% in Japan, 22% in Cyprus, 23% in [Milan](36511.md), Italy, 31% in [Dubai](211583.md), 35% in Chișinău, Moldova and Uzbekistan, 36% in Kyrgyzstan and 38% in [Rome](25458.md), Italy.[^cite_note-voteabroad-109] In total, exit polls organized by exiled Russian activists across 44 countries showed Davankov gaining more votes than Putin in all but five countries.[^cite_note-113]

The election was also held in Moldova's [Transnistria](376581.md), an internationally unrecognized state. 46,179 people with Russian citizenship voted in the election, the lowest turnout in a Russian presidential election in the last 18 years. 97% voted for Putin, with the other three candidates not having even obtained 1,000 votes combined.[^cite_note-114] According to [TRT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_Radio_and_Television_Corporation) Russian, the low turnout indicated changes in the political activity of Transnistrians with Russian citizenship, with over 73,000 having participated in the last election six years ago. Furthermore, it stated Moldovan analysts believed the low turnout indicated a trend that Transnistria was moving away from Russia and that Moldovan President [Maia Sandu](40796149.md)'s pro-European policy was influencing the region.[^cite_note-115]

## Election observers

The domestic watchdog [Golos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golos_\(election_monitor\)), having been previously labelled a "[foreign agent](39572380.md)" in 2013 after documenting fraud in the [2011 parliamentary vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Russian_legislative_election) and the [2012 presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Russian_presidential_election), was not allowed to send [election observers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_monitoring).[^cite_note-116][^cite_note-117]

On 29 January 2024, the [OSCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OSCE)'s [Office for Democratic Institutions and Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_for_Democratic_Institutions_and_Human_Rights) (ODIHR) announced that the organisation would not participate in international monitoring of election, citing the lack of an invitation from Russia.[^cite_note-118]

On 17 March 2024, the Chair of the [Central Election Commission of the Russian Federation](9345768.md) (CEC), [Ella Pamfilova](11005479.md), announced that 1,115 international observers and experts from 129 countries were monitoring the electoral process.[^cite_note-119]

2024 Russian presidential election candidates: [Vladislav Davankov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladislav_Davankov), [Leonid Slutsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonid_Slutsky_\(politician\)), [Vladimir Putin](32817.md), [Nikolai Kharitonov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Kharitonov) (left to right) on [Red Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Square) after the end of the elections on 18 March 2024

On 6 August 2023, Kremlin spokesman [Dmitry Peskov](9214683.md) told *The New York Times* that "our presidential election is not really democracy, it is costly bureaucracy. Mr. Putin will be re-elected next year with more than 90 percent of the vote". Later he clarified that this was his personal opinion.[^cite_note-Peskov-122] In an interview with the [RBK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RBK_Group) news agency, Peskov said that Russia "theoretically" does not need to hold presidential elections because "it's obvious that Putin will be reelected."[^cite_note-123]

On 6 November 2023, journalist [Yekaterina Duntsova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yekaterina_Duntsova) announced her intention to run for the presidency in the 2024 election; she said she would run as an independent candidate on an [anti-war](70159281.md) platform.[^cite_note-124] The next month, her nomination documents were rejected by the [Central Election Commission](9345768.md).[^cite_note-125] On 31 May 2024, Dunstsova was declared a "[foreign agent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_foreign_agents_in_Russia)" by Russian authorities.[^cite_note-126]

In November 2023, nationalist ex-militia commander [Igor Girkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igor_Girkin) announced his intention to run as a candidate in the 2024 elections, describing elections in Russia as a "sham" in which "the only winner \[referring to Putin] is known in advance".[^cite_note-127]

In January 2024, citing unidentified sources in the Kremlin, the independent news outlet *Vyorstka* reported that the CEC, at the behest of the Kremlin, will likely reject [Boris Nadezhdin](71749458.md)'s registration due to his [criticism of Putin](38212364.md) and anti-war stances.[^cite_note-128] In late January 2024, a source in the [Putin administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidential_Administration_of_Russia) told the Latvia-based news outlet *[Meduza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meduza_\(website\))*: "There's a portion of the electorate that wants the war to end. If \[Putin's opponent in the elections] decides to cater to this demand, they may get a decent percentage. And \[the Putin administration] doesn't need that."[^cite_note-meduza-kremlin-129][^cite_note-130] [Russian state media](15861222.md) intensified a [smear campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smear_campaign) against Nadezhdin in the weeks leading up to the election. On 30 January 2024, Kremlin propagandist and television presenter [Vladimir Solovyov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Solovyov_\(TV_presenter\)) warned Nadezhdin: "I feel bad for Boris. The fool didn't realize that he's not being set up to run for president but for a criminal case on charges of betraying the Motherland."[^cite_note-meduza-kremlin-129]

Following the CEC's decision to ban him from running, Nadezhdin wrote in his Telegram channel: "I do not agree with the decision of the CEC... Participating in the presidential election in 2024 is the most important political decision in my life. I am not backing down from my intentions."[^cite_note-131]

Prior to the release of official results, former president and deputy head of the [Security Council of Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Security_Council_of_Russia) [Dmitry Medvedev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dmitry_Medvedev) congratulated Putin on his "splendid victory".[^cite_note-mtvow-132] After his victory was confirmed, Putin held a news conference on 18 March calling his win a vindication of his policy of defying the West and his decision to invade Ukraine.[^cite_note-ajvictory-133] He also described the result as an indication of "trust" and "hope" in him.[^cite_note-APvictory-86] Exiled Russian dissident [Leonid Volkov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonid_Volkov_\(politician\)) said that Putin's percentage of victory had "not the slightest relation to reality".[^cite_note-134]

### International

Most international observers did not expect the election to be either [free or fair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_and_fair_election),[^cite_note-135] with Putin having increased political repressions after launching his [full-scale war with Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine) in 2022.[^cite_note-136][^cite_note-tmt-87.28-137] In an interview with [TV3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TV3_\(Latvian_TV_channel\)) on 4 March 2024, Latvian justice minister [Inese Lībiņa-Egnere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inese_L%C4%ABbi%C5%86a-Egnere) implied that [Russians in the country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russians_in_Latvia) who would participate in the election to be held in the Russian embassy in [Riga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riga) could face criminal liability for justifying the invasion of Ukraine under Latvian law.[^cite_note-138] On 14 March, Lībiņa-Egnere appeared to have reversed her stance, stating that participation in the elections "does not equate to supporting Putin" and is "not punishable in any way"; she added that Latvia "does not want to provoke an international row and intends to act like a democratic state".[^cite_note-139] On 11 March, [Armands Ruks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armands_Ruks?action=edit\&redlink=1), the head of the [Latvian State Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_Police_\(Latvia\)) announced that voters at the embassy would be subjected to police screenings before entering.[^cite_note-140]

The [Russian ambassador to Moldova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_ambassadors_of_Russia_to_Moldova), [Oleg Vasnetsov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oleg_Vasnetsov?action=edit\&redlink=1), was summoned to the [Ministry of Foreign Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Foreign_Affairs_\(Moldova\)) of Moldova on 12 March following Russia's decision to open six polling stations in Transnistria for the election, which Moldovan foreign minister [Mihai Popșoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mihai_Pop%C8%99oi) described as "unacceptable". The Moldovan government had previously agreed to open only one polling station in the Russian embassy in Chișinău as per international law as it claimed.[^cite_note-141] Russia's opening of polling stations in Transnistria was also condemned by [Romania](25445.md), the United States[^cite_note-142] and France.[^cite_note-143] On 19 March, Popșoi announced the expulsion of a Russian diplomat in response to the holding of elections in Transnistria.[^cite_note-144]

The Ukrainian foreign ministry called on international media and public figures "to refrain from referring to this farce as 'elections' in the language of democratic states."[^cite_note-\:0-145] The United States condemned voting in Russian-occupied areas of Ukraine, with State Department Spokesperson [Matthew Miller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Miller_\(spokesperson\)) saying that the US would "never recognize the legitimacy or outcome of these sham elections held in sovereign Ukraine".[^cite_note-\:0-145] On 15 March, Ukraine's ambassador to the [United Nations](31769.md), [Sergiy Kyslytsya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergiy_Kyslytsya), released a joint statement on behalf of Ukraine, the [European Union](9317.md) and 56 other countries including the US condemning the holding of the elections in occupied parts of the country.[^cite_note-146] The Ukrainian government said it would not press charges against its residents of occupied areas who participate in the election, saying that they were being forced to vote.[^cite_note-147] The [UN Security Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UN_Security_Council) condemned the holding of the elections in occupied territories of Ukraine with deputy secretary-general [Rosemary DiCarlo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosemary_DiCarlo) saying that "holding elections in another UN member state's territory without its consent is in manifest disregard for the principles of sovereignty and territorial integrity" and were "invalid" under international law.[^cite_note-148]

On the first day of regular voting on 15 March, [European Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Council) president [Charles Michel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Michel) sarcastically congratulated Putin for winning a "landslide victory" in the elections starting that day, adding that there was "No opposition. No freedom. No choice."[^cite_note-apsuspense-101] On 18 March, the European Union condemned "the illegal holding of so-called elections' in the territories of Ukraine" and said it would never recognize them. The EU's foreign policy chief [Josep Borrell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josep_Borrell) added that the election was held in the context of a shrinking political space.[^cite_note-149]

Following the release of the results, Germany described the vote as a "pseudo-election" under an authoritarian ruler reliant on censorship, repression and violence.[^cite_note-bbclandslide-150] The UK's Foreign Secretary, [David Cameron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Cameron) condemned "the illegal holding of elections on Ukrainian territory",[^cite_note-bbclandslide-150] adding that the vote was "not what free and fair elections look like". A spokesperson for the [United States National Security Council](44026.md) described the election as "obviously not free nor fair given how Mr. Putin has imprisoned political opponents and prevented others from running against him",[^cite_note-ajvictory-133] but said that the US would recognize Putin as president.[^cite_note-151] In his evening address on 17 March, Ukrainian President [Volodymyr Zelenskyy](59210523.md) described the election as an "imitation" with "no legitimacy", adding that Putin was "addicted to power and does everything he can to rule forever" and that "There is no evil he will not commit to prolong his personal power."[^cite_note-152] Italian foreign minister [Antonio Tajani](1767298.md) described the election as "neither free nor fair", while Czech foreign minister [Jan Lipavský](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Lipavsk%C3%BD) called the election a "farce and parody".[^cite_note-153] Lithuanian foreign minister [Gabrielius Landsbergis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabrielius_Landsbergis) called the voting a "procedure that is supposed to resemble elections", adding that "Some might call it reappointment, lacking any legitimacy."[^cite_note-aplithuania-154] The French foreign ministry said the "conditions for a free, pluralist and democratic election were not met", but praised "the courage of the many Russian citizens who have peacefully demonstrated their opposition to this attack on their fundamental political rights."[^cite_note-155] [South Korea](27019.md) refrained from commenting on the election and said that "it would make considerations of 'appropriateness' in regard to a possible congratulatory cable to Putin."[^cite_note-156]

Leaders of countries with neutral or friendly relations with Russia sent congratulations to Putin on his victory,[^cite_note-202] along with President of [Republika Srpska](347422.md) [Milorad Dodik](4218129.md), members of the Moldovan opposition, and [Hamas](13913.md) political chairman [Ismail Haniyeh](41207463.md).[^cite_note-203][^cite_note-204] Presidents [Aslan Bzhania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aslan_Bzhania) and [Alan Gagloev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Gagloev), of the pro-Russian breakaway states of [Abkhazia](18933375.md) and [South Ossetia](318381.md) respectively, also congratulated Putin.[^cite_note-205][^cite_note-206]

### Protests



[Russian immigrants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_emigration_during_the_Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine) outside the Russian embassy in Yerevan, Armenia as part of the "[Noon Against Putin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noon_Against_Putin)" event, 17 March 2024

On 1 February 2024, jailed Kremlin critic [Alexei Navalny](29174356.md) and his allies called on supporters to protest Putin and the invasion of Ukraine on the last day of the election on 17 March all going to vote against Putin at the same time.[^cite_note-207]As was the case in the [2018 presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Russian_presidential_election), the most prominent [opposition](38212364.md) leader,[^cite_note-208][^cite_note-209] Navalny, was barred from running due to a prior criminal conviction seen as politically motivated.[^cite_note-New_York_Times2-210][^cite_note-Gomozova2-211][^cite_note-212] Navalny [died in prison in February 2024](76110861.md), weeks before the election, under suspicious circumstances.[^cite_note-213][^cite_note-ABC_News2-214] Following Navalny's death, there were calls from [Andrius Kubilius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrius_Kubilius), [Mikhail Khodorkovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Khodorkovsky), and Navalny's widow, [Yulia Navalnaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yulia_Navalnaya) for the EU to recognize the Russian elections as illegitimate.[^cite_note-215][^cite_note-216][^cite_note-217] Navalnaya called for Russians critical of Putin to join the "[Noon Against Putin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noon_Against_Putin)" initiative to form long queues at polling stations at noon on 17 March before proceeding to vote for anyone other than Putin, spoil their ballots or cast Navalny's name.[^cite_note-218] On the day of the action, Navalnaya joined queues outside the [Russian embassy in Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embassy_of_Russia,_Berlin)[^cite_note-219] for about six hours before casting her vote for her husband and praising protesters for giving her "hope that everything is not in vain".[^cite_note-bbclandslide-150] In Russia, queues formed at polling stations in Moscow and Saint Petersburg at noon.[^cite_note-220] Queues were also observed outside Russian diplomatic missions worldwide, including in [Belgrade](55904.md), [London](17867.md), [Podgorica](172136.md), [Tallinn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tallinn), [Paris](22989.md) and [Milan](36511.md).[^cite_note-apcrowd-103][^cite_note-bbclandslide-150][^cite_note-221][^cite_note-222]

In response to the protests, authorities in Moscow threatened to prosecute participants under Article 141 of the [Criminal Code of Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_Code_of_Russia), which prescribes five years' imprisonment for the obstruction of elections or citizens' electoral rights.[^cite_note-223] On 17 March, the Russian human rights group [OVD-Info](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OVD-Info) said that 80 people in 20 cities across Russia were arrested for election-related offences.[^cite_note-mtvow-132] In response to the Noon Against Putin protests abroad, foreign ministry spokesperson [Maria Zakharova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Zakharova) described the queues outside Russian embassies as evidence of support for the Kremlin.[^cite_note-224]

## Aftermath

Vladimir Putin was [inaugurated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth_inauguration_of_Vladimir_Putin) on 7 May 2024.[^cite_note-225][^cite_note-1tv.ru-226]

## 2024 problem

The "2024 problem"[^cite_note-227] referred to the end of Putin's presidential powers in 2024.[^cite_note-fedorov-228] Analysts saw it as a problem of how Putin's regime could survive his departure, and [Levada Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levada_Center) focus groups reduced the options to two: Putin keeping power or appointing a successor.[^cite_note-volkov-229]

Commentators outlined several scenarios:[^cite_note-gulf-230]

- **Competitive free elections** — the least likely option, with a successor emerging through an open political process.

  [^cite_note-gulf-230]

- **Appointing a successor** — one of the most likely options, with Putin backing a chosen candidate, as he himself had been [Boris Yeltsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boris_Yeltsin)'s successor and [Dmitry Medvedev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dmitry_Medvedev) had been his.

  [^cite_note-fedorov-228]

  [^cite_note-gulf-230]

  [^cite_note-politek4-231]

- **Amending the Constitution** — lifting the term limit so Putin could run again, an option [Valery Zorkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valery_Zorkin) floated but which Putin publicly rejected.

  [^cite_note-politek4-231]

  [^cite_note-gulf-230]

  [^cite_note-232]

- **Keeping power without the presidency** — Putin taking another post as in 2008–2012 (e.g. an empowered [Prime Minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_heads_of_government_of_Russia), [Chairman of the State Duma](5157139.md), [Security Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Security_Council_of_Russia) or [State Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_Council_\(Russia\))) and possibly running again in 2030. In January 2020 he announced constitutional amendments expanding the powers of the Duma and State Council, fuelling speculation he might lead a strengthened State Council after leaving office.

  [^cite_note-bloomber-233]

  [^cite_note-wegren-234]

  [^cite_note-235]

A central difficulty, analysts noted, was whether a successor could shield Putin as Putin had shielded Yeltsin, and whether the system he built could function without him.[^cite_note-236][^cite_note-dilemma-237]
