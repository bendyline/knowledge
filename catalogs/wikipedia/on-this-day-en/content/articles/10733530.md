Percentage of population using the Internet in 2022. Note that Africa has significantly less internet usage.

[^cite_note-\:0-1]

The **Internet in Africa** is limited by a lower penetration rate when compared to the rest of the world. Measurable parameters such as the number of [Internet service providers](100245.md) (ISPs) subscriptions, overall number of [hosts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Host_\(network\)), [IXP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Exchange_Point)-traffic, and overall available [bandwidth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandwidth_\(computing\)) are indicators that Africa is far behind the "[digital divide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_divide)". Moreover, Africa itself exhibits an inner digital divide, with most Internet activity and infrastructure concentrated in [South Africa](17416221.md), Morocco, and [Egypt](8087628.md), as well as smaller economies like [Mauritius](19201.md) and the Seychelles. In general, only 38% of the African population has access to the Internet as of 2025.[^cite_note-2] Only 0.4% of the African population has a fixed-broadband subscription. The majority of internet users use it through mobile broadband.[^cite_note-\:742-3][^cite_note-\:75-4][^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6]

During the [COVID-19 pandemic](62750956.md), many people who were not connected to the internet lost access to health care and education. Production in all industries was seriously harmed.[^cite_note-\:742-3][^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9]

While the telecommunications market in Africa is still in its early stages of development, it is also one of the fastest-growing in the world. In the 2000s, [mobile telephone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_telephone) service in Africa has been rising, and mobile telephone use is now substantially more widespread than fixed-line telephony. Telecommunication companies in Africa are looking at [Broadband Wireless Access](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadband_Wireless_Access) technologies as the key to make Internet available to the population at large. Projects are being completed that aim at the realization of [Internet backbones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_backbone) that might help cut the cost of [bandwidth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandwidth_\(computing\)) in African countries.

The [International Telecommunication Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Telecommunication_Union) held the first Connect the World meeting in [Kigali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kigali), [Rwanda](25645.md) (in October 2007) as a demonstration that the development of telecommunications in Africa is considered a key intermediate objective for the fulfillment of the [Millennium Development Goals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennium_Development_Goals).[^cite_note-10]

### Current trend

As of December 2020, [Kenya](188171.md) had an internet penetration of approximately 85.2. This high rate is mainly because Kenya is home to [M-Pesa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M-Pesa), which is a mobile wallet provider and the offered secure payment system encourages internet access. As of October 2020, the majority of web traffic in leading digital markets in Africa originated from mobile devices in [Nigeria](21383.md), one of the countries with the biggest number of internet users worldwide. Across the nation, 74 percent of web traffic was generated via smartphones and only 24 percent via PC devices. This is connected to the fact that mobile connections are much cheaper and do not require the infrastructure that is needed for traditional desktop PCs with fixed-line internet connections.[^cite_note-12]

### Context



Internet access point in [Kigali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kigali), [Rwanda](25645.md)

Obstacles to the accessibility of Internet services in [Africa](5334607.md) include generally low levels of [computer literacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_literacy) in the population, poor infrastructures, and high costs of Internet services. Power availability is also scarce, with vast rural areas that are not connected to power grids as well as frequent [black-outs](296636.md) in major urban areas such as [Dar es Salaam](8500.md).[^cite_note-ITU-13]

In 2000, [Subsaharan Africa](27067.md) as a whole had fewer fixed telephone lines than [Manhattan](45470.md), and in 2006, Africa contributed to only 2% of the world's overall telephone lines in the world.[^cite_note-ITU-13] As a consequence of this general lack of connectivity, most Africa-generated network traffic (something between 70%[^cite_note-Waters-14] and 85%[^cite_note-ITU-13]) is [routed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Routing) through servers that are located elsewhere (mainly [Europe](9239.md)).

Overall [bandwidth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandwidth_\(computing\)) in Africa is scarce, and its irregular distribution clearly reflects the African "inner digital divide". In 2007, 16 countries in Africa had just one international Internet connection with a capacity of 10 [Mbit/s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mbit/s) or lower, while South Africa alone had over 800 Mbit/s. The main backbones connecting Africa to the rest of the world via [submarine cables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_communications_cable), i.e., [SAT-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SAT-2) and [SAT-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SAT-3), provide for a limited bandwidth. In 2007, all these international connections from Africa amounted to roughly 28,000 Mbit/s, while Asia had 800,000 Mbit/s and Europe had over 3,000,000 Mbit/s. The total bandwidth available to Africa was less than that available to [Norway](21241.md) alone (49,000 Mbit/s).[^cite_note-ITU-13]

As a consequence of the scarce overall bandwidth provided by cable connections, a large section of [Internet traffic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_traffic) in Africa goes through expensive [satellite links](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_link).[^cite_note-Waters-14] In general, thus, the cost of [Internet access](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_access) (and even more so broadband access) is unaffordable for most of the population.[^cite_note-ITU-13] According to the Kenyan ISPs association, high costs are also a consequence of the subjection of African ISPs to European ISPs and the lack of a clear international regulation of inter-ISP cost partition. For example, while [ITU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Telecommunication_Union) has long ratified that the cost of inter-provider telephonic connections must be charged to all involved providers in equal parts, in 2002, the Kenyan ISP association has denounced that all costs of Internet traffic between Europe and Africa are charged to African providers.[^cite_note-BBC2002-15]

According to 2011 estimates, about 13.5% of the African population has Internet access.[^cite_note-17] While Africa accounts for 15.0% of the world's population, only 6.2% of the World's Internet subscribers are Africans.[^cite_note-WorldStats-18] Africans who have access to broadband connections are estimated to be in percentage of 1% or lower.[^cite_note-Waters-14][^cite_note-Pingdom-19] In September 2007, African broadband subscribers were 1,097,200, with a major part of these subscriptions from large companies or institutions.[^cite_note-Pingdom-19]

Internet access is also irregularly distributed, with 2/3 of overall online activity in Africa being generated in South Africa (which only accounts for 5% of the continent's population).[^cite_note-WorldStats-18] Most of the remaining 1/3 is in Morocco and Egypt.[^cite_note-ITU-13] The largest percentage of Internet subscribers are found in small economies such as Seychelles, where as much as 37% of the population has Internet access (while in South Africa this value is 11% and in Egypt it is 8%).[^cite_note-ITU-13]

It has been noted, anyway, that data on Internet subscribers only partially reflect the actual number of Internet users in Africa, and the impact of the network on African daily life and culture.[^cite_note-Cornu-20][^cite_note-UNU-21] For example, [cybercafes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cybercafe) and [Internet kiosks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_kiosk) are common in the urban areas of many African countries. There are also other informal means to "access" the Internet; for example, couriers that print [e-mail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E-mail) messages and deliver them by hand to recipients in remote locations, or [radio stations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_station) that broadcast information taken from the Internet.[^cite_note-Cornu-20]

### IXP traffic

An indirect measure that is sometimes used to assess the penetration of Internet technology in a given area is the overall amount of data traffic at [Internet exchange points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_exchange_point) (IXPs). On African IXPs, traffic can be measured in [kbit/s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kbit/s) (kilobits per second) or Mbit/s (megabits per second), while in the rest of the world it is typically in the order of magnitude of [Gbit/s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gbit/s) (gigabits per second). The main IXP of [Johannesburg](58893.md), [JINX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannesburg_Internet_Exchange) (which is also the largest IXP in Africa) has about 6.5 Gbit/s traffic (in Sep 2012).[^cite_note-JINX_Stats-25]

IXP traffic, anyway, is only a measure of local network traffic (mainly e-mail), while most of African generated traffic is routed through other continents, and most Web content created in Africa is hosted on [Web servers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_server) located elsewhere.[^cite_note-Pingdom-19] Additionally, measurable data do not consider [private peering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_peering), i.e., inter-ISP traffic that does not go through IXPs. For example, the main academic network in South Africa, [TENET](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TENET_\(network\)), has 10 Gbit/s private peering with ISP [Internet Solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Solutions) both in Johannesburg and [Cape Town](6653.md).[^cite_note-Pingdom-19]

### Regulation

The privatization of the telecommunication market, as well as the regulation of the competition in this market, are in an early stage of development in many Africa countries. Kenya and Botswana have started a privatization process for [Telkom Kenya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telkom_Kenya) and [Botswana Telecommunications Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botswana_Telecommunications_Corporation) (BTC), respectively.[^cite_note-ITU-13] The mobile telephony market is generally more open and dynamic, and even more so is the Internet market.[^cite_note-ITU-13]

The table below depicts the percentage of African countries where telecommunications markets (fixed line telephony, mobile telephony, Internet) are monopolistic, partially competitive, or fully competitive, either *de iure* or *de facto* (data refer to 2007).[^cite_note-ITU-13]

|  | Internet | Mobile telephones | Fixed telephones |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monopolistic | 10% | 9% | 55% |
| Partially competitive | 12% | 41% | 23% |
| Fully competitive | 69% | 43% | 25% |

The regulation of network businesses and the establishment of authorities to control them is widely recognised as a relevant objective by most African governments. A model for such regulation is provided by Morocco; after an authority was established in 1998, and [Meditel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meditel) entered the market in 1999 to compete with the main incumbent [Maroc Telecom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maroc_Telecom), the situation has been quickly developing.[^cite_note-ITU-13] Based on such experiences and on the directions provided by ITU, most African countries now have local Internet authorities and are defining local regulation of the Internet market. In 2007, 83% of African countries had their own authority for Internet services and data traffic.[^cite_note-ITU-13]

## Benefits of Internet Access in Africa

It is widely recognized that increased availability of Internet technology in Africa would provide several key benefits. Specifically, some of the major issues of the continent might be tackled by applications of this technology, as demonstrated by some initiatives that have already been started and that proved successful. For example, organizations such as [RANET](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_and_Internet_for_the_Communication_of_Hydro-Meteorological_and_Climate-Related_Information?action=edit\&redlink=1) (RAdio and interNET for The communication of Hydro-Meteorological and Climate-Related Information) and the [ACMAD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Centre_of_Meteorological_Application_for_Development) (African Centre of Meteorological Application for Development) use Internet to develop reliable weather models for [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel) and other areas in Africa, with dramatic benefits for local agricultures.[^cite_note-Cornu-20]

Internet-based [telemedicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telemedicine) and [distance education](8997.md) could improve [quality of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quality_of_life) in the most remote rural areas of Africa.[^cite_note-26] The availability of information on the network could benefit [education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education) in general, counterbalancing the general lack of local libraries.[^cite_note-Cornu-20] It has also been suggested that [e-Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E-Government) applications could indirectly alleviate widespread political issues such as they would definitely help bridge the gap between the institutions and remote rural areas. Most [Web 2.0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_2.0) applications developed in Africa insofar have actually been created by governments.[^cite_note-ITU-13]

African economy might also benefit from broadband availability, for example as a consequence of the applicability of [e-commerce](9611.md) and [outsourcing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outsourcing) business models that have long proved effective in Europe and North America.[^cite_note-ITU-13] Currently there are many small businesses (Cybercafes, local ISPs or Wireless ISPs) that benefit from broadband availability via satellite to provide Internet connectivity solutions to local customers.

One technology that has been utilized in many African countries for the provision of Internet broadband connectivity is [VSAT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Very-small-aperture_terminal), which allows businesses to access the European or US Internet backbone via satellite in regions that lack terrestrial Internet access. Fiber in Africa has been restricted to big coastal cities facing North Atlantic, South Atlantic, and Indian Oceans. According to World Bank data only 37% of Africa's 1.2 billion people actually live in those regions. Therefore, satellite remains to be the most effective and viable way to reach rural areas, and thus a major portion of Africa's population. Satellite access in Africa is popular on KU band and C band,[^cite_note-27] with C band being the preferred access method in countries that have heavy rainfall.

## Evolution and perspectives

### Internet availability

The African telecommunication market is growing at a faster rate than in the rest of the world.[^cite_note-Livraghi2008-23] In the 2000s this has especially been true for the mobile telephony market, that between 2004 and 2007 grew three times as fast as the world's average.[^cite_note-28] In 2005, over 5 billion [USD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USD) have been invested in Africa in telecommunication infrastructures.[^cite_note-Waters-14]

Internet in Africa is now growing even faster than mobile telephony. Between 2000 and 2008, Internet subscriptions have grown by 1030.2%, versus the world's average of 290.6%.[^cite_note-WorldStats-18]

### Infrastructure development



African undersea cables

A number of projects have been started that aim at bringing more bandwidth to Africa, in order to cut down costs for both operators and end users. At least three projects for an underseas backbone in the [Indian Ocean](14580.md) have been started. [EASSy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EASSy) (East African Submarine cable System), sponsored by the [World Bank](36752.md) and the [Development Bank of Southern Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Development_Bank_of_Southern_Africa), is a cable system that will connect [Mtunzini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mtunzini) (South Africa) and [Port Sudan](213572.md) (Sudan), with branches to several countries on the eastern coast of Africa. The Kenyan government has started a similar project named [TEAMS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TEAMS_\(cable_system\)) (The East Africa Marine System), with the collaboration of [Etisalat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etisalat).[^cite_note-Balancing398-29] A third project, [SEACOM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SEACOM_\(African_cable_system\)), is completely African-owned.[^cite_note-30] SEACOM bandwidth has already been sold to several customers, including the South African network [TENET](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TENET_\(network\)).[^cite_note-Alston-31] On the eastern coast of the continent of Africa, the Africa Coast to Europe cable connects Gibraltar to South Africa and lands in Gulf of Guinea nations, and allows for more access to connectivity. The Eastern African Submarine System, a 10,000 km cable network, is another example.[^cite_note-\:742-3][^cite_note-32]

In South Africa, the [SANReN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SANReN) network, with a 500 Gbit/s core, has been designed to become the fastest academic network in the world; the universities of [Witwatersrand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witwatersrand) and Johannesburg are already using a bandwidth of 10 Gbit/s provided by this network.

According to the [European Commission](9974.md), a 10% rise in digital coverage could result in a more than 1% increase in African GDP. The European Investment Bank makes funding emerging developments on the continent a priority, in line with the EU's plan for African digital transformation.[^cite_note-33][^cite_note-\:3-34] The European Investment Bank is providing a €25 million for a 600 km undersea cable in Mauritania, to provide digital access to more people.[^cite_note-\:742-3][^cite_note-35]

### Access

Efforts to connect previously disconnected parts of the world have been compared to previous rounds of infrastructure in Africa. The recent linking of East Africa to the global fibre-optic network generated similar visions and hopes to those that emerged in the [Victorian era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_era) when railways were used to connect the previously disconnected.[^cite_note-36]

With bandwidth becoming more available and less costly, the first to benefit will be institutions and companies that already have Internet access. In order for the network to reach a larger part of the population, solutions are needed for the [last mile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_mile_\(telecommunications\)) problem, i.e., to make bandwidth available to the final user. To be feasible for Africa, last mile solutions must be found that take into account the limited penetration of fixed telephony lines, especially in rural areas. Of about 400.000 rural communities that are estimated to exist in Africa, less than 3% have [PSTN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PSTN) access. Note that providing network access to rural communities is one of Millennium Goals defined by the [World Summit on the Information Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Summit_on_the_Information_Society).

Most studies on this subject identify [Broadband Wireless Access](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadband_Wireless_Access) (BWA) technologies such as [WiMAX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WiMAX) as the most promising solution for the end user's Internet access in Africa.[^cite_note-ITU-13] These technologies can also benefit from the wide availability of the mobile telephony network. Even in smaller countries like [Seychelles](27288.md), most Internet users already access the network via the GSM network.[^cite_note-ITU-13] Providers that have [3G](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3G) licenses will be able to provide WiMAX services.[^cite_note-ITU-13]

Some experimentation is already being conducted in a few countries. In [Kenya](188171.md), the Digital Village Scheme project aims at providing government services in rural areas via wireless access. In Nigeria, Horizon Wireless is running a broadband (3.5 [GHz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GHz)) wireless network. Since 2007, [MTN Rwanda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MTN_Group) has been working to provide broadband wireless access in [Kigali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kigali).[^cite_note-ITU-13] In Algeria, the Icosnet ISP and Aperto Networks have been collaborating for a business WiMAX solution. The South African authority [ICASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ICASA) has already assigned WiMAX licences to several providers, and [Neotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neotel) is implementing WiMAX-based last mile solutions in [Johannesburg](58893.md), [Pretoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pretoria), [Cape Town](6653.md) and [Durban](313650.md).[^cite_note-37]

Mobile technology is the primary infrastructure for telecom services in Africa, with operators focusing their efforts on it. In certain markets, mobile networks handle up to 99% of voice and data traffic. Fixed-line infrastructure is slower, more expensive, has a larger reach, and has fewer difficulties.[^cite_note-\:742-3][^cite_note-38] The number of mobile customers in Africa is expanding faster than everywhere else. By rapidly linking entrepreneurs, startups, and enterprises with existing and future clientele, digital platforms are creating possibilities for them. Mobile financial services allow for immediate payment of products and services.[^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40][^cite_note-41]

According to a survey conducted in 2022, the percentage of banks offering digital products or services ranges from at least 80% in Central Africa to more than 95% in West Africa (mostly driven by Nigeria). The top three most often requested services are domestic money transfers (87%), receiving payments from clients (85%), and paying bills or suppliers (79.6%).[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44]

In the 2020s, in an effort to bridge the [digital divide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_divide), several African countries granted licenses to companies who offer [satellite internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_internet) or other electronic communications offerings.[^cite_note-45]
