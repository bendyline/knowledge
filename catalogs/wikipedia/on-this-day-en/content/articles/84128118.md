**Hurricane Lowell** was a long-lived and very powerful [tropical cyclone](8282374.md) that impacted the western [Hawaiian Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaiian_Islands), making a direct hit on [Ni'ihau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ni'ihau) and [Kauaʻi](70961.md). Lowell was the first hurricane to directly hit the island of Kauaʻi since [Hurricane Iniki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Iniki) in 1992.[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-4] The fourteenth named storm, fifth hurricane, fourth [major hurricane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_hurricane), and second [Category 5 hurricane](3611403.md) of the [2026 Pacific hurricane season](66237695.md), Lowell formed on August 27 and reached Category 5 intensity on September 2. In doing so, it became the first Category 5 hurricane in the [Central Pacific basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_cyclone_basins#CPAC) since [Hurricane Walaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Walaka) in [2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Pacific_hurricane_season). Increasing [wind shear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_shear) and [eyewall replacement cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eyewall_replacement_cycle) caused it to weaken to Category 3 status by September 4, but the following day, it rapidly intensified to Category 5 intensity once again. Increasing wind shear caused it to weaken to a Category 2 hurricane by the time it made its direct hit on [Kaua'i County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaua'i_County), and Lowell became post-tropical by September 11.

Lowell's powerful winds damaged structures and knocked out power for most Kaua'i County customers. Overall, the storm caused \$1 billion of damage in Hawai'i and killed at least three.

On August 24, the [National Hurricane Center](255317.md) (NHC) first noted a low potential for eventual [tropical cyclogenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_cyclogenesis), related to a [trough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trough_\(meteorology\)) located east-southeast of [Hawaii](13270.md). The weather system produced [thunderstorms](70807.md) as it drifted northeastward across the eastern Pacific Ocean.[^cite_note-5] By August 25, the NHC assessed a high likelihood – 70% – for a tropical cyclone to form.[^cite_note-6] That day, a weak and small [low-pressure area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-pressure_area) developed within the system.[^cite_note-7] On August 26, the low became more organized due to favorable environmental conditions.[^cite_note-8] Late on August 27, the NHC classified the system as Tropical Storm Lowell about 1,530 miles (2,460 km) east-southeast of Hawaii. The storm had a closed circulation, producing winds of 40 mph (65 km/h) alongside well-organized bands of [convection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convection_\(meteorology\)).[^cite_note-disc1-9]

Upon its formation, Lowell was moving to the east-northeast over an area of warm [ocean temperatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_surface_temperature).[^cite_note-disc1-9] The storm gradually strengthened amid some northeasterly [wind shear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_shear).[^cite_note-10] Lowell's circulation became exposed from the thunderstorms, and the storm became disorganized. Late on August 28, Lowell turned to the west, steered by a [ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ridge_\(meteorology\)) to its north. Around that time, the NHC noted uncertainty in the storm's future, due to potential interaction with [Tropical Storm Karina](66237695.md#Hurricane_Karina) to its east.[^cite_note-11] On August 29, thunderstorms reformed over the circulation, although it continued to be affected by wind shear through the next day.[^cite_note-12] On August 31, the convection became better organized after the wind shear decreased. By that time, the NHC anticipated that Lowell would undergo [rapid intensification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapid_deepening).[^cite_note-13] On September 1, Lowell attained hurricane status about 575 miles (925 km) to the southeast of Hilo, Hawaii.[^cite_note-14]



Lowell at its initial peak intensity south-southeast of the [Hawaiian Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaiian_Islands) on September 2

Late on September 1, Lowell intensified into a major hurricane, or a Category 3 on the [Saffir-Simpson scale](255313.md). The hurricane had an [eye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eye_\(cyclone\)) surrounded by a ring of deep convection.[^cite_note-15] Late on September 2, Lowell strengthened further into a Category 5 hurricane, with [maximum sustained winds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_sustained_wind) of 160 mph (260 km/h) and a minimum pressure of 927 mbar (27.4 inHg). Around that time, it was located about 445 mi (715 km) south of [Hilo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilo,_Hawaii).[^cite_note-16] Lowell became the first Category 5 hurricane in the Central Pacific since [Hurricane Walaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Walaka) in 2018.[^cite_note-17] After reaching peak intensity, Lowell weakened due to a combination of increased wind shear, dry air, and an [eyewall replacement cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eyewall_replacement_cycle).[^cite_note-18] On September 4, it briefly fell to Category 3 intensity due to another replacement cycle.[^cite_note-19] Later that day, the [Hurricane Hunters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Hunters) observed that Lowell re-intensified into a high-end Category 4 hurricane as the eyewall replacement cycle was complete.[^cite_note-20] Hurricane Hunters then observed that Lowell regained Category 5 intensity a few hours later, with maximum sustained winds of 160 mph (260 km/h) and a minimum central pressure of 922 mbar (27.2 inHg).[^cite_note-21] This made it the first [Pacific hurricane](3092184.md) to attain Category 5 intensity more than once since [Hurricane Ioke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Ioke) in 2006 and only one of four Pacific hurricanes to do so on record, along with [Hurricane Emilia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Emilia_\(1994\)) in 1994, Ioke, and [Hurricane Polo](84283796.md) a few weeks later.[^cite_note-m573-22]

Soon after attaining Category 5 status a second time, Lowell again weakened slightly as its eye become less defined, possibly due to wind shear and dry air. The hurricane slowed on September 5 after the ridge to its north began weakening.[^cite_note-23] This was partly due to an [upper-level low](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper-level_low), located north of Hawaii and moving to the southwest; the low caused Lowell to turn sharply northward.[^cite_note-24] As wind shear increased and sea surface temperatures decreased, Lowell weakened below major hurricane strength on September 7.[^cite_note-25] Around 09\:00 UTC on September 8, the center of Lowell passed about 40 mi (64 km) west of [Niʻihau](39688.md).[^cite_note-26][^cite_note-27] High wind shear caused the hurricane to weaken quickly, as the center became exposed from the convection.[^cite_note-28] On September 9, Lowell dropped to tropical storm status. It turned back to the west due to a building ridge, where dry air continued to affect the storm.[^cite_note-29] On September 11, the NHC redesignated Lowell as a [post-tropical cyclone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-tropical_cyclone), more than 24 hours since the storm produced thunderstorms near the center.[^cite_note-30]

### Influence of climate change

Further information: [Tropical cyclones and climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_cyclones_and_climate_change)

Scientists from Climate Central concluded that [anthropogenic climate change](5042951.md) has made the unusually warm sea surface temperatures along Lowell’s path up to 60 times more likely. As the hurricane intensified, it moved over waters that were 0.7°C to 1.1°C (1°F to 2°F) warmer than normal, which human-caused climate change made 10-20 times more probable.[^cite_note-31]

## Preparations

On September 6 (UTC), the NHC issued a hurricane watch for the eastern part of [Papahānaumokuākea Marine National Monument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papah%C4%81naumoku%C4%81kea_Marine_National_Monument) and a tropical storm watch for [Kauai County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kauai_County).[^cite_note-32] [Līhuʻe Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lihue_Airport) was shut down in preparation of the hurricane.[^cite_note-33] Later that day, the agency upgraded the watches to warnings.[^cite_note-34] A flood watch was also in effect for the main Hawaiian Islands through September 8, with heavy rainfall expected to cause flash flooding and mudslides, especially on [Kauaʻi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kauai) and parts of the [Big Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaii_\(island\)).[^cite_note-35] Due to further eastward shifts in the forecast path of the storm, a hurricane watch was issued for Kauai County on September 7, 2026 (UTC) and further upgraded to a hurricane warning later that day.[^cite_note-36] A few hours later, still more eastward shifts in the track forecast prompted a tropical storm watch, then a tropical storm warning, for [Oahu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oahu).[^cite_note-37] Early on September 8 (UTC), a tornado watch was issued for Kauai County and adjacent waters, the first tornado watch issued for the state of Hawaii on record.[^cite_note-38]

On September 3, Hawaii governor [Josh Green](41279962.md) issued a state of emergency due to the threat from Lowell and potentially Karina.[^cite_note-39] The state activated 129 [Hawaii National Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaii_National_Guard) members while also sending six high-water vehicles to [Kauai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kauai).[^cite_note-40] Two state parks were closed to campers due to the hurricane. Campers were notified to vacate the parks immediately on September 4.[^cite_note-41]

Many [Ni'hau](39688.md) residents had traveled to Kauaʻi for a funeral ahead of Lowell's close pass by the island, and were unable make proper preparations.[^cite_note-\:1-42]

## Impact



Rocks are seen pushed onto residential properties in Kekaha, Hawaii, after Hurricane Lowell's surf and storm surge impacts.

Prior to the brunt of impacts from Lowell, Kauaʻi's only weather radar was knocked out.[^cite_note-43] The hurricane dropped heavy rainfall across Hawaii, peaking at 21.11 in (536 mm) in Kilohana on Kauaʻi. Wind gusts in the state peaked at 92 mph (148 km/h) at Puʻu Lua.[^cite_note-44] Sustained winds to 59 mph (95 km/h) and a gust of 79 mph (127 km/h) were recorded at the [Līhuʻe Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C4%ABhu%CA%BBe_Airport).[^cite_note-45] Powerful waves and storm surge moved into coastal areas such as [Kekaha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kekaha,_Hawaii) and [Poʻipu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Po%CA%BBip%C5%AB,_Hawaii), causing extensive damage to structures and roadways. In Kekaha, [Kaumualiʻi Highway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaii_Route_50) fronting Kekaha Beach was completely obstructed by rocks and road fragments.[^cite_note-46] [Kauaʻi Island Utility Cooperative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaua%CA%BBi_Island_Utility_Cooperative) reported that strong winds from Lowell knocked out power to over 91% of Kaua'i County.[^cite_note-47] Power outages at the Waimea Wastewater Treatment Plant led to the spill of 20,000 gallons of wastewater.[^cite_note-48]



A house in Waimea, Kauai, Hawaii, is seen with damage from Hurricane Lowell's powerful winds.

Near Baby Beach in Poʻipū, a home was swept off of its foundation and onto neighboring properties.[^cite_note-49][^cite_note-50] In Poʻipū, storm surge paired with high surf damaged parts of the Beach House Restaurant and the Sheraton Kauaʻi Resort, which were among those hit the hardest.[^cite_note-51][^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53][^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55] A house in [Hanapēpē](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanap%C4%93p%C4%93,_Hawaii) lost its roof as a result of strong winds.[^cite_note-56] [Port Allen Boat Harbor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Allen,_Hawaii) was extensively damaged.[^cite_note-57] Storm surge and surf also contributed to the destruction of Waimea Pier.[^cite_note-58] Further west in Kekaha, multiple homes were damaged by hurricane-force winds and storm surge.[^cite_note-59] In the commercial area of Līhuʻe, Hokulei Village and [Kukui Grove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kukui_Grove_Center) sustained minor damages to their properties.[^cite_note-60] Storm surge caused multiple shipping containers to be taken from Kaumālapa‘u Harbor, [Lānaʻi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C4%81na%CA%BBi)'s only commercial harbor, into the ocean. Bollards protecting the edge of the harbor were moved out of place or destroyed, damaging the harbor's protective seawall and causing multiple sinkholes to form.[^cite_note-\:1-42]

A 74-year-old man in Kaua‘i was killed by the storm surge related to Lowell.[^cite_note-61] A 59-year-old man was found dead in [Wainiha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wainiha,_Hawaii).[^cite_note-62] On Oʻahu, a tree fell on a homeless man's tent, killing him inside.[^cite_note-e764-63] Oahu recorded 21,000 power outages.[^cite_note-64]

On September 18, it was reported that 18 homes in Kaua’i had been destroyed, with at least a 100 sustaining major damage. More than 700 additional homes were damaged in the county.[^cite_note-65][^cite_note-66]

On September 7, the [Hawaiʻi State Department of Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaii_State_Department_of_Education) cancelled school for Kauaʻi and Oʻahu in advance of the storm's approach. Due to the damage left on Kauaʻi, the department extended the closure of schools on the island until September 11.[^cite_note-76]

On September 8, the [Līhuʻe Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C4%ABhu%CA%BBe_Airport) was reopened to travelers. [HDOT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaii_Department_of_Transportation) advised that travelers should expect delays and allow extra time to check in and get through TSA. The following day, [Hawaiian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaiian_Airlines) and [Alaska Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaska_Airlines) issued a notice that 'longer than usual' lines at the airport were occurring.[^cite_note-77][^cite_note-78] The island's two major shopping centers, Kukui Grove and The Shops at Kukui'ula were closed due to power loss.[^cite_note-79][^cite_note-80]

[Ni'hau](39688.md) residents were reported safe by island management, and there was a supply of food and water available for residents.[^cite_note-\:1-42]

In response to cleanup efforts by residents, the County of Kauaʻi reopened the Kekaha Landfill and transfer stations across the island.[^cite_note-81] Collection fees were waived temporarily.[^cite_note-82] A wastewater spill was reported at the Waimea Wastewater Treatment Plant.[^cite_note-83] In response to food and ice shortages caused by prolonged power outages, food distribution sites were set up in key points across the island.[^cite_note-84] Local businesses such as Meme's Farmstand and The Musubi Truck offered free food to the community.[^cite_note-85][^cite_note-86]

On September 9, Governor Josh Green reported that initial damage estimates indicate the storm will cost more than US\$400 million, exceeding the damage from [Hurricane Lala](83950433.md), which struck Hawaii a few weeks prior.[^cite_note-87][^cite_note-88] Governor Green also stated that early in the recovery process, Lowell seemed to have caused more damage than [Hurricane Iniki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Iniki) in 1992, the costliest tropical cyclone to hit the [State of Hawaiʻi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_of_Hawai%CA%BBi) to date, with early estimates placing the total damage cost between \$500 million and \$1 billion.[^cite_note-89][^cite_note-90][^cite_note-91] By September 23, Governor Green stated that the state had spent \$112.4 million on post-Lowell recovery estimates.
