**Kuwait Airways** ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language): الخطوط الجوية الكويتية, *_al-Ḫuṭūṭ al-Jawiyyah al-Kuwaītiyyah_*) is the [flag carrier](465816.md) of [Kuwait](7515890.md),[^cite_note-Flottau-2018-3] wholly owned by the government of Kuwait. Its head office is situated on the grounds of [Kuwait International Airport](464414.md), Al Farwaniyah Governorate. It operates scheduled international services throughout the [Middle East](19323.md), to the Indian subcontinent, Europe, Southeast Asia and North America, from its main base at Kuwait International Airport.

## History



Kuwait National Airways [Douglas DC-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_DC-3) in 1955

The carrier traces its history back to 1953,[^cite_note-5] when *Kuwait National Airways* was formed by a group of Kuwaiti businessmen; initially, the government took a 50% interest.[^cite_note-Flight1958-539-6][^cite_note-FI1965-203/11-7]<sup>: 211 </sup> That year, a five-year management contract was signed with *British International Airlines* (BIA),[^cite_note-8] a [BOAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BOAC) subsidiary in Kuwait that operated charter flights and provided maintenance services.[^cite_note-Flight1958-539-6][^cite_note-FI1973-459-9] Two [Dakotas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_C-47_Skytrain) were bought,[^cite_note-FI1965-203/11-7]<sup>: 211 </sup> and operations started on 16 March 1954.[^cite_note-FI1999-84-4] The carrier transported 8,966 passengers in its first year of operations.[^cite_note-10] In July 1955, the name *Kuwait Airways* was adopted.[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12] In May 1958,[^cite_note-FI1999-84-4] a new contract for management and operation was signed, directly with BOAC this time.[^cite_note-13] BIA was taken over by Kuwait Airways in April 1959.[^cite_note-FI1999-84-4][^cite_note-14]



Kuwait Airways De Havilland DH.106 Comet 4C at London Heathrow Airport in 1964



A Kuwait Airways Trident at London Heathrow in 1974

On 8 August 1962,[^cite_note-FI1965-203/11-7]<sup>: 210 </sup> Kuwait Airways became the first foreign customer to order the [Trident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawker_Siddeley_Trident) when two aircraft of the type were acquired, and an option for a third was taken. The deal was valued at [£](270673.md)5.5 million, and also included a [Comet 4C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Havilland_Comet). At the same time, the carrier also had a £3 million order in place for three [BAC One-Elevens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BAC_One-Eleven), with an option for a fourth.[^cite_note-FI1962-221/2-15]<sup>: 221 </sup> The airline took delivery of the first Comet of its own in January 1963,[^cite_note-16][^cite_note-17] but Comet operations had started in July the previous year with an aircraft on lease from [MEA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East_Airlines).[^cite_note-FI1963-223/6-18]<sup>: 225 </sup> In August 1963, a second Comet was ordered.[^cite_note-19][^cite_note-20] The delivery of this second airframe established an unofficial record in early 1964 when it flew between London and Kuwait, a distance of 2,888 miles (4,648 km), at 461 miles per hour (742 km/h) on average.[^cite_note-21] On 1 June 1963, the government increased its participation in the airline to 100%.[^cite_note-FI1965-587-22] In March 1964, the carrier added its first European destination to the route network when flights to London were inaugurated using Comet equipment; from that time, services between London and some points in the Middle East, including [Abadan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abadan), [Bahrain](18933277.md), [Beirut](37428.md), [Dhahran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhahran), [Doha](26214389.md) and Kuwait, started being operated in a pool agreement between the carrier and BOAC and MEA.[^cite_note-23][^cite_note-24] A month later, the airline absorbed [Trans Arabia Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans_Arabia_Airways).[^cite_note-25]



A Kuwait Airways [Boeing 707-320C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_707-320C) in 1978. Three aircraft of the type were ordered in November 1967.

[^cite_note-FI1967-980-26]

In April 1965, the route network had expanded to include Abadan, [Baghdad](4492.md), Bahrain, Beirut, [Bombay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombay), [Cairo](6293.md), [Damascus](8914.md), Doha, [Frankfurt](10992.md), Geneva, [Jerusalem](16043.md), [Karachi](17123.md), London, Paris and [Tehran](57654.md). At this time, the fleet comprised two Comet 4Cs, three [DC-6Bs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DC-6B), two [Twin Pioneers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_Pioneer) and three [Viscount 700s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vickers_Viscount); the carrier had two Trident 1Es and three [One-Elevens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-Eleven) pending delivery.[^cite_note-FI1965-587-22] The first Trident was handed over by the aircraft manufacturer in March 1966,[^cite_note-27] and the second followed in May the same year.[^cite_note-Flight_International-1966-28] In the interim, a third aircraft of the type was ordered.[^cite_note-Flight_International-1966-28] On the other hand, the One-Elevens were never delivered: in January 1966, the carrier stated that the simultaneous introduction of both types of aircraft was not possible due to a tightened budget, and postponed their delivery;[^cite_note-29] it was informed late that year that the airline would not take them.[^cite_note-30][^cite_note-32] Three [Boeing 707-320Cs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_707-320C) were ordered in November 1967.[^cite_note-FI1967-980-26] The carrier made its first profit ever in 1968, with a [net income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Net_income) of £910,000.[^cite_note-FI1973-668-33]

During 1972, Kuwait Airways' fifth consecutive profitable year, the airline had a net profit of £2.9 million. By May 1973, the fleet had reduced to five Boeing 707-320C aircraft.[^cite_note-FI1973-668-33] That year, flights to [Colombo](56636.md) were launched.[^cite_note-34] In March 1975, Faisal Saud Al-Fulaij, who employed 1,800, was the corporation's chairman.[^cite_note-35] In a deal worth US\$14 million, two additional ex-[Pan American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_American_World_Airways) Boeing 707-320Cs were subsequently purchased that year, with the first one entering the fleet in May.[^cite_note-36] The carrier ordered its first [Boeing 737](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_737) that year, slated for delivery in February 1976.[^cite_note-37] Kuwait Airways became the [Boeing 727's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_727) 96th worldwide customer in 1979 when it ordered three of these aircraft for delivery in late 1980 and early 1981.[^cite_note-38]



A Kuwait Airways [Airbus A310-300](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbus_A310-300), 2004

By July 1980, chairmanship was held by Ghassan Al-Nissef, the number of employees had grown to 5,400 and the fleet comprised eight Boeing 707-320Cs, one [Boeing 737-200](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_737-200), three [Boeing 747-200Bs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_747-200B) and one [JetStar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_Jetstar); three [Boeing 727-200s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_727-200) were pending delivery.[^cite_note-39] In mid 1980, six [Airbus A310-200s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbus_A310-200) were ordered to replace the Boeing 707s on routes to Asia, Europe and the Middle East, with deliveries starting in 1983;[^cite_note-40] five more A310 aircraft were added to the order late that year.[^cite_note-41]

After India's air market was deregulated in 1992, Kuwait Airways and [Gulf Air](377403.md) participated in the formation of [Jet Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_Airways), each holding a 20% equity stake,[^cite_note-42] with a total investment estimated at [US\$](18717338.md)8 million.[^cite_note-43] Following the enactment of a law that banned the investment of foreign carriers in domestic Indian operators, both airlines had to divest their shareholding in the Indian company.[^cite_note-44] Kuwait Airways' 20% stake in Jet Airways was sold to the chairman [Naresh Goyal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naresh_Goyal) for [US\$](18717338.md)4 million.[^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46]



Kuwait Airways [Airbus A340-300](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbus_A340-300), 2014. The carrier received the first aircraft of the type in March 1995.

[^cite_note-Kuwaiti_A340_Delivered-47]

In July 1996,[^cite_note-Boeing-1996-48] the carrier modified a previous order that included [Boeing 747](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_747) aircraft,[^cite_note-49] and placed an order worth US\$280 million for two [Boeing 777-200s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_777-200),[^cite_note-Boeing-1996-48] with purchase rights for another aircraft of the type.[^cite_note-50] The operation made Kuwait Airways the 22nd customer of the type worldwide.[^cite_note-Boeing-1996-48] The airframer handed over the first Boeing 777-200 in early 1998.[^cite_note-51][^cite_note-52] In December 1998, a code-share agreement was signed with [Trans World Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans_World_Airlines) to begin in the spring of 1999.[^cite_note-53]

In October 2007, the new CEO pledged that the airline should be privatised to compete efficiently against other airlines. He says that the airline will encounter difficulty in advancing, especially in fleet renewal, without privatisation.[^cite_note-54]

Flights to [Iraq](7515928.md) were resumed in November 2013; Kuwait Airways had discontinued services to the country in 1990 following the [invasion of Kuwait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasion_of_Kuwait).[^cite_note-55] After a 17-year hiatus, the carrier resumed flying to [Munich](19058.md) in July 2015.[^cite_note-56][^cite_note-Duclos-2015-57] Also in July 2015, the airline restarted flights to [Istanbul-Atatürk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istanbul-Atat%C3%BCrk); the city had not been served for three years.[^cite_note-Duclos-2015-57] [Bangalore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangalore) was added to the carrier's network in October 2015.[^cite_note-58]

Kuwait Airways faced allegations of discrimination for refusing to sell tickets to Israeli passport holders on certain routes, prompting a U.S. Department of Transportation investigation that ordered the airline to stop denying transport to Israeli citizens on U.S.-related international routes.[^cite_note-59][^cite_note-60] In response, the airline cited Kuwaiti law, discontinued its JFK–London service in 2015, and later won a 2017 German court case upholding its right to deny Israeli passport holders transit through Kuwait.[^cite_note-61][^cite_note-62]

## Corporate affairs and identity

### Ownership

Kuwait Airways is wholly owned by the [government of Kuwait](16688.md), as of August 2023.[^cite_note-Kuwait_Airways_in_talks_with_lessors_amid_delivery_delays-2023-63]

#### Privatisation plans

Privatisation started being considered in the mid 1990s, in a period that followed the [Gulf War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_War) when the carrier experienced a heavy loss on its assets.[^cite_note-Centre_for_Aviation-2008-64] The company was turned into a corporation in 2004.[^cite_note-65] A draft decree for its privatisation was approved by the government on 21 July 2008. Plans were to sell up to 35% of the stake to a long-term investor and another 40% allotted to the public, whereas the government would hold the remaining 25%. These plans also contemplated the exclusion of domestic carrier competitors, such as [Jazeera Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazeera_Airways), as potential bidders. Furthermore, the government also committed to keeping the workforce invariant for at least five years and those who were not to be retained would be offered the opportunity to be transferred to other government dependencies without altering their salaries and holding similar working conditions.[^cite_note-Centre_for_Aviation-2008-64]

In 2011, the privatisation committee valued the carrier at US\$805 million, following advice by the [Citigroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citigroup), [Ernst & Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_&_Young) and Seabury.[^cite_note-Centre_for_Aviation-2011-66] The process was expected to be concluded by March 2011.[^cite_note-67] However, in October that year, the committee recommended the airline to go through a reorganisation process before continuing with the privatisation programme,[^cite_note-68] something that was approved by Kuwait's Council of Ministers.[^cite_note-Centre_for_Aviation-2011-66] The privatisation draft was amended[^cite_note-69][^cite_note-70] and the government signed a contract with the [International Air Transport Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Air_Transport_Association) for the provision of consultation expertise.[^cite_note-71] The law for the privatisation of Kuwait Airways Corporation was passed in January 2013.[^cite_note-72]

### Key people

As of November 2023, Abdulmohsen Salem Alfagaan holds the position as [chairman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chairman).\[^cite_note-[www.ch-aviation.com-73](http://www.ch-aviation.com-73/)]

### Headquarters

The Kuwait Airways headquarters is located on the grounds of [Kuwait International Airport](464414.md) in [Al Farwaniyah Governorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Farwaniyah_Governorate), Kuwait. The 42,000 square metres (450,000 sq ft) head office was built for 15.8 million [Kuwaiti dinars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuwaiti_dinars) ([US \$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_\$) 53.6 million). Ahmadiah Contracting & Trading Co. served as the main contractor. The headquarter was constructed from 1992 to 1996. The construction of the head office was the first time that structural glazing for curtain walls was used in the State of Kuwait.[^cite_note-74] The previous headquarters was on the grounds of the airport.[^cite_note-75]

### Fleet development

In October 2013, Kuwait Airways had one of the oldest aircraft fleets in the Middle East, with an average age of 20 years.[^cite_note-95] That month, the carrier opened its [maintenance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_maintenance) facilities to the press for them to check that the fleet was kept in condition,[^cite_note-96] amid rumours of deficiencies in their maintenance.[^cite_note-97] In December the same year, the carrier signed a [memorandum of understanding](1018279.md) with [Airbus](26220236.md) for the acquisition of 15 [A320neos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbus_A320neo) and ten [A350-900s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbus_A350-900).[^cite_note-98][^cite_note-99][^cite_note-100] These aircraft would be handed over between 2019 and 2022.[^cite_note-Gulf_Business-2013-101] For the interim period, the deal includes the lease of seven [A320s](164933.md) and five [A330-200s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbus_A330-200) from the aircraft manufacturer;[^cite_note-102] deliveries would start in late 2014.[^cite_note-Gulf_Business-2013-101] In a deal valued at US\$4.4 billion,[^cite_note-103] the order including ten A350-900s and 15 A320neos was confirmed in February 2014.[^cite_note-104][^cite_note-105] Kuwait Airways' intentions to purchase ten [Boeing 777-300ERs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_777-300ER) were informed in November 2014.[^cite_note-106][^cite_note-107] The order was firmed up a month later for US\$3.3 billion[^cite_note-108][^cite_note-109][^cite_note-110] with deliveries expected to start in November 2016.[^cite_note-111] Also in December 2014, Kuwait Airways took delivery of its first sharkleted Airbus A320 as part of the airline's fleet renewal programme.[^cite_note-112] By March 2015, Kuwait Airways received four leased aircraft of the type, marking the first fleet upgrade in 17 years.[^cite_note-113][^cite_note-114] The carrier became a new customer for the Airbus A330 when it received the first aircraft of the type in June 2015.[^cite_note-115][^cite_note-116]

Following the airline's rebranding initiative in October 2016, Kuwait Airways received its first Boeing 777-300ER in December 2016, marking the arrival of the airline's first fully owned new aircraft in nearly twenty years.[^cite_note-Dron-2016-77][^cite_note-Kaminski-Morrow-2016-78] Introduced in 1995,[^cite_note-Kuwaiti_A340_Delivered-47] the [Airbus A340-300](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbus_A340-300) was retired from service by the airline in 2017.[^cite_note-117] In October 2018, Kuwait Airways amended a pre-existing commitment with Airbus for 10 [A350-900s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A350-900) by reducing it to five of these aircraft and ordered eight [Airbus A330-800s](43315216.md), which were scheduled to be delivered from March 2019.[^cite_note-Flottau-2018-3][^cite_note-118] The first two [Airbus A330-800s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbus_A330-800) were handed over to the airline by the aircraft manufacturer in October 2020.[^cite_note-119]

In August 2019, Kuwait Airways Chairman Yousef A. M. J. Alsaqer stated that the airline plans to spend \$2.5 billion on new aircraft due to be delivered by 2026.[^cite_note-120]

## Services

Kuwait Airways offers Royal Class and First Class passengers the option to check-in at the comfort of their home in where a limousine and an airline crew member will check-in the passengers, collect the luggage, and issue boarding passes at home. A car service to drive passengers to the airport is also provided upon request. This service is only offered while outbound from Kuwait International Airport.[^cite_note-121] Kuwait Airways is one of the few airlines which does not serve [alcoholic drinks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcoholic_drinks) on its flights.[^cite_note-122]

## Incidents and accidents

### Kuwait Airways Flight 032

On 30 June 1966, Kuwait Airways Flight KU32, a [Hawker Siddeley Trident 1C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawker_Siddeley_Trident_1C), was operating a scheduled international flight from Beirut, Lebanon, to Kuwait City. The flight proceeded uneventfully until the descent phase. At 20\:28 GMT, the aircraft was cleared to descend from FL300 to FL95 near the DY NDB, maintaining this altitude until passing the beacon at 20\:39 GMT. The pilot-in-command elected to conduct a visual approach, and the crew reported sighting the airport’s rotating beacon and lights.[^cite_note-baaa-acro-123]

During the approach to Runway 33R, the aircraft was positioned six miles (9.7 km) from the airport at 2,300 feet (700 m) with an airspeed of 178 [knots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knot_\(unit\)) (330 km/h; 205 mph). The autopilot remained engaged, and the co-pilot was occupied with landing checks. The aircraft’s intended approach speed was 154 [knots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knot_\(unit\)) (285 km/h; 177 mph) with a (700 feet (210 m))/min descent rate, but it continued descending at (1,500 feet (460 m))/min. At 20\:46 GMT, the aircraft impacted terrain approximately four km (2.5 mi) short of the runway threshold, slightly left of the centerline, at an elevation of 185 feet (56 m) feet.[^cite_note-baaa-acro-123]

The accident resulted from an [unstable approach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unstable_approach) rate and failure to follow company regulations. The corrective action of calling for 10,500 rpm was insufficient to regain level flight, and a delayed power request was ineffective. The pilot-in-command did not follow approach procedures, with landing checks incomplete and an incorrect altimeter setting set during the incident, There were 72 passengers and 11 crew members on board with no deaths in the incident.[^cite_note-baaa-acro-123]

### Kuwait Airways during the Iraqi Invasion of Kuwait

Kuwait Airways suffered blows during the [Iraqi invasion of Kuwait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_invasion_of_Kuwait), at least 15 aircraft were seized by Iraq and taken to be used by [Iraqi Airways](179397.md). The [Revolutionary Command Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutionary_Command_Council_\(Iraq\)) decided that the Kuwait Airways company would be dissolved and all its property would be transported to the Iraqi Airways Company. This made Kuwait Airways file two legal actions against Iraqi Airways, these legal actions would take almost 22 years to end and were only settled in 2017. An A310-300 and A300 were seized by Iraqi forces, repainted in the Iraqi Airways livery, and given new registrations by the Iraqi Directorate of Air Safety. Iraqi Airways started to prep the 2 planes for commercial services by regularly maintaining them. In July and August 1992, Iran returned six Toulouse-built jets to Kuwait Airways after Kuwait paid them \$20 million for maintenance and storage. A pair of Tupolev Tu-124Vs, both G-IIIs, and one of the two BAe.125s were destroyed by coalition airstrikes during [Operation Desert Storm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Desert_Storm). These planes ended up being written-off. Additionally, there was a [Boeing 727](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_727) that was being used by Iraqi Airways before being ferried to Oman and later, returned to Kuwait after the war.[^cite_note-124]

After the war Kuwait Airways sought to seek compensation for the lost planes, at first, Kuwait was seeking \$1.2 billion. Iraq and Kuwait ended up settling on a deal that would see Iraq pay Kuwait \$500 million. \$300 million in cash and \$200 million in a Kuwaiti-Iraqi airline venture.[^cite_note-125]
