# Gasoline theft



A common method of gasoline theft involves destructively drilling a hole into a vehicle's fuel tank.

**Gasoline theft** is the crime of stealing [gasoline](23639.md). The theft can occur from a variety of sources, including vehicle fuel tanks, [filling station](61260.md) pumps, and industrial [pipelines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipeline_transport). The methods used by thieves range from traditional siphoning and destructive tank puncturing to sophisticated electronic manipulation of fuel pumps.

As a high-value commodity, gasoline is a frequent target for theft, which has a significant economic impact. In Greece, for example, annual losses from fuel theft are estimated to be around 120 million euros.[^cite_note-1] The crime is often correlated with fluctuations in fuel prices and can involve everyone from opportunistic individuals to large-scale organized crime rings. In Great Britain, police recorded a 77% surge in incidents of drivers leaving filling stations without paying for fuel in a single year, highlighting the growing scale of the problem.[^cite_note-2] For commercial vehicle fleets, preventing fuel theft is a major aspect of **[fleet management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fleet_management)**, leading to the widespread adoption of [telematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telematics) and other [fuel-management systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel-management_systems) to monitor and secure fuel supplies.

## From vehicle tanks

Theft from a vehicle's tank is a common form of gasoline theft, accomplished through various methods. For private vehicle owners, this often involves traditional siphoning or, more destructively, puncturing the fuel tank to drain the gasoline, which can cause significant and costly damage to the vehicle.[^cite_note-3]

For commercial fleets, where fuel is a major operational cost,[^cite_note-4] fuel theft is a significant operational concern managed through a combination of physical deterrents and advanced technology. Internal fraud, such as the misuse of company fuel cards or drivers siphoning fuel to sell, is also a concern.



A close-up of an aftermarket fuel level sensor, showing the long metallic probe and wiring connector.

Prevention and monitoring strategies are important components of modern [fleet management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fleet_management) and a key area of [fleet digitalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fleet_digitalization). A common technology used is [telematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telematics), which integrates a [GPS tracking unit](4105326.md) with various on-board sensors to provide real-time data on the vehicle's status.[^cite_note-5] These [fuel-management systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel-management_systems) can automatically monitor fuel levels using a [fuel level sensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_level_sensor?action=edit\&redlink=1) and are designed to detect theft by:

- Generating an alert if a sudden and significant drop in the fuel level occurs while the vehicle is stationary.

  [^cite_note-6]

- Comparing fuel card purchase data with the actual amount of fuel added to the tank.

- Identifying discrepancies between fuel consumed and distance traveled.

In addition to telematics, companies often employ physical security measures such as locking fuel caps and installing anti-siphoning devices that prevent a hose from being inserted into the tank. These technologies and practices help to reduce costs, prevent fraud, and improve the overall efficiency of fleet operations. While some thieves have turned to high-tech electronic devices to steal fuel, these are typically used to trick gas station pumps into dispensing fuel without payment, rather than to steal directly from vehicle tanks.[^cite_note-7]

## From filling stations

**Gasoline theft** (sometimes known colloquially as fill and fly, gas and dash, and **drive-off** in the US and [bilking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilking) in the UK) is the removal of [gasoline](23639.md) from a station without payment. The thief will usually use some form of decoy to prevent nearby witnesses from noticing the lack of payment until they have left the station. Common decoys include pretending to press the wrong buttons after the [credit card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credit_card) are swiped or having multiple people get gas at the same time, with one paying for another person and the other running off with both cars.

With typical gas thefts costing station owners in the range of \$50 per incident, many stores have fought back by installing better video equipment and requiring prepayment.

Since the [oil price](5137675.md) increases after 2004, a surge in fuel theft has occurred,[^cite_note-8] which has included license plate thefts (when gasoline is stolen with the vehicle having the original tags and the vehicle tags identifying the registered owner).

## From pipelines

Oil and gasoline are stolen by illegal taps in [petroleum pipelines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_pipeline), and billions of dollars' worth of product are stolen annually.[^cite_note-9] Targets are the [petroleum industry in Nigeria](3542988.md), the [Nembe Creek Trunk Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nembe_Creek_Trunk_Line#Oil_theft), the [Ceyhan-Kırıkkale Oil Pipeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceyhan-K%C4%B1r%C4%B1kkale_Oil_Pipeline#Oil_theft), and the [petroleum industry in Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_industry_in_Mexico#Oil_theft).

Organized crime and local people are often involved.[^cite_note-10]

Illegal taps have caused leaks and explosions such as the following:

- [2006 Abule Egba pipeline explosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_Abule_Egba_pipeline_explosion)

- [2006 Atlas Creek pipeline explosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_Atlas_Creek_pipeline_explosion)

- [2019 Tlahuelilpan pipeline explosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Tlahuelilpan_pipeline_explosion)
