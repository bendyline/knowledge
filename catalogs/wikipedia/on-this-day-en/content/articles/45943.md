# Swastika

For other uses, see [Swastika (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swastika_\(disambiguation\)).



The swastika is a symbol with many styles and meanings and has been used in many cultures and religions around the world for millennia.



The [appropriation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_appropriation) of the swastika by the [Nazi Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Party) (1920–1945) is the most recognisable modern usage of the symbol in the [Western world](21208200.md).

The **swastika** ([/ˈswɒstɪkə/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [*SWOS-tih-kə*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key), Sanskrit: [\[ˈsʋɐstikɐ\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Sanskrit), [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit) [Devanagari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devanagari): स्वस्तिक ; **卐** or **卍**) is an ancient cross-cultural geometrical symbol that has been used in many cultures and religions of [Eurasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasia), as well as a few in [Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_Africa) and the [Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas), for thousands of years. The swastika was and continues to be used as a symbol of divinity and spirituality in several [religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion), including [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism), [Buddhism](3267529.md), and [Jainism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism).[^cite_note-britswast-1][^cite_note-Sullivan2001p216-2][^cite_note-snodgrass82-3][^cite_note-Cort_2001_17-4][^cite_note-p.97-5] In the [Western world](21208200.md), it is predominantly associated with [Nazism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazism), being [appropriated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_appropriation) and widely displayed in [Nazi symbolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_symbolism). Most notably, it was used in the [flag of Nazi Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_Nazi_Germany). This appropriation continues with the symbol's popularity among [neo-Nazis](54361.md) around the world.[^cite_note-britswast-1][^cite_note-BBC_News_2014-6][^cite_note-Roden-7][^cite_note-Olson-8]

The English word *swastika* is originally of the [Sanskrit language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_language) ([स्वस्तिक](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E0%A4%B8%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%B5%E0%A4%B8%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%A4%E0%A4%BF%E0%A4%95#Sanskrit), lit. 'conducive to well-being').[^cite_note-britswast-1][^cite_note-etymology-9] In Hinduism, the right-facing symbol (卐) is called *swastika*, symbolizing *[surya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surya)*, prosperity, and good luck; while the left-facing symbol (卍) is sometimes called **_sauvastika_**, symbolising night or [tantric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tantra) aspects of [Kali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kali).[^cite_note-britswast-1] In [Jain symbolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_symbols), it is a part of the [Jain flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_flag),[^cite_note-jainqq.org-10] and represents [Suparshvanatha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suparshvanatha)—the seventh of 24 [Tirthankaras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirthankara) (spiritual teachers and saviours). In [Buddhist symbolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_symbolism), it represents the auspicious footprints of the [Buddha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddha).[^cite_note-britswast-1][^cite_note-silverblatt109-11][^cite_note-12] In different [Indo-European traditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European_culture), the swastika symbolises fire, lightning bolts and the Sun.[^cite_note-Greg-13] The symbol is found in the archaeological remains of the [Indus Valley Civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Valley_Civilization)[^cite_note-14] and the [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic)-era [Samarra culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samarra_culture) of [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia). It has also been recorded in early [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_art) and [Christian artwork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_art).[^cite_note-britswast-1][^cite_note-15]

The swastika was seen as [a symbol of auspiciousness and good luck for most of the Western world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_use_of_the_swastika_in_the_early_20th_century) until the 1930s.[^cite_note-BBC_News_2014-6] It was first used as a symbol of international [antisemitism](1078.md) by far-right Romanian politician [A. C. Cuza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._C._Cuza) prior to [World War I](4764461.md).[^cite_note-16][^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18] Wider public perception would only change once the German Nazi Party adopted the swastika as an emblem of the so-called [Aryan race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryan_race). As a result of [World War II](32927.md) and [the Holocaust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Holocaust), Western societies continue to strongly associate the symbol with [Nazism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazism), antisemitism,[^cite_note-holocaust2009-19][^cite_note-wiener463-20] [white supremacism](33178.md),[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-22] or simply [evil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evil).[^cite_note-23][^cite_note-24] As a consequence, displaying it is [prohibited by law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bans_on_Nazi_symbols) in several countries, with exceptions for religious symbolism. However, the swastika remains a symbol of good luck and prosperity in Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, and other Asian communities. It continues to be a peaceful cultural symbol in countries like [Sri Lanka](26750.md), [Mongolia](19271.md), [India](14533.md), [Japan](15573.md), [China](5405.md) and [Nepal](171166.md). It carries various other meanings for people around the world, such as the [Akan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akan_people), [Hopi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopi), [Navajo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navajo), and [Tlingit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tlingit) peoples.

## Etymology and nomenclature

The word *swastika* is derived from the Sanskrit root *swasti*, which is composed of *su* 'good, well' and *asti* 'is; it is; there is'.[^cite_note-Schliemann347-27] The word *swasti* occurs frequently in the [Vedas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas) as well as in classical literature, meaning 'health, luck, success, prosperity', and it was commonly used as a greeting.[^cite_note-M-W-28][^cite_note-29] The final *ka* is a common suffix that could have multiple meanings.[^cite_note-30]

According to the 19th-century Sanskrit scholar [Monier Monier-Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monier_Monier-Williams), most scholars consider the swastika to have originally been a [solar symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_symbol).[^cite_note-M-W-28] The sign implies well-being, something fortunate, lucky, or auspicious.[^cite_note-M-W-28][^cite_note-31] It is alternatively spelled in contemporary texts as *svastika*,[^cite_note-32] and other spellings were occasionally used in the 19th and early 20th century, such as *suastika*.[^cite_note-33] It was derived from the [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit) term ([Devanagari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devanagari) [स्वस्तिक](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E0%A4%B8%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%B5%E0%A4%B8%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%A4%E0%A4%BF%E0%A4%95)), which transliterates to *_svastika_* under the commonly used [IAST transliteration system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Alphabet_of_Sanskrit_Transliteration), but is pronounced closer to *swastika*.

The earliest known use of the word swastika is in [Pāṇini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C4%81%E1%B9%87ini)'s [Aṣṭādhyāyī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A%E1%B9%A3%E1%B9%AD%C4%81dhy%C4%81y%C4%AB), which uses it to explain a Sanskrit grammar rule, in the context of a type of identifying mark on a cow's ear.[^cite_note-Schliemann347-27] Pāṇini lived in or before the 4th century BCE,[^cite_note-34][^cite_note-35] possibly in 6th or 5th century BCE.[^cite_note-36][^cite_note-37]

An early use of *swastika* in a European text was in 1871, in the publications of [Heinrich Schliemann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Schliemann), who discovered more than 1,800 ancient instances of swastikas and variants while excavating the [Hisarlik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hisarlik) mound near the Aegean Sea coast to study the history of Troy. Schliemann linked his findings to the Sanskrit *swastika*.[^cite_note-boissoneaultsm-38][^cite_note-39][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMees200857–58-40]

By the 19th century, the term *swastika* was adopted into English,[^cite_note-41] replacing the previous *gammadion* from Greek γαμμάδιον. In 1878, the Irish scholar [Charles Graves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Graves_\(bishop\)) used *swastika* as the common English name for the symbol, after defining it as equivalent to the French term *croix gammée* – a cross with arms shaped like the Greek letter [gamma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma) (Γ).[^cite_note-42] Shortly thereafter, the British antiquarians [Edward Thomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Thomas_\(antiquarian\)) and [Robert Sewell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Sewell_\(historian\)) separately published their studies about the symbol, using *swastika* as the common English term.[^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44]

The "reversed" swastika was probably first conceived by [Eugène Burnouf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eug%C3%A8ne_Burnouf) among European scholars in 1852 and later taken up by [Schliemann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Schliemann) in *Ilios* (1880), based on a letter from [Max Müller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_M%C3%BCller) quoting Burnouf. The term *sauwastika* is used in the sense of 'backward swastika' by [Eugène Goblet d'Alviella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eug%C3%A8ne_Goblet_d'Alviella) (1894): "In India it \[the *gammadion*] bears the name of *swastika*, when its arms are bent towards the right, and *sauwastika* when they are turned in the other direction."[^cite_note-45]

Other names for the symbol include:

In various European languages, it is known as the *[fylfot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fylfot)*, *gammadion*, *tetraskelion*, or *cross cramponnée* (a term in Anglo-Norman [heraldry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraldry)); German: *Hakenkreuz*; French: *croix gammée*; Italian: *croce uncinata*; [Latvian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latvian_Language): *[ugunskrusts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ugunskrusts)*. In [Mongolian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongolian_Language) it is called хас (*khas*) and mainly used in seals. In [Balti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltistan)/[Tibetan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibetic_languages) language it is called *yung drung*.[^cite_note-49][^cite_note-50][^cite_note-51]

## Appearance





Left: the left-facing sauwastika is a sacred symbol in the [Bon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bon) and [Mahāyāna Buddhist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mah%C4%81y%C4%81na) traditions. Right: the right-facing swastika appears commonly in [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism), [Jainism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism) and [Sri Lankan Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sri_Lankan_Buddhism).

[^cite_note-John_Powers-52]

[^cite_note-53]

All swastikas are bent crosses based on a [chiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiral) symmetry, but they appear with different [geometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry) details: as compact crosses with short legs, as crosses with large arms, and as motifs in a pattern of unbroken lines. Chirality describes an absence of [reflective symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflective_symmetry), with the existence of two versions that are [mirror images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror_image) of each other. The mirror-image forms are typically described as left-facing or left-hand (卍) and right-facing or right-hand (卐).

The compact swastika can be seen as a chiral irregular [icosagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icosagon) (20-sided [polygon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygon)) with fourfold (90°) [rotational symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotational_symmetry). Such a swastika proportioned on a 5 × 5 square grid and with the broken portions of its legs shortened by one unit can [tile the plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tessellation) by [translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation_\(geometry\)) alone. The main Nazi flag swastika used a 5 × 5 diagonal grid, but with the legs unshortened.[^cite_note-54]

### Written characters





卍 and 卐 characters.

The swastika was adopted as a standard character in [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Written_Chinese), "[卍](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%8D%8D#Translingual)" ([pinyin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinyin): *wàn*) and as such entered various other [East Asian languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Asian_languages), including [Chinese script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_character). In Japanese, the symbol is called "[卍](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%8D%8D#Japanese)" ([Hepburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepburn_romanization): *manji*) or "[卍字](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%8D%8D%E5%AD%97#Japanese)" (*manji*).

The swastika is included in the [Unicode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode) character sets of two languages. In the Chinese block, it is U+534D [卍](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%8D%8D) (left-facing) and U+5350 for the swastika [卐](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%8D%90) (right-facing);[^cite_note-55] The latter has a mapping in the original [Big5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big5) character set,[^cite_note-56] but the former does not (although it is in Big5+[^cite_note-57]). In Unicode 5.2, two swastika symbols and two swastikas were added to the [Tibetan block](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibetan_\(Unicode_block\)): swastika U+0FD5 ࿕ RIGHT-FACING SVASTI SIGN, U+0FD7 ࿗ RIGHT-FACING SVASTI SIGN WITH DOTS, and swastikas U+0FD6 ࿖ LEFT-FACING SVASTI SIGN, U+0FD8 ࿘ LEFT-FACING SVASTI SIGN WITH DOTS.[^cite_note-58]

## Origin

European uses of swastikas are often treated alongside [cross symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_symbol) in general, such as the [sun cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_cross) of [Bronze Age religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prehistoric_religion). Beyond its certain presence in the "[proto-writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-writing)" symbol systems, such as the [Vinča script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vin%C4%8Da_script),[^cite_note-59] which appeared during the [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic),[^cite_note-60] nothing certain is known about the symbol's origin.

### North Pole



Approximate representation of the *Tiānmén* 天門 ('Gate of Heaven') or *Tiānshū* 天樞 ('Pivot of Heaven') as the processional north celestial pole, with [α Ursae Minoris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polaris) as the [pole star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_star), with the spinning [Chariot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Dipper) constellations in the four phases of time. *[Tiān](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ti%C4%81n)*, generally translated as 'heaven' in [Chinese theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_theology), refers to the northern [celestial pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_pole) (北極 *Běijí*), the pivot and the vault of the sky with its spinning constellations. The celestial pivot can be represented by *wàn* 卍 ('myriad things').

According to [René Guénon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ren%C3%A9_Gu%C3%A9non), the swastika represents the North Pole, and the rotational movement around a centre or immutable axis (*[axis mundi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_mundi)*), and only secondly it represents the [Sun](26751.md) as a reflected function of the North Pole. As such, it is a symbol of life, of the vivifying role of the supreme principle of the universe, the [absolute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_\(philosophy\)) God, in relation to the cosmic order. It represents the activity (the Hellenic *[Logos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos)*, the Hindu *[Om](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Om)*, the Chinese *[Taiyi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_theology)*, 'Great One') of the principle of the universe in the formation of the world.[^cite_note-61] According to Guénon, the swastika in its polar value has the same meaning of the [yin and yang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yin_and_yang) symbol of the Chinese tradition, and of other traditional symbols of the working of the universe, including the letters Γ ([gamma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_\(letter\))) and G, symbolising the [Great Architect of the Universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Architect_of_the_Universe) of [Masonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freemasonry) thought.[^cite_note-62][^cite_note-63]

According to the scholar Reza Assasi, the swastika represents the north [ecliptic North Pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecliptic_North_Pole?action=edit\&redlink=1) centred in [ζ Draconis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeta_Draconis), with the constellation [Draco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Draco_\(constellation\)) as one of its beams. He argues that this symbol was later attested as the four-horse chariot of [Mithra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mithra) in ancient Iranian culture. They believed the cosmos was pulled by four heavenly horses who revolved around a fixed centre in a clockwise direction. He suggests that this notion later flourished in Roman [Mithraism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mithraism), as the symbol appears in Mithraic iconography and astronomical representations.[^cite_note-64]

According to the Russian archaeologist [Gennady Zdanovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gennady_Zdanovich), who studied some of the oldest examples of the symbol in [Sintashta culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sintashta_culture), the swastika symbolises the universe, representing the spinning constellations of the [celestial north pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_north_pole) centred in [α Ursae Minoris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polaris), specifically the [Little](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Dipper) and [Big Dipper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Dipper) (or Chariots), or Ursa Minor and Ursa Major.[^cite_note-ru-sled.ru-65] Likewise, according to René Guénon-the swastika is drawn by visualising the Big Dipper/Great Bear in the four phases of revolution around the pole star.[^cite_note-66]

### Comet



Depiction of comets from the *[Book of Silk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Silk)*, [Han dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_dynasty), 2nd century BCE

In their 1985 book *[Comet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comet_\(book\))*, [Carl Sagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Sagan) and [Ann Druyan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ann_Druyan) argue that the appearance of a rotating [comet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comet) with a four-pronged tail as early as 2,000 years BCE could explain why the swastika is found in the cultures of both the [Old World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_World) and the [pre-Columbian Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Columbian_Americas). The [Han dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_dynasty) *[Book of Silk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Silk)* (2nd century BCE) depicts such a comet with a swastika-like symbol.[^cite_note-67]

Bob Kobres, in a 1992 paper, contends that the swastika-like comet on the Han-dynasty manuscript was labelled a "long-tailed pheasant star" (*dixing*) because it resembled a bird's foot or footprint.[^cite_note-kobres-68] Similar comparisons had been made by J. F. Hewitt in 1907,[^cite_note-69] as well as a 1908 article in *[Good Housekeeping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_Housekeeping)*.[^cite_note-70] Kobres goes on to suggest an association of mythological birds and comets also outside of China.[^cite_note-kobres-68]

### Four winds



[Pima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pima_people) symbol of the four winds

In [Native American culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_American_culture), particularly among the [Pima people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pima_people) of [Arizona](21883824.md), the swastika is a symbol of the four winds. Anthropologist [Frank Hamilton Cushing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Hamilton_Cushing) noted that among the Pima, the symbol of the four winds is made from a cross with the four curved arms (similar to a broken [sun cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_cross)) and concludes "the right-angle swastika is primarily a representation of the circle of the four wind gods standing at the head of their trails, or directions."[^cite_note-71]

## Historical uses

### Prehistory



Artifacts with swastika patterns found in [Mezine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mezine)

The earliest known swastikas are from 10,000 to 17,000 BCE – part of "an intricate meander pattern of joined-up swastikas" found on a late [Paleolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleolithic) figurine of a bird, carved from [mammoth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mammoth) ivory, found in [Mezine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mezine), Ukraine.[^cite_note-72][^cite_note-73] It has been suggested that this swastika may be a stylised picture of a [stork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stork) in flight.[^cite_note-74] As the carving was found near [phallic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phallus) objects, this may also support the idea that the pattern was a fertility symbol.[^cite_note-BBC_News_2014-6]

In the mountains of [Iran](14653.md), there are swastikas or spinning wheels inscribed on stone walls, which are estimated to be more than 7,000 years old. One instance is in Khorashad, [Birjand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birjand), on the holy wall Lakh Mazar.[^cite_note-75][^cite_note-76]

Mirror-image swastikas (clockwise and counter-clockwise) have been found on ceramic pottery in the [Devetashka cave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devetashka_cave), Bulgaria, dated to 6,000 BCE.[^cite_note-77]

In South Asia, swastika symbols first appear in the archaeological record around[^cite_note-Kathleen_M._Nadeau_2010_87-78] 3000 BCE in the Indus Valley Civilisation.[^cite_note-Heller_2008-79][^cite_note-80] It also appears in the [Bronze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age) and [Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age) cultures around the [Black Sea](3386.md) and the [Caspian Sea](19653787.md). In all these cultures, swastika symbols do not appear to occupy any marked position or significance, appearing as just one form of a series of similar symbols of varying complexity. In the [Zoroastrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism) religion of [Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Persia), the swastika was a symbol of the revolving sun, infinity, or continuing creation.[^cite_note-81][^cite_note-82] It is one of the most common symbols on [Mesopotamian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia) coins.[^cite_note-britswast-1] In England, Neolithic or Bronze Age stone carvings of the symbol have been found on [Ilkley Moor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilkley_Moor), such as the [Swastika Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swastika_Stone).[^cite_note-83]

Swastikas have also been found on pottery in archaeological digs in Africa, in the area of [Kush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kush) and on pottery at the [Jebel Barkal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jebel_Barkal) temples,[^cite_note-84] in [Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age) designs of the northern [Caucasus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caucasus) ([Koban culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koban_culture)), and in [Neolithic China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic_China) in the [Majiayao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majiayao_culture) culture.[^cite_note-GW-85]

Swastikas are also seen in Egypt during the Coptic period. Textile number T.231-1923 held at the V\&A Museum in London includes small swastikas in its design. This piece was found at Qau-el-Kebir, near [Asyut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asyut), and is dated between 300 and 600 CE.[^cite_note-86]

The *Tierwirbel* (the German for "animal whorl" or "whirl of animals"[^cite_note-87]) is a characteristic motif in Bronze Age Central Asia, the [Eurasian Steppe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasian_Steppe), and later also in Iron Age [Scythian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scythian) and [European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age_Europe) ([Baltic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltic_peoples)[^cite_note-88] and [Germanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_peoples)) culture, showing rotational symmetric arrangement of an [animal motif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_style), often four birds' heads. Even wider diffusion of this "Asiatic" theme has been proposed to the Pacific and even North America (especially [Moundville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moundville_Archaeological_Site)).[^cite_note-89]

#### Caucasus



Armenian *[arevakhach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arevakhach)*

In Armenia, the swastika is called the "[arevakhach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arevakhach)" and "kerkhach" ([Armenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_language): կեռխաչ)[^cite_note-Concise_Armenian_Encyclopedia_p._663-92] and is the ancient symbol of eternity and eternal light (i.e., God). Swastikas in Armenia were found on Copper Age petroglyphs, predating the Bronze Age. During the Bronze Age, it was depicted on [cauldrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauldron), belts, [medallions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medallion), and other items.[^cite_note-93]

&#x20;Swastikas can also be seen on early Medieval churches and fortresses, including the principal tower in Armenia's historical capital city of [Ani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ani).[^cite_note-Concise_Armenian_Encyclopedia_p._663-92] The same symbol can be found on [Armenian carpets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_carpet), cross-stones (*[khachkar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khachkar)*), and in medieval manuscripts, as well as on modern monuments as a [symbol of eternity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_eternity_sign).[^cite_note-94]

Old [petroglyphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroglyph) of four-beam and other swastikas were recorded in [Dagestan](751099.md), in particular, among the [Avars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avars_\(Caucasus\)).[^cite_note-Debirov-95] According to [Vakhushti of Kartli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vakhushti_of_Kartli), the tribal banner of the [Avar khans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avar_Khanate) depicted a wolf with a standard with a double-spiral swastika.[^cite_note-96]

Petroglyphs with swastikas were depicted on medieval [Vainakh tower architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chechen_tower_architecture) (see sketches by scholar Bruno Plaetschke from the 1920s).[^cite_note-Plaetschke-97] Thus, a rectangular swastika was made in engraved form on the entrance of a residential tower in the settlement [Khimoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khimoy), [Chechnya](6095.md).[^cite_note-Plaetschke-97]

#### Europe

See also: [Swastika (Germanic Iron Age)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swastika_\(Germanic_Iron_Age\))

Iron Age attestations of swastikas can be associated with [Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-Europeans) cultures such as the [Illyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illyrians),[^cite_note-Stipčević-99] [Indo-Iranians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Iranians), [Celts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celt), [Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greeks), [Italics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italic_peoples), [Germanic peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swastika_\(Germanic_Iron_Age\)) and [Slavs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavs). In [Sintashta culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sintashta_culture)'s "[Country of Towns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Country_of_Towns)", ancient [Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-Europeans) settlements in southern Russia, it has been found a great concentration of some of the oldest swastika patterns.[^cite_note-ru-sled.ru-65]

Swastika shapes have been found on numerous artefacts from [Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age) Europe.[^cite_note-Concise_Armenian_Encyclopedia_p._663-92][^cite_note-100][^cite_note-101][^cite_note-Stipčević-99][^cite_note-MigSym-46]

The swastika shape appears on various Germanic [Migration Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Migration_Period) and [Viking Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viking_Age) artifacts, such as the 3rd-century [Værløse Fibula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%A6rl%C3%B8se_Fibula) from Zealand, Denmark, the [Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goths) spearhead from [Brest-Litovsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brest-Litovsk), today in Belarus, the 9th-century [Snoldelev Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snoldelev_Stone) from [Ramsø](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rams%C3%B8), Denmark, and numerous Migration Period [bracteates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bracteate) drawn left-facing or right-facing.[^cite_note-OLDTIDENS-102]

The [pagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_paganism) [Anglo-Saxon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Saxons) [ship burial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship_burial) at [Sutton Hoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sutton_Hoo), England, contained numerous items bearing swastikas, now housed in the collection of the [Cambridge Museum of Archaeology and Anthropology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Archaeology_and_Anthropology,_University_of_Cambridge).[^cite_note-DAVIDSON83-103] A swastika is clearly marked on a hilt and sword belt found at [Bifrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bifrons) in Kent, in a grave of about the 6th century.

[Hilda Ellis Davidson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilda_Ellis_Davidson) hypothesised that the swastika symbol was associated with [Thor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thor), possibly representing his [Mjolnir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mjolnir) – symbolic of thunder – and possibly being connected to the Bronze Age sun cross.[^cite_note-DAVIDSON83-103] Davidson cites "many examples" of swastika symbols from Anglo-Saxon graves of the pagan period, with particular prominence on cremation urns from the cemeteries of East Anglia.[^cite_note-DAVIDSON83-103] Some of the swastikas on the items, on display at the Cambridge Museum of Archaeology and Anthropology, are depicted with such care and art that, according to Davidson, they must have possessed special significance as a [funerary symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funerary_art).[^cite_note-DAVIDSON83-103] The [runic inscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runic_inscription) on the 8th-century [Sæbø sword](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%A6b%C3%B8_sword) has been taken as evidence of the swastika as a symbol of Thor in [Norse paganism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norse_paganism).

The bronze frontispiece of a ritual pre-Christian (c. 350–50 BCE) shield found in the [River Thames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Thames) near [Battersea Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battersea_Bridge) (hence "[Battersea Shield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battersea_Shield)") is embossed with 27 swastikas in bronze and red enamel.[^cite_note-104] An [Ogham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ogham) stone found in Aglish, [County Kerry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Kerry), Ireland ([CIIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CIIC) 141) was modified into an early Christian gravestone, and was decorated with a [cross pattée](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_patt%C3%A9e) and two swastikas.[^cite_note-105] The [Book of Kells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Kells) (c. 800 CE) contains swastika-shaped ornamentation. Some swastikas have been found embossed in [Galician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galicia_\(Spain\)) metalwork and carved in stone, mostly from the [Castro culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castro_culture) period, although there are also contemporary examples (imitating old patterns for decorative purposes).[^cite_note-106][^cite_note-107]



*[Ugunskrusts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ugunskrusts)* motif

The ancient [Baltic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balts) thunder cross symbol (*pērkona krusts* or *perkūno kryžius* (cross of [Perkūnas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perk%C5%ABnas)); also fire cross, *[ugunskrusts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ugunskrusts)*) is a swastika symbol used to decorate objects, traditional clothing, and in [archaeological excavations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excavation_\(archaeology\)).[^cite_note-Guénon-2001-108][^cite_note-109]

In [Lithuania](17675.md), since [ancient times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Lithuania), the swastika—found on objects crafted from antler, wood, metal, and clay—has served as a significant cultural and religious emblem deeply rooted in Baltic tradition among the [Lithuanians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuanians). The researchers of [Klaipėda University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klaip%C4%97da_University) discovered that there was no standardized or canonical form of the symbol: on single-sided artifacts, the swastika’s arms could rotate either clockwise or counterclockwise, whereas two-sided items might display both orientations simultaneously, suggesting an inclusive or multifaceted symbolic intention. Importantly, the contexts in which the swastika appears are often linked to two deities in [Lithuanian mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuanian_mythology): Perkūnas, the god of thunder, and [Kalvelis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalvis), the blacksmith. This association reinforces the concept of the swastika as a manifestation of the “fire cross”—an equilateral cross symbolizing fire or thunder—an enduring motif within Baltic and ancient Lithuanian religious iconography.[^cite_note-110]

According to painter Stanisław Jakubowski, the "little sun" (Polish: *słoneczko*) is an [Early Slavic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Slavs) pagan symbol of the Sun; he claimed it was engraved on wooden monuments built near the final resting places of fallen Slavs to represent eternal life. The symbol was first seen in his collection of Early Slavic symbols and architectural features, which he named *Prasłowiańskie motywy architektoniczne* (Polish: *Early Slavic Architectural Motifs*). His work was published in 1923.[^cite_note-Old_Slavic_Symbols-111]

The [Boreyko coat of arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boreyko_coat_of_arms) with a red swastika was used by several noble families in the [Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Lithuanian_Commonwealth).[^cite_note-112]

According to [Boris Kuftin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boris_Kuftin), the [Russians](59510.md) often used swastikas as a decorative element and as the basis of ornamentation on traditional woven products.[^cite_note-Kuftin-113] Many can be seen on women's folk costumes from the [Meshchera Lowlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meshchera_Lowlands).[^cite_note-Kuftin-113]

According to some authors, Russian names popularly associated with the swastika include *veterok* ("breeze"),[^cite_note-echoMsk-114] *ognevtsi* ("little flames"), "geese", "hares" (a towel with a swastika was called a towel with "hares"), or "little horses".[^cite_note-Bogdasarov2002-115] The similar word "*koleso*" ("wheel") was used for rosette-shaped amulets, such as a [hexafoil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexafoil)-thunder wheel ) in folklore, particularly in the [Russian North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_North).[^cite_note-Ivanits-116][^cite_note-Garshol-117]

An object very much like a hammer or a double axe is depicted among the magical symbols on the drums of [Sami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sami_people) *[noaidi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noaidi)*, used in their religious ceremonies before Christianity was established. The name of the Sami thunder god was [Horagalles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horagalles), thought to derive from "Old Man Thor" (*Þórr karl*). Sometimes on the drums, a male figure with a hammer-like object in either hand is shown, and sometimes it is more like a cross with crooked ends, or a swastika.[^cite_note-DAVIDSON83-103]

### Southern and eastern Asia

The icon has been of spiritual significance to Indian religions such as Hinduism, Buddhism, and Jainism.[^cite_note-p.97-5][^cite_note-britswast-1] The swastika is a sacred symbol in the [Bön](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%B6n) religion, native to [Tibet](31516.md).

#### Hinduism

Hindu swastikas

The swastika is an important Hindu symbol.[^cite_note-britswast-1][^cite_note-p.97-5] The swastika symbol is commonly used before entrances or on doorways of homes or temples, to mark the starting page of financial statements, and [mandalas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala) constructed for rituals such as weddings or welcoming a newborn.[^cite_note-britswast-1][^cite_note-Lander2013p27-119]

The swastika has a particular association with [Diwali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diwali), being drawn in *[rangoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rangoli)* (coloured sand) or formed with [deepak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deepak) lights on the floor outside Hindu houses and on wall hangings and other decorations.[^cite_note-120]

In the diverse traditions within Hinduism, both clockwise and counterclockwise swastikas are found, each with different meanings. The counter-clockwise or left hand symbol is sometimes called *sauwastika* or *sauvastika*.[^cite_note-britswast-1] The clockwise swastika is a solar symbol ([Surya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surya)), suggesting the motion of the Sun in India (the northern hemisphere), where it appears to enter from the east, then ascend to the south at midday, exiting to the west.[^cite_note-britswast-1] A counter-clockwise swastika is less used; it connotes the night, and in tantric traditions it is an icon for the goddess [Kali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kali), the terrifying form of [Devi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devi) [Durga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durga).[^cite_note-britswast-1] The symbol also represents activity, karma, motion, wheel, and in some contexts the lotus.[^cite_note-Sullivan2001p216-2][^cite_note-snodgrass82-3] According to Norman McClelland, its symbolism for motion and the Sun may be from shared prehistoric cultural roots.[^cite_note-121]

#### Buddhism



Swastika at the Main Hall of [Zenkō-ji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zenk%C5%8D-ji) Buddhist temple (Japan)

In [Buddhism](3267529.md), the swastika is considered to symbolise the auspicious footprints of the Buddha.[^cite_note-britswast-1][^cite_note-silverblatt109-11] A left-facing swastika is often imprinted on the chest, feet, or palms of [Buddha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddha) images. It is an iconic symbol for the Buddha in many parts of Asia and homologous with the [*dharma wheel*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharmachakra).[^cite_note-snodgrass82-3] The shape symbolises eternal cycling, a theme found in the *[samsara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sa%E1%B9%83s%C4%81ra_\(Buddhism\))* doctrine of Buddhism.[^cite_note-snodgrass82-3]

The swastika symbol is common in [esoteric tantric traditions of Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esoteric_Buddhism), as well as in Hinduism, where it is associated with [chakra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chakra) theories and other meditative aids.[^cite_note-Lander2013p27-119] The clockwise symbol is more common, and contrasts with the counter-clockwise version common in the Tibetan [Bon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bon) tradition and locally called *yungdrung*.[^cite_note-122]

In East Asia, the swastika is prevalent in Buddhist monasteries and communities. It is commonly found in Buddhist temples, religious artifacts, Buddhist texts, and schools founded by Buddhist groups. It also appears as a design or motif (either singularly or woven into a pattern) on textiles, architecture, and various decorative objects, symbolizing luck and good fortune. The icon is also found as a sacred symbol in the Bon tradition, but in the left-facing orientation.[^cite_note-John_Powers-52][^cite_note-lee86-123]

#### Jainism



Jain symbol (*Prateek*) containing a swastika



The official [Jain flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_flag) with swastika; its four hands representing the four possible reincarnations of [soul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C4%ABva_\(Jainism\)), including heaven, hell, human, and plant or animal.

[^cite_note-jainqq.org-10]

&#x20;In [Jainism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism), it is a symbol of the seventh *[tīrthaṅkara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirthankara)*, [Suparśvanātha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suparshvanatha).[^cite_note-britswast-1] In the [Śvētāmbara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9Av%C4%93t%C4%81mbara) tradition, it is also one of the *[aṣṭamaṅgala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashtamangala)* or eight auspicious symbols. All [Jain temples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_temple), and holy books must contain the swastika, and ceremonies typically begin and end with creating a swastika mark several times with rice around the altar. Jains use rice to make a swastika in front of statues and then put an offering on it, usually a ripe or dried fruit, a sweet ([Hindi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindi_language): मिठाई *miṭhāī*), or a coin or currency note. The four arms of the swastika symbolise the four places where a soul could be reborn in *[samsara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sa%E1%B9%83s%C4%81ra_\(Jainism\))*, the cycle of birth and death – *[svarga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Svarga)* "heaven", *[naraka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naraka_\(Jainism\))* "hell", *manushya* "humanity" or *tiryancha* "as flora or fauna" – before the soul attains *[moksha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moksha_\(Jainism\))* "salvation" as a *[siddha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siddha#Siddha_in_Jainism)*, having ended the cycle of birth and death and become [omniscient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omniscience).[^cite_note-Cort_2001_17-4]

#### Prevalence in southern Asia

In Bhutan, India, Nepal, and Sri Lanka, the swastika is common. Temples, businesses and other organisations, such as the Buddhist libraries, [Ahmedabad Stock Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmedabad_Stock_Exchange) and the Nepal Chamber of Commerce,[^cite_note-124] use the swastika in reliefs or logos.[^cite_note-lee86-123] Swastikas are ubiquitous in Indian and Nepalese communities, located on shops, buildings, transport vehicles, and clothing. The swastika remains prominent in Hindu ceremonies such as weddings. A left-facing swastika symbol is found in tantric rituals.[^cite_note-britswast-1]

[Musaeus College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musaeus_College) in Colombo, Sri Lanka, a Buddhist girls' school, has a left-facing swastika in its school logo.

&#x20;In India, *Swastik* and *Swastika*, with their spelling variants, are first names for males and females, respectively, for instance, [Swastika Mukherjee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swastika_Mukherjee). The [Emblem of Bihar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emblem_of_Bihar) contains two swastikas.

In Bhutan, swastika motifs are found in architecture, fabrics, and religious ceremonies.

Among the predominantly Hindu population of [Bali](4147.md), in Indonesia, swastikas are common in temples, homes, and public spaces. Similarly, the swastika is a common icon associated with Buddha's footprints in Theravada Buddhist communities of Myanmar, Thailand, and Cambodia.[^cite_note-lee86-123]

The [Tantra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tantra)-based new religious movement [Ananda Marga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ananda_Marga) (Devanagari: आनन्द मार्ग, meaning 'Path of Bliss') uses a motif similar to the Raëlians, but in their case, the apparent star of David is defined as intersecting triangles with no specific reference to Jewish culture.

#### Spread to eastern Asia

Main articles: [Chinese auspicious ornaments in textile and clothing § Wan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_auspicious_ornaments_in_textile_and_clothing#Wan); and [List of Chinese symbols, designs, and art motifs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Chinese_symbols,_designs,_and_art_motifs)

The swastika is an auspicious symbol in China where it was introduced from India with [Buddhism](3267529.md).[^cite_note-pacificasiamuseum-125] In 693, during the [Tang dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tang_dynasty), it was declared as "the source of all good fortune" and was called *wan* by [Wu Zetian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wu_Zetian) becoming a Chinese word.[^cite_note-pacificasiamuseum-125] The Chinese character for *wan* (pinyin: *wàn*) is similar to a swastika in shape and has two different variations:《卐》and 《卍》. As the Chinese character *wan* (卐 or 卍) is homonym for the Chinese word of "ten thousand" (万) and "infinity", as such the Chinese character is itself a symbol of immortality[^cite_note-126] and infinity.[^cite_note-Dusenberry-2004-127]<sup>: 175 </sup> It was also a representation of [longevity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longevity).[^cite_note-Dusenberry-2004-127]<sup>: 175 </sup>

The Chinese character *wan* could be used as a stand-alone《卐》or《卍》or as be used as pairs《卐 卍》in Chinese visual arts, decorative arts, and clothing due to its auspicious connotation.[^cite_note-Dusenberry-2004-127]<sup>: 175 </sup>

Adding the character *wan* (卐 or 卍) to other auspicious Chinese symbols or patterns can multiply that wish by 10,000 times.[^cite_note-pacificasiamuseum-125][^cite_note-Dusenberry-2004-127]<sup>: 175 </sup> It can be combined with other Chinese characters, such as the Chinese character *[shou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shou_\(character\))*《壽》for longevity where it is sometimes even integrated into the Chinese character *shou* to augment the meaning of longevity.[^cite_note-Dusenberry-2004-127]<sup>: 175 </sup>

The paired swastika symbols (卐 and 卍) are included, at least since the [Liao Dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liao_Dynasty) (907–1125 CE), as part of the [Chinese writing system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_character) and are [variant characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variant_Chinese_characters) for 《萬》 or 《万》 (*wàn* in Mandarin, 《만》(*man*) in Korean, Cantonese, and Japanese, *vạn* in Vietnamese) meaning "[myriad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myriad)".[^cite_note-128]

The character *wan* can also be stylized in the form of the *[xiangyun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xiangyun_\(Auspicious_clouds\))*, Chinese auspicious clouds.



The [mon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mon_\(emblem\)) (family crest) of the [Hachisuka clan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hachisuka_clan)

When the Chinese writing system was introduced to Japan in the 8th century, the swastika was adopted into the Japanese language and culture. It is commonly referred to as the *manji* (lit. '10,000-character'). Since the Middle Ages, it has been used as a *[mon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mon_\(emblem\))* by various Japanese families, such as the [Tsugaru clan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsugaru_clan), the [Hachisuka clan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hachisuka_clan), and around 60 clans that belong to the [Tokugawa clan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokugawa_clan).[^cite_note-129] The city of [Hirosaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hirosaki) in [Aomori Prefecture](61732.md) designates this symbol as its official flag, which stemmed from its use in the emblem of the [Tsugaru clan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsugaru_clan), the lords of [Hirosaki Domain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hirosaki_Domain) during the [Edo period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo_period).

In Japan, the swastika is also used as a [map symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Japanese_map_symbols) and is designated by the Survey Act and related Japanese governmental rules to denote a [Buddhist temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_temple).[^cite_note-130] Japan has considered changing this due to occasional controversy and misunderstanding by foreigners.[^cite_note-telegraph290721-131] The symbol is sometimes censored in international versions of Japanese works, such as anime.[^cite_note-CBR-2021-132] Censorship of this symbol in Japan and in Japanese media abroad has been subject to occasional controversy related to freedom of speech, with critics of the censorship arguing it does not respect history nor freedom of speech.[^cite_note-telegraph290721-131][^cite_note-CBR-2021-132]



Sayagata pattern

In [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_art) and [Japanese art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_art), swastikas are often found as part of a repeating pattern. One common pattern, called *sayagata* in Japanese, comprises left- and right-facing swastikas joined by lines.[^cite_note-133] As the negative space between the lines has a distinctive shape, the sayagata pattern is sometimes called the *key fret* motif in English.

Many [Chinese religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_folk_religion) make use of swastika symbols, including [Guiyidao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guiyidao) and [Shanrendao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanrendao). The [Red Swastika Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Swastika_Society), formed in China in 1922 as the philanthropic branch of Guiyidao, became the largest supplier of emergency relief in China during [World War II](32927.md), in the same manner as the [Red Cross](15487.md) in the rest of the world. The Red Swastika Society abandoned mainland China in 1954, settling first in Hong Kong and then in Taiwan. They continue to use the red swastika as their symbol.[^cite_note-134]

The [Falun Gong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falun_Gong) [qigong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qigong) movement, founded in China in the early 1990s, uses a symbol that features a large swastika surrounded by four smaller (and rounded) ones, interspersed with [yin-and-yang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yin_and_yang) symbols.[^cite_note-135]

### Islamic usage



Spandrels of Chashmai Ayyub,[Vobkent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vobkent)

The swastika was used in pre-Islamic lands and continued to be used into the Islamic era. Some of its earliest documented uses date back to the Palaces of the [Umayyad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Caliphate) Caliphs [Walid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khirbet_Minya) (705–715) and [Hisham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hisham's_Palace) (724–743). It would subsequently under the [Abbasids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Caliphate) on the gates of [Raqqa](858965.md), in [Muslim Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_Spain) at [Cordoba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C%C3%B3rdoba,_Spain) and particularly in [Persianate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persianate_society) world (Anatolia to India) from the [Seljuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seljuk_Empire) period (1037–1194) onwards. It was also used on textiles and metalwork.[^cite_note-136] Its use on mausoleums, such as the [Gonbad-e-Sorkh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonbad-e_Sorkh,_Maragheh) (1147), seems to have been used to symbolize the soul and resurrection.[^cite_note-137][^cite_note-138] Its use on gateways may have been used as an ancient sun symbol, symbolising the solstice as the gateway of heaven.[^cite_note-139]



Four Muhammad/Ali motifs



Motif in [Al-Rukniyah Madrasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Rukniyah_Madrasa)

In the 13th century [Mamluk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamluk_Sultanate) lands, a complex [square Kufic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_Kufic) design known as the 'Four Muhammad/Ali' became prominent; featuring a fourfold repetition of 'Muhammad' which can simultaneously be read as 'Ali' in the interstices, forming a swastika in the centre. The motif became very popular, spreading widely throughout [Ottoman](22278.md) lands and to Iran.[^cite_note-140]

### Classical Europe



Various [meander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meander_\(art\)) patterns, a.k.a. *Greek keys*

[Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) architectural, clothing, and coin designs are replete with single or interlinking swastika motifs. There are also gold-plate [fibulae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibula_\(brooch\)) from the 8th century BCE, decorated with an engraved swastika.[^cite_note-141] Related symbols in classical Western architecture include the cross, the three-legged triskele or [triskelion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triskelion), and the rounded [lauburu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lauburu). The swastika symbol is also known in these contexts by several names, especially *gammadion*,[^cite_note-142] or rather the tetra-gammadion. The name *gammadion* comes from its being seen as being made up of four Greek [gamma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma) (Γ) letters. Ancient Greek architectural designs are replete with the interlinking symbol.

In [Greco-Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_in_Ancient_Greece) art and architecture, and in [Romanesque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanesque_architecture) and [Gothic art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_art) in the West, isolated swastikas are relatively rare, and the swastika is more commonly found as a repeated element in a border or tessellation. Swastikas often represented perpetual motion, reflecting the design of a rotating windmill or watermill. A meander of connected swastikas makes up the large band that surrounds the [Augustan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus) [Ara Pacis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ara_Pacis).

A design of interlocking swastikas is one of several [tessellations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tessellation) on the floor of the [cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amiens_Cathedral) of [Amiens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amiens), France.[^cite_note-143] A border of linked swastikas was a common Roman architectural motif,[^cite_note-144] and can be seen in more recent buildings as a neoclassical element. A swastika border is one form of [meander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meander_\(art\)), and the individual swastikas in such a border are sometimes called [*Greek keys*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meander_\(art\)). There have also been swastikas found on the floors of [Pompeii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompeii).[^cite_note-145]

Swastikas were widespread among the [Illyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illyrians), symbolising the Sun and the fire. The swastika has been preserved by the [Albanians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albanians) since Illyrian times as a [pagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albanian_paganism) symbol commonly found in a variety of contexts of Albanian folk art, including [traditional tattooing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albanian_traditional_tattooing), grave art, jewellery, clothes, and house carvings. The swastika ([Albanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albanian_language): *kryqi grepç* or *kryqi i thyer*, "hooked cross") and other crosses in Albanian tradition represent the Sun ([Dielli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dielli_\(Albanian_paganism\))) and the fire ([zjarri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zjarri_\(Albanian_paganism\)), evidently called with the theonym [Enji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enji_\(deity\))). In [Albanian paganism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albanian_paganism), fire is regarded as the offspring of the Sun and fire calendar rituals are practiced to give strength to the Sun and to [ward off evil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apotropaic).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETreimer197132-148][^cite_note-FOOTNOTETirta200469–70,_75,_80,_113,_116,_250-149]

### Medieval and early modern Europe

#### Middle Ages

In Christianity, the swastika is used as a hooked version of the [Christian Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Cross), the symbol of Christ's victory over death. Some Christian churches built in the [Romanesque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanesque_architecture) and [Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_architecture) eras are decorated with swastikas, carrying over earlier Roman designs. Swastikas are prominently displayed in a [mosaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosaic) in the [Saint Sophia Cathedral, Kyiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Sophia_Cathedral,_Kyiv), dating from the 12th century. They also appear as a repeating ornamental motif on the so-called [Sarcophagus of Stilicho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarcophagus_of_Stilicho) in the [Basilica of Sant'Ambrogio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_of_Sant'Ambrogio) in [Milan](36511.md).[^cite_note-150]

A ceiling painted in 1910 in the [Grenoble Archaeological Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grenoble_Archaeological_Museum) (the former church of St Laurent) has many swastikas. A proposed direct link between it and a swastika floor mosaic in the [Amiens Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amiens_Cathedral), which was built on top of a pagan site at [Amiens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amiens), France, in the 13th century, is considered unlikely. The [stole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stole_\(vestment\)) worn by a priest in the 1445 [painting of the Seven Sacraments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Sacraments_Altarpiece) by [Rogier van der Weyden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogier_van_der_Weyden) presents the swastika form simply as one way of depicting the cross.

Swastikas also appear in art and architecture during the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance) and [Baroque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baroque) era. The fresco *[The School of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_School_of_Athens)* shows an ornament made out of swastikas, and the symbol can also be found on the facade of the *Santa Maria della Salute*, a Roman Catholic church and minor basilica located at [Punta della Dogana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punta_della_Dogana) in the [Dorsoduro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorsoduro) [sestiere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sestiere_\(Venice\)) of the city of [Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venice).

In the [Polish First Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Lithuanian_Commonwealth), swastika symbols were also popular with the nobility. Several noble houses, e.g., Boreyko, Borzym, and Radziechowski from Ruthenia, also had swastikas as their [coat of arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coat_of_arms). The family reached its height in the 14th and 15th centuries, and its crest appears in many heraldic books from that period.

The swastika was also a heraldic symbol, for example, on the [Boreyko coat of arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boreyko_coat_of_arms), used by noblemen in Poland and Ukraine. In the 19th century, a swastika was one of the Russian Empire's symbols and was used on coinage as a backdrop to the [Russian eagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coat_of_arms_of_Russia#1721%E2%80%931917:_Russian_Empire).[^cite_note-ruskolan.xpomo.com-151]

#### Rediscovery by Heinrich Schliemann

At [Troy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troy) near the [Dardanelles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dardanelles), [Heinrich Schliemann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Schliemann)'s 1871–1875 [archaeological excavations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeological_excavations) discovered objects decorated with swastikas.[^cite_note-Schliemann_1875-152]<sup>: 101–105 </sup>[^cite_note-Boxer-2000-153][^cite_note-Heller-2000-154]<sup>: 31 </sup>[^cite_note-Quinn-1994-155]<sup>: 31 </sup> Hearing of this, the director of the [French School at Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_School_at_Athens), [Émile-Louis Burnouf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89mile-Louis_Burnouf), wrote to Schliemann in 1872, stating "the Swastika should be regarded as a sign of the [Aryan race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryan_race)". Burnouf told Schliemann that "It should also be noted that the Jews have completely rejected it".[^cite_note-Gere-2006-156]<sup>: 89 </sup> Accordingly, Schliemann believed the Trojans to have been Aryans: "The primitive Trojans, therefore, belonged to the Aryan race, which is further sufficiently proved by the symbols on the round terra-cottas".[^cite_note-Schliemann_1875-152]<sup>: 157 </sup>[^cite_note-Gere-2006-156]<sup>: 90 </sup> Schliemann accepted Burnouf's interpretation.[^cite_note-Gere-2006-156]<sup>: 89 </sup>

> This winter, I have read in Athens many excellent works of celebrated scholars on Indian antiquities, especially [Adalbert Kuhn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adalbert_Kuhn), *Die Herakunft des Feuers*; [Max Müller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_M%C3%BCller)'s *Essays*; [Émile Burnouf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89mile_Burnouf), *La Science des Religions* and *Essai sur le Vêda*; as well as several works by [Eugène Burnouf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eug%C3%A8ne_Burnouf); and I now perceive that these crosses upon the Trojan terra-cottas are of the highest importance to archæology.

— Heinrich Schliemann, *Troy and Its Remains*, 1875[^cite_note-Schliemann_1875-152]<sup>: 101 </sup>

Schliemann believed that the use of swastikas spread widely across Eurasia.[^cite_note-157]

> ... I am now able to prove that ... the 卍, which I find in Émile Burnouf's Sanscrit lexicon, under the name of "*suastika*," and with the meaning εὖ ἐστι, or as the sign of good wishes, were already regarded, thousands of years before Christ, as religious symbols of the very greatest importance among the early progenitors of the Aryan races in [Bactria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bactria) and in the villages of the [Oxus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxus), at a time when [Germans](152735.md), Indians, [Pelasgians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelasgians), [Celts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celts), [Persians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persians), [Slavonians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavonians) and [Iranians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_peoples) still formed one nation and spoke one language.

— Heinrich Schliemann, *Troy and Its Remains*, 1875[^cite_note-Schliemann_1875-152]<sup>: 101–102 </sup>

Schliemann established a link between the swastika and Germany. He connected objects he excavated at Troy to objects bearing swastikas found in Germany near [Königswalde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6nigswalde) on the [Oder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oder).[^cite_note-Boxer-2000-153][^cite_note-Heller-2000-154]<sup>: 31 </sup>[^cite_note-Quinn-1994-155]<sup>: 31 </sup>[^cite_note-Gere-2006-156]<sup>: 90 </sup>



[Lithograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithograph) of [potsherds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potsherds) found at Bishop's Island (German: *Bischofsinsel*) near [Königswalde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6nigswalde) and published in *[Zeitschrift für Ethnologie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeitschrift_f%C3%BCr_Ethnologie?action=edit\&redlink=1)* in 1871. Schliemann believed the motif on the potsherd in figure 1 to be a swastika.

> For I recognise at the first glance the "suastika" upon one of those three pot bottoms, which were discovered on Bishop's Island near Königswalde on the right bank of the Oder, and have given rise to very many learned discussions, while no one recognised the mark as that exceedingly significant religious symbol of our remote ancestors.

— Heinrich Schliemann, *Troy and Its Remains*, 1875[^cite_note-Schliemann_1875-152]<sup>: 102 </sup>

[Sarah Boxer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Boxer), in an article in 2000 in *[The New York Times](30680.md)*, described this as a "fateful link".[^cite_note-Boxer-2000-153] According to [Steven Heller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Heller_\(design_writer\)), "Schliemann presumed that the swastika was a religious symbol of his German ancestors which linked ancient [Teutons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teutons), [Homeric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeric) Greeks and [Vedic India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic_India)".[^cite_note-Heller-2000-154]<sup>: 31 </sup> According to Bernard Mees, "Of all of the pre-runic symbols, the swastika has always been the most popular among scholars" and "The origin of swastika studies must be traced to the excitement generated by the archaeological finds of Heinrich Schliemann at Troy".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMees200857-158]

After his excavations at Troy, Schliemann began digging at [Mycenae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycenae). According to Cathy Gere, "Having burdened the swastika symbol with such cultural, religious and racial significance in *Troy and Its Remains*, it was incumbent on Schliemann to find the symbol repeated at Mycenae, but its occurrence turned out to be disappointingly infrequent".[^cite_note-Gere-2006-156]<sup>: 91 </sup> Gere writes that "He did his best with what he had":[^cite_note-Gere-2006-156]<sup>: 91 </sup>

> The cross with the marks of four nails may often be seen; as well as the 卍, which is usually also represented with four points indicating the four nails, thus ࿘. These signs cannot but represent the *suastika*, formed by two pieces of wood, which were laid across and fixed with four nails, and in the joint of which the holy fire was produced by friction by a third piece of wood. But both the cross and the 卍 occur for the most part only on the vases with geometrical patterns.

— Heinrich Schliemann, *Mycenæ*, 1878[^cite_note-Schliemann_1878-159]<sup>: 66–68 </sup>

Gere points out that although Schliemann wrote that the motif "may often be seen", his 1878 book *Mycenæ* contained no illustrations of examples.[^cite_note-Gere-2006-156]<sup>: 91 </sup> Schliemann described "a small and thick terra-cotta disk" on which "are engraved a number of 卍's, the sign which occurs so frequently in the ruins of Troy", but as Gere notes, he did not publish an illustration.[^cite_note-Schliemann_1878-159]<sup>: 77 </sup>[^cite_note-Gere-2006-156]<sup>: 91 </sup>



Gold *[repoussé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repouss%C3%A9)* roundel from grave III of [Grave Circle A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grave_Circle_A,_Mycenae), whose central motif Schliemann thought "derived" from the swastika.

[^cite_note-Schliemann_1878-159]

: 165–166&#x20;

Among the [gold grave goods at Grave Circles A and B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_grave_goods_at_Grave_Circles_A_and_B) was a *[repoussé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repouss%C3%A9)* roundel in grave III of [Grave Circle A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grave_Circle_A,_Mycenae), the ornamentation of which Schliemann thought was "derived" from the swastika:

> The curious ornamentation in the centre, which so often recurs here, seems to me to be derived from the ࿘, the more so as the points which are thought to be the marks of the nails, are seldom missing; the artist has only added two more arms and curved all of them.

— Heinrich Schliemann, *Mycenæ*, 1878[^cite_note-Schliemann_1878-159]<sup>: 165–166 </sup>

According to Gere, this motif is "completely dissimilar" to the swastika, and that Schliemann was "straining desperately after the same connection".[^cite_note-Gere-2006-156]<sup>: 91 </sup> Nevertheless, the [Mycenaean Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycenaean_Greeks) and the [Trojan people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trojan_people) both came to be identified as representatives of the Aryan race: "Despite the difficulties with linking the symbolism of Troy and Mycenae, the common Aryan roots of the two peoples became something of a truism".[^cite_note-Gere-2006-156]<sup>: 91 </sup>

The house Schliemann had had built in [Panepistimiou Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panepistimiou_Street) in Athens by 1880, [Iliou Melathron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iliou_Melathron), is decorated with swastika symbols and motifs in numerous places, including the ironwork railing and gates, the window bars, the ceiling fresco of the entrance hall, and the entire floor of one room.[^cite_note-Gere-2006-156]<sup>: 117–123 </sup>

Following Schliemann, academic studies on the swastika were published by [Ludvig Müller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludvig_M%C3%BCller_\(numismatist\)?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Michał Żmigrodzki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micha%C5%82_%C5%BBmigrodzki?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Eugène Goblet d'Alviella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eug%C3%A8ne_Goblet_d'Alviella), [Thomas Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Wilson_\(anthropologist\)?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Oscar Montelius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Montelius) and [Joseph Déchelette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_D%C3%A9chelette).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMees200857-158]

#### German occultism and pan-German nationalism

On 24 June 1875, [Guido von List](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guido_von_List) commemorated the 1500th anniversary of the German victory over the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire) at the [Battle of Carnuntum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Carnuntum) by burying a swastika of eight wine bottles beneath the [Heidentor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heidentor) (lit. 'Heathens' Gate') in the ruins of [Carnuntum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnuntum).[^cite_note-Goodrick-Clarke-2004-160]<sup>: 35 </sup> In 1891, List began to claim that [heraldry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraldry)'s [division of the field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_of_the_field) was derived from the shapes of [runes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runes).[^cite_note-Goodrick-Clarke-2004-160]<sup>: 71 </sup> He claimed that the medieval German *[Vehmgericht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vehmgericht)* was a survival of the pre-Christian [Armanist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armanist) [priest-kings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacred_king) and that the cryptic letters "SSGG" inscribed on vehmic knives represented a double [sig rune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sig_rune) followed by two swastikas.[^cite_note-Goodrick-Clarke-2004-160]<sup>: 76 </sup>

In 1897, [Max Ferdinand Sebaldt von Werth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Ferdinand_Sebaldt_von_Werth?action=edit\&redlink=1) published *Wanidis* and *Sexualreligion*, which according to [Nicholas Goodrick-Clarke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Goodrick-Clarke) in *[The Occult Roots of Nazism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Occult_Roots_of_Nazism)*, "described the sexual-religion of the Aryans, a sacred practice of [eugenics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugenics) designed to maintain the purity of the race".[^cite_note-Goodrick-Clarke-2004-160]<sup>: 51 </sup> Both works were "illustrated with the magical curved-armed armed swastika".[^cite_note-Goodrick-Clarke-2004-160]<sup>: 51 </sup> Influenced by Sebaldt, List published in *[Der Scherer – erstes illustriertes Tiroler Witzblatt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Der_Scherer_%E2%80%93_erstes_illustriertes_Tiroler_Witzblatt?action=edit\&redlink=1)* an article ("*Germanischer Lichtdienst*") which claimed the swastika was a sacred symbol of the Aryans representing the "[fire-whisk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_drill_\(tool\))" (*Feuerquirl*) with which the [creator deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creator_deity) [Mundelföri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mundelf%C3%B6ri) had begun the world.[^cite_note-Goodrick-Clarke-2004-160]<sup>: 52 </sup> In September 1903, List published an article discussing the creation of the universe, the "old-Aryan sexual religion", [reincarnation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reincarnation), [karma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karma), "[Wotanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wotanism_\(Guido_von_List\))", and "[Armanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armanism)" from his [theosophical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theosophical) viewpoint, which was illustrated by [triskelions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triskelions) and various swastikas in the Viennese occult journal *[Die Gnosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Die_Gnosis?action=edit\&redlink=1)*.[^cite_note-Goodrick-Clarke-2004-160]<sup>: 41, 52 </sup> According to Goodrick-Clarke, "This article marked the first stage in List's articulation of a Germanic occult religion, the principal concern of which was racial purity".[^cite_note-Goodrick-Clarke-2004-160]<sup>: 52 </sup>

Between 1905 and 1907, List published articles in the *[Leipziger Illustrierte Zeitung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipziger_Illustrierte_Zeitung)* arguing that the swastika, the triskelion, and the sun-wheel were all "[Armanist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armanist)" occult symbols ([Armanen runes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armanen_runes)) concealed in [German heraldry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_heraldry), and in 1908 his *[Das Geheimnis der Runen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Das_Geheimnis_der_Runen)* (lit. '*The Secret of the Runes*') argued that the swastika or Armanen rune "[Gibor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibor)" was represented in [blazons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blazon) including different [heraldic crosses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraldic_crosses) and kinked versions of the [ordinaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_\(heraldry\)) [pale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pale_\(heraldry\)), [bend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bend_\(heraldry\)), and [fess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fess).[^cite_note-Goodrick-Clarke-2004-160]<sup>: 72 </sup> List argued that the swastika, triskelion, and other Armanen runes had been concealed in 15th-century [rose windows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rose_windows) and curvilinear [tracery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tracery) in [late Gothic architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Gothic_architecture).[^cite_note-Goodrick-Clarke-2004-160]<sup>: 74 </sup>



Table from [Guido von List](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guido_von_List)'s 1908 *[Das Geheimnis der Runen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Das_Geheimnis_der_Runen)* (lit. '*The Secret of the Runes*') illustrating his ideas about swastikas and his [Ariosophist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariosophist) [Armanen runes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armanen_runes) in [German heraldry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_heraldry) and [Gothic architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_architecture).

List's 1908 book *Die Rita der Ario-Germanen* (lit. '*The Rite of the Ario-Germans*') featured chapter headings with triskelions, swastikas, and other symbols. The work laid out his belief in an ancient priestly *[Armanenschaft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armanenschaft)* of Wotanist initiates and identified the "Ario-Germans" as a "race" identical with [Helena Blavatsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helena_Blavatsky)'s theosophical fifth "[root race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_race)".[^cite_note-Goodrick-Clarke-2004-160]<sup>: 52–53 </sup> List's 1910 *Die Religion der Ario-Germanen* (lit. '*The Religion of the Ario-Germans*') discussed [Yuga cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuga_cycle) and the [Kali Yuga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kali_Yuga), proposing a mathematical relationship with the *[Grímnismál](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gr%C3%ADmnism%C3%A1l)* of the [Edda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edda).[^cite_note-Goodrick-Clarke-2004-160]<sup>: 53 </sup> His *Die Bilderschrift der Ario-Germanen* (lit. '*The Picture-Writing of the Ario-Germans*') of the same year connected Blavatsky's Hindu-inspired cosmic cycles ([kalpas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalpa_\(time\))) with the realms of [Muspelheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muspelheim) (*Muspilheim*), [Asgard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asgard), [Vanaheimr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanaheimr) (*Wanenheim*), and [Midgard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midgard), each with a corresponding symbol. Blavatsky's first Astral and second [Hyperborean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperborea) races List connected with the descendants of [Ymir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ymir) and [Orgelmir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orgelmir), her third [Lemurian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lemuria) race was his race of [Thrudgelmir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrudgelmir), her fourth [Atlantean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantis) race his descendants of [Bergelmir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bergelmir), and Blavatsky's fifth root race List identified as the "Ario-Germans".[^cite_note-Goodrick-Clarke-2004-160]<sup>: 53–54 </sup> According to Goodrick-Clarke, List again argued that the clockwise swastika was a holy symbol of the "Ario-Germans":

> A series of anti-clockwise triskelions and swastikas and inverted triangles symbolized stages of cosmic evolution in the downsweep of the cycle (i.e., the evolution from unity to multiplicity), while their clockwise and upright counterparts connoted the return path to the godhead. The skewed super-imposition of these 'falling' and 'rising' sigils created complex sigils like the [hexagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexagram) and the [Maltese Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maltese_Cross). List asserted that these latter symbols were utterly sacred, because they embraced the two antithetical forces of all creation: as the representative symbols of the zenith of multiplicity at the outermost limit of the cycle, they denoted the Ario-German god-man, the highest form of life ever to evolve in the universe.[^cite_note-Goodrick-Clarke-2004-160]<sup>: 54 </sup>

&#x20;List's 1914 *Die Ursprache der Ario-Germanen* (lit. '*The Proto-Language of the Ario-Germans*') adopted the geological ideas of theosophist [William Scott-Elliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Scott-Elliot) and claimed that fragments of Atlantis remained part of Europe, pointing to [rocking stones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocking_stones) in [Lower Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Austria) and European [megaliths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megaliths) as evidence. From [Jörg Lanz von Liebenfels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%B6rg_Lanz_von_Liebenfels), List took on occult ideas about the Aryan homeland [Arktogäa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arktog%C3%A4a?action=edit\&redlink=1) (a lost polar continent), and struggle the Ario-German [master races](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_race) and the non-Aryan slave races, and the [Knights Templar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knights_Templar).[^cite_note-Goodrick-Clarke-2004-160]<sup>: 54–55 </sup> List believed that the Templars had been adepts of "[Armanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armanism)" during the Middle Ages' Christian ascendancy, and that they had been suppressed for worshipping the Maltese cross that List believed to be derived from a superimposed clockwise and counter-clockwise swastika and which he identified with [Baphomet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baphomet).[^cite_note-Goodrick-Clarke-2004-160]<sup>: 61–62 </sup> Members of the inner circle of the [Guido von List Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guido_von_List_Society), the [Hoher Armanen-Orden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoher_Armanen-Orden) (HAO), expressed their membership of the occult priesthood with swastikas. [Heinrich Winter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Winter?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Friedrich Oskar Wannieck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Oskar_Wannieck?action=edit\&redlink=1), and [Georg Hauerstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Hauerstein?action=edit\&redlink=1) senior's first wife all had their graves decorated with swastikas.[^cite_note-Goodrick-Clarke-2004-160]<sup>: 65 </sup>



Flag of the [Order of the New Templars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_the_New_Templars) designed 1907 with a swastika used as [*völkisch*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%B6lkisch_movement) (German ethno-nationalist) symbol

Lanz, a former [Cistercian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cistercian), established the [Order of the New Templars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_the_New_Templars) or ONT (*Ordo Novi Templi* lit. 'Order of the New Temple') in imitation of the Knights Templar whose [monastic rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monastic_rule) had been written by the Cistercian [Bernard of Clairvaux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_of_Clairvaux) and whom Lanz believed had aimed to establish "a [Greater Germanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-Germanism) [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monastic_order)-state, which would encompass the entire [Mediterranean area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_area) and extend its [sphere of influence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphere_of_influence) deep into the [Middle East](19323.md)" whose eventual suppression had been a triumph of racial inferiority over the "Ario-Christian" eugenics practised by the Templars.[^cite_note-Goodrick-Clarke-2004-160]<sup>: 108 </sup> As the headquarters of his revived Templar Order and as a museum of Aryan anthropology, Lanz bought [Burg Werfenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burg_Werfenstein?action=edit\&redlink=1) on the Danube, where on Christmas Day 1907, he hoisted his [heraldic banner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraldic_banner) (*[gules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gules)*, an eagle's wing *[argent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argent)*) and the flag of the ONT: a swastika *gules* surrounded by four [fleurs-de-lis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fleurs-de-lis) *[azure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azure_\(heraldry\))* on a field *[or](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Or_\(heraldry\))*.[^cite_note-Goodrick-Clarke-2004-160]<sup>: 106, 109, 113 </sup>

#### Post-Schliemann popularity

See also: [Western use of the swastika in the early 20th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_use_of_the_swastika_in_the_early_20th_century)

The swastika symbol became a popular symbol in the Western world in the early 20th century, and was often used for ornamentation. It symbolised many things to Europeans, most commonly good luck and auspiciousness.[^cite_note-holocaust2009-19]

The Benedictine choir school at [Lambach Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambach_Abbey), Upper Austria, which Hitler attended for several months as a boy, had a swastika chiselled into the monastery portal and also the wall above the spring grotto in the courtyard by 1868. Their origin was the personal [coat of arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coat_of_arms) of [Theoderich Hagn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoderich_Hagn?action=edit\&redlink=1), abbot of the monastery in Lambach, which bore a golden swastika with slanted points on a blue field.[^cite_note-161]

The British author and poet [Rudyard Kipling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudyard_Kipling) used the symbol on the cover art of several of his works, including *[The Five Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Five_Nations)* (1903), which pairs it with an elephant. Once Adolf Hitler and the Nazis came to power, Kipling ordered that swastikas should no longer adorn his books. In 1927, a red swastika [defaced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defacement_\(flag\)) by a Union Jack was proposed as a flag for the [Union of South Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_of_South_Africa).[^cite_note-162]

The logo of [H/f. Eimskipafjelag Íslands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eimskip)[^cite_note-163] was a swastika, called "Thor's hammer", from its founding in 1914 until the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War), when it was discontinued and changed to read only the letters Eimskip.

The swastika was also used by the women's paramilitary organisation [Lotta Svärd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lotta_Sv%C3%A4rd), which was banned in 1944 in accordance with the [Moscow Armistice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_Armistice) between Finland and the [allied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II) [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) and Britain.



Blue swastika insignia as well as black swastika emblem of the [Finnish Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finnish_Air_Force) (1934–1945)

Also, the insignias of the [Cross of Liberty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_the_Cross_of_Liberty), designed by Gallen-Kallela in 1918, have swastikas. The 3rd class Cross of Liberty is depicted in the upper left corner of the standard of the President of Finland, who is the grand master of the order, too.[^cite_note-164]



Latvian Air Force roundel until 1940

Latvia adopted the swastika for its [Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latvian_Air_Force) in 1918/1919 and continued its use until the [Soviet occupation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_occupation_of_Latvia_in_1940) in 1940.[^cite_note-165][^cite_note-166] The cross itself was maroon on a white background, mirroring the colours of the Latvian flag. Earlier versions pointed counterclockwise, while later versions pointed clockwise and eliminated the white background.[^cite_note-167][^cite_note-168] Various other [Latvian Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latvian_Army) units and the Latvian War College[^cite_note-169] (the predecessor of the [National Defence Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Defence_Academy_of_Latvia)) also had adopted the symbol in their battle flags and insignia during the [Latvian War of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latvian_War_of_Independence).[^cite_note-170] A stylised fire cross is the base of the [Order of Lāčplēsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_L%C4%81%C4%8Dpl%C4%93sis), the highest military decoration of Latvia for participants of the War of Independence.[^cite_note-171] The [Pērkonkrusts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C4%93rkonkrusts), an ultra-nationalist political organisation active in the 1930s, also used the fire cross as one of its symbols.



Emblem of the [Lithuanian Nationalist Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuanian_Nationalist_Union) during the [Interbellum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interbellum)



Lithuanian Monument to Freedom in [Rokiškis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roki%C5%A1kis)

The swastika symbol ([Lithuanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuanian_language): *sūkurėlis*) is a traditional Baltic ornament,[^cite_note-Guénon-2001-108][^cite_note-172] found on relics dating from at least the 13th century.[^cite_note-Sarmatas-173] The *sūkurėlis* for Lithuanians represents the history and memory of their Lithuanian ancestors as well as the [Baltic people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balts) at large.[^cite_note-Sarmatas-173] There are monuments in Lithuania, such as the Freedom Monument in [Rokiškis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roki%C5%A1kis), where swastikas can be found.[^cite_note-Sarmatas-173] In Lithuania, the swastika was first used on a flag in 1924 by the [Lithuanian Nationalist Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuanian_Nationalist_Union).[^cite_note-174]

Starting in 1917, [Mikal Sylten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikal_Sylten)'s staunchly [antisemitic](1078.md) periodical, *[Nationalt Tidsskrift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalt_Tidsskrift)* took up the swastika as a symbol, three years before [Adolf Hitler](2731583.md) chose to do so.[^cite_note-ØS-175]

The left-handed swastika was a favourite sign of the last Russian Empress [Alexandra Feodorovna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandra_Feodorovna_\(Alix_of_Hesse\)). She wore a talisman in the form of a swastika, put it everywhere for happiness, including on her suicide letters from [Tobolsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobolsk),[^cite_note-176] later drew with a pencil on the wall and in the window opening of the room in the [Ipatiev House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ipatiev_House), which served as the place of the last imprisonment of the royal family and on the wallpaper above the bed.[^cite_note-177]

The [Russian Provisional Government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Provisional_Government) of 1917 printed several new banknotes with right-facing, diagonally rotated swastikas in their centres.[^cite_note-178] The banknote design was initially intended for the Mongolian national bank but was re-purposed for [Russian rubles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_ruble) after the February revolution. Swastikas were depicted, and some Soviet credit cards ([sovznaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovznak)) were printed with clichés in circulation in 1918–1922.[^cite_note-179]

During the [Russian Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Civil_War), swastikas were present in the symbolism of the uniform of some units of the [White Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Army) [Asiatic Cavalry Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asiatic_Cavalry_Division) of [Baron Ungern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_von_Ungern-Sternberg) in Siberia and [Bogd Khanate of Mongolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogd_Khanate_of_Mongolia), which is explained by the significant number of Buddhists within it.[^cite_note-180] The [Red Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Army)'s ethnic [Kalmyk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalmyk_people) units wore distinct armbands featuring a swastika with "РСФСР" (Roman: "RSFSR") inscriptions on them.[^cite_note-181]

#### New religious movements

Besides its use as a religious symbol in [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism), [Buddhism](3267529.md), and [Jainism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism), which can be traced back to pre-modern traditions, the swastika was also incorporated into a large number of [new religious movements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_religious_movements) which were established in the West in the modern period.

In the 1880s, the U.S.-origined [Theosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theosophical_Society) adopted a swastika as part of its seal, along with an [Om](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Om), a hexagram or [star of David](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_of_David), an [Ankh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ankh), and an [Ouroboros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouroboros). Unlike the much more recent [Raëlian movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ra%C3%ABlian_movement), the Theosophical Society symbol has been free from controversy, and the seal is still used. The current seal also includes the text "There is no religion higher than truth."[^cite_note-182]

The [Raëlian Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ra%C3%ABlism), whose adherents believe extraterrestrials created all life on earth, uses a symbol that is often the source of considerable controversy: an interlaced [star of David](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_of_David) and a swastika. The Raëlians say the Star of David represents infinity in space, whereas the swastika represents infinity in time – no beginning and no end in time, and everything being cyclic.[^cite_note-183] In 1991, the symbol was changed to remove the swastika, out of respect to the victims of [the Holocaust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Holocaust), but as of 2007 it has been restored to its original form.[^cite_note-184]

The swastika is a holy symbol in [neopagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neopaganism) [Germanic Heathenry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heathenry_\(new_religious_movement\)), along with the [hammer of Thor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mj%C3%B6lnir) and [runes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runes). This tradition – which is found in Scandinavia, Germany, and elsewhere – considers the swastika to be derived from a Norse symbol for the sun. Their use of the symbol has led people to accuse them of being a neo-Nazi group.[^cite_note-185][^cite_note-186][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMees2008141,_193–194,_210–211,_226–227-187]

### Nazism

Further information: [Fascist symbolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fascist_symbolism), [Nazi symbolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_symbolism), and [Occultism in Nazism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occultism_in_Nazism)

Before the Nazis, the swastika (tilted at 45 degrees), was already in use as a symbol of the German nationalist [Völkisch movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%B6lkisch_movement). In [post-World War I Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weimar_Republic), the newly established [Nazi Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Party) formally adopted the swastika in 1920.[^cite_note-holocaust2009-19][^cite_note-188] The Nazi Party emblem was a black swastika rotated 45 degrees on a white circle on a red background. This insignia was used on the party's flag, badge, and armband. [Adolf Hitler](2731583.md) also designed his personal standard using a black swastika sitting flat on one arm, not rotated.[^cite_note-189]



The flag of the [Nazi Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Party) (National Socialist German Workers' Party, NSDAP)



The national flag of [Germany](21212.md) (1935–1945), which differs from the NSDAP flag in that the white circle with the swastika is off-center

In his 1925 work *[Mein Kampf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mein_Kampf)*, Hitler writes: "I myself, meanwhile, after innumerable attempts, had laid down a final form; a flag with a red background, a white disk, and a black hooked cross in the middle. After long trials I also found a definite proportion between the size of the flag and the size of the white disk, as well as the shape and thickness of the hooked cross."

When Hitler created a flag for the Nazi Party, he sought to incorporate both the swastika and "those revered colours expressive of our homage to the glorious past and which once brought so much honour to the German nation". (Red, white, and black were the colours of the [flag of the old German Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_the_German_Empire).) He also stated: "As National Socialists, we see our program in our flag. In red, we see the social idea of the movement; in white, the nationalistic idea; in the hooked cross, the mission of the struggle for the victory of the [Aryan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryan_race) man, and, by the same token, the victory of the idea of creative work."[^cite_note-190]

The swastika was also understood as "the symbol of the creating, effecting life" (*das Symbol des schaffenden, wirkenden Lebens*) and as "race emblem of Germanism" (*Rasseabzeichen des Germanentums*).[^cite_note-191]

The concepts of [racial hygiene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_hygiene) and [scientific racism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_racism) were central to Nazism.[^cite_note-192][^cite_note-193] High-ranking Nazi theorist [Alfred Rosenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Rosenberg) noted that the [Indo-Aryan peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Aryan_peoples) were both a model to be imitated and a warning of the dangers of the spiritual and racial "confusion" that, he believed, arose from the proximity of races. The Nazis co-opted the swastika as a symbol of the Aryan master race.

On 14 March 1933, shortly after Hitler's appointment as Chancellor of Germany, the NSDAP flag was hoisted alongside Germany's national colours. As part of the [Nuremberg Laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuremberg_Laws), the NSDAP flag – with the swastika slightly offset from centre – was adopted as the sole national flag of Germany on 15 September 1935.[^cite_note-194]

### Americas

The swastika has been used in the art and iconography of multiple indigenous peoples of North America, including the Hopi, Navajo, and Tlingit.[^cite_note-196] Swastikas were founds on pottery from the Mississippi valley and on copper objects in the [Hopewell Mounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopewell_Culture_National_Historical_Park) in [Ross County, Ohio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ross_County,_Ohio), and on objects associated with the [Southeastern Ceremonial Complex (S.E.C.C.)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeastern_Ceremonial_Complex#Motifs).[^cite_note-197][^cite_note-198] To the [Hopi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopi) it represents the wandering Hopi clan. The [Navajo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navajo) symbol, called *tsin náálwołí* ("whirling log"), represents humanity and life, and is used in healing rituals.[^cite_note-Sanchez-2018-199][^cite_note-200] A brightly coloured [First Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Nations_in_Canada) saddle featuring swastika designs is on display at the [Royal Saskatchewan Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Saskatchewan_Museum) in Canada.[^cite_note-201]

Before the 1930s, the symbol for the [45th Infantry Division of the United States Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/45th_Infantry_Division_\(United_States\)) was a red diamond with a yellow swastika, a tribute to the large Native American population in the southwestern United States. It was later replaced with a [thunderbird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thunderbird_\(mythology\)) symbol.

In the 20th century, traders encouraged Native American artists to use the symbol in their crafts; it was used by the US Army 45th Infantry Division, an all-Native American division.[^cite_note-202][^cite_note-203][^cite_note-Ayres-2021-204] The symbol lost popularity in the 1930s due to its associations with Nazi Germany. In 1940, in part due to government encouragement, community leaders from several Native American tribes issued a statement promising not to use the symbol.[^cite_note-205][^cite_note-Sanchez-2018-199][^cite_note-Kane-2017-206][^cite_note-Ayres-2021-204] However, the symbol has continued to be used by Native American groups, both in reference to the original symbol and as a memorial to the 45th Division, despite external objections to its use.[^cite_note-Olson-8][^cite_note-Ayres-2021-204][^cite_note-Kane-2017-206][^cite_note-207][^cite_note-208][^cite_note-209] The symbol was used on state road signs in [Arizona](21883824.md) from the 1920s until the 1940s.[^cite_note-210]

The town of [Swastika, Ontario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swastika,_Ontario), and the hamlet of [Swastika, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swastika,_New_York) were named after the symbol.

From 1909 to 1916, the [K-R-I-T automobile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-R-I-T_Motor_Car_Company), manufactured in Detroit, Michigan, used a right-facing swastika as its trademark.



Flag of the Guna Yala people (since 1925) bears their ancient symbol *Naa Ukuryaa*

The [flag of the Guna people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guna_people#Flag) (also "Kuna Yala" or "Guna Yala") of Panama, adopted in 1925, has a swastika symbol that they call *Naa Ukuryaa*. According to one explanation, this ancestral symbol symbolises the octopus that created the world, its tentacles pointing to the four cardinal points.[^cite_note-211] In 1942, a ring was added to the centre of the flag to distinguish it from the [Nazi Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Party)'s symbol (this version subsequently fell into disuse).[^cite_note-Panama_-_Native_Peoples-212]

### Modern adoptions

A *[ugunskrusts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ugunskrusts)* ('fire cross') is used by the [Baltic neopagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltic_neopaganism) religions [Dievturība](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dievtur%C4%ABba) in Latvia and [Romuva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romuva_\(religion\)) in Lithuania.[^cite_note-214]

In the early 1990s, the former dissident and one of the founders of Russian neo-paganism [Alexey Dobrovolsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexey_Dobrovolsky) first gave the name "[kolovrat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolovrat_\(symbol\))" (Russian: коловрат, literally 'spinning wheel') to a four-beam swastika, identical to the Nazi symbol, and later transferred this name to an eight-beam rectangular swastika.[^cite_note-215] The eight-beam swastika dates back all the way to Ancient Greece, with some ceramics containing the eight-beamed symbol.[^cite_note-216][^cite_note-217] A necklace found in Ukraine via metal detection is estimated to date back to the 11th century and also contained the kolovrat symbol, providing some solid evidence of its presence among the Slavic people at the time. A six-beamed variant is located in the tower of the [Vang Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vang_Stave_Church) in [Karpacz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karpacz), [Poland](22936.md).[^cite_note-218] According to the historian and religious scholar Roman Shizhensky, Dobrovolsky took the idea of the swastika from the work "The Chronicle of [Oera Linda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oera_Linda_Book)"[^cite_note-219] by the Nazi ideologist [Herman Wirth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Wirth), the first head of the [Ahnenerbe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahnenerbe).[^cite_note-Shizhensky_2012-220] Dobrovolsky introduced the eight-beam "kolovrat" as a symbol of "resurgent paganism."[^cite_note-221] He considered this version of the Kolovrat a pagan sign of the sun and, in 1996, declared it a symbol of the uncompromising "national liberation struggle" against the "[Zhyd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhyd) yoke".[^cite_note-222] According to Dobrovolsky, the meaning of the "kolovrat" completely coincides with the meaning of the Nazi swastika.[^cite_note-223] The *kolovrat* is the most commonly used religious symbol within neopagan [Slavic Native Faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavic_Native_Faith) (a.k.a. Rodnovery).[^cite_note-224][^cite_note-225]

In 2005, authorities in Tajikistan called for the widespread adoption of the swastika as a national [symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbol). President [Emomali Rahmonov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emomali_Rahmonov) declared the swastika an [Aryan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryan) symbol, and 2006 "the year of Aryan culture", which would be a time to "study and popularise Aryan contributions to the history of the world civilisation, raise a new generation (of Tajiks) with the spirit of national self-determination, and develop deeper ties with other ethnicities and cultures".[^cite_note-226]

## Modern controversy



Swastikas marking downed *[Luftwaffe](17885.md)* aircraft on the fuselage of a [Supermarine Spitfire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supermarine_Spitfire) of [No. 601 Squadron RAF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No._601_Squadron_RAF). A *[fasces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fasces)* indicates a *[Regia Aeronautica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regia_Aeronautica)* aircraft.

Because of its use by Nazi Germany, the swastika since the 1930s has been largely associated with Nazism. In the aftermath of World War II, it has been considered a symbol of hate in the West,[^cite_note-227] and of white supremacy in many Western countries.[^cite_note-228]

As a result, all use of it, or its use as a Nazi or hate symbol, is prohibited in some countries, including Germany. In some countries, such as the United States (in the 2003 case *[Virginia v. Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia_v._Black)*), the highest courts have ruled that local governments can prohibit the use of the swastika, along with other symbols such as cross-burning, if the use is intended to intimidate others.[^cite_note-wiener463-20]

### Germany

Further information: [*Strafgesetzbuch* section 86a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strafgesetzbuch_section_86a)

The German and Austrian post-war [criminal code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strafgesetzbuch) makes the public showing of the swastika, the [sig rune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sig_rune), the [Celtic cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_cross) (specifically [the variations used by white power activists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_cross#White_supremacist_symbolism)), the *[wolfsangel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfsangel)*, the [Odal SS-rune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odal_SS-rune) and the *[Totenkopf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totenkopf)* skull illegal, except for certain enumerated exemptions. It is also censored from the reprints of 1930s railway timetables published by the *[Reichsbahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reichsbahn)*. The swastikas on Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain temples are exempt, as religious symbols cannot be banned in Germany.[^cite_note-229]

A controversy was stirred by the decision of several police departments to begin inquiries against anti-fascists.[^cite_note-230] In late 2005 police raided the offices of the [punk rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punk_rock) label and mail order store "Nix Gut Records" and confiscated merchandise depicting crossed-out swastikas and fists smashing swastikas. In 2006, the [Stade](306032.md) police department started an inquiry against anti-fascist youths using a placard depicting a person dumping a swastika into a trash can. The placard was displayed in opposition to the campaign of right-wing nationalist parties for local elections.[^cite_note-231]

On Friday, 17 March 2006, a member of the *[Bundestag](3768.md)*, [Claudia Roth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claudia_Roth) reported herself to the German police for displaying a crossed-out swastika in multiple demonstrations against [neo-Nazis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Nazis), and subsequently got the Bundestag to suspend her immunity from prosecution. She intended to show the absurdity of charging anti-fascists with using fascist symbols: "We don't need prosecution of non-violent young people engaging against right-wing extremism." On 15 March 2007, the [Federal Court of Justice of Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Court_of_Justice_of_Germany) (*Bundesgerichtshof*) held that the crossed-out symbols were "clearly directed against a revival of national-socialist endeavours", thereby settling the dispute for the future.[^cite_note-232][^cite_note-233][^cite_note-234]

On 9 August 2018, Germany lifted the ban on the usage of swastikas and other Nazi symbols in video games. "Through the change in the interpretation of the law, games that critically look at current affairs can for the first time be given a USK age rating," USK managing director Elisabeth Secker told CTV. "This has long been the case for films and with regards to the freedom of the arts, this is now rightly also the case with computer and videogames."[^cite_note-235][^cite_note-236]

### Outside Europe

The manufacture, distribution, or broadcasting of a swastika, with the intent to propagate Nazism, is a crime in Brazil as dictated by article 20, paragraph 1, of federal statute 7.716, passed in 1989. The penalty is a two- to five-year prison term and a fine.[^cite_note-248]

The public display of [Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi)-era German flags (or any other flags) is protected by the [First Amendment to the United States Constitution](31653.md), which guarantees the right to [freedom of speech](21401843.md).[^cite_note-249] The Nazi *[Reichskriegsflagge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reichskriegsflagge)* has also been seen on display at white supremacist events within United States borders, side by side with the [Confederate battle flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flags_of_the_Confederate_States_of_America#Battle_flag).[^cite_note-250]

In 2010, the [Anti-Defamation League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Defamation_League) (ADL) downgraded the swastika from its status as a Jewish hate symbol, saying "We know that the swastika has, for some, lost its meaning as the primary symbol of Nazism and instead become a more generalised symbol of hate."[^cite_note-Dickter-251] The ADL notes on their website that the symbol is often used as "shock graffiti" by juveniles, rather than by individuals who hold white supremacist beliefs, but it is still a predominant symbol among American white supremacists (particularly as a tattoo design) and used with antisemitic intention.[^cite_note-ADL-252]

In 2022, [Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_\(Australia\)) was the first Australian state to ban the display of the Nazi swastika. People who intentionally break this law will face a one-year jail sentence, a fine of 120 penalty units (\$23,077.20 AUD as of 2023, equivalent to £12,076.66 or US\$15,385.57), or both.[^cite_note-253][^cite_note-254]

### Media

In 2010, [Microsoft](19001.md) officially spoke out against players of the [first-person shooter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-person_shooter) *[Call of Duty: Black Ops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Call_of_Duty:_Black_Ops)* using the swastika. In *Black Ops*, players can customise their name tags to represent whatever they want. The swastika can be created and used, but [Stephen Toulouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Toulouse), director of [Xbox Live](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xbox_Live) policy and enforcement, said players with the symbol on their name tag will be banned (if someone reports it as inappropriate) from Xbox Live.[^cite_note-Goldman_2010-255]

In the *[Indiana Jones Stunt Spectacular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiana_Jones_Stunt_Spectacular)* at [Disney Hollywood Studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disney_Hollywood_Studios) in Florida, the swastikas on German trucks, aircraft, and actor uniforms in the re-enactment of a scene from *[Raiders of the Lost Ark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raiders_of_the_Lost_Ark)* were removed in 2004.[^cite_note-Telotte_2008_p.201-256]

### Use by neo-Nazis&#x20;

As with many [neo-Nazi](54361.md) groups across the world, the [American Nazi Party](364176.md) used the swastika as part of its flag before its first dissolution in 1967. The symbol was chosen by the organisation's founder, [George Lincoln Rockwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Lincoln_Rockwell).[^cite_note-257] It was "re-used" by successor organisations in 1983, without the publicity Rockwell's organisation enjoyed.

The swastika, in various iconographic forms, is one of the hate symbols identified in use as [graffiti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graffiti) in US schools, and is described as such in a 1999 US Department of Education document, "Responding to Hate at School: A Guide for Teachers, Counsellors and Administrators", edited by Jim Carnes, which provides advice to educators on how to support students targeted by such hate symbols and address hate graffiti. Examples given show that it is often used alongside other white supremacist symbols, such as those of the [Ku Klux Klan](16779.md), and note a ["three-bladed" variation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triskelion) used by [skinheads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_power_skinhead), white supremacists, and "[some South African extremist groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afrikaner_Weerstandsbeweging)".[^cite_note-258]

The neo-Nazi [Russian National Unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_National_Unity) group's branch in Estonia is officially registered under the name "Kolovrat" and published an extremist newspaper in 2001 under the same name.[^cite_note-Lihachev-259] A criminal investigation found the paper included an array of racial epithets. One Narva resident was sentenced to one year in jail for distribution of *Kolovrat*.[^cite_note-260] The Kolovrat has since been used by the [Rusich Battalion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rusich_Group), a Russian militant group known for its operation during the [war in Donbas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_in_Donbas_\(2014%E2%80%932022\)).[^cite_note-261][^cite_note-262] In 2014 and 2015, members of the Ukrainian [Azov Regiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azov_Regiment) were seen with swastika tattoos.[^cite_note-263][^cite_note-264][^cite_note-265][^cite_note-266]

### Western misinterpretation of Asian use

Since the end of the 20th century and through the early 21st century, confusion and controversy have arisen when personal-use goods bearing traditional Jain, Buddhist, or Hindu symbols have been exported to the West, notably to North America and Europe, and have been interpreted by purchasers as Nazi symbols. This has resulted in several such products being boycotted or pulled from shelves.

When a ten-year-old boy in [Lynbrook, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynbrook,_New_York), bought a set of [Pokémon cards](431322.md) imported from Japan in 1999, two of the cards contained the left-facing Buddhist swastika. The boy's parents misinterpreted the symbol as the right-facing Nazi swastika and filed a complaint to the manufacturer. [Nintendo of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nintendo_of_America) announced that the cards would be discontinued, explaining that what was acceptable in one culture was not necessarily so in another; their action was welcomed by the [Anti-Defamation League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Defamation_League), which recognised that there was no intention to offend, but said that international commerce meant that "Isolating \[the swastika] in Asia would just create more problems."[^cite_note-Heller_2008-79]

In 2002, [Christmas crackers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christmas_cracker) containing plastic toy red pandas sporting swastikas were pulled from shelves after customer complaints in Canada. The manufacturer, based in China, said the symbol was presented in a traditional sense, not as a reference to the Nazis, and apologised to customers for the cross-cultural mix-up.[^cite_note-267]

In 2020, the retailer [Shein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shein) pulled a necklace featuring a left-facing swastika pendant from its website after receiving backlash on social media. The retailer apologized for the lack of sensitivity but noted that the swastika was a Buddhist symbol.[^cite_note-268]

#### Swastika as distinct from *Hakenkreuz* debate&#x20;

"Hakenkreuz" redirects here. For the defunct German newspaper, see [*Hakenkreuzbanner*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hakenkreuzbanner).

Beginning in the early 2000s, partially as a reaction to the publication of a book titled *The Swastika: Symbol Beyond Redemption?* by [Steven Heller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Heller_\(design_writer\)),[^cite_note-Heller_2008-79] there has been a movement by [Hindus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism), [Buddhists](3267529.md), and [Native Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_Americans_in_the_United_States) to "reclaim" the swastika as a sacred symbol.[^cite_note-BBC_News_2014-6][^cite_note-269] These groups argue that the swastika is distinct from the [Nazi symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_symbol). However, Hitler said that the Nazi symbol was the same as the Oriental symbol. On 13 August 1920, speaking to his followers in the [Hofbräuhaus am Platzl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hofbr%C3%A4uhaus_am_Platzl) of Munich, Hitler said of the Nazi symbol: "You will find this cross as a swastika as far as India and Japan, carved in the temple pillars. It is the swastika, which was once a sign of established communities of Aryan Culture."[^cite_note-270]

The main barrier to the effort to "reclaim", "restore", or "reassess" the swastika comes from the decades of extremely negative association in the Western world following the Nazi Party's adoption of it in the 1920s. As well, [white supremacist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_supremacist) groups still cling to the symbol as an icon of power and identity.[^cite_note-ADL-252]

Many media organizations in the West also continue to describe [neo-Nazi](54361.md) usage of the symbol as a swastika, or sometimes with the "Nazi" adjective written as "Nazi Swastika".[^cite_note-USA_Today,_Marc_Ramirez,_August_28,_2022-271][^cite_note-272] Groups that oppose this media terminology do not wish to censor such usage, but rather to shift coverage of antisemitic and hateful events to describe the symbol in this context as a *Hakenkreuz* or 'hooked cross'.[^cite_note-273]
