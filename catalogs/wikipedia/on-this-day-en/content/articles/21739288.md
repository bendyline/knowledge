**Bangladesh Jamaat-e-Islami**[^cite_note-6] is a major [right-wing](56522.md) to [far-right](221220.md) [political party in Bangladesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_party_in_Bangladesh). It emerged from the [East Pakistani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Pakistan) wing of the [Jamaat-e-Islami Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamaat-e-Islami_Pakistan) (JIP) in 1979. A part of the [Islamist](15012.md) movement [Jamaat-e-Islami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamaat-e-Islami), it is the largest Islamist political party in the country, as well as one of the two contemporary mainstream parties, alongside the [Bangladesh Nationalist Party](309760.md) (BNP).

The party's origin lies in the original Jamaat-e-Islami organization founded by [Sayyid Abul A'la Maududi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abul_A'la_Maududi) in 1941 in [British India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_India). Jamaat's predecessor, the JIP, though actively participating in the pro-democracy movements of East Pakistan, opposed the [independence of Bangladesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_of_Bangladesh) and supported [Pakistani Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistani_Forces) in the [Liberation War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh_Liberation_War) in 1971. It reportedly organized several pro-Pakistan groups, and participated in the [genocide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh_genocide) and other [mass atrocities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh_Liberation_War#Atrocities). After a period of post-independence ban on religion-based parties between 1972 and 1976, local members of the JIP merged with several other parties to form the [Islamic Democratic League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Democratic_League) (IDL) in ahead of the [general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1979_Bangladeshi_general_election). In 1979, the IDL was dissolved and the members of the erstwhile East Pakistan JIP reconvened to form the modern-day Bangladesh Jamaat-e-Islami.

The Jamaat played crucial roles in the [1990 mass uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1990_Bangladesh_mass_uprising), 1995 pro-[caretaker government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caretaker_government_of_Bangladesh) movement and 2024 [July Uprising](78109234.md). Two of its leaders served in the BNP's [coalition government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Khaleda_ministry) between 2001 and 2006. However, between [2009 and 2024](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premiership_of_Sheikh_Hasina), leaders of the party were [prosecuted](33827498.md) for [war crimes](48626.md) of 1971, its registration was cancelled and was eventually banned. Following the July Uprising, the ban was lifted and its registration was reinstated. Since [2026](72614737.md), it has been the main opposition to the BNP-led government in the [Jatiya Sangsad](577333.md).

Today, Jamaat's politics differ to the Maududi's vision for a totalitarian [Islamic state](2646438.md), as it has since sought to portray itself [moderate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moderatism) and [centrist](39619035.md).[^cite_note-bergman-7] The party supports a [welfarist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welfarist) economy, and runs charity programmes. The party is controversial for its role in the Liberation War, ideological links to [Islamic extremism](8734632.md), [human rights abuses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights_abuses), and contradictory positions on women and [sharia](28840.md). As of 2026, Jamaat's strongest demographics include younger and educated people[^cite_note-laila2026-8] and [socially conservative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_conservatism) women.[^cite_note-netranews-9]

[Shafiqur Rahman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shafiqur_Rahman) has been serving as the [emir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emir_of_Bangladesh_Jamaat-e-Islami) (leader) and [Miya Ghulam Parwar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miya_Ghulam_Parwar) has been serving as the secretary-general of the party since 2019. The party maintains a network of affiliated organizations, including the [Bangladesh Islami Chhatra Shibir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh_Islami_Chhatra_Shibir).

## History

### Origins

The origin of modern Bangladesh Jamaat-e-Islami party lies in the historical [Jamaat-e-Islami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamaat-e-Islami) movement, founded by *Maulana* [Sayyid Abul A'la Maududi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abul_A'la_Maududi) during the [British Raj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Raj).[^cite_note-10] The movement was established under the name of "[Jamaat-e-Islami Hind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamaat-e-Islami_Hind)" at Islamia Park, [Lahore](125315.md) on 26 August 1941, as an "organizational platform" for promoting Maududi's vision of an [Islamic state](2646438.md). Maududi was deeply concerned about the perceived decline of religious values due to the impact of Western secular ideologies on Muslim communities in India. Thus, he envisioned the Jamaat to be more than a political party – a "comprehensive Islamic movement" influencing all aspects of life through activities in social welfare, education, and political activism.[^cite_note-efas-11] Though the movement's stated goal was to promote social and [political Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_Islam), many analysts think that it was mainly formed from as an [anti-communist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-communist) force, which received favour from the British rulers.[^cite_note-hasnat-1]

Initially, Jamaat-e-Islami opposed the creation of a separate state of [Pakistan](23235.md) for the Muslims of India, arguing that concept violated the Islamic doctrine of the *[ummah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ummah)*.[^cite_note-Oh_Irene_2007-12] He saw the partition as creating a temporal border that would divide [Muslims](19541.md) from one another,[^cite_note-13] while advocating for the whole of India to be reclaimed for Islam.[^cite_note-14] However, Maududi eventually concluded supporting the creation of a Muslim-majority Pakistan as an opportunity to establish his envisioned Islamic state.[^cite_note-efas-11]

After the creation of Pakistan, Jamaat-e-Islami divided into separate Indian and Pakistani national organisations. When [East Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Pakistan) became independent as Bangladesh, the East Pakistan wing split out of the [Jamaat-e-Islami Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamaat-e-Islami_Pakistan) and became Jamaat-e-Islami Bangladesh.[^cite_note-efas-11][^cite_note-HHPBMM2010:_171-15][^cite_note-16]

### In East Pakistan (1955–1971)

Following the establishment of Pakistan, significant imbalance emerged between the eastern and the western wings of the country. The [East Bengal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Bengal) (later East Pakistan) remained structurally disadvantaged comparing to its western counterpart, in terms of both political and economic conditions. This also contributed to regional fractionalization of the party. For example, East Bengal chapter of Jamaat supported the [Bengali Language Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengali_Language_Movement) in 1952, which was opposed by the central party in West Pakistan as they classified it as "calls for a greater role for [Bengali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengalis) identity in the State".[^cite_note-efas-11]

Though initially favouring [martial law](50477.md), the East Pakistan Jamaat-e-Islami participated in the democratic movement against president [Ayub Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayub_Khan_\(Field_Marshal\)) following his government's family laws, which they deemed as un-Islamic.[^cite_note-efas-11] Emir of Jamaat-e-Islami East Pakistan branch [Ghulam Azam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghulam_Azam) was a member of the all-party democratic alliance was formed in 1965 against Khan's rule, which also included [Maulana Abdul Hamid Khan Bhashani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdul_Hamid_Khan_Bhashani) and [Bangabandhu Sheikh Mujibur Rahman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheikh_Mujibur_Rahman).[^cite_note-efas-11][^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19]

During the late 1960s, Jamaat‑e‑Islami strongly resisted the rising [socialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist) programs promoted by leaders such as [Zulfikar Ali Bhutto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zulfikar_Ali_Bhutto) in [West Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Pakistan) and Bhashani in East Pakistan. The party actively campaigned against these agendas, organized a large coalition of Islamic scholars and clerics, over a hundred *[ulema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulema)*, who issued statements denouncing the [Pakistan Peoples Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan_Peoples_Party)'s socialism as "[atheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheism)" and "[anti‑Islam](2394308.md)".[^cite_note-20][^cite_note-21]

As the [Six-Point Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six_Point_movement) gained momentum in East Pakistan from 1966 onwards, Jamaat-e-Islami adopted a firm stance against it. The Six-Point Movement, put forth by the Awami League, demanded for extensive autonomy for East Pakistan and was viewed by [Bengali nationalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengali_nationalism) as a charter for self-rule. The party viewed the movement as a divisive, secessionist agenda that threatened the solidarity of the Muslim nation and the integrity of Pakistan, as Jamaat-e-Islami's [pan-Islamist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-Islamism) ideology emphasized the unity of the country based on Islamic principles.[^cite_note-22]

In the [1970 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1970_Pakistani_general_election), Jamaat failed to gain any [National Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Assembly_of_Pakistan) seat from East Pakistan.[^cite_note-hasnat-1]

#### Bangladesh Liberation War (1971)



[Ghulam Azam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghulam_Azam), leader of the East Pakistan Jamaat, meeting with the [Governor of East Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governor_of_East_Pakistan) [Tikka Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tikka_Khan), 4 April 1971

In 1971, Jamaat-e-Islami Pakistan opposed the independence of Bangladesh and the dismemberment of Pakistan. Ghulam Azam, then head of Jamaat-e-Islami East Pakistan, delivered several speeches after 25 March 1971, excerpts of which were regularly published in the party's mouthpiece, *[The Daily Sangram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Daily_Sangram)*. On 30 June in Lahore, while speaking to journalists, Azam stated that his party was making every effort to suppress what he described as the activities of "miscreants" in East Pakistan, and that many Jamaat workers had been killed by these elements as a result of their efforts.[^cite_note-sangram1971-23]

The party actively supported the [military intervention in East Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Searchlight) and participated in a variety of [human rights abuses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights_abuse) against the civilian population of the region.[^cite_note-efas-11] It played a key role in organizing pro‑Pakistan collaborationist bodies. On 4 April 1971, twelve pro‑Pakistan leaders, including [Nurul Amin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nurul_Amin), Azam, and [Syed Khwaja Khairuddin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syed_Khwaja_Khairuddin), met [General Tikka Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tikka_Khan) of the [Pakistan Army](1965925.md) and assured him of their cooperation in suppressing the independence movement. Following a series of meetings, they announced the formation of the [East Pakistan Central Peace Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Pakistan_Central_Peace_Committee), which initially included 140 members, among them 96 Jamaat‑e‑Islami members who began training at an Ansar camp on Khanjahan Ali Road in [Khulna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khulna).[^cite_note-24][^cite_note-25] The committee was also alleged to have been involved in recruiting [Razakar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Razakars_\(Pakistan\)) paramilitaries to assist the Pakistan Army.[^cite_note-26]

On 12 October 1971, President [Yahya Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yahya_Khan) declared that elections would take place between 25 November and 9 December. Jamaat‑e‑Islami East Pakistan decided that the party would participate in the local elections. According to a government declaration issued on 2 November, 53 candidates were to be elected without contest, and Jamaat‑e‑Islami secured 14 of these uncontested seats.[^cite_note-muldhara-27][^cite_note-herald-28]

On 2 December 1971, according to a report in *[The Daily Ittefaq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Daily_Ittefaq)*, Azam held a one‑hour and ten‑minute meeting with President Khan in [Rawalpindi](343524.md). After the meeting, Azam addressed a press conference where he urged the public to give their full support to the armed forces in dealing with the ongoing crisis. He again described the [Mukti Bahini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mukti_Bahini) as an enemy force and stated that the Razakars were sufficient to confront them, further calling for an increase in the number of Razakars.[^cite_note-29]

### In Bangladesh (1971–present)

#### Ban (1972–1976)

Following the [independence of Bangladesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_of_Bangladesh), religion-based politics was banned by the government of [Sheikh Mujibur Rahman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheikh_Mujibur_Rahman), then-prime minister of Bangladesh.[^cite_note-hasnat-1] Jamaat's top leaders fled to Pakistan, and Ghulam Azam's citizenship was scrapped by the Bangladeshi government.[^cite_note-autogenerated3-30] Azam first fled to Pakistan and organized "*East Pakistan Recovery Week*". As information about his participation in the killing of civilians came to light "a strong groundswell of resentment against" East Pakistan JI leadership developed and Azam and [Maulana Abdur Rahim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdur_Rahim_\(scholar\)) were sent to Saudi Arabia. In Saudi Arabia, Azam and some of his followers successfully appealed for donations to "defend Islam" in Bangladesh, asserting that the ruling government was undermining Islam by imposing [secularism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secularism) and socialism, and the Hindu minority there were "killing Muslims and burning their homes."[^cite_note-BtNA-48-31][^cite_note-efas-11] Many of the Jamaat-aligned leaders in Bangladesh, hiding their identity, mixed with then-active opposition groups in Bangladesh, such as the [Jatiya Samajtantrik Dal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jatiya_Samajtantrik_Dal).[^cite_note-hasnat-1]

#### Rehabilitation (1976–1991)



[Abbas Ali Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbas_Ali_Khan), the acting emir of the Jamaat, speaking in a rally at [Baitul Mukarram](1345138.md), 1980s

In 1975, [Sheikh Mujibur Rahman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheikh_Mujibur_Rahman) was [assassinated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assassination_of_Sheikh_Mujibur_Rahman) by a group of officers of [Bangladesh Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh_Army), which resulted a drastic change in Bangladeshi politics. Post-Mujibur governments were immediately recognized by both Saudi Arabia and Pakistan, and Jamaat-e-Islami resumed political activities in Bangladesh.[^cite_note-autogenerated3-30] Following the [Sipahi–Janata Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sipahi%E2%80%93Janata_Revolution) of November 1975, Bangladesh's *[de facto](43594.md)* military leader and later president Major General [Ziaur Rahman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ziaur_Rahman) actively pushed for the [Islamization in the country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamization_in_Bangladesh).

On 4 May 1976, president [ASM Sayem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Sadat_Mohammad_Sayem) issued the Proclamation Order III that abolished the Article 38 of the [constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Bangladesh). The article prohibited the formation of political parties or groupings based on religious orientation. Abolition of the article allowed religiously motivated political groups to participate in the elections,[^cite_note-ajis-32] paving the way for the return of groups like the [Muslim League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh_Muslim_League), Jamaat-e-Islami & [Nizam-e-Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nizam-e-Islam_Party) previously banned for their opposition to the Liberation War.[^cite_note-33]

In 1976, Jamaat-aligned leaders, uniting with some other Islamist parties, formed a political platform named [Islamic Democratic League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Democratic_League) (IDL). According to Jamaat's own website, six Jamaat-aligned leaders were elected to the [Jatiya Sangsad](577333.md) in [1979 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1979_Bangladeshi_general_election) from this platform.[^cite_note-hasnat-1] President Ziaur Rahman also allowed Azam to return to Bangladesh as the leader of Jamaat-e-Islami.[^cite_note-autogenerated3-30]

In May 1979, [Abbas Ali Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbas_Ali_Khan) presided a convention of Jamaat-aligned IDL leaders under the name of "Jamaat-e-Islami". At the convention, Azam's speech was read out for the first time in independent Bangladesh. Jamaat's new constitution and the party's four-point programme was approved in the convention. On 27 May 1979, Jamaat-e-Islami Bangladesh publicly appeared and officially revealed itself in Bangladesh. Azam was elected as the emir of the party, but did not appear in public until 1981. As he wasn't given citizenship of Bangladesh until 1991, Khan served as the acting leader of the party.[^cite_note-hasnat-1]

Jamaat actively participed in the anti-Ershad movements throughout the 1980s. It participated in the [1986 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1986_Bangladeshi_general_election) and secured 10 seats, and boycotted the [1988 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_Bangladeshi_general_election).[^cite_note-hasnat-1] However, unlike most other anti-Ershad opposition parties, the Jamaat didn't opposed the Eighth Amendment Bill that declared Islam as the [state religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_religion) of Bangladesh in 1988.[^cite_note-ajis-32]

#### Entry into mainstream politics (1991–2013)

Between 1991 and 2007, two [secular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secularism) political parties in Bangladesh, the [Awami League](404861.md) (AL) and [Bangladesh Nationalist Party](309760.md) (BNP), established strategic partnerships with the Jamaat. Previously, both had sought assistance from the party to set up a government or accelerate political movements for overthrowing the dictatorship. The alliances were primarily driven by the political advantage and "recognition of Islam's increasing significance among Muslim voters".[^cite_note-ajis-32]

After the end of [Ershad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hussain_Muhammad_Ershad)'s regime in 1990, Jamaat participated in the [1991 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1991_Bangladeshi_general_election) and secured 18 seats. The BNP, which won most seats, needed [legislative support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence_and_supply) from a party to form government.[^cite_note-ajis-32] Jamaat extended support to the party on the condition of providing Azam with Bangladeshi citizenship.[^cite_note-hasnat-1] In 1992, however, mass protests began against Azam and Jamaat. The protests were headed by [Jahanara Imam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jahanara_Imam), an author who lost her elder son, [Shafi Imam Rumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shafi_Imam_Rumi) in the [Bangladesh War of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh_War_of_Independence). Azam's citizenship was challenged in a case that went to the [Bangladesh Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh_Supreme_Court), as he only held a Pakistani passport. Absent prosecution of Azam for war crimes, the Supreme Court ruled that he had to be allowed to have a Bangladeshi passport and the freedom to resume his political activities.[^cite_note-34]

From 1995, Jamaat allied with the AL and protested against the BNP government in support of the [caretaker government system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caretaker_government_of_Bangladesh).[^cite_note-ajis-32] The party boycotted the [February 1996 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/February_1996_Bangladeshi_general_election). Later, in the [June 1996 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/June_1996_Bangladeshi_general_election), which held under the caretaker government, Jamaat secured only three seats.[^cite_note-hasnat-1]

In 1998, the Jamaat for the first time agreed to join in a formal alliance with the BNP, despite having ideological differences with the party ([Bangladeshi nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladeshi_nationalism) and Islamism).[^cite_note-ajis-32] The BNP, Jamaat, [Jatiya Party (Ershad)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jatiya_Party_\(Ershad\)) and the [Jatiya Party (Naziur)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jatiya_Party_\(Naziur\)) united to form the [Four Party Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Party_Alliance) in 1999. The alliance got landslide victory in the [2001 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001_Bangladeshi_general_election), along with Jamaat got 17 seats. Following the election, the party got first chance to join a [coalition government](6036.md), and two of its senior leaders, [Motiur Rahman Nizami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motiur_Rahman_Nizami) and [Ali Ahsan Mohammad Mojaheed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_Ahsan_Mohammad_Mojaheed), the emir and the secretary-general respectively, became ministers in the [cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Khaleda_ministry) of [Khaleda Zia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khaleda_Zia).[^cite_note-hasnat-1] During its alliance with the BNP, the Jamaat decisively contributed to the gradual [Islamization in the country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamization_in_Bangladesh).[^cite_note-efas-11]

During the [2006–08 political crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006%E2%80%932008_Bangladeshi_political_crisis), [police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh_Police) arrested Jamaat chief and former Industry Minister Nizami from his home in a graft case on 19 May 2008.[^cite_note-35] Earlier, two former cabinet ministers of the immediate past BNP-Jamaat led coalition government, Abdul Mannan Bhuiyan and Shamsul Islam were sent to [Dhaka Central Jail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Dhaka_Central_Jail), after they surrendered before the court.[^cite_note-36][^cite_note-37]

In [2008 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Bangladeshi_general_election), the party only received two seats as a part of the alliance.[^cite_note-hasnat-1] The BNP was concerned as Jamaat had been their primary political partner in the Four-Party Alliance.[^cite_note-38] At the same year, the party had to change its name from "Jamaat-e-Islami Bangladesh" to "**Bangladesh Jamaat-e-Islami**" on the pressure of the [Bangladesh Election Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh_Election_Commission).[^cite_note-hasnat-1] Meanwhile, by the time, it already had emerged as the largest Islamist party in the country.[^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40]

#### Prosecution, deregistration and ban (2013–2024)

Further information: [International Crimes Tribunal (Bangladesh)](33827498.md) and [2013 Shahbag protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2013_Shahbag_protests)

After Awami League formed government, it started to prosecute [war crimes](48626.md) in the Liberation War. In 2011, the [International Crimes Tribunal](33827498.md) (ICT) was formed for the prosecution of war crimes. Many of the Jamaat's top leaders were jailed and executed on the verdict of ICT. In December 2013, Jamaat leader [Abdul Quader Mollah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdul_Quader_Mollah) was executed for the war crimes. In 2014, [Ghulam Azam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghulam_Azam) died in prison. In 2015 and 2016, Mojaheed and Nizami were executed for war crimes, respectively. The detainment and execution of top leaders made the party functionally weak. While international observers, including the [Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_the_High_Commissioner_for_Human_Rights), raised suspicions concerning the fairness of the trials, which targeted the ruling party's political opponents, the verdicts enjoyed widespread societal support. The condemnation of Mollah was the key motivation for the [Shahbag Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2013_Shahbag_protests) in 2013, which demanded Mollah's execution, ban on Jamaat, and boycott of affiliated organizations.

However, the trial also received counter protests from [Bangladesh Islami Chhatra Shibir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh_Islami_Chhatra_Shibir), the male student wing of Jamaat. In February 2013, following the verdict by the ICT and the announcement of death sentence of [Delwar Hossain Sayeedi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delwar_Hossain_Sayeedi), a leader of Jamaat-e-Islami,[^cite_note-tg3Oct2011-41][^cite_note-thedailystar.net-42] supporters of Jamaat and Shibir were involved in [anti-Hindu violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2013_Bangladesh_anti-Hindu_violence); law enforcement killed 44 protesters and wounded 250.[^cite_note-Hindu_temple-3-43][^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45] More than 50 temples were damaged, and more than 1,500 houses and business establishments of Hindus were torched in [Gaibandha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaibandha_District), [Chittagong](380451.md), [Rangpur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rangpur,_Bangladesh), [Sylhet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylhet), [Chapainawabganj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapainawabganj), [Bogra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogra) and in many other districts of the country,[^cite_note-49] By March 2013, more than 87 people had been killed by law enforcement agencies.[^cite_note-asianews-50] During the violence, Jamaat-e-Islami supporters called for the fall of the Awami League regime.

On 27 January 2009, the Supreme Court issued a ruling after 25 people from different Islamic organisations, including [Bangladesh Tarikat Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh_Tarikat_Federation)'s Secretary General Syed Rezaul Haque Chandpuri, [Zaker Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaker_Party)'s Secretary General Munshi Abdul Latif and Sammilita Islami Jote's President Maulana Ziaul Hasan, filed a joint petition. Jamaat-e-Islami chief Nizami, Secretary Mojaheed and the Election Commission Secretary were given six weeks time to reply, but they did not. The ruling asked to explain *"why the Jamaat's registration should not be declared illegal"*. As a verdict of the ruling, [High Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh_Supreme_Court) cancelled the registration of the Jamaat-e-Islami on 1 August 2013,[^cite_note-SC-51][^cite_note-52] ruling that the party is unfit to contest national polls because its charter puts [Allah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allah) above democratic process.[^cite_note-57] On 5 August 2013, the Supreme Court rejected Jamaat's plea against the High Court.[^cite_note-58] From that time, Jamaat became virtually absent from the mainstream politics.

Following the cancellation of registration, the party could not participated in [2014](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Bangladeshi_general_election), [2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Bangladeshi_general_election) and [2024 general elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Bangladeshi_general_election). Some of its leaders contested in the 2018 election from BNP's ticket, but could not secure any seats. The party boycotted the 2014 and 2024 elections.

The Jamaat-e-Islami and its student wing Shibir extended their support and actively participated in the [July Uprising](78109234.md) in 2024.[^cite_note-59] On this pretext, [Sheikh Hasina](623439.md)'s regime banned the party on 1 August 2024.[^cite_note-63]

#### Resurgence (2024–present)



Jamaat delegation led by [Shafiqur Rahman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shafiqur_Rahman) meeting with the [chief adviser](9219750.md) [Muhammad Yunus](548105.md), 28 November 2024

Following the [Resignation of Sheikh Hasina](77539154.md), the ban was reversed by the [interim government](77545215.md) on 28 August 2024.[^cite_note-64] Bangladesh Jamaat-e-Islami also regained registration after an order by the [Appellate Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appellate_Division,_Supreme_Court_of_Bangladesh) of the [Bangladesh Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh_Supreme_Court) on 1 June 2025.[^cite_note-65][^cite_note-66]

Following the [July Uprising](78109234.md), with BNP, it came out as one of the two mainstream parties in the country.[^cite_note-67] It formed [11 Party Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/11_Party_Alliance) with several Islamist parties and the [National Citizen Party](79340683.md). The party secured 68 seats in [2026 general election](72614737.md), and became the main opposition for the first time.[^cite_note-hasnat-1]

In July 2026, [Hefazat-e-Islam Bangladesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hefazat-e-Islam_Bangladesh), the largest [qawmi madrasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qawmi_madrasa)-based Islamic advocacy group in Bangladesh, expressed concern over the *en masse* joining of qawmi madrasa students in Jamaat, as they consider Jamaat not upholding their version of *[aqidah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqidah)*.[^cite_note-68]

Jamaat's rally at [Suhrawardy Udyan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suhrawardy_Udyan) demanding [proportional representation](23298.md), 2025

Jamaat leaders and publications have variously described their party as "modern [liberal democratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_democratic)",[^cite_note-69][^cite_note-70] "[Muslim democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_democracy)",[^cite_note-bergman-7] "[progressive and centrist Islamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberalism_and_progressivism_within_Islam)"[^cite_note-71] and "moderate [Islamist](15012.md)".[^cite_note-72] Although third-party independent sources have classified it as [right-wing](56522.md)[^cite_note-73][^cite_note-74][^cite_note-75][^cite_note-76][^cite_note-77] to [far-right](221220.md)[^cite_note-78][^cite_note-79][^cite_note-80][^cite_note-81][^cite_note-82][^cite_note-83][^cite_note-84] in the [left-right spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left-right_political_spectrum). Apart from being Islamist, the party has also been described as [conservative](6675.md),[^cite_note-laila2026-8][^cite_note-85][^cite_note-86] [neo-Islamist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-Islamism),[^cite_note-saj-87] and [reformist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reformism).[^cite_note-88] In recent times, however, according to AKM Waresul Islam, professor at [North South University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_South_University), Jamaat has attempted to reposition itself from being a conservative right-wing party to a [centre-right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre-right_politics) party.[^cite_note-89]

Originally, [Jamaat-e-Islami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamaat-e-Islami)'s founder Sayyid Abul A'la Maududi developed the idea of "[theo-democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_and_democracy#Abul_A'la_Maududi)", a combination of [democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy) and [theocracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theocracy) whereby the state operates in full accordance with [Islamic law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_law) in all aspects of life.[^cite_note-gup-90] He criticized both the [popular sovereignty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_sovereignty) as contradictory to *ḥākimīyah* (sovereignty of the [God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Islam)) and the [European](9239.md) style-theocracy where power is bestowed to the [clergies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clergy), rather envisioned an ideal state that is ruled by the entire [Muslim community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ummah) in accordance with [sharia](28840.md).[^cite_note-91][^cite_note-gup-90] In Maududi's envisioned sharia-based [totalitarian state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totalitarian_state), inspired by the [Benito Mussolini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benito_Mussolini)'s [Fascist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Fascist_Party) and the [Communist Party of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union), democracy, freedom, individualism, and equal rights for people would be non-existent, and all non-Muslims would enjoy "inferior" rights as *[Dhimmis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhimmi)*, obligated to pay *[jizya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jizya)*.[^cite_note-saj-87] However, Bangladesh Jamaat-e-Islami's present "neo-Islamist" politics might differ Maududi's visions.[^cite_note-saj-87]

In 2012, Jamaat had to amend some fundamental principles of its constitution to meet the conditions for the registration of parties set by the [Election Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_Commission_of_Bangladesh), as the previous version allegedly conflicted to the "democratic and noncommunal structure" of the [Constitution of Bangladesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Bangladesh) and Representation of the People Order, 1972. After the amendments, the party replaced the establishment of an "Islamic system" with the "democratic system" as its stated goal. Likewise, "efforts to establish Islam as given by [Allah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Islam) and shown by the [Messenger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad)" was replaced with "to establish a society based on equality and justice through a democratic system and to achieve the pleasure of Allah Almighty" as main objective.[^cite_note-92]

Following the execution and imprisonment of senior leaders convicted of involvement in the [1971 genocide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1971_Bangladesh_genocide), Jamaat experienced internal fragmentation.[^cite_note-93] The reformist factions proposed ideological moderation and democratic participation, while hardliners insisted on maintaining traditional Islamist aims. Multiple splinter groups emerged, some more radical, some abandoning formal politics.[^cite_note-94]

### Social stance

In recent times, the party has become open on female-related issues, such as [right to work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_to_work) outside and [freedom of clothing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_to_clothing).[^cite_note-95] Similarly, the party also expressed support to the rights of religious minorities and protection of their properties.[^cite_note-96] The party also retracted from its stated goal of the establishment of a [sharia-based state](2646438.md), and focused on the creation of an "honest people's government".[^cite_note-saj-87] Despite these initiatives, the party still officially rejects female leadership and continues to uphold its traditional philosophy of introducing sharia through "electoral process".[^cite_note-saj-87]

Under Shafiqur Rahman's leadership, the party has attracted a wide range of politicians, religious minorities and the [freedom fighters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mukti_Bahini).[^cite_note-97] Additional secretary-general of the party Mahbub Zubayer claimed that there were "hundreds of thousands" of freedom fighters within Jamaat, a claim which is difficult to verify independently.[^cite_note-bt-98] However, there are two gazetted freedom fighters who were elected as the [Member of Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Member_of_Parliament_\(Bangladesh\)) in 2026 — [Md. Abdul Wares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Md._Abdul_Wares) for [Gaibandha-5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaibandha-5) and [Gazi Nazrul Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gazi_Nazrul_Islam) for [Satkhira-4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satkhira-4).[^cite_note-bt-98]

### Economic stance

Maududi was a staunch critic of [capitalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalism).[^cite_note-99] According to a Jamaat publication titled *An Introduction to Bangladesh Jamaate Islami*, the party aims to turn Bangladesh into an "Islamic [welfare state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welfare_state)".[^cite_note-100] It also runs charities that provide religious, social, food, and medical services at the local level, which helped the party build a strong network at the grassroots.[^cite_note-mubashar2025-101]

Since the party's resurgence following the July Uprising, the party has focused on [welfarist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welfarism) issues like [social justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_justice) and [anti-corruption](58111406.md).[^cite_note-islamicleft-102] The party supports extensive [tax cuts](455990.md), reducing [VAT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_Added_Tax), [interest-free loans](33728114.md) and [public private ownership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public-private_partnership) for factories with workers' ownership.[^cite_note-103][^cite_note-104] Shafi Md Mostofa claimed that the party is rebranding itself in the aftermath of the revolution by putting itself in the "[Islamic left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_left)".[^cite_note-islamicleft-102]

### 2026 manifesto

Before [2026 general election](72614737.md), the party unveiled its [electoral manifesto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_manifesto) under the slogan "An uncompromising Bangladesh in the national interest", which prioritises 26 points:[^cite_note-105]

1. Building an uncompromising state based on independence, sovereignty and national interest;

2. Establishing a humane Bangladesh free from discrimination, founded on justice and fairness;

3. Empowering youth and prioritising their role in state governance;

4. Creating a safe, dignified and participatory state for women;

5. Ensuring a drug-free, extortion-free and terrorism-free society through overall improvement in law and order;

6. Building a modern, smart, technology-driven society;

7. Creating large-scale employment in technology, manufacturing, agriculture and industry;

8. Free applications for government jobs, merit-based recruitment and elimination of all discrimination;

9. Reforming the banking and financial sectors to restore confidence and build a sustainable, transparent, business-friendly economy;

10. Ensuring a fair electoral environment, including elections under [proportional representation](23298.md) (PR), strengthening the caretaker government system, and consolidating effective democracy;

11. Bringing about an agricultural revolution through technology use and increased support for farmers

12. Ensuring completely adulteration-free food security by 2030 and implementing the "Three-Zero Vision" — zero [environmental degradation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_degradation), zero waste and zero flood risk — to build a green, clean Bangladesh

13. Promoting small and medium enterprises and ensuring investment-friendly industrialisation and job creation;

14. Increasing workers' wages and living standards and ensuring safe, decent working conditions, especially for women;

15. Guaranteeing voting rights and all other rights of [expatriates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladeshi_diaspora) and ensuring their realistic participation in nation-building;

16. Establishing equal citizenship for all as [Bangladeshis](28958994.md), not as majority or minority, and ensuring special support for disadvantaged groups;

17. Providing modern, universal healthcare and gradually ensuring free advanced treatment for the poor and helpless;

18. Fundamentally reforming the education system in line with global needs and gradually ensuring free education;

19. Keeping prices within people's purchasing power and ensuring access to all basic needs;

20. Overhauling transport systems and reducing travel time between the capital and divisional cities to two to three hours; improving regional and urban transport;

21. Ensuring low-cost housing for lower- and middle-income families;

22. Continuing trials and reforms to fully dismantle the [fascist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fascist_\(insult\)) system and prevent its return;

23. Introducing a universal social security system to ensure safe working lives and international-standard protection for all citizens; and

24. Establishing good governance through transparency and accountability and building a happy, prosperous welfare state, with honest leadership and institutional reform to eliminate corruption.

### Voter base

Following the [July Uprising](78109234.md), surveys show that the Jamaat's strongest demographic base are younger and more educated voters, who are likely to be impressed by the perceived discipline and integrity of the party.[^cite_note-laila2026-8] Likewise, [socially conservative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_conservative) women also favour the party.[^cite_note-netranews-9] According to [David Bergman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bergman_\(journalist\)), voters who are primarily concerned to the perceived [Indian influence in Bangladesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_influence_in_Bangladesh), are also likely to vote for the party.[^cite_note-bergman-7]

A June 2025 conducted survey by the [South Asian Network on Economic Modeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Asian_Network_on_Economic_Modeling) (SANEM) on the [Bangladeshis](28958994.md) aged between 15 and 35 found that the Jamaat's projected vote share was comparatively high among the young males, comparing with the young females of the country. The survey found that 22.21% males and 20.57% females intended to vote the party in [2026 general election](72614737.md). The survey also found that 21.45% youths overall in the country intended to vote the party.[^cite_note-106]

## Organization

The party has a three-level decision-making body – Central Executive Council, Central Majlis-e-Shura and Central Working Council. Executive Council is the highest organ of the party, which is responsible for reviewing and executing the policies made by the Majlis-e-Shura and the Working Council. As of 2026, there are no women in the Central Executive Council.[^cite_note-jobayer-107]

## Controversies

Bangladesh Jamaat-e-Islami has been the subject of extensive controversy since the country's independence, largely due to its documented opposition to the [1971 Liberation War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh_Liberation_War), convictions of its senior leaders for war crimes, allegations of violent extremism, repeated involvement in political violence, and sustained criticism from human rights groups, international observers, and the Bangladeshi public.[^cite_note-109] According to Ankita Sanyal, though the incumbent party chief Rahman reportedly sought "unconditional apology" multiple times for the mistakes committed since 1947 (including 1971), its leaders continue to indulge in [historical negationism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_negationism) related to 1971.[^cite_note-110]

### Allegations of militant links and ideological extremism

Bangladeshi security agencies have repeatedly reported ideological ties between Jamaat, its student wing [Islami Chhatra Shibir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islami_Chhatra_Shibir), and extremist organizations such as [Jamaat-ul-Mujahideen Bangladesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamaat-ul-Mujahideen_Bangladesh) (JMB) and [Harkat-ul-Jihad al-Islami](5592225.md) (HuJI-B).[^cite_note-111] Historically, the Islami Chhatra Shibir has frequently been labeled one of the most violent student organizations in South Asia.[^cite_note-112] Although Jamaat denies institutional ties,[^cite_note-113] extremism investigations have reported overlaps in recruitment patterns, ideological messaging, and activist networks.

### Violence and human rights abuses

Following the war-crime verdict by ICT, Jamaat and Islami Chhatra Shibir engaged in violent street movements, including arson attacks, clashes with police, vandalism, and shutdowns of transportation networks during 2013–14 violence.[^cite_note-114] Jamaat–Shibir activists were widely reported to have engaged in firebombing, destruction of public property, and violent attacks on security forces.[^cite_note-115] Its supporters were accused of murdering opponent political party activists and instigating religious riots by spreading fraudulent news.[^cite_note-START-1-116][^cite_note-DS-0002-117]

Activists from Jamaat and particularly Islami Chhatra Shibir have been implicated in violent attacks, hacking assaults, extortion, and intimidation of rival student groups and journalists.[^cite_note-118] Reports by [Human Rights Watch](46530.md) document repeated incidents in which Jamaat–Shibir supporters engaged in election-period violence, intimidation, and attacks on minority communities during political unrest.[^cite_note-119]

### International condemnation and public perception

Jamaat's wartime role and ideological positions have attracted global criticism. Pakistan and Turkey condemned executions of Jamaat leaders, while India supported the war-crimes trials.[^cite_note-120] Western governments expressed due-process concerns but acknowledged the severity of the crimes associated with Jamaat leadership.[^cite_note-121]

Public perception of Jamaat declined sharply after the rise of the [Shahbag movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2013_Shahbag_protests) in 2013, in which millions demanded a ban on the party and harsher punishment for convicted war criminals.[^cite_note-122]

### Contradictory positions

According to [The Diplomat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Diplomat_\(magazine\)), the party has been accused of "[doublespeak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doublespeak)" on minority, women and sharia-related issues. In 2008, the party amended its constitution to include non-Muslims as "associated members" in the pressure of the Election Commission. Yet, full membership or *rukon* is constitutionally restricted for the non-Muslims, means that the non-Muslims cannot participate in the decision-making, leadership or policy formulation, which challenges the party's claimed so-called "inclusive politics". Besides, its constitution continue to advocate for the implementation of sharia, yet, in recent times, the party's top leaders have shown tendency of avoiding the issue in formal statements. Despite this, such advocacy continue to surface in the grassroots level, and, even, there have been accusations against the party of describing voting as a "religious duty" and linking it to the attainment of [paradise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jannah).[^cite_note-thediplomat-108]

Although not stated in the constitution, the party *[de facto](43594.md)* bars women from leadership position, arguing religious obligations. This contradictory stance doesn't only come to challenge the [Constitution of Bangladesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Bangladesh), but also questions its alignment with the Bangladesh Nationalist Party government between 2001 and 2006 under the female leader [Khaleda Zia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khaleda_Zia).[^cite_note-thediplomat-108]
