# Climatological normal



Annual U.S. temperature compared to the 20th-century average for each U.S. Climate Normals period from 1901–1930 to 1991–2020.

**Climatological normal** or **climate normal** (**CN**) is a 30-year average of a weather variable for a given [time of year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_of_year).[^cite_note-var-1] Most commonly, a CN refers to a particular [month of year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Month_of_year), but it may also refer to a broader scale, such as a specific [meteorological season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteorological_season).[^cite_note-2] More recently, CN have been reported for narrower scales, such as [day of year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Day_of_year) and even hourly scale.[^cite_note-3]

Climatological normals are used as an average or baseline to evaluate climate events and provide context for year-to-year variability. Normals can be calculated for a variety of weather variables including temperature and precipitation and rely on data from [weather stations](206018.md). Variability from the 30-year averages is typical and [climate variability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_variability) looks at the magnitude of extremes.[^cite_note-var-1] **Climatological standard normals** are overlapping periods updated every decade: 1971–2000, 1981–2010, 1991–2020, etc.

The term "normal" first appeared in the literature by [Heinrich Wilhelm Dove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Wilhelm_Dove) in 1840 and the concept was formalized by the [International Meteorological Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Meteorological_Committee) in 1872.[^cite_note-Guttman-4] The use of the 30-year period of normals began in 1935 with the 1901-30 period.[^cite_note-5] The continued use of 30 year normals has increasingly been called into question due to substantial evidence that the [stationarity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stationary_process) of climate statistics can no longer be taken for granted due to [climate change](5042951.md).[^cite_note-Guttman-4][^cite_note-6] This has led to alternative definitions such as "Optimal Climate Normal" and the "Hinge Fit" approach to supplement the standard 30 year normals which are still commonly used.[^cite_note-7] More recently, modern smoothing techniques such as spline-based methods have also been proposed and used operationally to estimate the expected (mean) state of climate variables for the coming year or years .[^cite_note-8]
