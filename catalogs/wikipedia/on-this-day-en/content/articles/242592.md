A **fisherman** or **fisher** is someone who captures [fish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish) and other animals from a [body of water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_of_water), or gathers [shellfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shellfish).[^cite_note-1]

Worldwide, there are about 38 million [commercial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_fishing) and [subsistence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artisan_fishing) fishers and [fish farmers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish_farming).[^cite_note-FAO-2] Fishermen may be professional or [recreational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recreational_fishing). Fishing has existed as a means of obtaining food since the [Mesolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesolithic) period.[^cite_note-Early_humans_followed_the_coast-3]

## History

Main article: [History of fishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_fishing)



Egyptians bringing in fish and splitting them for salting

Fishing has existed as a means of obtaining food since the [Mesolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesolithic) period. Fishing had become a major means of survival as well as a business venture.[^cite_note-4]

Fishing and fishermen have also influenced [Ancient Egyptian religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_religion); [mullets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mullet_\(fish\)) were worshipped as a sign of the arriving [flood season](50482.md). [Bastet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bastet) was often manifested in the form of a [catfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catfish). In [ancient Egyptian literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_religion), the process that [Amun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amun) used to create the world is associated with the [tilapia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tilapia)'s method of [mouth-brooding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mouth-brooding).

## Recreation



A fisherman and his catch, including small sharks, hooked on hand lines miles offshore in the [Seychelles](27288.md)

Main article: [Recreational fishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recreational_fishing)

Recreational fishing is fishing for pleasure or competition. It can be contrasted with commercial fishing, which is fishing for [economic profit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Profit_\(economics\)), or [subsistence fishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artisan_fishing), which is fishing for survival.

The most common form of recreational fishing is done with a [rod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fishing_rod), [reel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fishing_reel), [line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fishing_line), [hooks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish_hook) and any one of a wide range of [baits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fishing_bait). [Lures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fishing_lure) are frequently used in place of bait. Some people make handmade lures, including [plastic lures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soft_plastic_bait) and [artificial flies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_fly).

The practice of catching or attempting to catch fish with a hook is called [angling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angling), and fishers using this technique are sometimes referred to as *anglers*. When angling, it is sometimes expected or required that the fish be [caught and released](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catch_and_release). [Big-game fishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big-game_fishing) is fishing from boats to catch large open-water species such as [tuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuna), [sharks](43617.md) and [marlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marlin). [Noodling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noodling) and [trout tickling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trout_tickling) are also recreational activities.

## Safety issues



A Royal Air Force search and rescue Sea King helicopter comes to the aid of the French fishing vessel *Alf* during a storm in the Irish Sea.

See also: [Work in Fishing Convention 2007](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Work_in_Fishing_Convention_2007)

The [fishing industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fishing_industry) is hazardous for Artisan fishers. Between 1992 and 1999, US commercial [fishing vessels](10079376.md) averaged 78 deaths per year. The main contributors to fatalities are:[^cite_note-FAO_Profile-6]

- inadequate preparation for emergencies

- poor vessel maintenance and inadequate safety equipment

- lack of awareness of or ignoring stability issues.

Many fishermen, while accepting that fishing is dangerous, staunchly defend their independence. Many proposed laws and additional regulation to increase safety have been defeated because fishers oppose them.[^cite_note-FAO_Profile-6]

### Alaska

Alaska's commercial fishermen work in one of the world's harshest environments. The hardships they endure include isolated fishing grounds, high winds, seasonal darkness, very cold water, icing, and short fishing seasons, when very long work days are the norm. Fatigue, physical [stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_\(medicine\)), and financial pressures face most Alaska fishermen through their careers. Out of 948 work-related deaths in Alaska during 1990–2006, one-third (311) were of fishermen. This is estimated to be equivalent to an annual [fatality rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatality_rate) of 128 per 100,000 workers/year. This fatality rate is 26 times the overall U.S. work-related fatality rate of about 5 per 100,000 workers/year for the same time period.[^cite_note-NIOSH-7]

While the work-related fatality rate for commercial fishermen in Alaska is still very high, it does appear to be decreasing: since 1990, there has been a 51 percent decline in the annual fatality rate. The successes in commercial fishing are due in part to the [U.S. Coast Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Coast_Guard) implementing new safety requirements in the early 1990s. These safety requirements contributed to 96 percent of commercial fishermen surviving vessel sinkings/capsizings in 2004, whereas in 1991, only 73 percent survived. While the number of occupational deaths of commercial fishermen in Alaska has been reduced, there is a continuing pattern of losing 20 to 40 vessels every year. There are still about 100 fishermen who must be rescued each year from cold Alaska waters. Successful rescue is still dependent on the expertly trained personnel of the US Coast Guard [Search and Rescue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Search_and_Rescue) operations, but their efforts can be hindered by the harshness of seas and the weather. Furthermore, the people involved in Search and Rescue operations are themselves at considerable risk of injury or death during these rescue attempts.[^cite_note-NIOSH-7]
