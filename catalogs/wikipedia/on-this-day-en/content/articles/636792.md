[Football (soccer)](10568.md) is among the most popular sports in [Japan](15573.md),[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3] together with [baseball](3850.md), [tennis](29773.md), [golf](19568112.md), [sumo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumo), and [combat sports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combat_sports).[^cite_note-4] Around 40% of Japanese people consider themselves football fans.[^cite_note-5] The nationwide organization of football, the [Japan Football Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Football_Association), administers the professional football leagues, including [J.League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J.League), which is considered by many the most successful football league in Asia.[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10] Japan is also the country with the most comprehensively developed football in Asia in both [men](993546.md) and [women](3190583.md) as well as in both [futsal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_national_futsal_team) and [beach soccer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_national_beach_soccer_team).

## Football

See also: [Japan Football Association § History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Football_Association#History)

Although the official English name of the [Japan Football Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Football_Association) uses the term "football", the term *sakkā* (サッカー), derived from "soccer", is much more commonly used than *futtobōru* (フットボール). The JFA's Japanese name is *Nippon Sakkā Kyōkai*.

From 1885 to around 1908 in the [Meiji era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_era), *fūtobōru* (フートボール) was the most commonly used term, and *assoshieshon* (アッソシエーション) also appeared; these were often written together with *[kemari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kemari)* (蹴鞠), a ball game of the [Heian period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heian_period). During the [Taishō era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taish%C5%8D_era) and the early [Shōwa era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sh%C5%8Dwa_era), terms such as *ashiki futtobōru* (ア式フットボール), *ashiki shūkyū* (ア式蹴球), and *shūkyū* (蹴球) came into frequent use.[^cite_note-11] With previously exclusive Japanese terms replaced by American influence after the war, *sakkā* became more commonplace. In recent years, many professional teams have named themselves F.C.s (football clubs), with examples being [FC Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FC_Tokyo) and [Kyoto Sanga FC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto_Sanga_FC).

## History

The introduction of football in Japan is officially credited by the Japan Football Association, and numerous academic papers and books on the history of association football in Japan, to then Lieutenant-Commander [Archibald Lucius Douglas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archibald_Lucius_Douglas) of the Royal Navy and his subordinates, who from 1873 taught the game and its rules to Japanese navy cadets while acting as instructors at the [Imperial Japanese Navy Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Japanese_Navy_Academy) in Tsukiji, Tokyo.[^cite_note-12][^cite_note-13][^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15]

The first official football match in Japan is widely believed to have been held on February 18, 1888, between the [Yokohama Country & Athletic Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yokohama_Country_&_Athletic_Club) and [Kobe Regatta & Athletic Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kobe_Regatta_&_Athletic_Club). YC\&AC is the oldest running association football club in Japan as Association Football was introduced into the club on December 25, 1886, for training sessions starting from January 1887. The first Japanese association football club, founded as a football club, is considered to be [Tokyo Shukyu-dan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_Shukyu-dan), founded in 1917, which is now competing in the Tokyo Prefectural amateur league.

In the 1920s, football associations were organised and regional tournaments began in universities and high schools especially in Tokyo. In 1930, the [Japan national association football team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_national_association_football_team) was organised and had a 3–3 tie with [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China_national_football_team) for their first title at the [Far Eastern Championship Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Far_Eastern_Championship_Games). The Japan national team also participated in the [1936 Berlin Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1936_Summer_Olympics), the team had the first victory in an Olympic game with a 3–2 win over powerful [Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweden_men's_national_football_team).

Aside from the national cup, the [Emperor's Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor's_Cup) established in 1921, there had been several attempts at creating a senior-level national championship. The first was the All Japan Works Football Championship (AJWFC), established in 1948 and open only to company teams. The second was the All Japan Inter-City Football Championship (AJICFC), established in 1955 and separating clubs by cities (any club, works, university or autonomous, could represent their home city and qualify) but the Emperor's Cup remained dominated by universities until the late 1950s. All these tournaments were cups following [single-elimination](939680.md) formulas, similar to [Serie A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serie_A) in Italy before 1929.

The first organized national league, the [Japan Soccer League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Soccer_League), was organized in 1965 with eight amateur company clubs and replaced the AJWFC and AJICFC. At the [1968 Mexico Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1968_Summer_Olympics), the Japan national team, filled with the top JSL stars of the era, had its first big success winning third place and a bronze medal. Olympic success spurred the creation of a Second Division for the JSL and openings for the first few professional players, in the beginning, foreigners (mainly Brazilians), and a few from other countries, which also led to the country hosting its first international competition, the [1979 FIFA World Youth Championship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1979_FIFA_World_Youth_Championship). Japanese players, however, remained an amateur, having to work day jobs for the companies owning the clubs (or other companies if their clubs were autonomous). This limited the growth of the Japanese game, and many better Japanese players had to move abroad to make a living off the game, such as [Yasuhiko Okudera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yasuhiko_Okudera), the first Japanese player to play in a professional European club, ([1. FC Köln](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1._FC_K%C3%B6ln) of Germany). [UEFA](32332.md) and [CONMEBOL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CONMEBOL) aided the Japanese awareness of football by having the [Intercontinental Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intercontinental_Cup_\(1960%E2%80%932004\)) played in Tokyo as a neutral venue.



Japan national team at the [2018 FIFA World Cup](13327177.md) in Russia

In 1993, the [Japan Professional Football League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J.League) (commonly known as the *J.League*) was formed replacing the semi-professional Japan Soccer League as the new top-level club competition in Japan.[^cite_note-16] It consisted of some of the top clubs from the old JSL, fully professionalized, renamed to fit communities and with the corporate identity reduced to a minimum.[^cite_note-17] The new higher-standard league attracted many more spectators and helped the sport to hugely increase in popularity. The professionalized league also offered, and offers, incentives for amateur non-company clubs to become part of their ranks with no major backing from a company; major examples of community, non-company-affiliated clubs who rose through the [prefectural and regional ranks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_football_league_system) into the major leagues are [Albirex Niigata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albirex_Niigata) and [Oita Trinita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oita_Trinita).

Japan participated in its first-ever World Cup tournament at the [1998 FIFA World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1998_FIFA_World_Cup) held in France. In 2002, Japan co-hosted the [2002 FIFA World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2002_FIFA_World_Cup) with [Republic of Korea](27019.md). After this, the association football communities of both countries received the [FIFA Fair Play Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FIFA_Fair_Play_Award). The Japanese national team has reached the round of 16 on four occasions – as hosts in 2002, where they were knocked out by Turkey 1–0, in 2010, where they lost to Paraguay in penalties, in [2018](13327177.md) where they fell 2–3 to Belgium, and in the [2022 FIFA World Cup](17742072.md), where they lost at penalties against Croatia. Japan also qualified for the [2006 FIFA World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_FIFA_World_Cup) in Germany, the [2010 FIFA World Cup](168079.md) in South Africa, the [2014 FIFA World Cup](656933.md) in Brazil and the [2026 FIFA World Cup](36581929.md) in Canada, Mexico, and the United States.

## Football in fiction

See also: [Category\:Association football in anime and manga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category\:Association_football_in_anime_and_manga)

The first worldwide popular association football-oriented Japanese animation ([manga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manga)) series, *[Captain Tsubasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Captain_Tsubasa)*, was started in 1981. *Captain Tsubasa* was extremely popular among children of both genders in Japan. Its success led to much more association football manga being written, and it played a great role in association football history in Japan. Playing football became more popular than playing baseball in many schools throughout Japan from the 1980s due to the series.

*Captain Tsubasa* has also inspired the likes of prominent footballers such as [Hidetoshi Nakata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hidetoshi_Nakata),[^cite_note-18] [Seigo Narazaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seigo_Narazaki), [Zinedine Zidane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinedine_Zidane), [Francesco Totti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Totti), [Fernando Torres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fernando_Torres), [Christian Vieri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Vieri), [Giuseppe Sculli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giuseppe_Sculli), [James Rodríguez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Rodr%C3%ADguez), [Alexis Sánchez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexis_S%C3%A1nchez)[^cite_note-19] and [Alessandro Del Piero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alessandro_Del_Piero)[^cite_note-20] to play association football and choose it as a career. The inspiration for the character of [Tsubasa Oozora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsubasa_Oozora) came from a number of players, including most prominently [Musashi Mizushima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musashi_Mizushima), arguably the first Japanese footballer to play abroad, and whose move to [São Paulo FC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%A3o_Paulo_Futebol_Clube) as a ten-year-old boy was partly mimicked in the manga.[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-22]

The anime *[Giant Killing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giant_Killing)* revolves around a team's efforts to go from one of the worst professional teams in Japan to the best. Other works focusing on football include *[Hungry Heart: Wild Striker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungry_Heart:_Wild_Striker)* (from the same author of *Captain Tsubasa*), *[The Knight in the Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Knight_in_the_Area)*, *[Days](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Days_\(manga\))*, *[Inazuma Eleven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inazuma_Eleven)*, *[Aoashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aoashi)*, *[Be Blues!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Be_Blues!)* and *[Blue Lock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Lock)*.

## Women's football

Main article: [Women's football in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women's_football_in_Japan)

As in European countries, Japanese [women's football](191466.md) is organized on a promotion and relegation basis. The top flight of women's association football is the semi-professional [L. League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L._League) (currently billed as the *Nadeshiko League*). Most clubs are independent clubs, although the recent trend is to have women's sections of established J.League clubs.

The [national team](3190583.md) has enjoyed major success at the [FIFA Women's World Cup](244862.md), having achieved its greatest triumph ever by winning the [2011 FIFA Women's World Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_FIFA_Women's_World_Cup) in Germany[^cite_note-23] and finishing as runner-up in [2015](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_FIFA_Women's_World_Cup) in Canada.

## Championships and tournaments

### Domestic tournaments

- [J.League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J.League) (Japan Professional Football League) is the top national league in Japan with a J1, J2 and J3 League.

- [Japan Football League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Football_League) (JFL) is the national amateur league.

- [Emperor's Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor's_Cup) (since 1921) the national open cup.

- [J.League Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J.League_Cup) is the cup restricted to J.League members (usually J1 alone).

- [All Japan Adults Football Tournament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_Japan_Senior_Football_Championship), cup for clubs in regional leagues below JFL.

- [Japanese Regional Football Champions League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_Regional_Football_Champions_League), round-robin elimination tournament for the promotion of regional-league clubs into JFL.
