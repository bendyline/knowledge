**Media in Syria** encompasses the country’s newspapers, television and radio broadcasters, film industry, and online and social-media. The Syrian media landscape was defined by extensive state control and censorship under the [Assad government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assad_government). Since its fall in December 2024, Syria's media is in a transitional period in which its media institutions and legal environment continue to evolve.

The national language of Syria is Arabic but some publications and broadcasts are also disseminated in English and French.[^cite_note-European_Neighborhood_Journalism_Network-1]

While television was the most popular medium in Ba'athist Syria, the Internet became a widely utilized vehicle to disseminate content by 2013. In addition to its control of domestic media, the Ba'athist state also sought to control what Syrians saw by restricting coverage from outside sources.[^cite_note-2] Publications and broadcasts were monitored by members of the government.[^cite_note-European_Neighborhood_Journalism_Network-1] All [mass media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_media) outlets were under the supervision of the [Ministry of Information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Information_\(Syria\)). Third article of the 2013 Information Ministry guidelines stipulated that the purpose of all media outlets was "to enlighten public opinion" in line with the ideological doctrines "of the [Arab Socialist Ba’ath Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Socialist_Ba'ath_Party_%E2%80%93_Syria_Region) and the policy of the [state](57134800.md)".[^cite_note-3]

Following [Ba'ath party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ba'ath_Party_\(Syrian-dominated_faction\))'s [capture of power in 1963](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1963_Syrian_coup_d'%C3%A9tat), the state immediately banned all news outlets except which advanced party propaganda. Syrians have had no exposure to free media or independent press since then, with there being no space for independent journalism, newspapers, publications, journalists or websites un-affiliated with party organizations. The situation worsened further after 1970, with the [Ba'athist dictatorship](57134800.md) imposing additional [censorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship) policies that furthered its [totalitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totalitarianism) control of the society until the [Syrian Revolution](39750126.md) erupts across [Syria](7515849.md) which began on 15 March 2011 until the dictator [Bashar al-Assad](364813.md) and his [family](27334130.md) are overthrown on 8 December 2024 with the [Fall of Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Damascus_\(2024\)) to the government rebels which led the Assad family left Syria at midnight for [Russia](25391.md) which landed in [Moscow](19004.md) for a political asylum as refuge.[^cite_note-4] State propaganda machine was primarily used to monopolise [information access](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_access) and indoctrinate the Syrian population in [Ba'athist ideology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-ba'athism).[^cite_note-5]

Ba'athist Syria was one of the most dangerous places in the world for [journalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journalist) and was ranked 2nd worst in the [World Press Freedom Index](8264708.md) in 2024.[^cite_note-Syria-6][^cite_note-7] There were 28 journalists killed in combat in 2012.[^cite_note-BBC-SyriaProfile-8] Between 2011 and 2020, more than 700 civilian journalists have been killed in Syria and an estimated 78% of these killings were perpetrated by Ba'athist forces. More than 400 journalists were arrested or kidnapped in the course of the [Syrian civil war](30741795.md). More than a third of independent journalists reporting on the war were forced to flee after Assad regime's capture of territories in Southern and Eastern Syria during 2016-2018.[^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10] During the early years of the [Syrian revolution](39750126.md), there was a mass flight of local journalists due to persecution, threats, harassment, [torture](47702.md), [executions](5902.md) and [kidnappings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forced_abduction) from Ba'athist and pro-Assad militias. Ba'athist Syria had numerous laws such as "1965 law of protection against the [revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1963_Syrian_coup_d'%C3%A9tat)", "2011 media law", "2012 terrorism law", "2021 cybercrime law" which enabled the state to formally indict journalists and give a legal cover to media [censorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship).[^cite_note-11]

## Mass media in Ba'athist Syria

### Media

Public media journalists in Ba'athist Syria practiced self-censorship.[^cite_note-BBC-SyriaProfile-8] Public media consisted of television, print, film, radio and internet and social media.

#### Print

According to [Human Rights Watch](46530.md), *The Syrian Arab Establishment for Distribution of Printed Products*, which was affiliated with the [Ministry of Information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Information_\(Syria\)), vetted all newspapers prior to distribution. The only two private daily newspapers covering political topics that have succeeded in staying open are owned by businessmen closely tied to the Ba'athist regime: *[Baladna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baladna)* and *[Al-Watan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Watan_\(Syria\))*.[^cite_note-12] *Al-Watan*, a private daily formerly published by businessman [Rami Makhlouf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rami_Makhlouf), Bashar al-Assad's cousin, was launched in 2006. [United Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Group_\(Syria\)), a major advertising group owned by [Majd Suleiman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majid_Bahjat_Suleiman), son of a former senior intelligence officer ([Bahjat Suleiman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahjat_Suleiman)), owns and operated the private daily *[Baladna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baladna)*.[^cite_note-13]

Other Ba'athist and semi-official pro-Assadist private prints were permitted to be published in Ba'athist Syria such as [dailies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daily_newspaper): *[Al-Thawra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Thawra_\(newspaper\))*, *[Tishreen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tishreen_\(newspaper\))*, *[Al-Ba'ath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ba%CA%BDath)* and *[Al-Iqtissadiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Iqtissadiya)*.[^cite_note-14]

#### Television

Main article: [Television in Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_in_Syria)

There was one main broadcaster for both television and radio called the [General Organization of Radio and Television of Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Organization_of_Radio_and_TV_\(Syria\)) (ORTAS). It was founded in 1960 and was based in Damascus. The channel had programs in Arabic, English and French.[^cite_note-European_Neighborhood_Journalism_Network-1] TV was the most popular media in Ba'athist Syria.[^cite_note-BBC-SyriaProfile-8]

#### Film

Main article: [Cinema of Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinema_of_Syria)

The [Syrian film industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinema_of_Syria) was state-run by the Ministry of Culture, which controlled production through the National Organization for Cinema. The industry largely propaganda based, focusing on Ba'athist Syria's successes in agriculture, health, transportation and infrastructure.[^cite_note-Kosmorama-15]

### Pro-opposition media

The public did have access to Western and Middle Eastern radio stations and satellite TV, and [Qatar](8238258.md)-based [Al Jazeera](30204052.md) became very popular in Ba'athist Syria.[^cite_note-cp-28] In August 2012, a media centre utilized by foreign reporters in [Azaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azaz) was targeted by the [Syrian Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Arab_Air_Force) in an airstrike on a civilian area during [Ramadan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramadan).[^cite_note-29]

### Prohibitive measures against media

#### State of Emergency law

The constitution of the Syrian Arab Republic guaranteed the right to a [free press](105323.md) and [freedom of expression](21401843.md), but Syria was under a highly restrictive state of emergency law since the [Ba'ath Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ba'ath_Party) came to power in 1964 until 2011. Anyone wishing to establish an independent paper or periodical must apply for a license from the Ministry of Information.[^cite_note-cp-28] In 2011 the state of emergency was lifted.[^cite_note-41] This seems to have had no effect what-so-ever on the way the government conducted itself regarding the media, with Syria's ranking actually worsening the following year with journalistic organizations such as the [Committee to Protect Journalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_to_Protect_Journalists),[^cite_note-42] and [Reporters Without Borders](526054.md)[^cite_note-43] both ranking Syria as one of the top four most repressive countries in the world.

In April 2009, [Syrian Kurdish](3821855.md) journalist Faruq Haji Mustafa was arrested by the [Ba'athist secret police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_Security_Directorate) and has never been heard from again.[^cite_note-44]

#### Internet censorship

Main article: [Internet censorship in Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_Syria)

In 2012, there were over 5 million Internet users in Syria. Reporters Without Borders listed Ba'athist Syria as an “internet enemy” due to high levels of censorship. The Internet was controlled by the [Syrian Computer Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Computer_Society) (SCS) and the Syrian Telecommunications Establishment (STE).[^cite_note-45] The Assad regime monitored activity through the hacking of emails and social networking accounts and phishing. Simultaneously, the government released pro-Assad propaganda and engaged in [disinformation campaigns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disinformation_campaigns) to support its cause.[^cite_note-RSF-Syria-46] Ba'athist Syria's penal law required [Internet cafes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_cafes) to record all comments in the online chatrooms.[^cite_note-47] There was a two-day Internet blackout in 2012, which was orchestrated by the Assad regime.[^cite_note-BBC-SyriaProfile-8] Ba'athist authorities constantly blocked journalists and bloggers from attending and reporting on events by arresting and torturing them. This was not limited to Syrian journalists as several foreign journalists, such as the members of the [Associated Press](18935732.md) and [Reuters](18998750.md), were also arrested and expelled from the country over their reporting.[^cite_note-RSF-Syria-46]

#### Press freedom

[Reporters Without Borders](526054.md) ranked Ba'athist Syria 179th out of 180 countries in the world on its 2024 [Press Freedom Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Press_Freedom_Index).[^cite_note-Syria-6] On the Press Freedom Barometer for 2022, the organization reported that one journalist had been killed, while 27 journalists and two media workers had been imprisoned.[^cite_note-\:0-48]

## Transitional period after the fall of Assad

More than a year after the [fall of Assad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Assad) in December 2024, Syria’s media landscape remains in transition.[^cite_note-newmed-49] State broadcasting has resumed under the new authorities, while exiled and opposition outlets have gained greater access inside the country and foreign journalists have returned in larger numbers.[^cite_note-tvrela-50] The government has also begun consultations on a new media law covering broadcasters, news agencies, digital platforms and social media.[^cite_note-urgens-51] Press freedom groups have called for the law to guarantee editorial independence, establish an independent regulator, protect confidential sources and prevent journalists from being detained because of their work.[^cite_note-urgens-51] Despite the wider opening, serious concerns remain. Journalists have continued to face detention under Assad-era laws and restrictions have been imposed on several categories of content.[^cite_note-urgens-51] Political instability,[^cite_note-tvrela-50] weak legal and regulatory frameworks, poor infrastructure, limited funding and low public trust present major challenges.[^cite_note-newmed-49] Syria remained near the bottom of the 2025 [World Press Freedom Index](8264708.md) at 177th out of 180 countries,[^cite_note-tvrela-50] but its ranking improved significantly to 141th place in 2026.[^cite_note-\:3-52]
