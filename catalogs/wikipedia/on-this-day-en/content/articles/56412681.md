# Cryptocurrency and crime

[Cryptocurrency](36662188.md) has been used extensively in criminal activities, such as investment and romance scams (often called "[pig-butchering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pig_butchering_scam)"), [ransomware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ransomware) payments, thefts and exchange hacks, [money laundering](19390.md) and sanctions evasion, [darknet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darknet)-market transactions, and off-chain coercion to obtain private keys. Law enforcement notes that investment fraud and laundering are frequent criminal uses of cryptocurrencies, while noting that public blockchains can also aid law enforcement in tracing, seizures, and arrests.[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-2]

## Background

[Cryptocurrencies](36662188.md) are digital assets transferred on distributed ledgers without a central administrator. Transfers are borderless, typically irreversible, and control depends on possession of [private keys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public-key_cryptography) rather than accounts held by a single intermediary.[^cite_note-3]

Criminals use cryptocurrencies when they make certain offences easier than traditional methods. This ease is due, for example, to the ability to move funds quickly across borders, take payment without going through banks, and launder proceeds through lightly regulated services. These traits suit offences such as theft, investment fraud and "[pig-butchering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pig_butchering_scam)," [ransomware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ransomware) payments, darknet-market trade, [money laundering](19390.md) and [sanctions](411315.md) evasion.[^cite_note-IOCTA2024-4][^cite_note-UNODC-5][^cite_note-6]

Since 2019, authorities have tried to limit the criminal misuse of crypto by bringing "virtual assets" and virtual-asset service providers (VASPs) into anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing ([AML/CFT](4081569.md)) rules. The standards require licensing or registration, customer due diligence, and the "travel rule" for originator/beneficiary information. Governments pair these with supervision and enforcement (including asset seizures, sanctions, and joint takedowns) and with more cross-border information-sharing among financial-intelligence units and police.[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8][^cite_note-FATC2025-9][^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11]

However, offenders still work around these controls. Common methods include routing funds through non-compliant or offshore providers; peer-to-peer transfers and OTC brokers; mixers/tumblers, peel-chain transactions, and cross-chain "hops" via bridges; and other anonymity-enhancing tools, sometimes combined with stolen or synthetic identities at compliant platforms. Uneven implementation of AML/CFT standards (including the travel rule) across jurisdictions remains a widely noted gap, and decentralised or extra-jurisdictional services can be harder to disrupt; many investigations therefore still focus on identifying fiat on-/off-ramps and coordinating across borders.[^cite_note-12][^cite_note-UNODC-5][^cite_note-FATF2024-13][^cite_note-FATC2025-9]

## Methods

### Fraud

#### Exit scams and Ponzi schemes through initial coin offerings (ICOs)

Most [exit scams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exit_scam) (or *rugpulls*), as well as many ponzi schemes involving cryptocurrencies, are performed through [Initial Coin Offerings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_coin_offering) (ICOs),[^cite_note-26] and [Satis Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satis_Group?action=edit\&redlink=1) has reported that almost 80% of all projects launched through an ICO in 2017 were scams.[^cite_note-27] These scams usually involve the perpetrators attracting investments from mostly retail investors, inflating the price and subsequently abandoning the project in question after selling off their own shares.[^cite_note-28]

The novelty of ICOs accounts for the current lack of governmental regulation.[^cite_note-29] This lack of regulatory measures, as well as the pseudonymity of cryptocurrency transactions and their international nature across countless jurisdictions in many different countries, can make it much more difficult to identify and take legal action against perpetrators involved in these scams.[^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31] Since 2017, the [SEC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Securities_and_Exchange_Commission) has been actively pursuing groups and individuals responsible for ICO-related scams.[^cite_note-32]

#### Ponzi schemes

Ponzi schemes are another common method of utilizing blockchain-based technologies to commit fraud. Most schemes of this sort use [multi-level marketing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-level_marketing) schemes to encourage investors to conduct risky investments.[^cite_note-33] [Onecoin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OneCoin) is one of the more notable examples of cryptocurrency-ponzi schemes: founded in 2014 by [Ruja Ignatova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruja_Ignatova), OneCoin is estimated to have generated [US\$](18717338.md)4 billion in income.[^cite_note-De_Gruyter-34] While, at least in China, some of the investors' funds have been recovered and several members of the organisation arrested in the U.S., Ignatova herself is still at large.[^cite_note-35] [Quadriga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadriga_\(company\)) was another cryptocurrency ponzi scheme--this time in Canada--which involved \$190 million US dollars or \$250 million Canadian dollars and was investigated by both the [Royal Canadian Mounted Police](69080.md) and the [Federal Bureau of Investigation](11127.md) after the death of its owner, Gerald William Cotten, in December 2018.[^cite_note-36][^cite_note-37]

#### Money laundering

See also: [Money laundering](19390.md)

Due to the inability of third parties to de-pseudonymize crypto transactions, criminal entities have often resorted to using cryptocurrency to conduct money laundering.[^cite_note-38] ICOs lacking [KYC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Know_your_customer) guidelines and anti-money laundering procedures are often used to launder illicit funds, due to the pseudonymity they offer.[^cite_note-nestarcova44-39] By using ICOs, criminals launder these funds by buying tokens off of legitimate investors and selling them. This issue is intensified by the lack of measures against money laundering implemented by centralized cryptocurrency exchanges.[^cite_note-nestarcova44-39]

A well-known early example of money laundering using cryptocurrencies is [Silk Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silk_Road_\(marketplace\)). Shut down in 2013, with its founder [Ross Ulbricht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ross_Ulbricht) indicted for a money laundering conspiracy among other counts, the website was used for several illicit activities including money laundering solely using [bitcoin](28249265.md) as a form of payment.[^cite_note-40]

Apart from traditional cryptocurrencies, [Non-Fungible Tokens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-fungible_token) (NFTs) are also commonly used as part of money laundering activities.[^cite_note-41] NFTs are often used to perform [Wash Trading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wash_trade) by creating several different [wallets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptocurrency_wallet) for one individual, generating several fictitious sales and consequently selling the respective NFT to a third party.[^cite_note-42] According to a report by [Chainalysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chainalysis), these types of wash trades are becoming increasingly popular among money launderers due to the largely anonymous nature of transactions on NFT marketplaces.[^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44] Auction platforms for NFT sales may face regulatory pressure to comply with anti-money laundering legislation.[^cite_note-45]

##### Regulatory measures

Canada is generally regarded as the first state actor to implement regulatory measures dealing with money laundering conducted by the usage of cryptocurrencies.[^cite_note-46] By 2013, the [Financial Crimes Enforcement Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_Crimes_Enforcement_Network) (FinCEN) — in direct reference to the centralized exchange [Mt. Gox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mt._Gox) — issued regulations making it clear that all crypto-to-[fiat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiat_money) exchangers had to apply KYC- as well as anti-money laundering methods.[^cite_note-47] Any suspicious transactions, therefore, have to be reported to the authorities.[^cite_note-48] Centralized exchanges have to register as money transmitters, with the exact definition of who and what constitutes a *money transmitter* in the crypto sphere being somewhat blurred and regulations differing between the different states of the U.S.[^cite_note-49] An important exemption from these regulations is [decentralized exchanges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decentralized_finance#Decentralized_exchanges), due to the fact that they do not hold any fiat currency.[^cite_note-50]

As part of the *Fifth Anti-Money Laundering Directive* of 2018 and in an effort to combat money laundering and the financing of terrorism, the [European Union](9317.md) has issued a directive that all member-states must make sure that crypto exchanges are licensed and registered.[^cite_note-51] The EU is furthermore planning to take measures to ensure that all customers of cryptocurrency exchanges must verify their identity as part of the registration process.[^cite_note-schickleryahoo-52]

###### Regarding NFTs

Auction platforms for NFT sales may face regulatory pressure to comply with anti-money laundering legislation. A February 2022 study from the [United States Treasury](53667.md) assessed that there was "some evidence of money laundering risk in the high-value art market," including through "the emerging digital art market, such as the use of non-fungible tokens (NFTs)".[^cite_note-53] The study considered how NFT transactions may be a simpler option for laundering money through art by avoiding transportation or insurance complications in trading physical art. Several NFT exchanges were labeled as virtual asset service providers that may be subject to [Financial Crimes Enforcement Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_Crimes_Enforcement_Network) regulations.[^cite_note-54]

The European Union has yet to establish specific regulations to combat money laundering through NFTs. The [European Commission](9974.md) announced in July 2022 that it was planning to establish regulations by 2024.[^cite_note-55][^cite_note-schickleryahoo-52]

#### Giveaway scam

A cryptocurrency giveaway scam involves scammers compromising or impersonating celebrities, influencers, or well-known companies to falsely claim they are multiplying cryptocurrency or giving away free cryptocurrency via [airdrop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airdrop_\(cryptocurrency\)). A variety of methods are used to promote these scams, primarily through posts and [livestreams](18996908.md) on [social media](5897742.md).[^cite_note-56]

[YouTube](3524766.md) is commonly used to promote this scam. Popular accounts are hacked to stream pre-recorded videos of the impersonated figure, overlaid with fake giveaway announcements that often encourage viewers to visit the scammer's website.[^cite_note-57] In July 2020, [Apple](856.md) co-founder [Steve Wozniak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Wozniak) and 17 other victims filed a lawsuit against YouTube and its parent company, [Google](1092923.md), alleging that the platform allowed scammers to use their name, image and likeness in cryptocurrency giveaway scams.[^cite_note-58] After earlier dismissals based on [Section 230](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Section_230), a 2024 [California Court of Appeal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Courts_of_Appeal) ruling allowed the case to proceed, finding that YouTube's role may fall outside those legal protections, with the case now pending further proceedings in the lower court.[^cite_note-59]

In the [2020 Twitter account hijacking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Twitter_account_hijacking), 130 high-profile accounts, including those of multi-billionaire [Elon Musk](909036.md) and then [U.S. president Joe Biden](145422.md), were used to promote a bitcoin giveaway scam. Within minutes of the initial [tweets](68533868.md), more than 320 transactions had been sent to one of the wallet addresses, and over US\$110,000 worth of bitcoin had been deposited before the scam messages were removed by [Twitter](9988187.md).[^cite_note-60] [Coinbase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coinbase) blacklisted the bitcoin address and said they stopped over 1,000 transactions, totaling over US\$280,000, from being sent.[^cite_note-61]

#### Paper wallet generators

Paper wallet generators allow users to create a wallet address and corresponding private key. While not dubious in itself, fraudsters can create infected generators that secretly communicate the generated keys to the creator, giving them control of the wallet.[^cite_note-62]

In August 2017, a bad actor began advertising an online [IOTA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IOTA_\(technology\)) wallet seed generator. To gain the victim's trust, they linked to a legitimate [GitHub](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GitHub) repository, claiming that their website used the same code. In reality, the website used an intentionally predictable [random number generator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_number_generation), resulting in the same IOTA wallet seeds being generated. Each of these seeds was logged.[^cite_note-63] On January 19, 2018, the attacker drained approximately US\$3.94 million from wallets created during the six-month period. Profiles associated with the website on GitHub, [Reddit](3829005.md), and [Quora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quora) that had provided support to users were deleted, and the website was updated to display the message: "Taken down. Apologies."[^cite_note-64] In January 2019, [Europol](525329.md) arrested a 36-year-old man from [Oxford, England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford) believed to be behind the attack.[^cite_note-65]

#### Pig butchering

This section is an excerpt from [Pig butchering scam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pig_butchering_scam).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pig_butchering_scam\&action=edit)]

A [pig-butchering scam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pig_butchering_scam) or romance baiting scam is a form of online relationship and investment fraud in which perpetrators cultivate fake romantic or social relationships with victims before persuading them to invest money, often in fraudulent [cryptocurrency](36662188.md) schemes. Such scams are common on [social media](5897742.md) and [dating apps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dating_app), and often involve [catfishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catfishing), [investment fraud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Investment_fraud), and [romance scams](5836858.md).[^cite_note-Pig_butchering_scam_aura-66]

### Malware

#### Malware attacks

In February 2014, the Pony virus, which spread to between 100,000 and 200,000 computers through a [botnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botnet), was reported to have stolen up to US\$220,000 in cryptocurrency from 85 wallets.[^cite_note-67] Researchers later discovered updated versions with the ability to steal 30 types of cryptocurrencies.[^cite_note-68]

A type of Mac malware active in August 2013, Bitvanity posed as a vanity wallet address generator and stole addresses and private keys from other bitcoin client software.[^cite_note-southurst2014-69] A different trojan for [macOS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacOS), called CoinThief, was reported, in February 2014, to be responsible for multiple bitcoin thefts.[^cite_note-southurst2014-69] The software was hidden in versions of some cryptocurrency apps on [Download.com](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CNET_Download) and [MacUpdate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacUpdate).[^cite_note-southurst2014-69]

#### Stealers and drainers

A stealer, also known as a drainer or [infostealer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infostealer), is a type of malware designed to steal private information, including private keys from cryptocurrency wallets, enabling attackers to access and transfer the funds to their wallet. The most common infections scan computers for wallet files and upload them to a [remote server](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Server_\(computing\)), where they can be cracked.[^cite_note-70] Many stealers also incorporate [keyloggers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keystroke_logging) to record keystrokes, thus bypassing the need to crack the keys.[^cite_note-71]

#### Clipboard hijacking

Clipboard hijacking involves a malware that detects when a cryptocurrency address is copied to the [clipboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clipboard_\(computing\)), and quickly replaces it, in order to trick victims into sending their cryptocurrency to the attacker's address. The method is especially effective due to the difficulty of memorizing or manually typing wallet addresses and the irreversible nature of cryptocurrency transactions.[^cite_note-72]

#### Ransomware

Cryptocurrency is considered to be the "near-universal form of payment" for [ransomware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ransomware),[^cite_note-73] a type of [malware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malware) that [encrypts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encryption) a victim's data until a ransom is paid. Ransomware attacks are estimated to have generated US\$1.1 billion in 2019, US\$999 million in 2020, a record US\$1.25 billion in 2023, and US\$813 million in 2024.[^cite_note-Milmo-2025-16] In 2024, a record breaking US\$75 million ransom was paid to the Dark Angels ransomware group by an undisclosed [Fortune 500](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortune_500) company.[^cite_note-74]

#### Cryptojacking

This section is an excerpt from [Cryptojacking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptojacking).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Cryptojacking\&action=edit)]

[Cryptojacking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptojacking) is the act of exploiting a [computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer) to mine [cryptocurrencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptocurrencies), often through [websites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Website),[^cite_note-75][^cite_note-Cryptojacking_mm-76][^cite_note-77] against the user's will or while the user is unaware.[^cite_note-78] One notable piece of software used for cryptojacking was Coinhive, which was used in over two-thirds of cryptojacks before its March 2019 shutdown.[^cite_note-79] The cryptocurrencies mined the most often are privacy coins—coins with hidden transaction histories—such as [Monero](45714713.md) and [Zcash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zcash).[^cite_note-Cryptojacking_mm-76][^cite_note-80]

Like most malicious attacks on the computing public, the motive is profit, but unlike other threats, it is designed to remain completely hidden from the user. Cryptojacking [malware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malware) can lead to slowdowns and crashes due to straining of computational resources.[^cite_note-Cryptojacking_urlBrutal_cryptocurrency_mining_malware_crashes_your_PC_when_discovered_|_ZDNet-81]

Bitcoin mining by personal computers infected with malware is being challenged by dedicated hardware, such as [FPGA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field-programmable_gate_array) and [ASIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Application-specific_integrated_circuit) platforms, which are more efficient in terms of power consumption and thus may have lower costs than theft of computing resources.[^cite_note-82]

### Off-chain

#### Violent crime

Some mainstream news outlets have described cases in which kidnappers have targeted cryptocurrency holders in order to compel them to turn over access to their digital wallets. According to reporting citing data from blockchain analysis firm Chainalysis, more than 30 such "wrench attacks" were recorded in 2025, though the firm noted that many crimes may go unreported. One widely reported case involved a cryptocurrency investor who escaped from an apartment in Manhattan after allegedly being held captive for weeks, during which he was beaten and threatened with death in an attempt to force the transfer of digital assets.[^cite_note-83][^cite_note-84]

#### Energy theft

Electricity theft associated with proof-of-work cryptocurrency mining has been reported in multiple countries, with operators illegally bypassing or tampering with meters and power lines to reduce operating costs. In Malaysia, Reuters reported that the national utility company, [Tenaga Nasional Bhd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenaga_Nasional), estimated electricity losses of about 4.6 billion ringgit (US\$1.11 billion) from power theft linked to illegal cryptocurrency mining between 2020 and August 2025, citing a parliamentary reply from the energy ministry.[^cite_note-85]

### Fraud factories

[Fraud factories](72348121.md) in Asia traffic workers to scam Westerners into buying cryptocurrencies online.[^cite_note-86]

## Law-enforcement use

Law-enforcement agencies and financial-intelligence units report using public-blockchain records to trace illicit flows, identify counterparties at service providers, and recover assets through seizure and forfeiture.[^cite_note-87][^cite_note-IOCTA2024-4] In recent operations, tracing supported large seizures linked to investment-fraud schemes; seized assets are typically managed and disposed of by custodial authorities such as the [U.S. Marshals Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Marshals_Service).[^cite_note-88][^cite_note-89] Regulators and standard-setters note that FATF Recommendation 15 and related guidance shape cooperation with virtual-asset service providers, while also acknowledging barriers from privacy-enhancing techniques such as mixers and privacy coins.[^cite_note-FATF2024-13][^cite_note-90] U.S. oversight bodies have likewise highlighted both the promise of on-chain transparency and persistent challenges in sanctions and AML enforcement.[^cite_note-91]

### Blockchains

#### Bitcoin

There have been many cases of bitcoin theft.[^cite_note-Economist113013Pressure-149] As of December 2017, around 980,000 bitcoins—over five percent of all bitcoin in circulation[^cite_note-150]—had been lost on [cryptocurrency exchanges](3330391.md).[^cite_note-151]

One type of theft involves a third party accessing the private key to a victim's bitcoin address,[^cite_note-152] or an [online wallet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_wallet).[^cite_note-153] If the private key is stolen, all the bitcoins from the compromised address can be transferred. In that case, the network does not have any provisions to identify the thief, block further transactions of those stolen bitcoins, or return them to the legitimate owner.[^cite_note-winkles-154]

Theft also occurs at sites where bitcoins are used to purchase illicit goods. In late November 2013, an estimated US\$100 million in bitcoins were allegedly stolen from the online illicit goods marketplace [Sheep Marketplace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheep_Marketplace), which immediately closed.[^cite_note-hern2013-155] Users tracked the coins as they were processed and converted to cash, but no funds were recovered and no culprits were identified.[^cite_note-hern2013-155] A different black market, [Silk Road 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silk_Road_\(marketplace\)#Silk_Road_2.0), stated that, during a February 2014 hack, bitcoins valued at \$2.7 million were taken from [escrow accounts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escrow).[^cite_note-silk2-156]

Sites where users exchange bitcoins for cash or store them in "wallets" are also targets for theft. Inputs.io, an Australian wallet service, was hacked twice in October 2013 and lost more than \$1 million in bitcoins.[^cite_note-157] GBL, a Chinese bitcoin trading platform, suddenly shut down on 26 October 2013; subscribers, unable to log in, lost up to \$5 million worth of bitcoin.[^cite_note-gbl3-158] In late February 2014, Mt. Gox, one of the largest virtual currency exchanges, filed for bankruptcy in Tokyo amid reports that bitcoins worth US\$350 million had been stolen.[^cite_note-GoxBankrupt-159] Flexcoin, a bitcoin storage specialist based in [Alberta, Canada](717.md), shut down in March 2014 after saying it discovered a theft of about \$650,000 in bitcoins.[^cite_note-ligaya2014-160] [Poloniex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_Internet_Group), a digital currency exchange, reported in March 2014 that it had lost bitcoins valued at around \$50,000.[^cite_note-truong2014-161] In January 2015, the UK-based [bitstamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitstamp), the third busiest bitcoin exchange globally, was hacked and US\$5 million in bitcoins were stolen.[^cite_note-zdnet-107] In February 2015, a Chinese exchange named BTER lost bitcoins worth nearly \$2 million to hackers.[^cite_note-162]

A major bitcoin exchange, Bitfinex, was compromised by the 2016 Bitfinex hack, when nearly 120,000 bitcoins (around US\$71 million) were stolen.[^cite_note-163] Bitfinex was forced to suspend its trading. The theft was the second-largest bitcoin heist ever, dwarfed only by the Mt. Gox theft in 2014. According to *[Forbes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forbes)*, "All of Bitfinex's customers... will stand to lose money. The company has announced a cut of 36.067% across the board."[^cite_note-bitfinexheist-164] Following the hack, the company failed to refund customers, though efforts are continuing.[^cite_note-165] In 2022, the US government recovered 94,636 bitcoin (worth approximately \$3.6 billion at the time of recovery) from the 2016 thefts of the Bitfinex exchange, reported as the "largest financial seizure" in U.S. history.[^cite_note-Vanity_Fair-2022-166] By February 2022, the amount of bitcoin stolen in 2016 had increased in value to \$4.5 billion. Two people were arrested for the thefts in 2022:[^cite_note-167] married couple Ilya "Dutch" Lichtenstein and rapper Heather "Razzlekhan" Morgan were charged with conspiracy to commit money laundering and conspiracy to defraud the United States.[^cite_note-Vanity_Fair-2022-166]

On May 7, 2019, hackers stole over 7000 Bitcoins from the Binance Cryptocurrency Exchange, at a value of over 40 million US dollars. Binance CEO [Zhao Changpeng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Changpeng_Zhao) stated: "The hackers used a variety of techniques, including phishing, viruses, and other attacks... The hackers had the patience to wait, and execute well-orchestrated actions through multiple seemingly independent accounts at the most opportune time."[^cite_note-168]

Thefts have raised safety concerns. Charles Hayter, founder of the digital currency comparison website CryptoCompare, described cryptocurrency theft as "a reminder of the fragility of the infrastructure in such a nascent industry."[^cite_note-169] According to the hearing of the [U.S. House of Representatives Committee on Small Business](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_House_Committee_on_Small_Business) on April 2, 2014, "these vendors lack regulatory oversight, minimum capital standards and don't provide consumer protection against loss or theft."[^cite_note-170]

[Japan](15573.md) and the [United States](3434750.md) have accused North Korean hackers of stealing cryptocurrency worth over \$300 million from the Japan-based exchange DMM Bitcoin. The theft was attributed to the TraderTraitor group, believed to be part of the Lazarus Group, which is allegedly linked to North Korean authorities. The incident occurred in late May 2024, involving the theft of 4,502.9 bitcoin. The theft involved the hackers using [social engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_engineering_\(security\)) tactics to impersonate a recruiter on [LinkedIn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LinkedIn) and send a malicious pre-employment test to an employee at a crypto wallet software company. This allowed them to compromise the employee's system and manipulate a legitimate transaction request from DMM, resulting in the loss. The FBI and Japan's [National Police Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Police_Agency_\(Japan\)) are collaborating to combat North Korea's cybercrime activities, which date back to the mid-1990s and include a cyber-warfare unit known as [Bureau 121](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_121). The Lazarus Group has previously gained notoriety for its involvement in high-profile hacks, including the attack on [Sony Pictures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sony_Pictures) in retaliation for the film "The Interview."

In June 2016, hackers exploited a vulnerability in [The DAO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_DAO) to steal US\$50 million. Subsequently, the currency was forked into [Ethereum Classic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethereum_Classic), and Ethereum, with the latter continuing with the new blockchain without the exploited translations.[^cite_note-171][^cite_note-172]

In 2022, hackers created a signature account on a blockchain bridge called "Wormhole" and stole more than \$300 million worth of ether.[^cite_note-175]

### Other incidents

In 2015, two members of the Silk Road Task Force—a multi-agency federal task force that carried out the U.S. investigation of Silk Road—were convicted over charges pertaining to corruption.[^cite_note-Jeong-176] Former [DEA](146720.md) agent, Carl Mark Force, had attempted to extort Silk Road founder [Ross Ulbricht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ross_Ulbricht) ("Dread Pirate Roberts") by faking the murder of an informant. He pleaded guilty to money laundering, [obstruction of justice](240718.md), and extortion under color of official right, and was sentenced to 6.5 years in federal prison.[^cite_note-Jeong-176] Former [U.S. Secret Service](58221.md) agent, Shaun Bridges, pleaded guilty to crimes relating to his diversion of \$800,000 worth of bitcoins to his personal account during the investigation, and separately pleaded guilty to money laundering in connection to another cryptocurrency theft. Bridges was sentenced to almost eight years in federal prison.[^cite_note-177]

Gerald Cotten founded QuadrigaCX in 2013, after graduating from the [Schulich School of Business](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schulich_School_of_Business) in Toronto. Cotten acted as the sole curator of the exchange. Quadriga had no official bank accounts, since banks at the time had no method of managing cryptocurrency. In late 2018, QuadrigaCX, which was, by then, Canada's largest crypto exchange, lost US\$190 million in cryptocurrency when the owner died; he was the only one with knowledge of the password to a storage wallet. The exchange filed for bankruptcy in 2019.[^cite_note-178]

In 2018, Ellis Pinsky, 15 years old, was accused of orchestrating a scheme to steal millions of dollars' worth of cryptocurrencies from Michael Terpin, a prominent cryptocurrency investor. The scheme involved a social engineering technique known as a [SIM swap attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SIM_swap_attack). The case attracted significant attention due to Pinsky's young age and the substantial amount of money involved. It raised questions about the security of digital assets and the challenges in regulating and prosecuting crimes in the rapidly evolving world of cryptocurrencies. Pinsky later reached a settlement to return \$22 million in cryptocurrency to Terpin.[^cite_note-179][^cite_note-El_País-180][^cite_note-181][^cite_note-182][^cite_note-183] In May 2020, Pinsky experienced a home invasion by intruders searching for remaining stolen assets.[^cite_note-El_País-180] Michael Terpin, the founder and chief executive officer of Transform Group, a [San Juan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Juan,_Puerto_Rico), [Puerto Rico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptocurrencies_in_Puerto_Rico)-based company that advises blockchain businesses on public relations and communications, sued Ellis Pinsky in New York on May 7, 2020, for leading a "sophisticated cybercrime spree" that stole US\$24 million in cryptocurrency by hacking into Terpin's phone.[^cite_note-184]\[^cite_note-[www.msn.com-185](http://www.msn.com-185/)] Terpin also sued Nicholas Truglia and won a \$75.8 million judgment against Truglia in 2019 in California state court.\[^cite_note-[www.msn.com-185](http://www.msn.com-185/)]

On July 15, 2020, the [Twitter](9988187.md) accounts of prominent personalities and firms, including [Joe Biden](145422.md), [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama), [Bill Gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Gates), [Elon Musk](909036.md), [Jeff Bezos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeff_Bezos), [Apple](856.md), [Kanye West](523032.md), [Michael Bloomberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Bloomberg) and [Uber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uber) were [hacked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Twitter_account_hijacking). Twitter confirmed that it was a coordinated social engineering attack on their own employees. Twitter released its statement six hours after the attack took place. Hackers posted messages encouraging users to transfer Bitcoin to a Bitcoin wallet, which would then double the amount. The wallet's balance was expected to increase to more than \$100,000 as the message spread among Twitter followers.[^cite_note-186]

In 2021, US authorities carried out a raid on [James Zhong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Zhong)'s home in Gainesville, Georgia. Authorities found over 51,000 bitcoin that Zhong had stolen from Silk Road between 2012 and 2013. Through an error on Silk Road, Zhong was able to withdraw more bitcoin than was initially deposited. He concealed his identity and was able to evade authorities for nearly a decade. Zhong ended up pleading guilty to [wire fraud](905072.md) and was sentenced to 1 year and 1 day in prison along with a forfeiture of all bitcoin.[^cite_note-187]

In 2022, the [Federal Trade Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Trade_Commission) reported that \$139 million in cryptocurrency had been stolen by [romance scammers](5836858.md) in 2020.[^cite_note-VergeScam-188] Some scammers target dating apps with fake profiles.[^cite_note-189]

In early 2022, the Beanstalk cryptocurrency was stripped of its reserves, which were valued at more than US\$180 million, after attackers had managed to use borrowed US\$80 million in cryptocurrency to buy enough voting rights to transfer the reserves to their own accounts outside the system. It was initially unclear if such an exploit of governance procedures was illegal.[^cite_note-190]

In 2025, a Nigerian scammer impersonated Trump ally [Steve Witkoff](40901053.md) by creating a nearly identical email address to solicit a \$250,000 cryptocurrency donation from a political donor intended for the [Trump-Vance Inaugural Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_inauguration_of_Donald_Trump). The donor, deceived by the subtle change in the email domain, transferred 250,300 USDT.ETH, which was quickly laundered through multiple wallets. The FBI, with help from Tether and Binance, managed to recover and freeze about \$40,300 of the stolen funds, but over \$210,000 remains missing. Officials warn that the complexity of blockchain transactions makes recovering stolen crypto extremely difficult.[^cite_note-191]

Josh Garza, who founded the cryptocurrency startups GAW Miners and ZenMiner in 2014, acknowledged in a [plea agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plea_bargain) that the companies were part of a [pyramid scheme](48732.md), and pleaded guilty to wire fraud in 2015. The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission separately brought a civil enforcement action against Garza, who was eventually ordered to pay a judgment of US\$9.1 million plus \$700,000 in interest. The SEC's complaint stated that Garza, through his companies, had fraudulently sold "investment contracts representing shares in the profits they claimed would be generated" from mining.[^cite_note-192] Garza was later found guilty of fraud, and was ordered by the U.S. Attorney's Office District of Connecticut to pay US\$9 million and begin serving a 21-month sentence, commencing January 2019 .[^cite_note-193]

The cryptocurrency community refers to pre-mining, hidden launches, ICO or extreme rewards for the altcoin founders as "deceptive practices".[^cite_note-194] This is, at times, an inherent part of the cryptocurrency's design.[^cite_note-195] Pre-mining refers to the practice of generating the currency before its released to the public.[^cite_note-196]

[FTX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FTX) and [Alameda Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alameda_Research) founder and CEO [Sam Bankman-Fried](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Bankman-Fried) was indicted by the [U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_Southern_District_of_New_York) in December 2022 and charged with commodities and wire fraud, [securities fraud](2902209.md) and money laundering, as well as violating [campaign finance](339302.md) laws.[^cite_note-197][^cite_note-198]

In 2025, U.S. authorities led by the Department of Justice and the FBI's San Diego Field Office have seized approximately \$2.5 million in cryptocurrency linked to a series of sophisticated fraud schemes. The forfeiture, approved by U.S. District Court, targets criminals who exploited digital financial platforms to defraud victims, often through so-called "confidence schemes." The crackdown involved collaboration with various agencies and support from Tether, a crypto industry company, and aims to both compensate victims and deter future online fraud.[^cite_note-199]
