The **Antonov An-22 "Antei"** ([Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language): Ан-22 Антей, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Russian): *An-22 Antey*; lit. '[Antaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antaeus)'; [NATO reporting name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NATO_reporting_name): "**Cock**") is a retired heavy [turboprop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turboprop) [military transport aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_transport_aircraft) developed by the [Antonov Design Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonov_Design_Bureau) and produced by the [Tashkent State Aircraft Factory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tashkent_Aviation_Production_Association) in the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union). It was the first [wide-body transport aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wide-body_aircraft) and has continued to possess the largest cargo deck for any turboprop-powered aircraft in the world into the twenty-first century.[^cite_note-key_largest-1]

The An-22 was designed during the early 1960s; despite its large size, it had a relatively conventional configuration, possessing a high-mounted wing and being powered by four [Kuznetsov NK-12MA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuznetsov_NK-12MA) turboprop engines that each drove a pair of [contra-rotating propellers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contra-rotating_propellers). The [prototype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prototype) An-22 made its [maiden flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maiden_flight) on 27 February 1965 and was first publicly displayed outside the Soviet Union at the [Paris Air Show](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Air_Show) that same year. Entering service with the [Soviet Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Air_Force) in 1967, the type was widely used in its logistical role. It routinely performed long-distance [airlifts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airlift), such as to [Peru](170691.md) in July 1970 following the [Ancash earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1970_Ancash_earthquake). During the [Soviet invasion of Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet%E2%80%93Afghan_War), An-22s were used to transport [Soviet Airborne Troops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Airborne_Troops) (VDV) into the theatre. Other key operations during the Cold War era included transport duties in response to the [Chernobyl disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernobyl_disaster) and the delivery of Soviet military equipment to [Angola](701.md).

At the time of the [Dissolution of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union), the majority of the An-22s were located in Russia, and thus were adopted by the [Russian Aerospace Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Aerospace_Forces), which continued to operate the type until 2025. A few aircraft were in Ukraine at the time of the dissolution, and were operated for a time by the [charter cargo operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_charter) [Antonov Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonov_Airlines); charters included [NATO](21133.md)'s [SALIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NATO_Strategic_Airlift_Capability) programme. Russian use of the An-22 came to an abrupt end following the [crash of the last operational aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Ivanovo_Antonov_An-22_crash) during a test flight in December 2025.[^cite_note-twz20251209-2][^cite_note-\:0-3]

## Development



First prototype of the An-22 (CCCP-64459; currently UR-64459), pictured in 1991 at [Gostomel Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gostomel_Airport).



Front view



Bottom view

The impetus for the development of the Antonov An-22 can be traced back to the late 1950s and the military requirements of the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union), which sought a large [strategic airlifter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airlift#Strategic_airlift) to supplement the smaller [Antonov An-8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonov_An-8) and [An-12s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonov_An-12) transport aircraft that were being introduced around this time.[^cite_note-aw-4] The [Ministry of Defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Defense_\(Soviet_Union\)) sought an airlifter that could transport various outsized cargoes, including [intercontinental ballistic missiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intercontinental_ballistic_missile) (ICBMs) and military vehicles that were previously restricted to being transported by rail as well as various outsized cargoes.[^cite_note-key_largest-1] One specific desire was to expand the [Soviet Airborne Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Airborne_Forces)' capability to land with their then-new [BMD-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BMD-1) armoured vehicles. Accordingly, the prospectively airlifter's cargo hold had to accommodate no less than four BMD-1s (the preceding An-12 could only a single BMD-1).[^cite_note-aw-4]

During the early 1960s, the Antonov bureau produced a wooden mock up at its workshops in [Kyiv](585629.md), Ukraine, which was internally referred to as the *Model 100*.[^cite_note-aw-4] Originally designated as the *An-20*, the general configuration of this aircraft was fairly conventional, being powered by four engines that were installed on a high-mounted wing.[^cite_note-aw-4] Visually, the aircraft appeared to be an enlarged version of the earlier [Antonov An-12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonov_An-12), except that it was furnished with a [twin tail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_tail), which gave the An-22 better engine-out performance as well as a reduced height restrictions for hangars. On top of each tail [fin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fin) were sizable anti-[flutter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeroelasticity#Flutter) masses.

On 18 August 1964, the first of two [prototype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prototype), now designated the An-22, was rolled out; it performed its [maiden flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maiden_flight) on 27 February 1965.[^cite_note-aw-4][^cite_note-key_largest-1] This prototype was given the name [Antaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antaeus) (sometimes misspelled *Antheus*) and, after four months of test-flying, was publicly displayed at the 1965 [Paris Air Show](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Air_Show).[^cite_note-aw-4] All of the production standard An-22s were constructed at the [Tashkent State Aircraft Factory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tashkent_Aviation_Production_Association), the first pair of production aircraft were delivered to the Air Transport Wing at Ivanovo Airbase in 1969.[^cite_note-aw-4][^cite_note-key_largest-1]



Soviet 1965 [postage stamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postage_stamp) showcasing the An-22 success at the Paris Air Show

There was a few differences between the production standard An-22s and the prototype. One of the most visually apparent was that the prototype was provisioned with a fully-glazed nose, and thus lacked the nose-mounted [radar](25676.md) arrangement that was used on production aircraft. Instead, these aircraft had their radar apparatus mounted below the right wheel well fairing, just forward of the wheels.

Antonov worked on multiple improvements and derivatives of the An-22. One variant, which featured a modified electrical system, different navigation apparatus, and an additional augmented flight control system, was referred to by the company as the *An-22A*, but this designation was never used by the military.[^cite_note-aw-4] A civil airliner variant was also studied; it was to feature a lengthened fuselage and be capable of seating up to 724 passengers across its upper and lower decks, however, no such aircraft were ever built.[^cite_note-5][^cite_note-key_largest-1]

## Design

The Antonov An-22 was powered by a total of four [Kuznetsov NK-12MA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuznetsov_NK-12MA) [turboprop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turboprop) engines, each capable of generating up to 15,000 shp, which drove [contra-rotating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contra-rotating_propellers) [constant speed propellers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variable-pitch_propeller_\(aeronautics\)), in a similar arranged to the contemporary [Tupolev Tu-114](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tupolev_Tu-114) airliner. One advantage of the contra-rotating propeller arrangement was reduced noise.[^cite_note-key_largest-1] Both the propellers and exhaust from the engines produced a [slipstream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slipstream) over the wings and the large double-slotted [flaps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flap_\(aeronautics\)).

Amongst the traditional design features present on the An-22 was its use of a high-mounted wing, which permitted a relatively large cargo deck, possessing 33 m in length and a usable volume of 639 m<sup>3</sup>. This cargo deck was pressurized to a relatively low level, only 3.55 PSI / 0.245 bar, which permitted the use of a lighter airframe. A door equipped with a pressure [bulkhead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulkhead_\(partition\)) is located at frame 14, separating the cargo attendant's compartment from the main cargo deck; this arrangement permitted the rear cargo doors to be opened during flight for dropping paratroopers and equipment alike. To aid in the loading and unloading of cargo, a total of four cranes, which possessed a combined carrying capacity of 22,046lb (10 tonnes), were typically installed in the cargo bay.[^cite_note-key_largest-1]

The flight deck typically accommodated a pair of pilots, two engineers and a radio operator; the navigator sat at a separate station located below, within the aircraft’s nose.[^cite_note-key_largest-1] All crew members were located in the [fully pressurized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabin_pressurization) forward section of the aircraft, which comprised only a small area relative to the whole aircraft; in addition to the flight deck, it consisted of a compact [galley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galley_\(kitchen\)), [lavatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_lavatory), and several [bunk beds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunk_bed), across two decks.[^cite_note-key_largest-1]

While early versions of the An-22 were dependent upon the presence of an external [air start cart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_support_equipment#Air_start_unit) in order to start its engines, some later-build aircraft were provided with an onboard [auxiliary power unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auxiliary_power_unit) (APU).[^cite_note-key_largest-1] Further changes included a revised electrical system and alternative [navigational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigational) apparatus. It was common for the An-22 to fly with multiple spare tires onboard to mitigate potential damage from a hard impact while landing on unprepared surfaces.[^cite_note-key_largest-1] The An-22 had the capability to takeoff from austere, unpaved, and short airstrips, allowing airborne troops to perform [air-landing operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landing_operation). Into the twenty-first century, the type remained unique amongst strategic airlifters for its level of compatibility with compact unimproved airstrips.[^cite_note-key_largest-1] The [landing gear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landing_gear) was relatively rugged and thus suited to rough airstrips. Early production aircraft were able to [adjust tire pressures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Tire_Inflation_System) in flight to achieve optimum landing performance. However, this feature was not present on later-build An-22s.

## Operational history

The An-22 was originally built for the [Soviet Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Air_Force), several [Military Transport Aviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Transport_Aviation) units were equipped with the type. The 12th Mginsk Red Banner Military Transport Aviation Division (based at [Migalovo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Migalovo)) was one of the units which had its three regiments entirely equipped with the An-22s. Another unit that operated it was the 566th [Solnechnogorsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solnechnogorsk) Military Transport Aviation Regiment, which flew the An-22 between 1970 and 1987.

During its lengthy career, the An-22 set 41 separate would records pertaining to speed, payload and payload-to-altitude.[^cite_note-key_largest-1]

An early use of the An-22 was to deliver Soviet [humanitarian aid](434679.md) to [Peru](170691.md) in July 1970 following the [Ancash earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1970_Ancash_earthquake). One An-22 disappeared on 18 July during these relief flights. An-22s were also used to deliver Soviet military aid to [Egypt](8087628.md) and [Syria](7515849.md) during the [Yom Kippur War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yom_Kippur_War) in 1973, to [Angola](701.md) in 1975, and to [Ethiopia](187749.md) in 1977.[^cite_note-AI_p36-7-6]

During the early 1970s, officials within the Military Transport Aviation Command concluded that it still lacked sufficient strategic heavy airlift capacity, despite the presence of roughly 50 An-22s in its fleet, and that the USSR was not yet able to match the US's long-range heavy lift capabilities.[^cite_note-7] Rather than simply acquiring additional airlifters of an existing model, a substantial increase in payload capacity was also desired so that the same task could be completed with fewer trips.[^cite_note-cia1986-8] Accordingly, Anatonov ultimately produced the [An-124 Ruslan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonov_An-124_Ruslan), a larger [turbofan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbofan)-powered airlifter, although this did not fly until 1982.[^cite_note-9][^cite_note-helwing_anniversary2021-10]

Multiple An-22s from Migalovo were used for the initial deployment of the [Soviet Airborne Troops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Airborne_Troops) (VDV) during the 1979 [Soviet invasion of Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet%E2%80%93Afghan_War). One An-22 was shot down at takeoff (probably by an [SA-7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/9K32_Strela-2) missile) near [Kabul](16826.md) on 28 October 1984 with about 250 casualties as the aircraft was used as troop carrier.[^cite_note-AI_p37-11]

In 1984, An-22s were used to deliver [Mi-8](957233.md) helicopters to [Ethiopia](187749.md) during [drought](37335.md) relief operations.

In 1986, the 8th Military Air Transport Aviation Regiment used its aircraft to deliver key materials during the [Chernobyl disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernobyl_disaster) relief operation.

During 1987, the An-22s were used to deliver military equipment to [Angola](701.md). One year later, the fleet was used to deliver 15,000 tons and 1,000 personnel for the [1988 Armenian earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_Armenian_earthquake) relief operation.



View of an An-22 from behind

The An-22 were often seen at the [Le Bourget Air Show](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Air_Show), and in 1988 delivered an engine from the Antonov An-124 to the [Farnborough Airshow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farnborough_Airshow).

In late 1980s, numerous An-22s were put into storage.[^cite_note-key_largest-1] Around this time, the fleet was also being actively used to deliver [internal troops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_troops_of_the_Soviet_Union) to numerous regional conflicts that arose on the years running up to the [dissolution of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union).

At the time of the dissolution, the majority of the An-22s were located in Russian territory and thus were adopted by the newly-formed [Russian Aerospace Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Aerospace_Forces). Additionally, a handful of aircraft were in Ukraine; this led to several being refurbished and operated for a time by the [charter cargo operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_charter) [Antonov Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonov_Airlines). Reportedly, only two airframes were ever used commercially.[^cite_note-key_largest-1]

During the early 1990s, the first production An-22 was briefly leased to a pair of Bulgarian operators, [Sigi Air Cargo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigi_Air_Cargo?action=edit\&redlink=1) and [Air Sofia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Sofia).[^cite_note-key_largest-1]

In 1995, An-22s transported a Russian peacekeeping force from the [98th Guards Airborne Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/98th_Guards_Airborne_Division) during the [Bosnian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosnian_War).

Approximately 45 An-22s remained in service by the mid-1990s, mostly with the [Russian Air Force](1526308.md), being slowly replaced by the bigger turbofan-powered [Antonov An-124](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonov_An-124). Furthermore, several other An-22s were operated by an independent military transport aviation squadron at Migalovo base in [Tver](372316.md). Russian operations were dependent on support provided by Antonov; as a result of increased political tensions between Ukraine and Russia during the 2010s and 2020s, cumulating in the [Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine) in February 2022, this support was withdrawn, which hampered Russia's ability to maintain and repair its Antonov-built aircraft.[^cite_note-retirement_2024-12]

By December 2018, six An-22s were listed in service with the 76th Military Transport Air Squadron at Tver, although only three aircraft were reportedly airworthy. At the time, the type was planned to remain in Russian service until 2033.[^cite_note-ai119p20-1-13]

In September 2016, a single An-22 (registration number UR-09307) was returned to operational service for civil purposes with Antonov Airlines.[^cite_note-key_largest-1] Later that same year, it started flying aid into [Gao International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gao_International_Airport) in [Mali](19127.md), taking advantage of its ability to operate from rough airstrips as the infrastructure had been damaged by [war](36856978.md).[^cite_note-key_largest-1] This same aircraft later saw use under [NATO](21133.md)'s [SALIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NATO_Strategic_Airlift_Capability) programme, based at [Leipzig/Halle airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig/Halle_airport) for charter flights.[^cite_note-nato-14][^cite_note-15][^cite_note-key_largest-1]

Ukraine's last airworthy An-22 was damaged in February 2022 during the [Battle of Antonov Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Antonov_Airport) amid the initial combat of the Russian invasion of Ukraine.[^cite_note-16][^cite_note-key_largest-1]

In August 2024, reports indicated that Russia had ceased operating their remaining An-22s, and that Antei RF-09309 was being flown to Yekaterinburg to be prepared for a museum display.[^cite_note-retirement_2024-12] On 9 December 2025, the last operational An-22 crashed in the Ivanovo region, north-east of Moscow; seven people on board were presumed dead; witnesses on the ground stated that the aircraft had broken up in midair.[^cite_note-twz20251209-2][^cite_note-\:0-3]
