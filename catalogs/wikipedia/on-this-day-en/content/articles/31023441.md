**Docking and berthing of spacecraft** is the joining of two [space vehicles](37910.md). This connection can be temporary, or [partially permanent](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/semipermanent) such as for space station modules.

*Docking* specifically refers to joining of two separate free-flying space vehicles.[^cite_note-her-1][^cite_note-nasa20090317-2][^cite_note-ARDS-3][^cite_note-AdvanDock-4] *Berthing* refers to mating operations where a passive module/vehicle is placed into the mating interface of another space vehicle by using a [robotic arm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robotic_arm).[^cite_note-her-1][^cite_note-ARDS-3][^cite_note-AdvanDock-4] Because the modern process of un-berthing requires more crew labor and is time-consuming, berthing operations are unsuited for rapid crew evacuations in the event of an emergency.[^cite_note-5]

&#x20;The first spacecraft docking was performed between [Gemini 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemini_8) and an uncrewed [Agena Target Vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agena_Target_Vehicle) on March 16, 1966.

Spacecraft docking capability depends on [space rendezvous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_rendezvous), the ability of two spacecraft to find each other and [station-keep in the same orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_station-keeping). This was first developed by the United States for [Project Gemini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Gemini). It was planned for the crew of [Gemini 6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemini_6A) to rendezvous and manually dock under the command of [Wally Schirra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wally_Schirra), with an uncrewed [Agena Target Vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agena_Target_Vehicle) in October 1965, but the Agena vehicle exploded during launch. On the revised mission Gemini 6A, Schirra successfully performed a rendezvous in December 1965 with the crewed [Gemini 7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemini_7), approaching to within 0.3 metres (1 ft), but there was no docking capability between two Gemini spacecraft.[^cite_note-6] The first docking with an Agena was successfully performed under the command of [Neil Armstrong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neil_Armstrong) on [Gemini 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemini_8) on March 16, 1966. Manual dockings were performed on three subsequent Gemini missions in 1966.

The [Apollo program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_program) depended on [lunar orbit rendezvous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_orbit_rendezvous) to achieve its objective of landing men on the Moon. This required first a [transposition, docking, and extraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transposition,_docking,_and_extraction) maneuver between the [Apollo command and service module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_command_and_service_module) (CSM) mother spacecraft and the [Lunar Module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_Lunar_Module) (LM) landing spacecraft, shortly after both craft were sent out of Earth orbit on a path to the Moon. Then after completing the lunar landing mission, two astronauts in the LM had to rendezvous and dock with the CSM in lunar orbit, in order to be able to return to Earth. The spacecraft were designed to permit intra-vehicular crew transfer through a tunnel between the nose of the Command Module and the roof of the Lunar Module. These maneuvers were first demonstrated in [low Earth orbit](47568.md) on March 7, 1969, on [Apollo 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_9), then in lunar orbit in May 1969 on [Apollo 10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_10), then in six lunar landing missions, as well as on [Apollo 13](1770.md) where the LM was used as a rescue vehicle instead of making a lunar landing.

Unlike the United States, which used manual piloted docking throughout the Apollo, [Skylab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skylab), and [Space Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle) programs, the Soviet Union employed automated docking systems from the beginning of its docking attempts. The first such system, [Igla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igla_\(spacecraft_docking_system\)), was successfully tested on October 30, 1967, when the two uncrewed [Soyuz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soyuz_\(spacecraft\)) test vehicles [Kosmos 186](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kosmos_186) and [Kosmos 188](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kosmos_188) docked automatically in orbit.[^cite_note-MirHH-7][^cite_note-8] This was the first successful Soviet docking. Proceeding to crewed docking attempts, the Soviet Union first achieved rendezvous of [Soyuz 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soyuz_3) with the uncrewed [Soyuz 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soyuz_2) craft on October 25, 1968; docking was unsuccessfully attempted. The first crewed docking was achieved on January 16, 1969, between [Soyuz 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soyuz_4) and [Soyuz 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soyuz_5).[^cite_note-MAAS_Collection-9] This early version of the [Soyuz spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soyuz_\(spacecraft\)) had no internal transfer tunnel, but two cosmonauts performed an [extravehicular](9792.md) transfer from Soyuz 5 to Soyuz 4, landing in a different spacecraft than they had launched in.[^cite_note-NASA-10]

In the 1970s, the Soviet Union upgraded the Soyuz spacecraft to add an internal transfer tunnel and used it to transport cosmonauts during the [Salyut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salyut) space station program with the first successful space station visit beginning on 7 June 1971, when [Soyuz 11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soyuz_11) docked to [Salyut 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salyut_1). The United States followed suit, docking its Apollo spacecraft to the [Skylab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skylab) space station in May 1973. In July 1975, the two nations cooperated in the [Apollo-Soyuz Test Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo-Soyuz_Test_Project), docking an Apollo spacecraft with a Soyuz using a specially designed docking module to accommodate the different docking systems and spacecraft atmospheres.

Beginning with [Salyut 6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salyut_6) in 1978, the Soviet Union began using the uncrewed [Progress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progress_\(spacecraft\)) cargo spacecraft to resupply its space stations in low earth orbit, greatly extending the length of crew stays. As an uncrewed spacecraft, Progress rendezvoused and docked with the space stations entirely automatically. In 1986, the Igla docking system was replaced with the updated [Kurs system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurs_\(docking_navigation_system\)) on Soyuz spacecraft. Progress spacecraft received the same upgrade several years later.[^cite_note-MirHH-7]<sup>: 7 </sup> The Kurs system is still used to dock to the [Russian Orbital Segment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Orbital_Segment) of the [International Space Station](15043.md).

### Berthing



Flight Support Structure in *Columbia*'s payload bay under the 180 degree mark on the -V3 plane of the Hubble Space Telescope during [STS-109](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STS-109).

Berthing of spacecraft can be traced at least as far back as the berthing of payloads into the Space Shuttle payload bay.[^cite_note-11] Such payloads could be either free-flying spacecraft captured for maintenance/return, or payloads temporarily exposed to the space environment at the end of the [Remote Manipulator System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadarm). Several different berthing mechanisms were used during the Space Shuttle era. Some of them were features of the Payload Bay (e.g., the Payload Retention Latch Assembly), while others were airborne support equipment (e.g., the Flight Support Structure used for [HST servicing missions](40203.md#Servicing_missions_and_new_instruments)).

## Hardware

### Androgyny

Wiktionary logo

Look up ***[androgynous](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/androgynous)*** in Wiktionary, the free dictionary.

Docking/berthing systems may be either androgynous ([ungendered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermaphroditic_connector)) or non-androgynous ([gendered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_of_connectors_and_fasteners)), indicating which parts of the system may mate together.

Early systems for conjoining spacecraft were all non-androgynous docking system designs. Non-androgynous designs are a form of *gender mating*[^cite_note-nasa20090317-2] where each spacecraft to be joined has a unique design (male or female) and a specific role to play in the docking process. The roles cannot be reversed. Furthermore, two spacecraft of the same gender cannot be joined at all.

Androgynous docking (and later androgynous berthing) by contrast has an identical interface on both spacecraft. In an androgynous interface, there is a single design which can connect to a duplicate of itself. This allows system-level [redundancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redundancy_\(engineering\)) (role reversing) as well as rescue and collaboration between any two spacecraft. It also provides more flexible mission design and reduces unique mission analysis and training.[^cite_note-nasa20090317-2]

### Adapters

A docking or berthing adapter is a mechanical or electromechanical device that facilitates the connection of one type of docking or berthing interface to a different interface. Such interfaces may be docking/docking, docking/berthing, or berthing/berthing. Previously launched and planned to be launched adapters are listed below:

- ASTP Docking Module: An airlock module that converted U.S. Probe and Drogue to [APAS-75](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Androgynous_Peripheral_Attach_System). Built by [Rockwell International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockwell_International) for the 1975 [Apollo–Soyuz Test Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo%E2%80%93Soyuz_Test_Project) mission.

  [^cite_note-ASTP-33]

- [Pressurized Mating Adapter (PMA)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressurized_Mating_Adapter): Converts an active [Common Berthing Mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Berthing_Mechanism) to [APAS-95](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Androgynous_Peripheral_Attach_System#APAS-95). Three PMAs are attached to the [ISS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISS), PMA-1 and PMA-2 were launched in 1998 on [STS-88](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STS-88), PMA-3 in late 2000 on [STS-92](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STS-92). PMA-1 is used to connect the Zarya control module with Unity node 1, Space Shuttles used PMA-2 and PMA-3 for docking.

## Docking of uncrewed spacecraft



The Soft-Capture Mechanism (SCM) added in 2009 to the [Hubble Space Telescope](40203.md). The SCM allows both crewed and uncrewed spacecraft that utilize the [NASA Docking System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA_Docking_System) (NDS) to dock with Hubble.

For the first fifty years of spaceflight, the main objective of most *docking* and *berthing* missions was to transfer crew, construct or resupply a space station, or to test for such a mission (e.g. the docking between [Kosmos 186 and Kosmos 188](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kosmos_186_and_Kosmos_188)). Therefore, commonly at least one of the participating spacecraft was crewed, with a pressurized habitable volume (e.g. a space station or a lunar lander) being the target—the exceptions were a few fully uncrewed Soviet docking missions (e.g. the dockings of Kosmos 1443 and Progress 23 to an uncrewed [Salyut 7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salyut_7) or [Progress M1-5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progress_M1-5) to an uncrewed *[Mir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mir)*). Another exception were a few missions of the crewed US [Space Shuttles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle), like berthings of the [Hubble Space Telescope](40203.md) (HST) during the five HST servicing missions. The Japanese [ETS-VII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETS-VII) mission (nicknamed *Hikoboshi* and *Orihime*) in 1997 was designed to test uncrewed rendezvous and docking, but launched as one spacecraft which separated to join back together.

Changes to the crewed aspect began in 2015, as a number of economically driven commercial dockings of uncrewed spacecraft were planned. In 2011, two commercial spacecraft providers announced plans to provide [autonomous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_robot)/[teleoperated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teleoperation) [uncrewed resupply spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncrewed_resupply_spacecraft) for servicing other uncrewed spacecraft. Notably, both of these servicing spacecraft were intending to dock with satellites that weren't designed for docking, nor for in-space servicing.

The early business model for these services was primarily in near-[geosynchronous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geosynchronous) orbit, although [large delta-v](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta-v) [orbital maneuvering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_maneuver) services were also envisioned.[^cite_note-av20110321-40]

Building off of the 2007 [Orbital Express](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_Express) mission—a [U.S. government](195149.md)-sponsored mission to test in-space satellite servicing with two vehicles designed from the ground up for on-orbit refueling and subsystem replacement—two companies announced plans for commercial satellite servicing missions that would require docking of two uncrewed vehicles.

- [Space Infrastructure Servicing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Infrastructure_Servicing) (SIS) is a [spacecraft](37910.md) that was being developed by [Canadian](5042916.md) aerospace firm [MacDonald, Dettwiler and Associates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacDonald,_Dettwiler_and_Associates) (MDA)—maker of [Canadarm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadarm)—to operate as a small-scale in-space [refueling depot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propellant_depot) for [communication satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication_satellites) in [geosynchronous orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geosynchronous_orbit). [Intelsat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelsat) was a [requirements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_requirements_document) and [funding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_finance) partner for the initial demonstration satellite, intended for launch in 2015.

The SIS and MEV vehicles each planned to use a different docking technique. SIS planned to utilize a ring attachment around the [kick motor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apogee_kick_motor)[^cite_note-sn20110318-44] while the Mission Extension Vehicle would use a somewhat more standard insert-a-probe-into-the-nozzle-of-the-kick-motor approach.[^cite_note-av20110321-40]

A prominent spacecraft that received a mechanism for uncrewed dockings is the [Hubble Space Telescope](40203.md) (HST). In 2009 the [STS-125](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STS-125) shuttle mission added the Soft-Capture Mechanism (SCM) at the aft bulkhead of the space telescope. The SCM is meant for unpressurized dockings and will be used at the end of Hubble's service lifetime to dock an uncrewed spacecraft to de-orbit Hubble. The SCM used was designed to be compatible to the [NASA Docking System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA_Docking_System) (NDS) interface to reserve the possibility of a servicing mission.[^cite_note-softcap-45] The SCM will, compared to the system used during the five HST Servicing Missions to capture and berth the HST to the Space Shuttle, significantly reduce the rendezvous and capture design complexities associated with such missions. The NDS bears some resemblance to the APAS-95 mechanism, but is not compatible with it.[^cite_note-NDSdraft-46]

## Non-cooperative docking

Docking with a spacecraft (or other human made space object) that does not have an operable attitude control system might sometimes be desirable, either in order to salvage it, or to initiate a controlled [de-orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_entry). Some theoretical techniques for docking with non-cooperative spacecraft have been proposed so far.[^cite_note-ieee200610-47] Yet, with the sole exception of the [Soyuz T-13](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soyuz_T-13) mission to salvage the crippled [Salyut 7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salyut_7) space station, as of 2006, all spacecraft dockings in the first fifty years of spaceflight had been accomplished with vehicles where both spacecraft involved were under either piloted, autonomous or telerobotic [attitude control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacecraft_attitude_control).[^cite_note-ieee200610-47] In 2007, however, a demonstration mission was flown that included an initial [test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_test) of a [non-cooperative spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NEXTSat) captured by a [controlled spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASTRO_\(satellite\)) with the use of a robotic arm.[^cite_note-sfn20070704-48] Research and modeling work continues to support additional [autonomous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_robot) noncooperative capture missions in the coming years.[^cite_note-xu201009-49][^cite_note-yoshida2004-50]

### Salyut 7 space station salvage mission

Main article: [Soyuz T-13](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soyuz_T-13)



Commander Vladimir Dzhanibekov (left) with [Oleg Grigoryevich Makarov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oleg_Grigoryevich_Makarov) (right) on a 1978 Soviet postage stamp



Doctor of technical sciences [Viktor Savinykh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viktor_Savinykh) with [Vladimir Kovalyonok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Kovalyonok) pictured on a Soviet postage stamp commemorating a [Salyut 6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salyut_6) mission

[Salyut 7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salyut_7), the tenth space station of any kind launched, and [Soyuz T-13](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soyuz_T-13) were docked in what author David S. F. Portree describes as "one of the most impressive feats of in-space repairs in history".[^cite_note-Mir-15] Solar tracking failed and due to a telemetry fault the station did not report the failure to mission control while flying autonomously. Once the station ran out of electrical energy reserves it ceased communication abruptly in February 1985. Crew scheduling was interrupted to allow Soviet military commander [Vladimir Dzhanibekov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Dzhanibekov)[^cite_note-51] and technical science flight engineer [Viktor Savinykh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viktor_Savinykh)[^cite_note-52] to make emergency repairs.

All Soviet and Russian space stations were equipped with automatic rendezvous and docking systems, from the first space station Salyut 1 using the IGLA system, to the [Russian Orbital Segment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Orbital_Segment) of the [International Space Station](15043.md) using the [Kurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurs_\(docking_system\)) system. The Soyuz crew found the station was not broadcasting radar or telemetry for rendezvous, and after arrival and external inspection of the tumbling station, the crew judged proximity using handheld laser rangefinders.

Dzhanibekov piloted his ship to intercept the forward port of Salyut 7, matched the station's rotation and achieved soft dock with the station. After achieving hard dock they confirmed that the station's electrical system was dead. Prior to opening the hatch, Dzhanibekov and Savinykh sampled the condition of the station's atmosphere and found it satisfactory. Attired in winter fur-lined clothing, they entered the cold station to conduct repairs. Within a week sufficient systems were brought back online to allow robot cargo ships to dock with the station. Nearly two months went by before atmospheric conditions on the space station were normalized.[^cite_note-Mir-15]

Orbital Express: ASTRO (left) and NEXTSat (right), 2007

Non-cooperative rendezvous and capture techniques have been theorized, and [one mission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_Express) has successfully been performed with uncrewed spacecraft in orbit.[^cite_note-sfn20070704-48]

A typical approach for solving this problem involves two phases. First, [attitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacecraft_attitude_control) and [orbital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_\(dynamics\)) changes are made to the "chaser" spacecraft until it has zero relative motion with the "target" spacecraft. Second, docking maneuvers commence that are similar to traditional cooperative spacecraft docking. A standardized docking interface on each spacecraft is assumed.[^cite_note-nmsu2008-53]

NASA has identified automated and autonomous rendezvous and docking — the ability of two spacecraft to rendezvous and dock "operating independently from human controllers and without other back-up, \[and which requires technology] advances in sensors, software, and [realtime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-time_Control_System) [on-orbit positioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_station-keeping) and [flight control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attitude_control_\(spacecraft\)#Attitude_control_algorithms), among other challenges" — as a critical technology to the "ultimate success of capabilities such as in-orbit [propellant storage and refueling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propellant_depot)," and also for complex operations in assembling mission components for interplanetary destinations.[^cite_note-nasa20100525-54]

The Automated/Autonomous Rendezvous & Docking Vehicle (ARDV) is a proposed [NASA Flagship Technology Demonstration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flagship_technology_demonstrations) (FTD) mission, for flight as early as 2014/2015. An important NASA objective on the proposed mission is to advance the technology and demonstrate automated rendezvous and docking. One mission element defined in the 2010 analysis was the development of a laser proximity operations sensor that could be used for non-cooperative vehicles at distances between 1 metre (3 ft 3 in) and 3 kilometers (2 mi). Non-cooperative docking mechanisms were identified as critical mission elements to the success of such autonomous missions.[^cite_note-nasa20100525-54]

[Grappling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grapple_Fixture) and connecting to non-cooperative space objects was identified as a top technical challenge in the 2010 NASA Robotics, tele-robotics and autonomous systems roadmap.[^cite_note-nasa201011-55]

A docking/berthing connection is referred to as either "soft" or "hard". Typically, a spacecraft first initiates a *soft dock* by making contact and latching its docking connector with that of the target vehicle. Once the soft connection is secured, if both spacecraft are pressurized, they may proceed to a *hard dock* where the docking mechanisms form an airtight seal, enabling interior hatches to be safely opened so that crew and cargo can be transferred.

## Berthing spacecraft and modules

Please help [improve this section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special\:EditPage/Docking_and_berthing_of_spacecraft) by [adding citations to reliable sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Referencing_for_beginners). Unsourced material may be challenged and [removed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia\:Verifiability#Burden_of_evidence). *(October 2025)*_&#x20;([Learn how and when to remove this message](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Maintenance_template_removal))_ |

Docking and undocking describe spacecraft using a docking port, without assistance and under their own power. Berthing takes place when a spacecraft or unpowered module cannot use a docking port or requires assistance to use one. This assistance may come from a spacecraft, such as when the [Space Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle) used its robotic arm to push ISS modules into their permanent berths. In a similar fashion the [Poisk module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisk_\(ISS_module\)) was permanently berthed to a docking port after it was pushed into place by a [modified Progress spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progress_M-MIM2) which was then discarded. The [Cygnus resupply spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cygnus_\(spacecraft\)) arriving at the ISS does not connect to a [docking port](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Androgynous_Peripheral_Attach_System#APAS-95), instead it is pulled into a berthing mechanism by the station's robotic arm and the station then closes the connection. The [berthing mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_berthing_mechanism) is used only on the [US segment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Orbital_Segment) of the ISS, the [Russian segment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Orbital_Segment) of the ISS uses [docking ports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Androgynous_Peripheral_Attach_System#APAS-95) for permanent berths.
