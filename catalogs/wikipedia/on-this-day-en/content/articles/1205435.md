**Jet fuel** or **aviation turbine fuel** (**ATF**, also abbreviated **avtur**) is a type of [aviation fuel](642298.md) designed for use in [aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft) powered by [gas-turbine engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_turbine). It is colorless to straw-colored in appearance. The most commonly used fuels for commercial aviation are Jet A and Jet A-1, which are produced to a standardized international specification. The only other jet fuel commonly used in civilian turbine-engine powered aviation is Jet B, which is used for its enhanced cold-weather performance.

Jet fuel is a mixture of a variety of [hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon). Because the exact composition of jet fuel varies widely based on [petroleum](23195.md) source, it is impossible to define jet fuel as a ratio of specific hydrocarbons. Jet fuel is therefore defined as a performance specification rather than a chemical compound.[^cite_note-1] Furthermore, the range of molecular mass between hydrocarbons (or different carbon numbers) is defined by the requirements for the product, such as the freezing point or smoke point. [Kerosene](16992.md)-type jet fuel (including Jet A and Jet A-1, JP-5, and JP-8) has a [carbon number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_number) distribution between about 8 and 16 (carbon atoms per molecule); wide-cut or [naphtha](184342.md)-type jet fuel (including Jet B and JP-4), between about 5 and 15.[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-Rand2010-3]

## History

Fuel for [piston-engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocating_engine) powered aircraft (usually a high-[octane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octane_rating) [gasoline](23639.md) known as [avgas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avgas)) has a high [volatility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volatility_\(chemistry\)) to improve its [carburetion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carburetor) characteristics and high [autoignition temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autoignition_temperature) to prevent [preignition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preignition) in high compression aircraft engines. Turbine engines (as with [diesel engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diesel_engine)) can operate with a wide range of fuels because fuel is injected into the hot combustion chamber. Jet and [gas turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_Turbine) ([turboprop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turboprop), [helicopter](8286923.md)) aircraft engines typically use lower cost fuels with higher [flash points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_point), which are less flammable and therefore safer to transport and handle.

The first [axial compressor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axial_compressor) jet engine in widespread production and combat service, the [Junkers Jumo 004](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junkers_Jumo_004) used on the [Messerschmitt Me 262A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messerschmitt_Me_262) fighter and the [Arado Ar 234B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arado_Ar_234) jet recon-bomber, burned either a special synthetic "J2" fuel or diesel fuel. Gasoline was a third option but unattractive due to high fuel consumption.[^cite_note-4] Other fuels used were kerosene or kerosene and gasoline mixtures.

Pressure to move from Jet fuel to **sustainable aviation fuel**, i.e. [Aviation biofuel](17903761.md) or [Electrofuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrofuel), has existed since before the 2016 [Paris Agreement](30242372.md).[^cite_note-wef23-5][^cite_note-abus1-6]

## Standards

Most jet fuels in use since the end of World War II are kerosene-based. Both British and American standards for jet fuels were first established at the end of World War II. British standards derived from standards for kerosene use for lamps—known as paraffin in the UK—whereas American standards derived from aviation gasoline practices. Over the subsequent years, details of specifications were adjusted, such as minimum freezing point, to balance performance requirements and availability of fuels. Very low temperature [freezing points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melting_point) reduce the availability of fuel. Higher [flash point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_point) products required for use on aircraft carriers are more expensive to produce.[^cite_note-Rand2010-3] In the United States, [ASTM International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASTM_International) produces standards for civilian fuel types, and the [U.S. Department of Defense](7279897.md) produces standards for military use. The [British Ministry of Defence](203793.md) establishes standards for both civil and military jet fuels.[^cite_note-Rand2010-3] For reasons of inter-operational ability, British and United States military standards are harmonized to a degree. In Russia and the [CIS](36870.md) members, grades of jet fuels are covered by the State Standard ([GOST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GOST)) number, or a Technical Condition number, with the principal grade available being TS-1.

Jet A specification fuel has been used in the United States since the 1950s and is usually not available outside the United States[^cite_note-shelljet-7] and a few Canadian airports such as [Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toronto_Pearson_International_Airport), [Montreal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montr%C3%A9al%E2%80%93Trudeau_International_Airport), and [Vancouver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vancouver_International_Airport),[^cite_note-8] whereas Jet A-1 is the standard specification fuel used in most of the rest of the world,[^cite_note-11] the main exceptions being Russia and the [CIS](36870.md) members, where TS-1 fuel type is the most common standard. Both Jet A and Jet A-1 have a [flash point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_point) higher than 38 °C (100 °F), with an [autoignition temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autoignition_temperature) of 210 °C (410 °F).[^cite_note-exxonmobil.com-12] Jet A-1 is also known as AVTUR (aviation turbine) fuel.[^cite_note-natologman-13]

Vehicles, pipelines, and storage tanks containing Jet A or Jet A-1 should be marked with black bands, and for vehicles and tanks should also be marked with "Jet A" or "Jet A-1" in white text on a black background.[^cite_note-14]

### Differences between Jet A and Jet A-1

The differences between Jet A and Jet A-1 are twofold. The primary difference is the lower freezing point of Jet A-1 fuel:[^cite_note-shelljet-7]

- Jet A's is −40 °C (−40 °F)

- Jet A-1's is −47 °C (−53 °F)

The other difference is the mandatory addition of an [antistatic additive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antistatic_agent) to Jet A-1 fuel.

Jet A-1 fuel must meet:

- IATA Guidance Material (Kerosene Type), NATO Code F-35.

Jet A fuel must reach ASTM specification D1655 (Jet A).[^cite_note-Csgnetwork.com-15]

### Jet B

Jet B is a naphtha-kerosene fuel that is used for its enhanced cold-weather performance. However, Jet B's lighter composition makes it more dangerous to handle.[^cite_note-Csgnetwork.com-15] For this reason, it is rarely used, except in very cold climates. A blend of approximately 30% kerosene and 70% gasoline, it is known as wide-cut fuel. It has a very low freezing point of −60 °C (−76 °F), and a low [flash point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_point) as well. It is primarily used in northern [Canada](5042916.md) and [Alaska](624.md), where the extreme cold makes its low freezing point necessary, and which helps mitigate the danger of its lower flash point. Jet B is also known as AVTAG (aviation turbine gasoline[^cite_note-21]).

### GOST standards

The [GOST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GOST) standard 10227 specifies civilian fuels, among which are TS-1, T-1, T-1S, T2 and RT.[^cite_note-exm1-22] Military fuels such as T-1pp,[^cite_note-ru1-23] T-8V (aka T-8B) and T-6 are specified by GOST 12308.[^cite_note-exm1-22] Icing inhibitors are specified by GOST 8313.[^cite_note-exm1-22] Some researchers refer to T-6 as "ram rocket fuel";[^cite_note-iip1-24] others have patented a method used to produce T-1pp from a mixture of T-6 and RT,[^cite_note-ru1-23] the latter of which has been characterized as "unified Russian fuel for sub- and supersonic aircraft".[^cite_note-yano11-25]

#### TS-1

TS-1 is a jet fuel made to Russian standard [GOST 10227](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GOST_10227?action=edit\&redlink=1) for enhanced cold-weather performance. It has somewhat higher volatility than Jet A-1 (flash point is 28 °C (82 °F) minimum). It has a very low freezing point, below −50 °C (−58 °F).[^cite_note-26]

## Additives

The DEF STAN 91-091 (UK) and ASTM D1655 (international) specifications allow for certain additives to be added to jet fuel, including:[^cite_note-DEF_STAN_91-91-27][^cite_note-ASTM_D1655-28]

- [Antioxidants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antioxidant) to prevent gumming, usually based on [alkylated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkylation) [phenols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenol), e.g., AO-30, AO-31, or AO-37;

- [Antistatic agents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antistatic_agent), to dissipate [static electricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Static_electricity) and prevent sparking; [Stadis 450](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stadis_450?action=edit\&redlink=1), with [dinonylnaphthylsulfonic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinonylnaphthylsulfonic_acid) (DINNSA) as a component, is an example

- [Corrosion inhibitors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corrosion_inhibitor), e.g., [DCI-4A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DCI-4A?action=edit\&redlink=1) used for civilian and military fuels, and [DCI-6A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DCI-6A?action=edit\&redlink=1) used for military fuels;

- [Fuel system icing inhibitor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_system_icing_inhibitor) (FSII) agents, e.g., [2-(2-Methoxyethoxy)ethanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-\(2-Methoxyethoxy\)ethanol) (Di-EGME); FSII is often mixed at the point-of-sale so that users with heated fuel lines do not have to pay the extra expense.

- [Biocides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biocide) are to remediate microbial (i.e., bacterial and fungal) growth present in aircraft fuel systems. Two biocides were previously approved for use by most aircraft and turbine engine [original equipment manufacturers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Original_equipment_manufacturer) (OEMs); [Kathon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kathon) FP1.5 Microbiocide and Biobor JF.

  [^cite_note-Lombardo-29]

  &#x20;Biobor JF is currently the only biocide available for aviation use. Kathon was discontinued by the manufacturer due to several airworthiness incidents. Kathon is now banned from use in aviation fuel.

  [^cite_note-30]

- [Metal deactivator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal_deactivator) can be added to reduce the negative effects of [trace metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trace_metal) on the thermal stability of the fuel. The one allowable additive is the chelating agent [salpn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salpn_ligand) (*N,N′*-bis(salicylidene)-1,2-propanediamine).

As the aviation industry's jet kerosene demands have increased to more than 5% of all refined products derived from crude, it has been necessary for the refiner to optimize the yield of jet kerosene, a high-value product, by varying process techniques.

New processes have allowed flexibility in the choice of crudes, the use of coal tar sands as a source of molecules and the manufacture of synthetic blend stocks. Due to the number and severity of the processes used, it is often necessary and sometimes mandatory to use additives. These additives may, for example, prevent the formation of harmful chemical species or improve a property of a fuel to prevent further engine wear.

## Water in jet fuel

It is very important that jet fuel be free from water contamination. During flight, the temperature of the fuel in the tanks decreases, due to the low temperatures in the upper atmosphere. This causes precipitation of the dissolved water from the fuel. The separated water then drops to the bottom of the tank because it is denser than the fuel. Since the water is no longer in solution, it can form droplets which can supercool to below 0 °C (32 °F). If these supercooled droplets collide with a surface they can freeze and may result in blocked fuel inlet pipes.[^cite_note-31] This was the cause of the [British Airways Flight 38](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Airways_Flight_38) accident. Removing all water from fuel is impractical; therefore, fuel heaters are usually used on commercial aircraft to prevent water in fuel from freezing.

There are several methods for detecting water in jet fuel. A visual check may detect high concentrations of suspended water, as this causes the fuel to become hazy in appearance. An industry standard chemical test for the detection of free water in jet fuel uses a water-sensitive filter pad that turns green if the fuel exceeds the specification limit of 30 ppm (parts per million) free water.[^cite_note-32]

## Military jet fuels

A sailor inspects a sample of JP-5 jet fuel aboard an amphibious transport dock ship

Military organizations around the world use a different classification system of JP (for "Jet Propellant") numbers. Some are almost identical to their civilian counterparts and differ only by the amounts of a few additives; Jet A-1 is similar to [JP-8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JP-8), Jet B is similar to [JP-4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JP-4_\(fuel\)).[^cite_note-shell-33] Other military fuels are highly specialized products and are developed for very specific applications.

JP-1

was an early jet fuel

[^cite_note-34]

&#x20;specified in 1944 by the United States government (AN-F-32). It was a pure kerosene fuel with high [flash point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_point) (relative to aviation gasoline) and a freezing point of −60 °C (−76 °F). The low freezing point requirement limited availability of the fuel and it was soon superseded by other "wide cut" jet fuels which were kerosene-naphtha or kerosene-gasoline blends. It was also known as **avtur**.

JP-2

an obsolete type developed during World War II. JP-2 was intended to be easier to produce than JP-1 since it had a higher freezing point, but was never widely used.

[^cite_note-Beyond_1994-35]

JP-3

was an attempt to improve availability of the fuel compared to JP-1 by widening the cut and loosening tolerances on impurities to ensure ready supply. In his book *Ignition! An Informal History of Liquid Rocket Propellants*, [John D. Clark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Drury_Clark) described the specification as, "remarkably liberal, with a wide cut (range of distillation temperatures) and with such permissive limits on olefins and aromatics that any refinery above the level of a Kentucky [moonshine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moonshine)r's pot still could convert at least half of any crude to jet fuel".

[^cite_note-36]

&#x20;It was even more volatile than JP-2 and had high evaporation loss in service.

[^cite_note-Beyond_1994-35]

[JP-4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JP-4)

was a 50-50 kerosene-gasoline blend. It had lower [flash point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_point) than JP-1, but was preferred because of its greater availability. It was the primary [United States Air Force](32090.md) jet fuel between 1951 and 1995. Its [NATO](21133.md) code is **F-40**. JP-4 is also known as **AVTAG/FSII** (aviation turbine gasoline/fuel systems icing inhibitor) fuel.

[^cite_note-natologman-13]

[^cite_note-shellmil-37]

JP-5

is a yellow kerosene-based jet fuel developed in 1952 for use in aircraft stationed aboard [aircraft carriers](2219.md), where the risk from fire is particularly great. JP-5 is a complex mixture of hydrocarbons, containing [alkanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkane), [naphthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycloalkane), and [aromatic hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aromatic_hydrocarbon) that weighs 6.8 pounds per U.S. gallon (0.81 kg/L) and has a high [flash point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_point) (min. 60 °C or 140 °F).

[^cite_note-38]

&#x20;Because some US [naval air stations](1864828.md), Marine Corps air stations and Coast Guard air stations host both sea and land based naval aircraft, these installations will also typically fuel their shore-based aircraft with JP-5, thus precluding the need to maintain separate fuel facilities for JP-5 and non-JP-5 fuel. Similarly, China named their navy fuel RP-5.

[^cite_note-39]

&#x20;Its freezing point is −46 °C (−51 °F), and it does not contain antistatic agents. JP-5 is also known as NCI-C54784. JP-5's NATO code is **F-44**. It is also called **AVCAT** (aviation carrier turbine) fuel.

[^cite_note-40]

The JP-4 and JP-5 fuels, covered by the MIL-DTL-5624 and meeting the British Specification DEF STAN 91-86 AVCAT/[FSII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_system_icing_inhibitor) (formerly DERD 2452),

[^cite_note-41]

&#x20;are intended for use in aircraft [turbine engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_turbine). These fuels require unique additives that are necessary for military aircraft and engine fuel systems.

JP-6

was developed for the [General Electric YJ93](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric_YJ93) afterburning [turbojet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbojet) engines used in the [North American XB-70 Valkyrie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_XB-70_Valkyrie) for sustained flight at Mach 3. It was similar to JP-5 but with a lower freezing point and improved thermal oxidative stability. When the XB-70 program was cancelled, the JP-6 specification, MIL-J-25656, was also cancelled.

[^cite_note-42]

[JP-7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JP-7)

was developed for the [Pratt & Whitney J58](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_&_Whitney_J58) afterburning [turbojet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbojet) engines used in the [Lockheed SR-71 Blackbird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_SR-71_Blackbird) for sustained flight at Mach 3+. It had a high [flash point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_point) required to prevent boiloff caused by aerodynamic heating. Its thermal stability was high enough to prevent coke and varnish deposits when used as a [heat sink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_sink) medium for aircraft air conditioning and hydraulic systems and engine accessories.

[^cite_note-43]

[JP-8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JP-8)

is a jet fuel, specified and used widely by the [U.S. military](32212.md). It is specified by MIL-DTL-83133 and British Defence Standard 91-87. JP-8 is a kerosene-based fuel. The United States military uses JP-8 as a "universal fuel" in both turbine-powered aircraft and diesel-powered ground vehicles. It was first introduced at NATO bases in 1978. Its NATO code is **F-34.** It is also known as AVTUR/FSII (aviation turbine/fuel systems icing inhibitor) fuel.

[^cite_note-natologman-13]

[^cite_note-shellmil-37]

[JP-9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JP-9_\(fuel\)?action=edit\&redlink=1)

is a gas turbine fuel for missiles, specifically the [Tomahawk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomahawk_\(missile_family\)) cruise missile, containing the [TH-dimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TH-dimer) (tetrahydrodimethyldicyclopentadiene) produced by catalytic hydrogenation of methylpentadiene dimer.

[JP-10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JP-10_\(fuel\))

is a gas turbine fuel for missiles, specifically the [AGM-86 ALCM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-86_ALCM) cruise missile.

[^cite_note-CRC,_Aviation_Fuel_Properties,_JP-10-44]

&#x20;It contains a mixture of (in decreasing order) [endo-tetrahydrodicyclopentadiene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endo-tetrahydrodicyclopentadiene), [exo-tetrahydrodicyclopentadiene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exo-tetrahydrodicyclopentadiene) (a [synthetic fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_fuel)), and [adamantane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adamantane). It is produced by [catalytic hydrogenation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalytic_hydrogenation) of [dicyclopentadiene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dicyclopentadiene). It superseded JP-9 fuel, achieving a lower low-temperature service limit of −65 °F (−54 °C).

[^cite_note-CRC,_Aviation_Fuel_Properties,_JP-10-44]

&#x20;It is also used by the Tomahawk jet-powered subsonic cruise missile.

[^cite_note-45]

[JPTS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JPTS)

was a combination of LF-1 [charcoal lighter fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charcoal_lighter_fluid) and an additive to improve thermal oxidative stability officially known as "Thermally Stable Jet Fuel". It was developed in 1956 for the [Pratt & Whitney J57](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_&_Whitney_J57) engine which powered the [Lockheed U-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_U-2) spy plane.

[^cite_note-46]

[Zip fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zip_fuel)

designates a series of experimental boron-containing "high energy fuels" intended for long range aircraft. The toxicity and undesirable residues of the fuel made it difficult to use. The development of the [ballistic missile](38025.md) removed the principal application of zip fuel.

Jet fuel is very similar to [diesel fuel](81761.md), and in some cases, may be used in [diesel engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diesel_engine). The possibility of environmental legislation banning the use of [leaded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetraethyllead) [avgas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avgas) (fuel in spark-ignited internal combustion engine, which usually contains [tetraethyllead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetraethyllead) (TEL), a toxic substance added to prevent [engine knocking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engine_knocking)), and the lack of a replacement fuel with similar performance, has left aircraft designers and pilot organizations searching for alternative engines for use in small aircraft.[^cite_note-47] As a result, only a few aircraft engine manufacturers, most notably [Thielert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thielert) and [Austro Engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro_Engine), have begun offering [aircraft diesel engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_diesel_engine) which run on jet fuel, which may simplify airport logistics by reducing the number of fuel types required. Jet fuel is available in most places in the world, whereas avgas is only widely available in a few countries which have a large number of [general aviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_aviation) aircraft. A diesel engine may be more fuel-efficient than an avgas engine. However, very few diesel aircraft engines have been certified by aviation authorities. Diesel aircraft engines are uncommon today, even though opposed-piston aviation diesel powerplants such as the [Junkers Jumo 205](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junkers_Jumo_205) family had been used during the Second World War.

Jet fuel is often used in diesel-powered ground-support vehicles at airports. However, jet fuel tends to have poor lubricating ability in comparison to diesel, which increases wear in fuel injection equipment. An additive may be required to restore its [lubricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lubricity). Jet fuel is more expensive than diesel fuel but the logistical advantages of using one fuel can offset the extra expense of its use in certain circumstances.

Jet fuel contains more sulfur, up to 1,000 ppm, which therefore means it has better lubricity and does not currently require a lubricity additive as all pipeline diesel fuels require. The introduction of Ultra Low Sulfur Diesel or ULSD brought with it the need for lubricity modifiers. Pipeline diesels before ULSD were able to contain up to 500 ppm of sulfur and were called Low Sulfur Diesel or LSD. In the United States LSD is now only available to the off-road construction, locomotive and marine markets. As more EPA regulations are introduced, more refineries are hydrotreating their jet fuel production, thus limiting the lubricating abilities of jet fuel, as determined by ASTM Standard D445.

[JP-8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JP-8), which is similar to Jet A-1, is used in [NATO](21133.md) diesel vehicles as part of the single-fuel policy.[^cite_note-48]

## Synthetic jet fuel

Main article: [Synthetic fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_fuel)

[Fischer–Tropsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fischer%E2%80%93Tropsch_process) (FT) [Synthesized Paraffinic Kerosene](17903761.md) (SPK) synthetic fuels are certified for use in United States and international aviation fleets at up to 50% in a blend with conventional jet fuel.[^cite_note-49] As of the end of 2017, four other pathways to SPK are certified, with their designations and maximum blend percentage in brackets: Hydroprocessed Esters and Fatty Acids (HEFA SPK, 50%); synthesized iso-paraffins from hydroprocessed fermented sugars (SIP, 10%); synthesized paraffinic kerosene plus aromatics (SPK/A, 50%); alcohol-to-jet SPK (ATJ-SPK, 30%). Both FT and HEFA based SPKs blended with JP-8 are specified in MIL-DTL-83133H.

Some synthetic jet fuels show a reduction in pollutants such as SOx, NOx, particulate matter, and sometimes carbon emissions.[^cite_note-50][^cite_note-51][^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53][^cite_note-54] It is envisaged that usage of synthetic jet fuels will increase air quality around airports which will be particularly advantageous at inner city airports.[^cite_note-55]

[Qatar Airways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qatar_Airways) became the first airline to operate a commercial flight on a 50\:50 blend of synthetic Gas to Liquid (GTL) jet fuel and conventional jet fuel. The natural gas derived synthetic kerosene for the six-hour flight from [London](17867.md) to [Doha](26214389.md) came from Shell's GTL plant in [Bintulu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bintulu), [Malaysia](3607937.md).[^cite_note-56] The world's first passenger aircraft flight to use only synthetic jet fuel was from [Lanseria International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanseria_International_Airport) to [Cape Town International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Town_International_Airport) on September 22, 2010. The fuel was developed by [Sasol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasol).[^cite_note-57]

Chemist [Heather Willauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heather_Willauer) is leading a team of researchers at the [U.S. Naval Research Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Naval_Research_Laboratory) who are developing a process to make jet fuel from seawater. The technology requires an input of electrical energy to separate [Oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen) (O<sub>2</sub>) and Hydrogen (H<sub>2</sub>) gas from seawater using an iron-based catalyst, followed by an [oligomerization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oligomer) step wherein carbon monoxide (CO) and hydrogen are recombined into long-chain hydrocarbons, using [zeolite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeolite) as the catalyst. The technology is expected to be deployed in the 2020s by U.S. Navy warships, especially nuclear-powered aircraft carriers.[^cite_note-58][^cite_note-59][^cite_note-Tozer2014-60][^cite_note-61][^cite_note-62][^cite_note-63]

On February 8, 2021, the world's first scheduled passenger flight flew with some synthetic kerosene from a non-fossil fuel source. 500 liters of synthetic kerosene was mixed with regular jet fuel. Synthetic kerosene was produced by Shell and the flight was operated by KLM.[^cite_note-Shell-64]

### USAF synthetic fuel trials

On August 8, 2007, [Air Force Secretary](305210.md) [Michael Wynne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Wynne) certified the B-52H as fully approved to use the FT blend, marking the formal conclusion of the test program. This program is part of the Department of Defense Assured Fuel Initiative, an effort to develop secure domestic sources for the military energy needs. The Pentagon hopes to reduce its use of crude oil from foreign producers and obtain about half of its aviation fuel from alternative sources by 2016. With the B-52 now approved to use the FT blend, the USAF will use the test protocols developed during the program to certify the [Boeing C-17 Globemaster III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_C-17_Globemaster_III) and then the [Rockwell B-1B Lancer](4952.md) to use the fuel. To test these two aircraft, the USAF has ordered 281,000 US gal (1,060,000 L) of FT fuel. The USAF intends to test and certify every airframe in its inventory to use the fuel by 2011. They will also supply over 9,000 US gal (34,000 L; 7,500 imp gal) to [NASA](18426568.md) for testing in various aircraft and engines.

The USAF has certified the B-1B, B-52H, C-17, [Lockheed Martin C-130J Super Hercules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_Martin_C-130J_Super_Hercules), [McDonnell Douglas F-4 Phantom](11759.md) (as QF-4 [target drones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Target_drone)), [McDonnell Douglas F-15 Eagle](11715.md), [Lockheed Martin F-22 Raptor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_Martin_F-22_Raptor), and [Northrop T-38 Talon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northrop_T-38_Talon) to use the synthetic fuel blend.[^cite_note-65]

The U.S. Air Force's C-17 Globemaster III, [F-16](11642.md) and F-15 are certified for use of hydrotreated renewable jet fuels.[^cite_note-66][^cite_note-one-67] The USAF plans to certify over 40 models for fuels derived from waste oils and plants by 2013.[^cite_note-one-67] The [U.S. Army](32087.md) is considered one of the few customers of [biofuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biofuel) large enough to potentially bring biofuels up to the volume production needed to reduce costs.[^cite_note-one-67] The [U.S. Navy](20518076.md) has also flown a [Boeing F/A-18E/F Super Hornet](845012.md) dubbed the "Green Hornet" at 1.7 times the speed of sound using a biofuel blend.[^cite_note-one-67] The [Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DARPA) (DARPA) funded a \$6.7 million project with [Honeywell UOP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UOP_LLC) to develop technologies to create jet fuels from biofeedstocks for use by the United States and NATO militaries.[^cite_note-68]

In April 2011, four USAF [F-15E Strike Eagles](495724.md) flew over the [Philadelphia Phillies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia_Phillies) opening ceremony using a blend of traditional jet fuel and synthetic biofuels. This flyover made history as it was the first flyover to use biofuels in the [Department of Defense](7279897.md).[^cite_note-69]

Jet fuel vs oil prices

[Oil prices increased about fivefold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2000s_energy_crisis) from 2003 to 2008, raising fears that world petroleum production is becoming [unable to keep up with demand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak_oil). The fact that there are few alternatives to [petroleum](23195.md) for aviation fuel adds urgency to the [search for alternatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_of_aviation#Alternative_fuels). Twenty-five airlines were bankrupted or stopped operations in the first six months of 2008, largely due to fuel costs.[^cite_note-80]

In 2015 ASTM approved a modification to Specification D1655 Standard Specification for Aviation Turbine Fuels to permit up to 50 ppm (50 mg/kg) of FAME ([fatty acid methyl ester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatty_acid_methyl_ester)) in jet fuel to allow higher cross-contamination from biofuel production.[^cite_note-81]

## Worldwide consumption of jet fuel

Worldwide demand of jet fuel has been steadily increasing since 1980. Consumption more than tripled in 30 years from 1,837,000 barrels/day in 1980, to 5,220,000 in 2010.[^cite_note-82] Around 30% of the worldwide consumption of jet fuel is in the US (1,398,130 barrels/day in 2012).

## Taxation

See also: [Aviation taxation and subsidies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviation_taxation_and_subsidies)

Article 24 of the [Chicago Convention on International Civil Aviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Convention_on_International_Civil_Aviation) of 7 December 1944 stipulates that when flying from one contracting state to another, the fuel that is already on board aircraft may not be taxed by the state where the aircraft lands, nor by a state through whose airspace the aircraft has flown. This is to prevent double taxation. It is sometimes suggested that the Chicago Convention precludes the taxation of aviation fuel. However, this is not correct. The Chicago Convention does not preclude a fuel tax on domestic flights or on refuelling before international flights.[^cite_note-faber2018-83]<sup>: 22 </sup>

Article 15 of the Chicago Convention is also sometimes said to ban fuel taxes. Article 15 states: "No fees, dues or other charges shall be imposed by any contracting State in respect solely of the right of transit over or entry into or exit from its territory of any aircraft of a contracting State or persons or property thereon." However, ICAO distinguishes between charges and taxes, and Article 15 does not prohibit the levying of taxes without a service provided.[^cite_note-faber2018-83]<sup>: 23 </sup>

In the European Union, commercial aviation fuel [is exempt from taxation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_fuel_taxation_in_the_European_Union), according to the 2003 [Energy Taxation Directive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_Taxation_Directive).[^cite_note-Energy_Taxation_Directive-84] EU member states may tax jet fuel via bilateral agreements, however no such agreements exist.[^cite_note-faber2018-83]

In the United States, most [states tax jet fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_taxes_in_the_United_States#Aviation_fuel_taxes).[^cite_note-U.S._Energy_Information_Administration-85]

## Health effects

General health hazards associated with exposure to jet fuel vary according to its components, exposure duration (acute vs. long-term), route of administration (dermal vs. respiratory vs. oral), and exposure phase (vapor vs. aerosol vs. raw fuel).[^cite_note-86][^cite_note-\:0-87] Kerosene-based hydrocarbon fuels are complex mixtures which may contain up to 260+ aliphatic and aromatic hydrocarbon compounds including toxicants such as benzene, n-hexane, toluene, xylenes, trimethylpentane, methoxyethanol, naphthalenes.[^cite_note-\:0-87] While time-weighted average hydrocarbon fuel exposures can often be below recommended exposure limits, peak exposure can occur, and the health impact of occupational exposures is not fully understood. Evidence of the health effects of jet fuels comes from reports on both temporary or persisting biological from acute, subchronic, or chronic exposure of humans or animals to kerosene-based hydrocarbon fuels, or the constituent chemicals of these fuels, or to fuel combustion products. The effects studied include: [cancer](105219.md), [skin conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skin_condition), [respiratory disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Respiratory_disease),[^cite_note-88] [immune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immune_disorder) and [hematological disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hematologic_disease),[^cite_note-89] [neurological effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurological_disorder),[^cite_note-90] [visual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_impairment) and [hearing disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ototoxicity),[^cite_note-91][^cite_note-92] renal and [hepatic diseases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liver_disease), [cardiovascular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiovascular_disease) conditions, [gastrointestinal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastrointestinal_disease) disorders, [genotoxic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genotoxicity) and [metabolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolic_disorder) effects.[^cite_note-\:0-87][^cite_note-93]

The Chicago Convention provides no obstacle to placing a tax on domestic or intra-EU aviation fuel. The Convention bans parties from imposing taxes on fuel already on board an aircraft when it lands in another country but it contains no prohibition on taxing the fuel sold to aircraft in a country. Further, the Chicago Convention is not applicable to domestic aviation. It is often suggested that the Chicago Convention exempts aviation fuel from taxation. However, the Chicago Convention only exempts fuels already on-board aircraft when landing, and retained on board when leaving, from taxation. Article 24 states: 'Fuel... on board an aircraft of a contracting State, on arrival in the territory of another contracting State and retained on board on leaving the territory of that State shall be exempt from customs duty, inspection fees or similar national or local duties and charges.' Therefore, Article 24 does not prohibit the taxing of fuel taken on board in a particular country but rather prohibits the taxation of fuel that was already on board the aircraft when it landed, i.e. Member States cannot tax aviation fuel purchased in another country that arrives on board the aircraft. The purpose of this Article is to prevent double taxation.
