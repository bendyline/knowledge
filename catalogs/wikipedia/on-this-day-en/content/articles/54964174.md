Across [Nigeria](21383.md), there are a series of disputes over [arable land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arable_land) between [Fulani herders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fulani_herders) and non-Fulani farmers. The conflicts have been especially prominent in the [Middle Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Belt) (North Central) since the return of democracy in 1999. More recently, they have deteriorated into attacks on farmers by Fulani herders.

Attacks have also taken place in Northwestern Nigeria against farmers who are mainly [Hausa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hausa_people), who are almost entirely Muslim. Many Fulani communities, who are usually farmers, have also been attacked and raided by Fulani bandits and other militias.[^cite_note-3] The conflict has taken on religious and ethnic dimensions: many of the farmers are Christians of various ethnicities while most of the herders are Muslim Fulani. Thousands of people have died since the attacks began. Sedentary farming in rural communities are often targets of attacks because of their vulnerability. There are fears that the conflict will spread to other West African countries, but that has often been downplayed by governments in the region. Attacks on herders have also led them to retaliating by attacking other communities.[^cite_note-foreignpolicy-4][^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6]

## Background

[Herder–farmer conflicts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomadic_conflict) in Nigeria have deep roots and date back to pre-colonial times (before the 1900s). However, these conflicts have become far more severe in recent decades due to [population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_growth) pressures, [climate change](5042951.md), and various other factors. During the British colonial era, herders and farmers would agree on a system called *burti*, in which specific migration routes were set up for herders, with mutual agreement from the farmers, [herders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herder), and local authorities. However, the *burti* system collapsed around the 1970s when farmers increasingly claimed ownership of lands along cattle migration paths, increasingly leading to conflicts.[^cite_note-Blench_2010-7]

Before, herders frequently exchanged milk for cereal grains with farming communities. However, in recent decades, milk is no longer being widely bartered as packaged beverages became more popular in towns.[^cite_note-Blench_2010-7]

Modern medicines have also made it possible for herders to move their livestock further south into the "[tsetse fly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsetse_fly) zone" in the south, whereas before, herders could not keep their cattle on a large scale due to [tropical diseases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_disease) in [humid climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humid_subtropical_climate) zones. Starting from those implemented by the British colonial administration, tsetse control programs have reduced the threat of diseases such as [trypanosomiasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trypanosomiasis). Today, herders also have easy access to drugs for [trypanosomiasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trypanosomiasis) and [dermatophilosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dermatophilosis) in order to keep their livestock alive. In addition, over the past several decades, herders have cross-bred [trypanosome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trypanosoma)-intolerant [zebu cattle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zebu_cattle) with [trypanosome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trypanosoma)-tolerant humpless breeds, thereby increasing the cattle's tolerance of [tropical diseases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_disease). All of these factors have enabled the widespread migration of Fulani herders into the southernmost areas of Nigeria, where they could easily sell their livestock for higher prices due to strong demand for beef and other meat products in Nigeria's populous southern towns and cities. However, in the south, they would encounter sedentary communities that have not historically had any experience with peacefully negotiating and co-existing with nomadic herders. Increasing ease of access to weapons and religious polarisation among both Christians and Muslims have added to the potential for violence.[^cite_note-Blench_2010-7]

Since the [Fourth Nigerian Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Nigerian_Republic)'s founding in 1999, farmer–herder violence has killed more than 19,000–28,551 people and displaced hundreds of thousands more,most of them are Christian[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10]. It followed a trend in the increase of farmer–herder conflicts throughout much of the western [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel), due to an expansion of agriculturist population and cultivated land at the expense of pasturelands; deteriorating environmental conditions, desertification and [soil degradation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_degradation);[^cite_note-time-11] [population growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_growth);[^cite_note-foreignpolicy-4] breakdown in traditional conflict resolution mechanisms of land and [water disputes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_disputes); and proliferation of [small arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_arm) and crime in rural areas.[^cite_note-12] Insecurity and violence have led many populations to create self-defence forces and ethnic and tribal militias, which have engaged in further violence. The majority of farmer–herder clashes have occurred between Muslim [Fulani herdsmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fulani_herdsmen) and farmers, exacerbating [hostilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hostilities).[^cite_note-13]

### Regional conflicts in Jos and Kaduna

The farmer/herder conflicts have been taking place in regions which have been unstable since the 2000s. Urban conflicts in [Jos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos) and [Kaduna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaduna_\(city\)) have been particularly violent and, despite violent clashes with the authorities, their causes have never been addressed politically. Conflicts might not have been addressed adequately because traditional authorities have not been fulfilling their role in colonial-era settlements.[^cite_note-18]

Over time the periodic clashes between herders and farmers in Northern and North-Central Nigeria have precipitated a general climate of insecurity. This widespread insecurity both allows for and is perpetuated by acts of broader [criminality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminality), in which gangs of bandits target locations in the area for raids, mass kidnappings, and looting.[^cite_note-19]

## Causes of the conflict

### Land conflicts

[Conflicts between farmers and herders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomadic_conflict) can be understood as a problem of access to land. The beginning of the 21st century witnessed an expansion of the [agriculturist](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/agriculturist) population and its cultivated land at the expense of pasturelands in the [Middle Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Belt). In an already politically unstable region, it has never been possible to ascertain a legal title to land for every farmer. As a result, [transhumance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transhumance) routes of herders were no longer available, especially in a context of global warming.[^cite_note-20]

### Climate change

Further information: [Climate change in Nigeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_in_Nigeria)

Deteriorating environmental conditions, [desertification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desertification) and [soil degradation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_degradation)[^cite_note-time-11][^cite_note-21][^cite_note-22] have led Fulani herdsmen from Northern Nigeria to change their [transhumance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transhumance) routes. Access to pastureland and watering points in the Middle Belt became essential for herdsmen travelling from the North of the country. It is often assumed that climate change is the driver of the conflict but a recent study suggests that climate change does not automatically cause the conflict.[^cite_note-auto-23] In actuality, regions [vulnerable to climate change](66204363.md) (Northern Regions) experience less farmer-herder conflict and less intense farmer-herder fighting.[^cite_note-auto-23] It is argued that ethnic conflict between farming and herding groups is the primary mechanism behind the farmer-herder conflict nexus.[^cite_note-auto-23]

## Responses

The Nigerian government has been unwilling to address the causes of the crisis.[^cite_note-24] Fighting [Boko Haram](23769768.md) in the North-East and facing rising levels of violence in different regions of the country, the government has nonetheless tried to implement a few measures, like in creating the [Agro Rangers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agro_Rangers), a force of 10,000 deployed across all states and the [Federal Capital Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Capital_Territory) to prevent attacks,[^cite_note-nscdcgovng-25][^cite_note-nigevoz-26][^cite_note-VoA-27] though its efficacy has been questioned.[^cite_note-bbc1-28]

Due to the widely perceived inefficacy of the Nigerian government, armed vigilante groups have sprung up in many farmer communities. This situation would often lead to vicious cycles of bloody feuds among farmers and herders. Local politicians and religious leaders have also exacerbated conflicts by recruiting members and frequently exaggerating claims.[^cite_note-Blench_2010-7]

Since 2012, there have been projects to create [transhumance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transhumance) corridors through the Middle Belt. Mostly supported by Northern lawmakers and opposed by their Southern counterparts, these endeavours have been rarely successful.[^cite_note-29]

In 2019, President [Muhammadu Buhari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammadu_Buhari) tried to create Rural Grazing Area ([RUGA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruga_policy)) settlements. His proposal was met with fierce criticism.[^cite_note-30] On 17 May 2021, the 17 Southern governors in Nigeria issued the [Asaba Declaration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asaba_Declaration), aimed at solving the crisis.[^cite_note-31]

Although [ranching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranching), where cattle are kept in enclosed parcels of land, has frequently been proposed as a solution to the crisis, this has proven to be highly unfeasible in Nigeria due to poor infrastructure (with unstable supplies of electricity, water, and fuel) and difficulties with acquisition and legal ownership of land.[^cite_note-Rachael_2018-32][^cite_note-Royal_News_2018-33] [Land grabbing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_grabbing) and [cattle rustling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cattle_rustling) are also potential difficulties that ranchers would have to deal with. Ranchers would also be unable to compete with nomadic herders with zero land-related costs.[^cite_note-34]

Since 2022, [Genocide Watch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genocide_Watch) has classified the conflicts as a genocide of Christians perpetrated by ethnic Fulani jihadists. The organisation places Nigeria on the stages "Stage 9: Extermination" and "Stage 10: Denial" in the [Ten Stages of Genocide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_stages_of_genocide) model developed by American scholar [Gregory Stanton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregory_Stanton).[^cite_note-35] In 2025, the American president [Donald Trump](4848272.md) said he would consider military intervention in the form of American troops or airstrikes if Nigeria could not control the situation, saying "they're killing record numbers of Christians in Nigeria".[^cite_note-36] Daniel Bwala, an adviser to the Nigerian president [Bola Tinubu](3034327.md), denied accusations of a Christian genocide,[^cite_note-Eboh-37] as has the [Economic Community of West African States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_Community_of_West_African_States).[^cite_note-38] *[The New York Times](30680.md)* says that attacks on Christians have received the most attention within Nigeria and internationally.[^cite_note-39] Analysts speaking to [Reuters](18998750.md) note that the majority of attacks from Islamist insurgents were against Muslims.[^cite_note-Eboh-37] Nigerian officials also said they welcomed assistance on dealing with the insurgency, but insisted Nigeria's territorial integrity must be maintained.[^cite_note-Eboh-37][^cite_note-40]
