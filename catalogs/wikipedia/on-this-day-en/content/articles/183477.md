**Iwate Prefecture** (岩手県, *Iwate-ken*; Japanese pronunciation: [\[iꜜ.wa.te, i.wa.teꜜ.keɴ\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Japanese)[^cite_note-2]) is a [prefecture of Japan](74517.md) located in the [Tōhoku region](202954.md) of [Honshu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honshu).[^cite_note-3] It is the second-largest Japanese prefecture (behind [Hokkaido](58092.md)) at 15,275 square kilometres (5,898 sq mi), with a population of 1,165,886 (as of July 1, 2023). Iwate Prefecture borders [Aomori Prefecture](61732.md) to the north, [Akita Prefecture](61729.md) to the west, and [Miyagi Prefecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miyagi_Prefecture) to the south.

[Morioka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morioka) is the capital and largest city of Iwate Prefecture; other major cities include [Ichinoseki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ichinoseki,_Iwate), [Ōshū](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%8Csh%C5%AB,_Iwate), and [Hanamaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanamaki,_Iwate).[^cite_note-4] Located on Japan's [Pacific Ocean](23070.md) coast, Iwate Prefecture features the easternmost point of Honshu at [Cape Todo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Todo), and shares the highest peaks of the [Ōu Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%8Cu_Mountains)—the longest [mountain range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_range) in Japan—at the border with Akita Prefecture. Iwate Prefecture is home to famous attractions such as [Morioka Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morioka_Castle), the [Buddhist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist) temples of [Hiraizumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiraizumi,_Iwate) including [Chūson-ji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%C5%ABson-ji) and [Mōtsū-ji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C5%8Dts%C5%AB-ji), the Fujiwara no Sato [movie lot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Movie_lot) and [theme park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theme_park) in Ōshū, and the Tenshochi park in [Kitakami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitakami,_Iwate) known for its huge, ancient [cherry trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherry_tree). Iwate has the lowest [population density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_density) of any prefecture outside [Hokkaido](58092.md), with 5% of its total land area having been designated as [national parks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_national_parks_of_Japan).

## Name

There are several theories about the origin of the name "Iwate", but the most well known is the tale *[Oni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oni_\(folklore\)) no tegata*, which is associated with the Mitsuishi or "Three Rocks" Shrine in Morioka. These rocks are said to have been thrown down into Morioka by an eruption of Mt. Iwate. According to the legend, there was once a [demon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oni) who often tormented and harassed the local people. When the people prayed to the [spirits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kami) of Mitsuishi for protection, this demon was immediately shackled to these rocks and forced to make a promise never to trouble the people again.[^cite_note-5] As a seal of his oath, the demon made a handprint on one of the rocks, giving rise to the name Iwate, its direct translation being "rock hand". Even now after rainfall, it is said that the demon's hand print can still be seen there.

## Culture

There are many present-day cultural foods popularly eaten in Iwate Prefecture, some of which include [walnuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walnut), [wanko soba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wanko_soba) (meaning "bowl noodles") and hittsumi-jiru (meaning "pull and tear", in reference to the way the dough is pulled and torn into oval shapes before being turned into noodles).[^cite_note-6] Iwate's prefectural capital [Morioka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morioka) is also popular for its apples, blooming in May and ready for [harvest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvest) from September to November.[^cite_note-7]

Japanese poet [Matsuo Bashō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matsuo_Bash%C5%8D) visited Iwate and wrote about it in the journey described in his major work *[Oku no Hosomichi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oku_no_Hosomichi)*. He was especially inspired by the town of [Hiraizumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiraizumi).

## History

See also: [Historic Sites of Iwate Prefecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historic_Sites_of_Iwate_Prefecture)

While the entire island of Honshū was claimed by the Japanese, or Yamato, government from earliest times, the imperial forces were unable to occupy any part of what would become Iwate until 802 when two powerful [Emishi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emishi) leaders, [Aterui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aterui) and More, surrendered at Fort Isawa.

The area now known as Iwate Prefecture was inhabited by the [Jōmon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C5%8Dmon_period) people who left their artifacts throughout the prefecture. For example, a large number of burial pits from the Middle Jōmon Period (2,800–1,900 BC) have been found in Nishida. Various sites from the Late Jōmon Period (1,900–1,300 BC) including Tateishi, Makumae and Hatten contain clay figurines, masks and ear and nose shaped clay artifacts. The Kunenbashi site in Kitakami City has yielded stone "swords", tablets and tools as well as clay figurines, earrings and potsherds from the Final Jōmon Period (1,300–300 BC).

The earliest mention of a Japanese presence dates to about 630 when the Hakusan Shrine was said to have been built on Mt. Kanzan in what is now Hiraizumi. At this time various Japanese traders, hunters, adventurers, priests and criminals made their way to Iwate. In 712 the province of [Mutsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutsu_Province), containing all of [Tōhoku](202954.md), was divided into [Dewa Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dewa_Province), the area west of the [Ōu Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%8Cu_Mountains) and Mutsu Province. In 729 Kokuseki-ji Temple was founded in what is now Mizusawa Ward, Oshu City by the itinerant priest [Gyōki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gy%C5%8Dki).

Little is known about relations between these Japanese frontiersmen and the native [Emishi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emishi) but in 776 they took a turn for the worse when large forces of the Yamato army invaded Iwate attacking the Isawa and Shiwa tribes in February and November of that year. More fighting occurred the next and following years but mostly in Dewa and the area south of present-day Iwate prefecture. This situation continued until March 787 when the Yamato army suffered a disastrous defeat in the Battle of Sufuse Village in what is now Mizusawa Ward, Oshu City. There the Emishi leaders and [Aterui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aterui) leading a large cavalry force trapped the Yamato infantry and pushed them into the Kitakami River where their heavy armour proved deadly. Over 1,000 soldiers drowned that day. The Japanese general Ki no Asami Kosami was "rebuked" by the [Emperor Kanmu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Kanmu) when he returned to Kyoto.

Since the Japanese could not win on the battlefield they resorted to other means to conquer the Emishi. Trade for superior quality iron wares and sake made the Emishi dependent on the Japanese for these valuable goods. Bribes were offered to the Emishi leaders in the form of Japanese citizenship and rank if they would defect. Finally a campaign of burning crops and kidnapping the Emishi women and children and relocating them to Western Japan was adopted.&#x20;

In 801, [Sakanoue no Tamuramaro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sakanoue_no_Tamuramaro) began a new campaign against the Isawa Emishi having moderate success. Finally on 15 April 802 the Emishi leaders More and Aterui surrendered with some 500 warriors. The captives were taken to Kyoto for an audience with the emperor and beheaded at Moriyama in Kawachi Province against the wishes of General Sakanoue. This act of cruelty enraged the Emishi leading to another twenty or more years of fighting.

After the surrender numerous forts were built on the Chinese model along the Kitakami River. In 802, Fort Isawa was built in what is now Mizusawa Ward, Oshu City, in 803, Fort Shiwa was built in what is now Morioka City, and in 812 Fort Tokutan was built also in Morioka.

In the latter part of the [Heian period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heian_period), the town of [Hiraizumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiraizumi) in what is now southern Iwate became the capital of the [Northern Fujiwara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Fujiwara). The warrior [Minamoto no Yoshitsune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minamoto_no_Yoshitsune) fled here after the [Genpei War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genpei_War).[^cite_note-8]

Until the [Meiji Restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_Restoration), the area of Iwate prefecture was part of [Mutsu Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutsu_Province).[^cite_note-9]

Iwate Prefecture was created in 1876, in the aftermath of the [Boshin Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boshin_War), which heralded the beginning of the Meiji Restoration.

## Geography

Iwate faces the Pacific Ocean to the east with sheer, rocky cliffs along most of the shoreline interrupted by a few sandy beaches. The border with [Akita Prefecture](61729.md) on the west is generally formed by the highest points of the [Ōu Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%8Cu_Mountains). [Aomori Prefecture](61732.md) is to the north and [Miyagi Prefecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miyagi_Prefecture) is to the south.

The Ōu mountains on the west still contain active volcanoes such as Mt. Iwate (at 2,038 metres (6,686 ft) the highest point in the prefecture) and [Mt. Kurikoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mt._Kurikoma) (1,627 metres (5,338 ft)). But the Kitakami Mountains running through the middle of the prefecture from north to south are much older and have not been active for thousands of years. Mt. Hayachine (1,917 metres (6,289 ft)) lies at the heart of the Kitakami range.

Besides these two mountain ranges and the rugged coastline, the prefecture is characterized by the Kitakami River which flows from north to south between the Ōu and Kitakami mountain ranges. It is the fourth longest river in Japan and the longest in Tōhoku. The basin of the Kitakami is large and fertile providing room for the prefecture's largest cities, industrial parks and farms.

In the past Iwate has been famous for its mineral wealth especially in the form of gold, iron, coal and sulfur but these are no longer produced. There is still an abundance of hot water for onsen, or hot springs, which is the basis of a thriving industry. The forests of the prefecture are another valuable resource. Before World War II the forests were mainly composed of beech but since then there has been a huge swing towards the production of faster growing Japanese cedar. Recently, though, there has been a push to restore the original beech forests in some areas.

As of 31 March 2019, 5% of the total land area of the prefecture was designated as [Natural Parks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_national_parks_of_Japan#History), namely [Towada-Hachimantai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Towada-Hachimantai_National_Park) and [Sanriku Fukkō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanriku_Fukk%C5%8D_National_Park) National Parks; [Kurikoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurikoma_Quasi-National_Park) and [Hayachine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hayachine_Quasi-National_Park) Quasi-National Parks; and [Goyōzan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goy%C5%8Dzan_Prefectural_Natural_Park), [Hanamaki Onsenkyō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanamaki_Onsenky%C5%8D_Prefectural_Natural_Park), [Kuji-Hiraniwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuji-Hiraniwa_Prefectural_Natural_Park), [Murone Kōgen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murone_K%C5%8Dgen_Prefectural_Natural_Park), [Oritsume Basenkyō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oritsume_Basenky%C5%8D_Prefectural_Natural_Park), [Sotoyama-Hayasaka Heights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sotoyama-Hayasaka_Prefectural_Natural_Park), and [Yuda Onsenkyō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuda_Onsenky%C5%8D_Prefectural_Natural_Park) Prefectural Natural Parks.[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11]

## Economy

Iwate's industry is concentrated around Morioka and specializes in semiconductor and communications manufacturing.

As of March 2011, the prefecture produced 3.9% of Japan's beef and 14.4% of broiler chickens.[^cite_note-12] In 2009, 866 tons of dolphins and whales were harvested off the coast of Iwate, accounting for more than half of Japan's total catch of 1,404 tons.[^cite_note-13]

## Demographics



Iwate prefecture population pyramid in 2020

As of October 1, 2022, Iwate Prefecture had a population of 1,180,595. Consistent with [population trends across Japan](15015085.md), Iwate has experienced a slow, but steady decline in growth since the end of World War II with a major contributing factor being an aging population. As of October 2019, 33.1% of the population was over 65 with 15.4% aged between 65 and 74 and 17.7% over 75.[^cite_note-14] Moreover, the 1950 census saw the most births in the prefecture with 45,968 reported, but since then there has been a major decline to only 7,220 births from October 2018 to September 2019. This is in contrast to the 17,681 deaths recorded during the same period. Finally, Iwate has a negative net migration rate (-2.8) with 21,265 permanently leaving the prefecture and 16,795 entering.

On 13 July 869, a [magnitude 8.6 earthquake and tsunami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/869_Jogan_Sanriku_earthquake) struck the coast of Iwate.

On 14 November 1230, volcanic activity was reported.

On 2 December 1611, a [magnitude 8.1 earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1611_Keicho_Sanriku_earthquake) and tsunami were reported to have killed over 3,000 horses and people.

In 1662 Morioka and its suburbs were hit by a large flood leaving 1,000 dead.

Volcanic activity was reported on Mt. Iwate on 23 March 1686 and 14 April 1687.

In 1700, a tsunami from the [1700 Cascadia earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1700_Cascadia_earthquake) struck Iwate Prefecture. No records from North America exist, but the event was reconstructed using Japanese records.

On 13 May 1717, the Hanamaki area was struck with a magnitude 7.6 earthquake opening cracks in the ground everywhere. There was also widespread destruction of houses and shops.

In Nanbu-han alone, 49,594 people starved to death in the famine of 1755.

Severe famines occurred in the region from 1783 to 1787 and again from 1832 to 1838.

Cholera outbreaks occurred in August 1879, in Miyako and Kuji.

In July 1882, a cholera outbreak in Kamaishi left 302 dead and warnings about drinking water were posted throughout the prefecture.

In April 1884, there was another outbreak of cholera in Kamaishi.

In September 1886, cholera outbreaks throughout Iwate left 312 dead.

On 15 June 1896, at 7\:32 am, a [magnitude 8.5 earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1896_Sanriku_earthquake) struck offshore. The ensuing tsunami sent waves onto the coast of Iwate at Yoshihama, in what is now Sanriku town, reaching 24 metres (79 ft) in height. 18,158 people died in Iwate alone while some 10,000 homes were destroyed. Fishermen fishing the ocean about 20 miles (32 km) offshore felt nothing, then returning home the next morning found the shore littered with their homes and the bodies of their loved ones.

In September 1899, dysentery spread throughout the prefecture killing 2,070 people.

There was a widespread crop failure due to violent storms in September 1902. Only 32,900 tons of rice were produced in Iwate, just 30% of the previous year's harvest.

In 1905, there was again a massive crop failure due to heavy rain and cold leading to famine in 1906. People were reduced to eating straw, acorns and roots.

In 1919, a small eruption occurred at Nishi-Iwate.[^cite_note-17]

On 3 March 1933, a [magnitude 8.1 earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1933_Sanriku_earthquake) struck offshore killing 3,008 people and destroying 7,479 homes. This is the fifth worst earthquake in Japan since 1923.

Small explosions shook Mt. Iwate throughout 1934 and 1935.

In August 1957, there was volcanic activity on Mt. Kurikoma.

There was volcanic activity on [Mt. Akita-Komagatake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Akita-Komagatake) from September to December 1970 with lava flows visible from Morioka.

In 2003, earthquakes struck on 26 May (M7.0 off the coast of Kesennuma, Miyagi Prefecture), 25 July (three jolts of M5.5, 6.2 and 5.3 in southern Iwate) and 26 September (M8.3 in Hokkaido but strongly felt in Iwate).

At 8\:43 am on 14 June 2008, Iwate was struck by a [7.2 magnitude earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Iwate%E2%80%93Miyagi_Nairiku_earthquake). The epicenter was about 8 kilometres (5.0 mi) underground in Ichinoseki City. Thirteen deaths were reported and massive landshifts occurred in Northern Miyagi and Southern Iwate.

On Friday, 11 March 2011, a [magnitude 9.0 earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_T%C5%8Dhoku_earthquake_and_tsunami) hit this area, triggering a large tsunami and extensive damage. The highest run up of water was measured at over 38 metres (125 ft).[^cite_note-18] The disaster destroyed 9,672 of the prefecture's fishing vessels, damaged 108 of 111 ports, wiped out nearly all of the prefecture's fish processing centers, and caused ¥371.5 billion in damage to the prefecture's fishing industry.[^cite_note-19]

On 26 February 2025, [a wildfire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%8Cfunato_wildfire) began to the southeast of Ōfunato, becoming Japan's largest fire in over 50 years. The fire grew to cover 2,900 ha (7,200 acres), damaged at least 78 buildings, and forced thousands to evacuate. It was declared contained on 9 March.[^cite_note-\:0-20]

On 20 April 2026, a strong offshore earthquake struck near Japan’s northeastern coast, triggering tsunami warnings for Iwate Prefecture. Small tsunami waves reached parts of the coastline, and authorities urged residents to evacuate to higher ground amid warnings of potentially larger waves.[^cite_note-21]

## Transportation

### Rail

Iwate is served by the [East Japan Railway Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Japan_Railway_Company) (JR East) which operates two high-speed [shinkansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinkansen) lines in the prefecture and seven local rail lines. The [Tōhoku Shinkansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C5%8Dhoku_Shinkansen) has stations at Ichinoseki, Oshu, Kitakami, Hanamaki, Morioka, Iwate Town and Ninohe. The [Akita Shinkansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akita_Shinkansen) starts at Morioka Station and connects to locations in Akita Prefecture.

JR East operates passenger and freight trains on the [Tōhoku Main Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C5%8Dhoku_Main_Line) or Tōhoku-honsen in Iwate but sold the track north of Morioka to the [Iwate Galaxy Railway Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iwate_Galaxy_Railway_Line) in 2002. The two lines share track with JR still running freight trains and some passenger trains over IGR track and IGR running occasional passenger trains as far south as Hanamaki. There is a large JR freight yard and maintenance facility in Yahaba.

Local lines include the Ofunato Line, the Kitakami Line, the Kamaishi Line, the Tazawako Line, the Yamada Line and the Hanawa Line.

Other lines include the [Sanriku Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanriku_Railway) which operates two lines along the coast, the North Rias Line and the South Rias Line.
