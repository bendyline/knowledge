A **hospital** is a healthcare institution providing patient treatment with specialized [medical science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_science) and auxiliary healthcare staff and medical equipment.[^cite_note-1] The best-known type of hospital is the **general hospital**, which typically has an [emergency department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergency_department) to treat urgent health problems ranging from fire and accident victims to a sudden illness. A **district hospital** typically is the major health care facility in its region, with many beds for [intensive care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intensive_care) and additional beds for patients who need long-term care.

Specialized hospitals include [trauma centers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trauma_center), [rehabilitation hospitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rehabilitation_hospital), [children's hospitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Children's_hospital), [geriatric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geriatric) hospitals, and hospitals for specific medical needs, such as [psychiatric hospitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychiatric_hospital) for [psychiatric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychiatric) treatment and other disease-specific categories. Specialized hospitals can help reduce [health care costs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_care_costs) compared to general hospitals.[^cite_note-bbc1-2] Hospitals are classified as general, specialty, or government depending on the sources of income received.

A [teaching hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teaching_hospital) campus combines patient care with teaching to health science students, [auxiliary healthcare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allied_health_professionals) students, and qualified [medical graduates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_degree) completing their postgraduate [residencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Residency_\(medicine\)) before licensure to practice. A health science facility smaller than a hospital is generally called a clinic. Hospitals have a range of departments (e.g. surgery and [urgent care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urgent_care)) and specialist units such as [cardiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiology). Some hospitals have [outpatient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outpatient) departments and some have chronic treatment units. Common support units include a [pharmacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hospital_pharmacy), [pathology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathology), and [radiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiology). Facilities that combine many health care functions, including general or specialized patient care, teaching, research, and so on, may use the term medical center. This term can also refer to an office complex with various unaffiliated health services or any kind of clinic or hospital.

A large hospital or medical center also often serves as the [administrative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Healthcare_administration) headquarters of a larger [health system](60976.md) which may have multiple sites.

Hospitals are typically funded by [public funding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publicly_funded_healthcare), health organizations ([for-profit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/For-profit_hospital) or nonprofit), [health insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_insurance) companies, or charities, including direct charitable donations. Historically, hospitals were often founded and funded by Christian [religious orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_orders), or by charitable individuals and leaders.[^cite_note-3]

Hospitals are currently staffed by professional physicians, surgeons, nurses, and [allied health professionals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allied_health_professionals). In the past, however, this work was usually performed by the members of founding religious orders or by [volunteers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volunteers). However, there are various Catholic religious orders, such as the [Alexians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexians) and the [Bon Secours Sisters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bon_Secours_Sisters) that still focus on hospital ministry in the late 1990s, as well as several other [Christian denominations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_denomination), including the [Evangelical-Lutherans](23371382.md) and [Methodists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodists), which run hospitals.[^cite_note-Granquist2015-4] [Deaconesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deaconess) have played a prominent role in healthcare.[^cite_note-5][^cite_note-Oldham1902-6] In accordance with the original meaning of the word, hospitals were original "places of hospitality", and this meaning is still preserved in the names of some institutions such as the [Royal Hospital Chelsea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Hospital_Chelsea), established in 1681 as a retirement and nursing home for veteran soldiers.

## Etymology

During the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), hospitals served different functions from modern institutions in that they were [almshouses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almshouses) for the poor, [hostels](233996.md) for [pilgrims](23763.md), or hospital schools. The word "hospital" comes from the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language) *hospes*, signifying a stranger or foreigner, hence a guest. Another noun derived from this, *hospitium* came to signify hospitality, that is the relation between guest and shelterer, hospitality, friendliness, and hospitable reception. By [metonymy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metonymy), the Latin word then came to mean a guest-chamber, guest's lodging, an [inn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inn).[^cite_note-7] *Hospes* is thus the root for the English words *host* (where the *p* was dropped for convenience of pronunciation) *[hospitality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hospitality)*, *[hospice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hospice)*, *hostel,* and *hotel*. The latter modern word derives from Latin via the [Old French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_French) romance word *hostel*, which developed a silent *s*, which letter was eventually removed from the word, the loss of which is signified by a [circumflex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumflex) in the [modern French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language) word *hôtel*. The German word *Spital* shares similar roots.

## Types

Some patients go to a hospital just for [diagnosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_diagnosis), treatment, or therapy and then leave ("[outpatients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outpatients)") without staying overnight; while others are "admitted" and stay overnight or for several days or weeks or months ("[inpatients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inpatients)"). Hospitals are usually distinguished from other types of medical facilities by their ability to admit and care for inpatients whilst the others, which are smaller, are often described as [clinics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinics).

### General and acute care

"General hospital" redirects here. For the American soap opera, see [General Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Hospital). For other uses, see [General Hospital (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Hospital_\(disambiguation\)).

The best-known type of hospital is the general hospital, also known as an acute-care hospital. These facilities handle many kinds of disease and injury, and normally have an emergency department (sometimes known as "accident & emergency") or [trauma center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trauma_center) to deal with immediate and urgent threats to health. Larger cities may have several hospitals of varying sizes and facilities. Some hospitals, especially in the United States and Canada, have their own ambulance service.

### District

Main article: [Regional hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_hospital)

See also: [Rural general hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rural_general_hospital)

A district hospital typically is the major health care facility in its region, with large numbers of beds for [intensive care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intensive_care), [critical care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_care_medicine), and long-term care.



University of Ghana Medical Hospital

In California, "district hospital" refers specifically to a class of healthcare facility created shortly after [World War II](32927.md) to address a shortage of hospital beds in many local communities.[^cite_note-DHLF2013-8][^cite_note-Knox2013-9] Even today, district hospitals are the sole public hospitals in 19 of California's counties,[^cite_note-DHLF2013-8] and are the sole locally accessible hospital within nine additional counties in which one or more other hospitals are present at a substantial distance from a local community.[^cite_note-DHLF2013-8] Twenty-eight of California's rural hospitals and 20 of its critical-access hospitals are district hospitals.[^cite_note-Knox2013-9] They are formed by local municipalities, have boards that are individually elected by their local communities, and exist to serve local needs.[^cite_note-DHLF2013-8][^cite_note-Knox2013-9] They are a particularly important provider of healthcare to uninsured patients and patients with [Medi-Cal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medi-Cal) (which is California's [Medicaid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicaid) program, serving low-income persons, some [senior citizens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senior_citizens), persons with [disabilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disabilities), children in [foster care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foster_care), and pregnant women).[^cite_note-DHLF2013-8][^cite_note-Knox2013-9] In 2012, district hospitals provided \$54 million in uncompensated care in California.[^cite_note-Knox2013-9]

### Specialized

A specialty hospital is primarily and exclusively dedicated to one or a few related [medical specialties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_specialties).[^cite_note-10] Subtypes include [rehabilitation hospitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rehabilitation_hospital), [children's hospitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Children's_hospital), seniors' ([geriatric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geriatric)) hospitals, [long-term acute care facilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long-term_acute_care_facility), and hospitals for dealing with specific medical needs such as [psychiatric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychiatric) problems (see [psychiatric hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychiatric_hospital)), cancer treatment, certain disease categories such as cardiac, oncology, or orthopedic problems, and so forth.

In Germany, specialised hospitals are called *Fachkrankenhaus*; an example is [Fachkrankenhaus Coswig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fachkrankenhaus_Coswig) (thoracic surgery). In India, specialty hospitals are known as *super-specialty hospitals* and are distinguished from multispecialty hospitals which are composed of several specialties.

Specialised hospitals can help reduce [health care costs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_care_costs) compared to general hospitals. For example, [Narayana Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narayana_Health)'s cardiac unit in [Bangalore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangalore) specialises in cardiac surgery and allows for a significantly greater number of patients. It has 3,000 beds and performs 3,000 paediatric cardiac operations annually, the largest number in the world for such a facility.[^cite_note-bbc1-2][^cite_note-fastcompany-11] Surgeons are paid on a fixed salary instead of per operation, thus when the number of procedures increases, the hospital is able to take advantage of [economies of scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economies_of_scale) and reduce its cost per procedure.[^cite_note-fastcompany-11] Each specialist may also become more efficient by working on one procedure like a [production line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Production_line).[^cite_note-bbc1-2]

### Teaching

Main article: [Teaching hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teaching_hospital)

A [teaching hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teaching_hospital) delivers healthcare to patients as well as training to prospective [medical professionals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_professionals) such as [medical students](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_students) and student [nurses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nursing). It may be linked to a medical school or nursing school, and may be involved in [medical research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_research). Students may also observe clinical work in the hospital.[^cite_note-12]

### Clinics

Clinics generally provide only outpatient services, but some may have a few inpatient beds and a limited range of services that may otherwise be found in typical hospitals.

## Departments or wards



Hospital beds per 1000 people

A hospital contains one or more wards that house [hospital beds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hospital_bed) for [inpatients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inpatient). It may also have acute services such as an [emergency department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergency_department), [operating theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operating_theatre), and [intensive care unit](6332859.md), as well as a range of medical specialty departments. A well-equipped hospital may be classified as a trauma center. They may also have other services such as a [hospital pharmacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hospital_pharmacy), [radiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiology), [pathology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathology), and [medical laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_laboratories). Some hospitals have outpatient departments such as [behavioral health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral_health) services, [dentistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dentistry), and [rehabilitation services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_medicine_and_rehabilitation). [Freestanding emergency departments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freestanding_emergency_department) are often associated with a hospital, but are located away from the hospital with the intention of providing greater emergency service coverage to a region.

A hospital may also have a department of nursing, headed by a [chief nursing officer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_nursing_officer) or [director of nursing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nursing_management#Director_of_nursing). This department is responsible for the administration of professional nursing practice, [research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nursing_research), and policy for the hospital.

Many units have both a nursing and a medical director that serve as administrators for their respective disciplines within that unit. For example, within an intensive care nursery, a medical director is responsible for physicians and medical care, while the nursing manager is responsible for all the nurses and nursing care.

Support units may include a [medical records department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_records_department), [release of information department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Release_of_information_department), technical support, [clinical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinical_engineering), facilities management, plant operations, dining services, and security departments.

### Remote monitoring

The [COVID-19 pandemic](62750956.md) stimulated the development of virtual wards across the British [NHS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NHS). Patients are managed at home, monitoring their own oxygen levels using an oxygen saturation probe if necessary and supported by telephone. [West Hertfordshire Hospitals NHS Trust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Hertfordshire_Hospitals_NHS_Trust) managed around 1200 patients at home between March and June 2020 and planned to continue the system after COVID-19, initially for respiratory patients.[^cite_note-13] [Mersey Care NHS Foundation Trust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersey_Care_NHS_Foundation_Trust) started a COVID Oximetry\@Home service in April 2020. This enables them to monitor more than 5000 patients a day in their own homes. The technology allows nurses, carers, or patients to record and monitor vital signs such as blood oxygen levels.[^cite_note-14]

AD 400, recorded examples of healing institutions.[^cite_note-15] According to the *[Mahavamsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahavamsa)*, the ancient chronicle of Sinhalese royalty, written in the sixth century AD, King [Pandukabhaya of Sri Lanka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pandukabhaya_of_Sri_Lanka) (r. 437–367 BC) had lying-in-homes and hospitals (Sivikasotthi-Sala).[^cite_note-16] A [hospital and medical training center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Guneshapur) also existed at [Gundeshapur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gundeshapur), a major city in southwest of the [Sassanid Persian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sassanid_Persian_Empire) founded in AD 271 by [Shapur I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shapur_I).[^cite_note-17] In [ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece), temples dedicated to the healer-god [Asclepius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asclepius), known as [Asclepeion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asclepeion) functioned as centers of medical advice, prognosis, and healing.[^cite_note-18] The Asclepeia spread to the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire). While public healthcare was non-existent in the Roman Empire, military hospitals called *valetudinaria* did exist stationed in military barracks and would serve the soldiers and slaves within the fort.[^cite_note-ziegler-19] Evidence exists that some civilian hospitals, while unavailable to the Roman population, were occasionally privately built in extremely wealthy Roman households located in the countryside for that family, although this practice seems to have ended in 80 AD.[^cite_note-20]

### Middle Ages

See also: [Byzantine medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_medicine), [Medieval medicine of Western Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_medicine_of_Western_Europe), and [Medicine in the medieval Islamic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine_in_the_medieval_Islamic_world)

&#x20;The declaration of [Christianity](5211.md) as an accepted religion in the Roman Empire drove an expansion of the provision of care.[^cite_note-21] Following the [First Council of Nicaea](11118.md) in AD 325 construction of a hospital in every cathedral town was begun, including among the earliest hospitals by [Saint Sampson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampson_the_Hospitable) in [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople) and by [Basil, bishop of Caesarea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basil_of_Caesarea) in modern-day Turkey.[^cite_note-22] By the twelfth century, Constantinople had two well-organised hospitals, staffed by doctors who were both male and female. Facilities included systematic treatment procedures and specialised wards for various diseases.[^cite_note-23]



Entrance to the [Qalawun complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qalawun_complex) in [Cairo](6293.md), Egypt, which housed the notable Mansuri hospital

The earliest general hospital in the Islamic world was built in 805 in [Baghdad](4492.md) by [Harun Al-Rashid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harun_Al-Rashid).[^cite_note-24][^cite_note-Glubb-25] By the 10th century, Baghdad had five more hospitals, while [Damascus](8914.md) had six hospitals by the 15th century, and [Córdoba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C%C3%B3rdoba,_Andalusia) alone had 50 major hospitals, many exclusively for the military, by the end of the 15th century.[^cite_note-aramco0317-26] The Islamic [bimaristan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bimaristan) served as a center of medical treatment, as well [nursing home](430716.md) and [lunatic asylum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunatic_asylum). It typically treated the poor, as the rich would have been treated in their own homes.[^cite_note-nlm-27] Hospitals in this era were the first to require medical licenses for doctors, and compensation for negligence could be made.[^cite_note-28][^cite_note-Alatas-29] Hospitals were forbidden by law to turn away patients who were unable to pay.[^cite_note-nlm-27] These hospitals were financially supported by [waqfs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waqf), as well as state funds.[^cite_note-aramco0317-26]

In India, [public hospitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_hospital) existed at least since the reign of [Firuz Shah Tughlaq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firuz_Shah_Tughlaq) in the 14th century. The [Mughal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_emperors) emperor [Jahangir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jahangir) in the 17th century established hospitals in large cities at government expense with records showing salaries and grants for medicine being paid for by the government.[^cite_note-30]

In China, during the [Song dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Song_dynasty), the state began to take on social welfare functions previously provided by Buddhist monasteries and instituted public hospitals, [hospices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hospice) and [dispensaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_dispensary).[^cite_note-31]

### Early modern and Enlightenment Europe



A hospital ward in 6th century [France](5843419.md)

In Europe the medieval concept of Christian care evolved during the 16th and 17th centuries into a secular one. In [England](9316.md), after the [dissolution of the monasteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_monasteries) in 1540 by King [Henry VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_VIII), the church abruptly ceased to be the supporter of hospitals, and only by direct petition from the citizens of London, were the hospitals [St Bartholomew's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Bartholomew's_Hospital), [St Thomas's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Thomas's_Hospital) and [St Mary of Bethlehem's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bethlem_Royal_Hospital) (Bedlam) endowed directly by [the Crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Crown); this was the first instance of secular support being provided for medical institutions.

In 1682, [Charles II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_II_of_England) founded the [Royal Hospital Chelsea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Hospital_Chelsea) as a retirement home for old soldiers known as [Chelsea Pensioners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chelsea_Pensioners), an instance of the use of the word "hospital" to mean an [almshouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almshouse).[^cite_note-32] Ten years later, [Mary II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_II_of_England) founded the [Royal Hospital for Seamen, Greenwich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenwich_Hospital,_London), with the same purpose.[^cite_note-33]

The [voluntary hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voluntary_hospital) movement began in the early 18th century, with hospitals being founded in London by the 1720s, including [Westminster Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Hospital) (1719) promoted by the [private bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_bank) [C. Hoare & Co](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._Hoare_&_Co) and [Guy's Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guy's_Hospital) (1724) funded from the bequest of the wealthy merchant, [Thomas Guy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Guy).

Other hospitals sprang up in London and other British cities over the century, many paid for by private subscriptions. St Bartholomew's in London was rebuilt from 1730 to 1759,[^cite_note-39] and the London Hospital, Whitechapel, opened in 1752.

These hospitals represented a turning point in the function of the institution; they began to evolve from being basic places of care for the sick to becoming centers of medical innovation and discovery and the principal place for the [education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_education) and training of prospective practitioners. Some of the era's greatest surgeons and doctors worked and passed on their knowledge at the hospitals.[^cite_note-40] They also changed from being mere homes of refuge to being complex institutions for the provision and advancement of medicine and care for sick. The [Charité](2559577.md) was founded in Berlin in 1710 by King [Frederick I of Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_I_of_Prussia) as a response to an outbreak of plague.

Voluntary hospitals also spread to [Colonial America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonial_America); [Bellevue Hospital](1839902.md) in New York City opened in 1736, first as a workhouse and then later as a hospital; [Pennsylvania Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania_Hospital) in Philadelphia opened in 1752, [New York Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weill_Cornell_Medical_Center), now Weill Cornell Medical Center[^cite_note-41] in New York City opened in 1771, and [Massachusetts General Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_General_Hospital) in Boston opened in 1811.

When the [Vienna General Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna_General_Hospital) opened in 1784 as the world's largest hospital, physicians acquired a new facility that gradually developed into one of the most important research centers.[^cite_note-42]

Another [Enlightenment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment) era charitable innovation was the dispensary; these would issue the poor with medicines free of charge. The London Dispensary opened its doors in 1696 as the first such clinic in the [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire). The idea was slow to catch on until the 1770s,[^cite_note-43] when many such organisations began to appear, including the [Public Dispensary of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Dispensary_of_Edinburgh) (1776), the Metropolitan Dispensary and Charitable Fund (1779) and the [Finsbury Dispensary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finsbury_Dispensary) (1780). Dispensaries were also opened in New York 1771, [Philadelphia](50585.md) 1786, and Boston 1796.[^cite_note-44]

The [Royal Naval Hospital, Stonehouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Naval_Hospital,_Stonehouse), Plymouth, was a pioneer of hospital design in having "pavilions" to minimize the spread of infection. [John Wesley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wesley) visited in 1785, and commented "I never saw anything of the kind so complete; every part is so convenient, and so admirably neat. But there is nothing superfluous, and nothing purely ornamented, either within or without." This revolutionary design was made more widely known by [John Howard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Howard), the philanthropist. In 1787 the French government sent two scholar administrators, [Coulomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles-Augustin_de_Coulomb) and [Tenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques-Ren%C3%A9_Tenon), who had visited most of the hospitals in Europe.[^cite_note-45] They were impressed and the "pavilion" design was copied in France and throughout Europe.

### 19th century



A ward of the hospital at [Scutari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%9Csk%C3%BCdar), where [Florence Nightingale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Nightingale) worked and helped to restructure the modern hospital

English physician [Thomas Percival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Percival) (1740–1804) wrote a comprehensive system of medical conduct, *[Medical Ethics; or, a Code of Institutes and Precepts, Adapted to the Professional Conduct of Physicians and Surgeons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_Ethics;_or,_a_Code_of_Institutes_and_Precepts,_Adapted_to_the_Professional_Conduct_of_Physicians_and_Surgeons)* (1803) that set the standard for many textbooks.[^cite_note-46] In the mid-19th century, hospitals and the medical profession became more professionalised, with a reorganisation of hospital management along more bureaucratic and administrative lines. The [Apothecaries Act 1815](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apothecaries_Act_1815) made it compulsory for medical students to practise for at least half a year at a hospital as part of their training.[^cite_note-47]

[Florence Nightingale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Nightingale) pioneered the modern profession of nursing during the [Crimean War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_War) when she set an example of compassion, commitment to patient care and diligent and thoughtful hospital administration. The first official nurses' training programme, the Nightingale School for Nurses, was opened in 1860, with the mission of training nurses to work in hospitals, to work with the poor and to teach.[^cite_note-48] Nightingale was instrumental in reforming the nature of the hospital, by improving [sanitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanitation) standards and changing the image of the hospital from a place the sick would go to die, to an institution devoted to recuperation and healing. She also emphasised the importance of [statistical measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistics) for determining the success rate of a given intervention and pushed for [administrative reform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_administration) at hospitals.[^cite_note-49]

By the late 19th century, the modern hospital was beginning to take shape with a proliferation of a variety of public and private hospital systems. By the 1870s, hospitals had more than trebled their original average intake of 3,000 patients. In continental Europe the new hospitals generally were built and run from public funds. The [National Health Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Health_Service), the principal provider of health care in the United Kingdom, was founded in 1948. During the nineteenth century, the Second Viennese Medical School emerged with the contributions of physicians such as [Carl Freiherr von Rokitansky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Freiherr_von_Rokitansky), [Josef Škoda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josef_%C5%A0koda), [Ferdinand Ritter von Hebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Ritter_von_Hebra), and [Ignaz Philipp Semmelweis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignaz_Philipp_Semmelweis). Basic medical science expanded and specialisation advanced. Furthermore, the first dermatology, eye, as well as ear, nose, and throat clinics in the world were founded in [Vienna](55866.md), being considered as the birth of specialised medicine.[^cite_note-50]

White H on blue background, used to represent hospitals in the US.

During peacetime, hospitals are often marked by symbols. A white 'H' on a blue background is often used in the United States. During military conflicts, a hospital may be marked with the emblem of the [red cross, red crescent or red crystal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emblems_of_the_International_Red_Cross_and_Red_Crescent_Movement) in accordance with the [Geneva Conventions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geneva_Conventions).

By the late 19th and early 20th centuries, medical advancements such as [anesthesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anesthesia) and sterile techniques that could make surgery less risky, and the availability of more advanced diagnostic devices such as [X-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray#Discovery), continued to make hospitals a more attractive option for treatment.[^cite_note-NYThospital-51]

Modern hospitals measure various efficiency metrics such as occupancy rates, the average length of stay, time to service, patient satisfaction, physician performance, patient readmission rate, inpatient mortality rate, and [case mix index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_mix_index).[^cite_note-52]

In the United States, the number of hospitalizations grew to its peak in 1981 with 171 admissions per 1,000 Americans and 6,933 hospitals.[^cite_note-NYThospital-51] This trend subsequently reversed, with the rate of hospitalization falling by more than 10% and the number of US hospitals shrinking from 6,933 in 1981 to 5,534 in 2016.[^cite_note-aha2016-53] Occupancy rates also dropped from 77% in 1980 to 60% in 2013.[^cite_note-54] Among the reasons for this are the increasing availability of more complex care elsewhere such as at home or the physicians' offices and also the less therapeutic and more life-threatening image of the hospitals in the eyes of the public.[^cite_note-NYThospital-51][^cite_note-CDCinfection-55] In the US, a patient may sleep in a hospital bed, but be considered outpatient and "under observation" if not formally admitted.[^cite_note-cmsdocs-56]

In the U.S., inpatient stays are covered under Medicare Part A, but a hospital might keep a patient under observation which is only covered under Medicare Part B, and subjects the patient to additional coinsurance costs.[^cite_note-cmsdocs-56] In 2013, the [Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centers_for_Medicare_&_Medicaid_Services) (CMS) introduced a "two-midnight" rule for inpatient admissions,[^cite_note-Modern_Healthcare-57] intended to reduce an increasing number of long-term "observation" stays being used for reimbursement.[^cite_note-cmsdocs-56] This rule was later dropped in 2018.[^cite_note-Modern_Healthcare-57] In 2016 and 2017, healthcare reform and a continued decline in admissions resulted in US hospital-based healthcare systems performing poorly financially.[^cite_note-58] Microhospitals, with bed capacities of between eight and fifty, are expanding in the United States.[^cite_note-59] Similarly, freestanding emergency rooms, which transfer patients that require inpatient care to hospitals, were popularised in the 1970s[^cite_note-Modern_Healthcare-2011-60] and have since expanded rapidly across the United States.[^cite_note-Modern_Healthcare-2011-60]

The [Catholic Church](606848.md) is the largest non-government provider of health care services in the world.[^cite_note-Geopolitics-61] It has around 18,000 clinics, 16,000 homes for the elderly and those with special needs, and 5,500 hospitals, with 65 percent of them located in developing countries.[^cite_note-World_Development_p.40-62] In 2010, the Church's [Pontifical Council for the Pastoral Care of Health Care Workers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontifical_Council_for_the_Pastoral_Care_of_Health_Care_Workers) said that the Church manages 26% of the world's health care facilities.[^cite_note-63]

## Funding

See also: [Health economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_economics)



[Clinical Hospital Dubrava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinical_Hospital_Dubrava) in [Zagreb](55906.md), Croatia

Modern hospitals derive funding from a variety of sources. They may be funded by private payment and [health insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_insurance) or [public expenditure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_expenditure), [charitable donations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charitable_donation).

In the United Kingdom, the [National Health Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Health_Service) delivers health care to legal residents funded by the state "free at the point of delivery", and emergency care free to anyone regardless of nationality or status. Due to the need for hospitals to prioritise their limited resources, there is a tendency in countries with such systems for 'waiting lists' for non-crucial treatment, so those who can afford it may take out private health care to access treatment more quickly.[^cite_note-GROW-64]

In the United States, hospitals typically operate privately and in some cases on a for-profit basis, such as [HCA Healthcare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HCA_Healthcare).[^cite_note-USATODAY-65] The list of procedures and their prices are billed with a [chargemaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chargemaster); however, these prices may be lower for health care obtained within [healthcare networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hospital_network).[^cite_note-66] Legislation requires hospitals to provide care to patients in life-threatening emergency situations regardless of the patient's ability to pay.[^cite_note-67] Privately funded hospitals which admit uninsured patients in emergency situations incur direct financial losses, such as in the aftermath of [Hurricane Katrina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Katrina).[^cite_note-USATODAY-65]

## Quality and safety

As the quality of health care has increasingly become an issue around the world, hospitals have increasingly had to pay serious attention to this matter. Independent external assessment of quality is one of the most powerful ways to assess this aspect of health care, and [hospital accreditation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hospital_accreditation) is one means by which this is achieved. In many parts of the world such accreditation is sourced from other countries, a phenomenon known as [international healthcare accreditation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_healthcare_accreditation), by groups such as [Accreditation Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accreditation_Canada?action=edit\&redlink=1) in Canada, the [Joint Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_Commission) in the U.S., the [Trent Accreditation Scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trent_Accreditation_Scheme) in Great Britain, and the [Haute Autorité de santé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haute_Autorit%C3%A9_de_sant%C3%A9?action=edit\&redlink=1) (HAS) in France. In England, hospitals are monitored by the [Care Quality Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Care_Quality_Commission). In 2020, they turned their attention to hospital food standards after seven patient deaths from [listeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Listeria) linked to pre-packaged sandwiches and salads in 2019, saying "Nutrition and hydration is part of a patient's recovery."[^cite_note-68]

The [World Health Organization](33583.md) reported in 2011 that being admitted to a hospital was far riskier than flying. Globally, the chance of a patient being subject to a treatment error in a hospital was about 10%, and the chance of death resulting from an error was about one in 300. 7% of hospitalised patients in developed countries, and 10% in developing countries, acquire at least one [health care-associated infection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hospital-acquired_infection). In the U.S., 1.7 million infections are acquired in hospital each year, leading to 100,000 deaths, figures much worse than in Europe where there were 4.5 million infections and 37,000 deaths.[^cite_note-69]

## Architecture

Aerial photograph of a hospital complex in Roscoff, France, located beside wide coastal tidal flats.

Aerial view of a coastal hospital complex in [Roscoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roscoff), France

Modern hospital buildings are designed to minimise the effort of medical personnel and the possibility of contamination while maximising the efficiency of the whole system. Travel time for personnel within the hospital and the transportation of patients between units is facilitated and minimised. The building also should be built to accommodate heavy departments such as radiology and operating rooms while space for special wiring, plumbing, and waste disposal must be allowed for in the design.[^cite_note-70]

However, many hospitals, even those considered "modern", are the product of continual and often badly managed growth over decades or even centuries, with utilitarian new sections added on as needs and finances dictate. As a result, Dutch architectural historian Cor Wagenaar has called many hospitals:

> "... built catastrophes, anonymous institutional complexes run by vast bureaucracies, and totally unfit for the purpose they have been designed for ... They are hardly ever functional, and instead of making patients feel at home, they produce stress and anxiety."[^cite_note-ODE-71]

Some newer hospitals now try to re-establish design that takes the patient's psychological needs into account, such as providing more fresh air, better views and more pleasant colour schemes. These ideas harken back to the late eighteenth century, when the concept of providing fresh air and access to the 'healing powers of nature' were first employed by hospital architects in improving their buildings.[^cite_note-ODE-71]

The research of [British Medical Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Medical_Association) is showing that good hospital design can reduce patient's recovery time. Exposure to daylight is effective in reducing depression.[^cite_note-72] Single-sex accommodation helps ensure that patients are treated in privacy and with dignity. Exposure to nature and hospital gardens is also important – looking out windows improves patients' moods and reduces blood pressure and stress levels. Open windows in patient rooms have also demonstrated some evidence of beneficial outcomes by improving airflow and increased microbial diversity.[^cite_note-73][^cite_note-74] Eliminating long corridors can reduce nurses' fatigue and stress.[^cite_note-75]

Another ongoing major development is the change from a ward-based system (where patients are accommodated in communal rooms, separated by movable partitions) to one in which they are accommodated in individual rooms. The ward-based system has been described as very efficient, especially for the medical staff, but is considered to be more stressful for patients and detrimental to their privacy. A major constraint on providing all patients with their own rooms is however found in the higher cost of building and operating such a hospital; this causes some hospitals to charge for private rooms.[^cite_note-76]
