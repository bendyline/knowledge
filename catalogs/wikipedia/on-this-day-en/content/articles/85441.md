# English law

Not to be confused with [Law of the United Kingdom](77750.md).

"English and Welsh law" redirects here. For Senedd-made legislation, see [Welsh law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_law). For UK Acts of Parliament only applying to Wales, see [Wales-only laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales-only_laws). For other uses, see [English law (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_law_\(disambiguation\)).



The [Royal Courts of Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Courts_of_Justice) is on the [Strand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strand,_London) in [London](17867.md). Together with its adjacent Thomas More Building and its outpost Rolls Building on Fetter Lane, it is the main seat of the [High Court of Justice](26569402.md) and the ordinary seat of the [Court of Appeal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_Appeal_of_England_and_Wales).

**English law** is the [common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law) [legal system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_national_legal_systems) of [England and Wales](275187.md), comprising mainly [criminal law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_criminal_law) and civil law, each branch having its own [courts](240527.md) and [procedures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procedural_law).[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3] The judiciary is [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judicial_independence), and legal principles like [fairness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procedural_justice), [equality before the law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equality_before_the_law), and the [right to a fair trial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_to_a_fair_trial) are foundational to the system.

## Principal elements

Although the [common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law) has, historically, been the foundation and prime source of English law, the most authoritative law is statutory [legislation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislation), which comprises [Acts of Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Act_of_Parliament), [regulations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statutory_Instrument)[^cite_note-4] and [by-laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/By-law). In the absence of any statutory law, the common law with its principle of *[stare decisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stare_decisis)* forms the residual source of law, based on judicial decisions, custom, and usage.[^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6]

Common law is made by sitting [judges](44719.md) who apply both [statutory law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_legislation) and established principles, which are derived from the [reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratio_decidendi) from [earlier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precedent) [decisions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_case). [Equity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equity_\(law\)) is the other historic source of judge-made law. Common law can be amended or repealed by [Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_England).[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8]

Not being a [civil law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_law_\(legal_system\)) system, it has no comprehensive [codification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codification_\(law\)).[^cite_note-9] However, most of its criminal law has been codified from its common law origins, in the interests both of certainty and of ease of prosecution.[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11] For the time being, [murder](3768697.md) remains a common law crime, rather than a statutory offence.[^cite_note-12][^cite_note-14]

Although Scotland and Northern Ireland form part of the United Kingdom and share Westminster as a primary legislature, they have separate legal systems outside English law.

[International treaties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_treaty) such as the [European Union](9317.md)'s [Treaty of Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Rome)[^cite_note-15] or the [Hague-Visby Rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hague-Visby_Rules) have [effect in English law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incorporation_of_international_law#Dualist_systems) only when adopted and ratified by Act of Parliament.[^cite_note-16] Adopted treaties may be subsequently denounced by executive action,[^cite_note-17] unless the denouncement or withdraw would affect rights enacted by Parliament. In this case, executive action cannot be used owing to the doctrine of [parliamentary sovereignty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_sovereignty). This principle was established in the case of *[R (Miller) v Secretary of State for Exiting the European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R_\(Miller\)_v_Secretary_of_State_for_Exiting_the_EU)* in 2017.

## Legal terminology

### Criminal law and civil law



Statue of [Lady Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lady_Justice) on the dome of the [Central Criminal Court of England and Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Criminal_Court_\(England_and_Wales\)) in the [City of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_London) (the "[Old Bailey](229046.md)")

[Criminal law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_criminal_law) is the law of crime and punishment whereby the [Crown](383064.md) prosecutes the accused. [Civil law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_law_\(common_law\)) is concerned with [tort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tort), contract, families, companies and so on. Civil law courts operate to provide a party[^cite_note-18] who has an [enforceable claim](3221417.md) against another party with a [remedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_remedy) such as [damages](8134.md) or a [declaration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declaratory_judgment).[^cite_note-19]

### Common law and civil law

In this context, [civil law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_law_\(legal_system\)) is the system of [codified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codification_\(law\)) law that is prevalent in the rest of Europe. Civil law is founded on the ideas of [Roman law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_law).[^cite_note-20]

By contrast, English law is the archetypal [common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law) jurisdiction, built upon [case law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_law).[^cite_note-21]

### Common law and equity

In this context, "common law" means the judge-made law of the [King's Bench](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_King's_Bench_\(England\)); whereas [equity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equity_\(law\)) is the judge-made law of the (now-defunct) [Court of Chancery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_Chancery).[^cite_note-22] Equity is concerned mainly with [trusts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_trust_law) and [equitable remedies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equitable_remedy). Equity generally operates in accordance with the principles known as the "[maxims of equity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxims_of_equity)".[^cite_note-24]

The reforming [Judicature Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judicature_Acts) of the 1880s amalgamated the courts into one Supreme Court of Judicature which was directed to administer both law and equity.[^cite_note-25] The [neo-Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Gothic) [Royal Courts of Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Courts_of_Justice) in The Strand, London, were built shortly afterwards to celebrate these reforms.

### Public law and private law

Public law[^cite_note-26] is the [law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law) governing relationships between individuals and the [state](12229.md). Private law encompasses relationships between private individuals and other private entities (but may also cover "private" relationships between the government and private entities).

### Legal remedies

A [remedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_remedy) is "the means given by law for the recovery of a [right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right), or of [compensation](22443529.md) for its infringement".[^cite_note-27] Most remedies are available only from the court, but some are "[self-help](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-help_\(law\))" remedies; for instance, a party who lawfully wishes to cancel[^cite_note-28] a contract may do so without leave;[^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31] and a person may take his own steps to "[abate a private nuisance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuisance_abatement)".&#x20;

Formerly, most civil actions claiming damages in the [High Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Court) were commenced by obtaining a [writ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writ#History#Rationalisation_of_writs) issued in the Queen's name. After 1979, writs have merely required the parties to appear, and writs are no longer issued in the name of the Crown.[^cite_note-32] After the [Woolf Reforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woolf_Reforms) of 1999, almost all civil actions other than those connected with insolvency are commenced by the completion of a Claim Form [^cite_note-33] as opposed to a writ, originating application, or a summons.[^cite_note-34]

**Primary legislation** in the UK may take the following forms:

- [Acts of Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_of_Parliament_in_the_United_Kingdom)

- [Acts of the Scottish Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_of_the_Scottish_Parliament)

- [Acts of the Senedd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Act_of_the_Senedd), or previously [Acts of the National Assembly for Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Act_of_the_National_Assembly_for_Wales) and [measures of the National Assembly for Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measures_of_the_National_Assembly_for_Wales)

- [Statutory rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statutory_rules_of_Northern_Ireland) of the [Northern Ireland Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Assembly)

[Orders in Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_in_Council) are a *[sui generis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sui_generis)* category of legislation.

**Secondary (or "delegated") legislation** in England includes:

- [Statutory instruments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statutory_instrument_\(UK\)) and [ministerial orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministerial_order_\(UK\))

- [By-laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/By-law) of [metropolitan boroughs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_borough), [county councils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_council), and [town councils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Town_council)

Statutes are cited in this fashion: "[Short Title](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_title) Year", e.g. [Theft Act 1968](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theft_Act_1968).[^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40] This became the usual way to refer to Acts from 1840 onwards; previously Acts were cited by their [long title](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_title) with the [regnal year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regnal_year) of the [parliamentary session](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_session) when they received [royal assent](153067.md), and the chapter number. For example, the [Pleading in English Act 1362](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleading_in_English_Act_1362) (which required [pleadings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleading_\(England_and_Wales\)) to be in English and not [Law French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_French)) was referred to as *[36 Edw. 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/36_Edw._3). c. 15,* meaning "36th year of the reign of [Edward III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_III_of_England), chapter 15".[^cite_note-41] (By contrast, American convention inserts "of", as in "[Civil Rights Act *of* 1964](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Rights_Act_of_1964)").

## Common law

Further information: [Common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law)

Common law is a term with historical origins in the legal system of England. It denotes, in the first place, the Anglo-Norman legal system that superseded and replaced [Anglo-Saxon law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_law) in England following the [Battle of Hastings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Hastings) in 1066. Throughout the [Late Medieval Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Medieval_Period), English law was codified through judge-made laws and precedents that were created in the proceedings of Royal justices in the [Circuit courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circuit_court) dictated by the [Eyres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eyre_\(legal_term\)) throughout the country (these themselves evolving from the early medieval [Itinerant courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Itinerant_court)). This body of legal scholarship was first published at the end of the 19th century, *The History of English Law before the Time of Edward I*,[^cite_note-42] in which [Pollock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Frederick_Pollock,_1st_Baronet) and [Maitland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederic_William_Maitland) expanded the work of [Coke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Coke) (17th century) and [Blackstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Blackstone) (18th century). Specifically, the law developed in England's [Court of Common Pleas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_Common_Pleas_\(England\)) and other common law courts, which became also the law of the colonies settled initially under the Crown of England or, later, of the [United Kingdom](31717.md), in [United States](3434750.md), [Canada](5042916.md), [Australia](4689264.md), [New Zealand](4913064.md), [South Africa](17416221.md), [Singapore](27318.md), [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent), [Israel](9282173.md) and elsewhere.&#x20;

This law further developed after those courts in England were reorganised by the [Supreme Court of Judicature Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_Judicature_Act) passed in the 1870s. It developed independently, in the legal systems of the United States and other jurisdictions, after their independence from the United Kingdom, before and after the 1870s. The term is used, in the second place, to denote the law developed by those courts, in the same periods, pre-colonial, colonial and post-colonial, as distinct from within the jurisdiction, or former jurisdiction, of other courts in England: the [Court of Chancery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_Chancery), the [ecclesiastical courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesiastical_court), and the [Admiralty court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Admiralty_court).

In the [Oxford English Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_English_Dictionary) (1933) "common law" is described as "The unwritten law of England, administered by the King's courts, which purports to be derived from ancient usage, and is embodied in the older commentaries and the reports of abridged cases", as opposed, in that sense, to statute law, and as distinguished from the equity administered by the Chancery and similar courts, and from other systems such as ecclesiastical law, and admiralty law.[^cite_note-43] For usage in the United States the description is "the body of legal doctrine which is the foundation of the law administered in all states settled from England, and those formed by later settlement or division from them".[^cite_note-44]

### Early development

In 1276, the concept of "[time immemorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_immemorial)" often applied in common law, was defined as being any time before 6 July 1189 (i.e. before [Richard I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_I_of_England)'s accession to the [English throne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_throne)). Since 1189, English law has been a common law, not a [civil law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_law_\(legal_system\)) system. In other words, no comprehensive codification of the law has taken place and [judicial precedents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precedent) are binding as opposed to persuasive. This may be a legacy of the [Norman Conquest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Conquest) of England in 1066, when a number of legal concepts and institutions from [Norman law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_law) were introduced to England.[^cite_note-45]

In the early centuries of English common law, the justices and [judges](44719.md) were responsible for adapting the system of [writs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writ) to meet everyday needs, applying a mixture of precedent and common sense to build up a body of internally consistent law. An example is the [Law Merchant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_Merchant) derived from the ["Pie-Powder" Courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_piepowders), named from a corruption of the [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_French) *pieds-poudrés* ("dusty feet") implying [ad hoc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ad_hoc) marketplace courts.

Following [Montesquieu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montesquieu)'s theory of the "separation of powers", only Parliament has the power to legislate. If a statute is ambiguous, then the courts have exclusive power to decide its true meaning, using the principles of [statutory interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statutory_interpretation). Since the courts have no authority to legislate, the "[legal fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_fiction)" is that they "declare" (rather than "create") the common law. The [House of Lords](13658.md) took this "declaratory power" a stage further in *DPP v Shaw*,[^cite_note-46] where, in creating the new crime of "conspiracy to corrupt public morals", [Viscount Simonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gavin_Simonds,_1st_Viscount_Simonds) claimed the court had a "residual power to protect the moral welfare of the state".[^cite_note-47][^cite_note-48] As Parliament became ever more established and influential, Parliamentary [legislation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislation) gradually overtook judicial law-making, such that today's judges are able to innovate only in certain, very narrowly defined areas.

### Overseas influences



A map of the British Empire under [Queen Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Victoria) at the end of the nineteenth century. "Dominions" refers to all territories belonging to [the Crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Crown).

#### Reciprocity

England exported its common law and statute law to most parts of the [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire). Many aspects of that system have survived after Independence from British rule, and the influences are often reciprocal. "English law" prior to the American Revolutionary Wars (American War of Independence) is still an influence on [American law](27552742.md), and provides the basis for many American legal traditions and principles.

After independence, English common law still exerted influence over American common law – for example, *[Byrne v Boadle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byrne_v_Boadle)* (1863), which first applied the *[res ipsa loquitur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Res_ipsa_loquitur)* doctrine. Jurisdictions that have kept to the common law may incorporate modern legal developments from England, and English decisions are usually persuasive in such jurisdictions.

In the United States, each state has its own supreme court with final appellate jurisdiction, resulting in the development of state common law. The US Supreme Court has the final say over federal matters. By contrast, in Australia, one national common law exists.[^cite_note-49]

#### Courts of final appeal

After Britain's colonial period, jurisdictions that had inherited and adopted England's common law[^cite_note-51] developed their courts of final appeal in differing ways: jurisdictions still under the British crown are subject to the [Judicial Committee of the Privy Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judicial_Committee_of_the_Privy_Council) in London.[^cite_note-52] For a long period, the British [Dominions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominion) used London's Privy Council as their final appeal court, although one by one they eventually established their local [supreme court](4913027.md). New Zealand was the last Dominion to abandon the Privy Council, setting up its own [Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_New_Zealand) in 2004.[^cite_note-53]

Even after independence, many former British colonies in the [Commonwealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_of_Nations) continued to use the Privy Council, as it offered a readily available high-grade service. In particular, several Caribbean Island nations found the Privy Council advantageous.

#### International law and commerce

Britain is a [dualist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dualism_\(law\)) in its relationship with international law, so international treaties must be formally ratified by Parliament and incorporated into statute before such [supranational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supranationalism) laws become binding in the UK.[^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55][^cite_note-56][^cite_note-57]

Britain has long been a major trading nation, exerting a strong influence on the law of [shipping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maritime_law) and [maritime trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_trade_law). The English law of [salvage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_salvage),[^cite_note-58] [collisions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collisions),[^cite_note-59] ship arrest,[^cite_note-60] and [carriage of goods by sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_Carriage_of_Goods_by_Sea)[^cite_note-61] are subject to international conventions which Britain played a leading role in drafting. Many of these conventions incorporate principles derived from English common law[^cite_note-62] and documentary procedures.[^cite_note-63]

## British jurisdictions

Main article: [Law of the United Kingdom § Legal jurisdictions](77750.md#Legal_jurisdictions)



The former [Middlesex Guildhall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middlesex_Guildhall) in [Parliament Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_Square) is now the location of the [Supreme Court of the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_the_United_Kingdom).

The United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland comprises three legal jurisdictions: England and Wales, Scotland, and Northern Ireland. Although Scotland and Northern Ireland form part of the United Kingdom and share the Parliament at Westminster as the primary legislature, they have separate legal systems. Scotland became part of the UK over 300 years ago, but [Scots law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scots_law) has remained remarkably distinct from English law.&#x20;

The UK's highest civil [appeal court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appeal_court) is the [Supreme Court of the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_the_United_Kingdom), whose decisions, and those of its predecessor the House of Lords, are binding on all three UK jurisdictions. Unless obviously limited to a principle of distinct English and Welsh, Scottish or Northern Irish law, as in *[Donoghue v Stevenson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donoghue_v_Stevenson)*, a Scots case that forms the basis of the UK's law of [negligence](21436.md).[^cite_note-64]

### Application of English law to Wales

See also: [Proposed Wales criminal justice system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proposed_Wales_criminal_justice_system)

Unlike [Scotland](26994.md) and [Northern Ireland](21265.md), [Wales](69894.md) is not a separate [jurisdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisdiction_\(area\)) within the [United Kingdom](31717.md). The customary laws of [Wales](69894.md) within the [Kingdom of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_England) were abolished by [King Henry VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_VIII_of_England)'s [Laws in Wales Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laws_in_Wales_Acts), which brought Wales into legal conformity with England. While [Wales](69894.md) now has a devolved [parliament (the Senedd)](238480.md), any legislation it passes [must adhere to circumscribed subjects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intra_vires) under the [Government of Wales Act 2006](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Wales_Act_2006), to other legislation of the [British Parliament](13964.md), or to any [Order in Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_in_Council) given under the authority of the 2006 Act.

Any reference to England in legislation between 1746 and 1967 is deemed to include Wales. As to later legislation, any application to Wales must be expressed under the [Welsh Language Act 1967](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Language_Act_1967) and the jurisdiction is, since, correctly and widely referred to as [England and Wales](275187.md).&#x20;

[Devolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devolution) has granted some political autonomy to [Wales](69894.md) via the [Welsh Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Parliament), which gained its power to pass primary legislation under the [Government of Wales Act 2006](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Wales_Act_2006), in force since the [2007 Welsh general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_Welsh_general_election). The legal system administered through civil and criminal courts is unified throughout [England and Wales](275187.md).&#x20;

This is different from [Northern Ireland](21265.md), for example, which did not cease to be a distinct [jurisdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisdiction_\(area\)) when its legislature was suspended (see [Northern Ireland (Temporary Provisions) Act 1972](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_\(Temporary_Provisions\)_Act_1972)). A major difference is use of the [Welsh language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_language), as laws concerning it apply in Wales and not in the rest of the [United Kingdom](31717.md). The [Welsh Language Act 1993](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Language_Act_1993) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom, which put the Welsh language on an equal footing with the English language in Wales with regard to the public sector. Welsh may also be spoken in Welsh courts.

There have been some calls from both Welsh academics and politicians for a separate [Welsh justice system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proposed_Welsh_justice_system).[^cite_note-65][^cite_note-66][^cite_note-\:1-67]
