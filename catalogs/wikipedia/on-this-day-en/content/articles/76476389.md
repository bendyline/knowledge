Parliamentary elections were held in [Bulgaria](3415.md) on 19 April 2026 to elect 240 members of the [National Assembly](2122384.md). The vote was triggered by the resignation of the [Zhelyazkov government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhelyazkov_government) on 11 December 2025 following widespread [anti-corruption protests](81751780.md). It marked the country's seventh [snap election](462915.md) since April 2021, the result of a five-year [political crisis in Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_crisis_in_Bulgaria). Key campaign issues included [political corruption](66220.md), the [cost of living](522934.md), [foreign electoral interference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_electoral_interference), and allegations of [vote buying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vote_buying).&#x20;

The political scene was shifted significantly by the January 2026 resignation of President [Rumen Radev](51575780.md), who entered parliamentary politics by forming the [centre-left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre-left) populist [Progressive Bulgaria](82553504.md) (PB) coalition. Radev campaigned on a platform to dismantle the [oligarchic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oligarchic) system. The [pro-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pro-European) conservative [GERB–SDS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GERB%E2%80%93SDS) and the liberal [We Continue the Change – Democratic Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/We_Continue_the_Change_%E2%80%93_Democratic_Bulgaria) (PP–DB) entered the race as the largest established blocs.

In an election that saw an increase in [voter turnout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voter_turnout) at 51%, PB obtained a [landslide victory](41053306.md) with 44% of the vote. This resulted in an [absolute majority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_majority) of seats, which observers suggested could resolve the prolonged [political crisis](52674452.md). Most other parties lost seats, and nearly 20% of the total vote went to parties that failed to meet the 4% [electoral threshold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_threshold). While PP–DB gained one seat, GERB–SDS lost half of its support and the [Movement for Rights and Freedoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Movement_for_Rights_and_Freedoms) (DPS) recorded its weakest result since 1994. Moreover, the [Bulgarian Socialist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgarian_Socialist_Party) (BSP)-led [BSP – United Left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BSP_%E2%80%93_United_Left) (BSP–OL) alliance finished short of the threshold, leaving it with no seats in the National Assembly for the first time since its foundation in 1991. As a result, the BSP, successor of the [Bulgarian Communist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgarian_Communist_Party) (BKP), will not be represented in the National Assembly for the first time since the [end of Communism in Bulgaria](2843767.md). Every [far-right](221220.md) party except [Revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revival_\(Bulgaria\)) (which lost 60% of its seats) also fell out of the Assembly.

Analysts characterized the result as a win for [Eurosceptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurosceptic) and [pro-Russian](2541825.md) sentiment, citing Radev's previous criticism of the [European Union](9317.md) (EU) and his willingness to pursue relations with Russia. These positions drew comparisons to the governments of [Viktor Orbán](750582.md) in Hungary and [Robert Fico](4127226.md) in Slovakia, although such characterizations remained a subject of debate. Following his victory, Radev received congratulations from both EU and Russian leadership, and maintained that his administration would pursue a pragmatic foreign policy.

## Background

See also: [Bulgarian political crisis (2021–2026)](72847983.md)

After seven snap elections, the National Assembly failed to form a long-term government as anti-corruption parties made a breakthrough in the April 2021 elections, which started the political crisis, which was further complicated by the entrance of far-right parties.[^cite_note-Schminke_2026-3] The [October 2024 Bulgarian parliamentary election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/October_2024_Bulgarian_parliamentary_election) produced a [minority government](271434.md) headed by Prime Minister [Rosen Zhelyazkov](74976890.md) (a member of [GERB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GERB)), comprising GERB–SDS, BSP–OL, and [There is Such a People](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There_is_Such_a_People) (ITN), with [Alliance for Rights and Freedoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alliance_for_Rights_and_Freedoms) (APS), which split from DPS as a result of a rift between two of the party's factions, supporting the government in confidence votes;[^cite_note-4] however, as a result of a ruling by the [Constitutional Court of Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_Court_of_Bulgaria) in March 2025, the far-right [Velichie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velichie) entered the National Assembly, bringing down the coalition to exactly the minimum 121 seats compared to the 119 seats held by the opposition.[^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7]

Following [DPS – New Beginning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DPS_%E2%80%93_New_Beginning) (DPS–NN) leader [Delyan Peevski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delyan_Peevski)'s commitment to support the government if the recalculation resulted in the loss of the government's majority, [Alliance for Rights and Freedoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alliance_for_Rights_and_Freedoms) (APS) withdrew from the government in April 2025 because they refused to support a government with Peevski.[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9] Days later, the government survived a [no-confidence vote](208101.md) due to Peevski's support, effectively creating a *de facto* [confidence and supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence_and_supply) agreement.[^cite_note-10] Since taking office in January 2025, the Zhelyazkov government survived several no-confidence votes,[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12] bringing the total to five by September 2025.[^cite_note-13] In December 2025, the Zhelyazkov government resigned after weeks of protests over the 2026 budget and [corruption](3608404.md) within the government.[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15] The Zhelyazkov government's primary commitment had been the country's entry into the [eurozone](184391.md) in 2026,[^cite_note-16][^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18] which it achieved in 2025 when Bulgaria had its final hurdles cleared and adopted the legal framework to make the entry possible.[^cite_note-19]

As President, Radev took a critical stance on adopting the eurozone and attempted to delay it by calling a referendum,[^cite_note-20] a move described as illegal because the Constitutional Court had already clarified in 2024 that Bulgaria was obliged to adopt the [euro](9472.md) by its [European treaties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_treaties) that were ratified when it joined the EU.[^cite_note-21] Radev's attempt was rejected by [Nataliya Kiselova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nataliya_Kiselova) (the BSP–OL-supported [Speaker of the National Assembly of Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speaker_of_the_National_Assembly_of_Bulgaria)),[^cite_note-22] and the decision was upheld by the Constitutional Court,[^cite_note-23] which Radev sharply criticised.[^cite_note-24] After it was confirmed there would be new elections in 2026, the eighth election of the political crisis, Radev resigned as President to contest the elections,[^cite_note-25] seeking to become Prime Minister and describing his political project as a "powerful political alternative" to return Bulgarians' trust in their state, with "clear rules for everyone".[^cite_note-26]

On 18 February 2026, President [Iliana Iotova](34677899.md) appointed a [caretaker government](240683.md) led by Prime Minister [Andrey Gyurov](81825567.md), a former member of [We Continue the Change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/We_Continue_the_Change) (PP) who left PP and the parliament in 2023, and set a parliamentary election to be held on 19 April 2026.[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28][^cite_note-29] Gyurov's government had asked the EU for help to fight off interference from Russia ahead of the election, warning foreign actors were trying to manipulate public opinion through social media networks and propaganda websites.[^cite_note-30] Radev responded by saying his party's likely success in the elections could trigger the "Romanian model", which would lead to annulled elections, such as in the [2024 Romanian presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Romanian_presidential_election), where a far-right candidate's first place finish in the first round was annulled by courts citing "foreign interference".[^cite_note-31]

## Electoral system

The 240 members of the National Assembly were elected by an [open list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_list) and [proportional representation](23298.md) system from 31 [multi-member constituencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-member_constituencies) ranging in size from four to nineteen seats. The electoral threshold was 4% for all political parties or [electoral coalitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_coalitions), with seats allocated according to the [largest remainder method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Largest_remainder_method) using a [Hare quota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hare_quota).[^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33][^cite_note-34]

## Campaign



President Radev in September 2025

In January 2026, Radev resigned as [President of Bulgaria](335070.md),[^cite_note-37] forming Progressive Bulgaria (PB), a centre-left party,[^cite_note-38] to contest the election. Among the significant issues of Bulgaria that were key to the campaign were a cost of living crisis, as well as corruption and vote buying, as Radev supported the anti-corruption protests and promised to get rid of the "oligarchic governance model".[^cite_note-Toshkov_2026-39][^cite_note-Lefkowitz_&_Mcallister_2026-40][^cite_note-Maguire_2026-41] At campaign rallies, he vowed to "remove the corrupt, oligarchic model of governance from political power."[^cite_note-Toshkov_2026-39][^cite_note-Lefkowitz_&_Mcallister_2026-40][^cite_note-Maguire_2026-41] Radev launched the electoral programme of PB on 19 March,[^cite_note-Radev_programme-42] the eve of the beginning of the official campaign period.[^cite_note-Campaign_period-43] In Radev's speech, he vowed to dismantle the oligarchic system that controlled state institution and pledged to prevent oligarchs from accessing public finance, promising to free the private sector from racketeering.[^cite_note-Radev_programme-42] 4,786 candidates, of whom 3,347 men and 1,439 women, ran for the National Assembly.[^cite_note-Campaign_period-43] According to preliminary data from the Central Electoral Commission (CEC), a total of 6,641,768 Bulgarian citizens were eligible to vote.[^cite_note-Campaign_period-43]

The campaign period was dominated with a crackdown on vote buying, with an initial police operation taking place in [Haskovo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haskovo) on 18 March resulting in the arrest of three people.[^cite_note-44] On 26 March, the caretaker Minister of the Interior [Emil Dechev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emil_Dechev) stated that breaches of election law were between 500% to 600% up compared with the same period before the October 2024 parliamentary election.[^cite_note-45] Further arrests relating to vote buying took place, with a 53-year-old man being arrested on 8 April who was in possession of more than [€](1797747.md)40,000 and two people being arrested on 9 April who were in possession of €88,720.[^cite_note-46] By 14 April, more than €1 million had been confiscated in operations against vote buying by police.[^cite_note-47] Caretaker Prime Minister Gyurov warned on 15 April that a scheme to buy votes using counterfeit [euro banknotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_banknotes) was being prepared.[^cite_note-48] A routine stop on 16 April upon a candidate driving a car resulted in the reveal of lists of names alongside a sum of euro banknotes, while a police operation upon four addresses on 17 April in [Varna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varna,_Bulgaria) resulted in a seizure of €200,000.[^cite_note-49] After the campaign period concluded on 17 April,[^cite_note-Campaign_period-43] [election silence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_silence) was imposed from 18 April to election day on 19 April.[^cite_note-50] The [Ministry of Interior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Interior_\(Bulgaria\)) revealed after the closure of polls on 19 April that DPS and GERB–SDS ranked first and second in official reports of vote buying, respectively generating 631 and 318 reports.[^cite_note-51][^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53]

## Results



Results of the election showed that PB was the most voted party in all [provinces of Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provinces_of_Bulgaria) except [Kardzhali ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kardzhali_Province).

## Aftermath

### Exit polls

Exit polls projected Radev's PB coalition to have won the election in a landslide with around 37–39% of the vote, with GERB–SDS falling to around 15% and PP–DB retaining its result of 13% from the last election.[^cite_note-Toshkov_2026-39][^cite_note-Lefkowitz_&_Mcallister_2026-40][^cite_note-Maguire_2026-41] The status of some parties was unclear, in particular that of BSP–OL as they were expected to enter the National Assembly but were within the margin of error or just above the threshold. Among the far-right or [right-wing populist](8354382.md) parties, only Revival was expected to cross the 4% threshold needed to enter the 240-seat [parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament). The exit poll by Trend predicted a voter turnout of 43.4% and that six parties could pass the 4% threshold.[^cite_note-Toshkov_2026-39] An updated exit poll by Alpha Research showed that PB was first with 44%, significantly ahead of GERB–SDS at 12.5% and with a turnout of 47%, in what would mark "one of the strongest results by a single party in a generation, sideline a party that has ruled on and off for decades, and may see an end to the instability that has resulted in eight elections in five years."[^cite_note-Lefkowitz_&_Mcallister_2026-40]

### International reactions

Following the election, Russia congratulated Radev, welcoming his victory, with Kremlin spokesman [Dmitry Peskov](9214683.md) saying: "Of course, we are impressed by the statements made by Mr Radev, who won the election, and by some other European leaders regarding their willingness to resolve problems through pragmatic dialogue."[^cite_note-Al_Jazeera_2026-69] Serbian President [Aleksandar Vučić](13349267.md) was among the first to congratulate Radev on his win.[^cite_note-Gall_2026-70] European Commission President [Ursula von der Leyen](2991932.md) and [European Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Council) President [António Costa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ant%C3%B3nio_Costa) also congratulated Radev, with von der Leyen saying she was looking forward to working with Radev "for the prosperity and security" of Europe.[^cite_note-Knapp_2026-71] NATO Secretary-General [Mark Rutte](4321056.md) said he would "look forward to continued cooperation on shared security challenges" with Radev.[^cite_note-Knapp_2026-71]

Czech MEP [Tomáš Zdechovský](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom%C3%A1%C5%A1_Zdechovsk%C3%BD) of [KDU–ČSL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KDU%E2%80%93%C4%8CSL) regretted the results, writing that "Bulgaria has sent a clear — and deeply concerning — signal" and that "Radev, a politician openly critical of support for Ukraine and long seen as sympathetic to Russia, appears to have secured a parliamentary majority. This is no longer just an election result, it is a serious geopolitical shift."[^cite_note-Knapp_2026-71] Austrian MEP [Andreas Schieder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andreas_Schieder) of [SPÖ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SP%C3%96) expressed disappointment with the results, describing Radev as "the next Putin friend" that would enter the [European Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Council) and that the election was a "setback for Europe".[^cite_note-Knapp_2026-71]

### Government formation

Before an outright majority for PB was reached, analysts speculated on at least two possible options for a [majority government](465640.md), which would bring the country some stability after seven elections in five years,[^cite_note-Henley_&_Maclachlan_2026-72] namely one with the pro-EU liberal reformists of PP–DB and one with BSP–OL and the nationalists. In his first remarks after the vote, Radev said that he could find common ground by looking in "one and the same direction" with pro-EU liberal reformists in order to promote anti-corruption and [judicial reforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judicial_reform). More specifically, Radev said he was "ready to go with different options so Bulgaria can have a functional and stable government".[^cite_note-Oliver_2026-73] Radev said that "Bulgaria will make efforts to continue its European path" and that "a strong Bulgaria and strong Europe" needed pragmatism because "Europe has fallen victim to its own ambition to be a moral leader in a world without rules."[^cite_note-Lefkowitz_&_Mcallister_2026-40] [Ivan Demerdzhiev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Demerdzhiev), former interior minister and candidate from PB, said in an interview with [bTV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BTV_\(Bulgaria\)) that the party has no intention of leading Bulgaria out of the EU, the eurozone, or [NATO](21133.md).[^cite_note-74]

Before results showed that his party had won an absolute majority, Radev said he was open to a [minority government](271434.md),[^cite_note-75] and told [Reuters](18998750.md) that his coalition would do "everything possible not to allow us to go \[to elections] again" as it was "ruinous for Bulgaria" and were ready to "consider different options so that Bulgaria can have a regular and stable government". As votes were being counted and confirmed an even larger PB's landslide win, Radev hailed it as a "victory of hope", further stating that "PB has won unequivocally – a victory of hope over distrust, a victory of freedom over fear." In turn, Borisov congratulated Radev, cautioning that "winning elections is one thing, governing is another."[^cite_note-Maguire_2026-41] With 96% of votes counted and PB's landslide win, five parties were set to enter the National Assembly compared with nine of the previous election.[^cite_note-Ivanov_2026-76] As a result of PB's win, Radev is considered the [Prime Minister-designate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Minister-designate); a former President, he would enter a position that is considered more powerful.[^cite_note-Chastand_2026-77] In the aftermath, Borislav Sarafov resigned as Bulgaria's acting prosecutor general and Vanya Stefanova became acting Prosecutor General instead.[^cite_note-78]

On 30 April, the first session of the newly elected [52nd National Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/52nd_National_Assembly_of_Bulgaria) took place, electing [Mihaela Dotsova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mihaela_Dotsova) from PB as speaker of the parliament.[^cite_note-79] Subsequently, President Iliana Iotova held constitutionally-mandated consultations with all parliamentary groups on 5 May.[^cite_note-80]

On 7 May, the first governing mandate was given by President Iotova to Rumen Radev, whose cabinet was elected by the National Assembly on 8 May.[^cite_note-81]

## Analysis

### National Assembly

With more than 60% of ballots counted, DPS was above the threshold while BSP–OL was not, with the CEC noting that PB had won about 45% and at least 132 seats, which was a majority in the 240-seat parliament.[^cite_note-Toshkov_2026-39][^cite_note-Lefkowitz_&_Mcallister_2026-40][^cite_note-Maguire_2026-41] Final results were expected to be available by 20 April.[^cite_note-Lefkowitz_&_Mcallister_2026-40] As turnout exceeded 50%, higher than predicted by exit polls and the highest since April 2021,[^cite_note-Maguire_2026-41] the electorate was seen as having voted in favour of politicians and parties promising significant changes and anti-corruption measures.[^cite_note-Gall_2026-70][^cite_note-Henley_&_Maclachlan_2026-72] Reflecting the electorate's call for changes, Evelina Koleva, a manager at a digital marketing company in [Sofia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sofia), told Reuters: "There is now an opportunity for the things people have been hoping to see change to actually become visible."[^cite_note-CNN_2026-82] Addressing the cost of living, which was one of the election's key issues, Tihomir Bezlov, a senior fellow at the Centre for the Study of Democracy in Sofia, said: "The country's main challenge is the economic crisis and the demographic crisis ... There do not seem to be many ideas in the winning camp on either of these issues."[^cite_note-CNN_2026-82]

In what was seen as a significant loss for [Boyko Borisov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boyko_Borisov) and the centre-right coalition of GERB–SDS, which had dominated the 2010s, Radev claimed an "uncontested victory".[^cite_note-Gall_2026-70] The incoming parliament would be less fragmented, with a significant decline in representation for parties associated with Borisov, and the new majority signalled an end to the prolonged political fragmentation that hindered the formation of stable governments in Bulgaria.[^cite_note-Simeonova_Tcherneva_2026-83] Radev's PB received 44% of the vote and won an outright majority of seats, the first time a party or alliance has had a majority in the National Assembly since 1997.[^cite_note-Ivanov_2026-76] GERB–SDS lost half of its support, receiving 13% of the vote, while PP–DB received 12% of the vote, barely changing its result from the last election.[^cite_note-Ivanov_2026-76]

DPS, the Turkish minority party, had its weakest result since 1994, while their splinter party APS lost all 19 seats.[^cite_note-Schminke_2026-3][^cite_note-PolitPro_2026-64] Nearly all far-right or right-wing populist parties did not cross the 4% election threshold, including Velichie, ITN (a party that rose in prominence in the previous series of elections), and [Morality, Unity, Honour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morality,_Unity,_Honour) (MECh), which were present in the previous National Assembly.[^cite_note-Schminke_2026-3][^cite_note-PolitPro_2026-64] The notable exception was Revival, which marginally crossed the threshold and lost more than half of its seats.[^cite_note-Ivanov_2026-76] The election also marked the first time in Bulgaria's modern history in which the BSP (the legal successor of the BKP and the main political party behind Radev's rise to prominence and that supported his first presidential bid) failed to enter the National Assembly.[^cite_note-Ivanov_2026-76] Alongside GERB and ITN, the BSP was part of the previous governing coalition, with the electorate voting against these parties, all of which suffered significant losses.[^cite_note-Ivanov_2026-76] Analysts argued that Radev's strategic ambiguity towards Russia and the EU had allowed him to take votes from both GERB and Revival.[^cite_note-Henley_2026-84] Political scientist Svetlin Tachev argued that PP–DB would have received stronger support if Radev had not entered the electoral race.[^cite_note-85]

### Comparisons to Eurosceptic and pro-Russian governments

The [Atlantic Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_Council) warned that a Radev-led government could "replace Hungary as Putin's proxy inside the EU and NATO", although it assessed that the influence would be "nowhere near that of Viktor Orbán, at least initially."[^cite_note-Dickinson_2026-86] It concluded that Radev's government would be bad news for Ukraine and would represent a significant win for Russia.[^cite_note-Dickinson_2026-86] *[Politico](9172097.md)* included Radev as one of the five "possible successors to Orbán as a key destabilizer in the EU", alongside Fico of Slovakia and Czech Republic Prime Minister [Andrej Babiš](40378134.md), further adding that his pro-Russian stances had drawn a strong rebuke from Ukrainian President [Volodymyr Zelenskyy](59210523.md) in 2023.[^cite_note-87] Reuters reported that Radev's campaign drew comparisons with Orbán when he talked about improving ties with Moscow and resuming the free flow of Russian oil and gas into Europe.[^cite_note-CNN_2026-82] [Raidió Teilifís Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raidi%C3%B3_Teilif%C3%ADs_%C3%89ireann) also observed that Radev could find common ground with Fico and Babis, arguing that his victory speech was Eurosceptic and there is a risk he becomes the next disrupter of EU after Orbán if he pushes to restore Russian oil imports to Bulgaria.[^cite_note-88] The [European Council on Foreign Relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Council_on_Foreign_Relations) (ECFR) published an analysis arguing that Radev would be closer to Fico than Orbán, is ready to criticise the [European Commission](9974.md), especially on the [EU energy policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EU_energy_policy) and the [European Green Deal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Green_Deal), and would be tough on [EU's enlargement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EU's_enlargement); however, it predicted that Radev would generally not try to block major EU decisions, such as on Ukraine, for fear of losing EU funds.[^cite_note-Simeonova_Tcherneva_2026-83] Another analysis maintained that in part because of Radev's dependence on PP–DB and the tide of public opinion, he could sometimes sound like Orban but would probably end up acting more like Fico.[^cite_note-89] *[NIN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NIN_\(magazine\))* also compared Radev to Fico rather than Orbán.[^cite_note-90] In an interview, MEP [Radan Kanev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radan_Kanev) said that Radev would be more likely to resemble Fico in "speaking one thing, \[and] doing a different one."[^cite_note-91]

[Al Jazeera English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Jazeera_English) reported that Radev's stance on foreign policy had drawn attention in Europe, leading to a "pro-Russian" label as he objected to a defence pact concluded between Bulgaria and Ukraine in March and had called for the resumption of Russian imports to Europe despite EU sanctions on Russian oil.[^cite_note-Al_Jazeera_2026-69] It added that some had branded Radev a Eurosceptic because he criticised aspects of EU policy, including reliance on renewable energy and [Bulgaria's adoption of the euro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgaria's_adoption_of_the_euro), saying in a campaign rally: "The coalition-makers introduced the euro in Bulgaria without asking you. And now, when you pay your bills, always remember which politicians promised you that you would be in the 'club of the rich'."[^cite_note-Al_Jazeera_2026-69] At the same time, it observed that Radev had said in his victory speech he would take a pragmatic approach.[^cite_note-Al_Jazeera_2026-69] *[The New York Times](30680.md)* also reported that Radev was perceived as pro-Russian and Eurosceptic on many issues, and that Radev's positioning on Russia had been a calculated balancing act to draw support from voters from all sides. It said analysts noted that he would have to clarify some of his positions as he would be making executive decisions for the first time.[^cite_note-92] The [BBC](19344654.md) framed Radev as a "pragmatic, somewhat pro-Russian leader, who has criticised EU sanctions, and called for constructive dialogue with the Kremlin."[^cite_note-93] *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)* described Radev as Moscow-friendly and concluded that it could be bad for the EU.[^cite_note-Henley_2026-84] Meanwhile, it reported that EU diplomats had said they did not expect Radev to seek to take over Orbán's role as the EU's chief disrupter.[^cite_note-Henley_2026-84]

Ruslan Stefanov, an analyst at the Centre for the Study of Diplomacy, described Radev as a politician of "considerable strategic ambivalence toward Russia" who has "tried to cater to extreme Eurosceptic, and typically also pro-Russian, voter groups."[^cite_note-94] Emilia Zankina of [Temple University Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_University_Rome) agreed, saying: "There is a great concern that he may try to steer the country away from its pro-European line."[^cite_note-Danailov_2026-95] Bulgarian political expert Dimitar Bechev told *Politico* that the biggest question is "what a future coalition will look like" and that Radev has to make a choice on the [rule of law](25166191.md), deciding whether to align with anti-corruption reformists or defend vested interests.[^cite_note-96] *[Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung](513356.md)* said that "almost nothing is known about his intentions, especially in the area of financial and fiscal policy."[^cite_note-Danailov_2026-95] An article published by [Novinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novinite) argued that Western media's binary of "NATO pilot or Putin sympathizer" was too simple and that Radev, like Bulgaria itself, exists in the gray zone between the East and the West, comparing him to [Péter Magyar](76453946.md) rather than Orbán.[^cite_note-Danailov_2026-95] Maria Simeonova of the Sofia bureau of the ECFR analysed Radev's Eurosceptic comments as being primary towards Bulgarians, stating: "His criticism, particularly regarding financial and military support for Ukraine or sanctions against Russia, will be aimed primarily at the domestic audience."[^cite_note-Danailov_2026-95] [Renew Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renew_Europe) politicians, such as [Valérie Hayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Val%C3%A9rie_Hayer) and [Nikola Minchev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikola_Minchev), branded the elections as a risk of another trojan horse in the EU.[^cite_note-97]
