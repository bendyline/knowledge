**Middlesbrough Football Club** ([/ˈmɪdəlzbrə/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/8/87/En-uk-Middlesbrough.ogg/En-uk-Middlesbrough.ogg.mp3](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/8/87/En-uk-Middlesbrough.ogg/En-uk-Middlesbrough.ogg.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup> [*MID-əlz-brə*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key)) is a professional [association football](10568.md) club based in [Middlesbrough](334775.md), [North Yorkshire](143759.md). They compete in the [Championship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EFL_Championship), the second tier of [English football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_football_league_system). Nicknamed "**the Boro**", they were formed in 1876 and are the 12th [oldest football league club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_football_clubs_in_England_and_Wales_by_year_formed) in England and Wales. The club have played at the [Riverside Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riverside_Stadium) since 1995,[^cite_note-RSStadium-1] having previously played at [Ayresome Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayresome_Park) for 92 years, from 1903 to 1995.

Middlesbrough were one of the founding members of the [Premier League](11250.md) in 1992, and have spent all but two seasons of their entire history as a professional club competing within the top two tiers of English football.[^cite_note-MFCHistBk1-2] Their highest league finish to date was third place in the top flight in the [1913–14 season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1913%E2%80%9314_in_English_football). The outbreak of the [First World War](4764461.md) stunted their push for a first top division title, though the club pushed again during the inter-war years, finishing fourth in the [1938–39 season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1938%E2%80%9339_Football_League) before the [Second World War](32927.md) halted the English leagues and again prevented a push for a first title. The club came within minutes of folding in 1986 before they were saved by a [consortium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consortium) led by board member and later chairman [Steve Gibson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Gibson_\(businessman\)).[^cite_note-PremierLeagueSite-3] A remarkable [recovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middlesbrough_F.C._survival_from_liquidation) saw the club immediately earn back-to-back promotions to the top division in the [1986–87](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1986%E2%80%9387_Middlesbrough_F.C._season) and [1987–88](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1987%E2%80%9388_Middlesbrough_F.C._season) seasons. The club were runners-up in the [FA Cup](11237.md) and [League Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EFL_Cup) in 1997[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5] while also being relegated following a controversial 3-point deduction,[^cite_note-6] and losing another League Cup [final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1998_Football_League_Cup_final) the following season. Under [Steve McClaren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_McClaren), the club won the League Cup in [2004](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Football_League_Cup_final), its first major silverware, and reached the [2006 UEFA Cup final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_UEFA_Cup_final). The club has played one Premier League season since relegation in 2009.

Middlesbrough is the only major professional football club in the greater [Teesside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teesside) area (the 14th biggest [urban area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_urban_areas_in_the_United_Kingdom) in England), the [Tees Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tees_Valley), and the county of [North Yorkshire](143759.md) (the largest county in England by area[^cite_note-English_Counties_By_Population_And_Area_2024-7]). The club has regional rivalries with [Newcastle United F.C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newcastle_United_F.C.) (the [Tyne–Tees derby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyne%E2%80%93Tees_derby)) and [Sunderland A.F.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunderland_A.F.C.) (the [Tees–Wear derby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tees%E2%80%93Wear_derby)).[^cite_note-fan_survey-8]

The club's traditional kit is red with white detailing, often in the form of a white chest band. The home shorts and sock colours have interchangeably been shifted between red and white, complementing the red shirt that was adopted in 1899.[^cite_note-9] The various crests throughout the club's history, the most recent of which was adopted in 2026,[^cite_note-newcrest-10] incorporates a lion within a roundel crest linking to Teesside landmarks. For the 2026–27 season this has been made golden with 150 years printed below it.

## History

For a season-by-season record of league and cup performances, see [List of Middlesbrough F.C. seasons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Middlesbrough_F.C._seasons).

Main article: [History of Middlesbrough F.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Middlesbrough_F.C.)

### Formation and early years (1876–1914)



Chart showing the progress of Middlesbrough's league finishes since the 1899–1900 season

Middlesbrough were formed in 1876, and won the [FA Amateur Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FA_Amateur_Cup) in 1895 and again in 1898. The club turned professional in 1889, but reverted to amateur status in 1892.[^cite_note-MFCHistBk1-2] They turned professional permanently in 1899.[^cite_note-Hist76-02-11] After three seasons, they won promotion to the [First Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Football_League_First_Division), where they would remain for the next 22 years.[^cite_note-MFCHistBk1-2]

In 1903, the club moved to [Ayresome Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayresome_Park), their home for the next 92 years. In 1905, the club sanctioned the transfer of [Alf Common](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alf_Common) for [£](270673.md)1,000, a record fee.[^cite_note-AlfCommon-12] In the same year, [Tim Williamson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tim_Williamson) became the first Middlesbrough player to play international football.[^cite_note-Hist03-WW2-13]

Over these early years in the top flight, their form fluctuated greatly, rising to sixth in [1907–08](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1907%E2%80%9308_in_English_football)[^cite_note-14] before dropping to 17th two seasons later.[^cite_note-15] The club rose to their highest league finish to date, third, in [1913–14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1913%E2%80%9314_in_English_football).[^cite_note-Hist03-WW2-13] The [First World War](4764461.md) soon intervened, and football was suspended.

### Ups and downs (1914–1966)

Before league football resumed, Middlesbrough won the Northern Victory League,[^cite_note-finish_table-16] but the team were unable to maintain their previous form and finished the [1919–20](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1919%E2%80%9320_in_English_football) season in mid-table. They remained in the First Division for the next few seasons, but were relegated in [1923–24](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1923%E2%80%9324_in_English_football) after finishing bottom, 10 points adrift of their nearest rivals.[^cite_note-LeagueResults-17] Three seasons later, they won the [Division Two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Football_League_Division_Two) title. During that season, debutant [George Camsell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Camsell), who had signed from [Third Division North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Division_North) side Durham City the previous season, finished with a record 59 league goals, which included nine hat-tricks. He would continue as top scorer for each of the next 10 seasons.[^cite_note-Camsell-18] Middlesbrough's tenure back in the top flight lasted only one season, and the club were relegated.[^cite_note-finish_table-16] They were promoted at the first attempt in [1928–29](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1928%E2%80%9329_in_English_football), winning another Second Division title.[^cite_note-finish_table-16] The club remained in the First Division until 1954.



[George Hardwick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Hardwick), a Middlesbrough and England player, later a manager and coach

The decade before the [Second World War](32927.md) saw the emergence of [Wilf Mannion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilf_Mannion) and [George Hardwick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Hardwick), both of whom would go on to become [England](9904.md) internationals in the years ahead.[^cite_note-19] Middlesbrough climbed to fourth in the last full season before the war, and were expected to challenge for the title the following season, but the war intervened.[^cite_note-MFCHistBk1-2] After the war, the club was unable to recover the form of the previous seasons before the war, hovering around mid-table and exiting in the early rounds of the [FA Cup](11237.md). Soon after the war, the team began to falter, and were relegated in [1953–54](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1953%E2%80%9354_in_English_football). This was the start of a 20-year spell outside the top division, but this was the spell too that saw the emergence of one of the club's top goalscorers, [Brian Clough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Clough), who scored 204 goals in 222 games, before he left for [Sunderland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunderland_A.F.C.).[^cite_note-Clough-20]

On 6 May 1950 Middlesbrough were represented by a Black player for the first time, Jamaican-born [Lindy Delapenha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindy_Delapenha) making his debut in an away game against Fulham on that date. In total he went on to make 270 appearances, scoring 92 goals, before he left for [Mansfield Town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mansfield_Town) after the 1957–58 season.[^cite_note-21] Over that period, Middlesbrough maintained reasonable progress in the Second Division, but were never serious contenders for promotion. After a fourth-place finish in [1962–63](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1962%E2%80%9363_in_English_football), the club endured a steady decline and were relegated to the [Third Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Football_League_Third_Division) for the first time in their history in 1966.[^cite_note-finish_table-16]

### Resurgence, 'Charlton's Champions', and financial crisis (1966–1994)

New manager [Stan Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stan_Anderson) returned the club to the second flight at the first attempt.[^cite_note-MFCHistBk1-2] Middlesbrough would not finish below ninth during the next six seasons in the Second Division, finishing 4th (just outside the top three promotion winning places at the time) on three of those occasions.[^cite_note-MFCHistBk1-2]

In 1973, [Jack Charlton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Charlton) took over as manager and guided the team back to the top flight. A team led on the pitch by [Willie Maddren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willie_Maddren) and [Bobby Murdoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobby_Murdoch), and including a young [Graeme Souness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graeme_Souness), clinched promotion with seven games to spare, finishing with a then-league record 65 points[^cite_note-mfc-website-history-22] (based on the 2 points for a win format), 15 points ahead of second-placed [Luton Town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luton_Town_F.C.).[^cite_note-MFCHistBk1-2][^cite_note-23][^cite_note-Hist45-86-24] After a very promising start to their first campaign back in the first division [Bob Paisley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Paisley), manager of eventual runners up Liverpool, tipped Middlesbrough as favourites to win the league, however they ultimately fell short finishing seventh. Middlesbrough won their first silverware as a professional side in [the 1975–76 season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1975%E2%80%9376_in_English_football), lifting the [Anglo-Scottish Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Scottish_Cup) in its inaugural season after a two-legged final win over [Fulham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fulham_F.C.).[^cite_note-Hist45-86-24][^cite_note-Anglo-Scottish-25]

In 1979, [John Neal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Neal_\(footballer,_born_1932\)) made the club's first international signing, with [Boško Janković](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bo%C5%BEo_Jankovi%C4%87) arriving from [Željezničar Sarajevo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FK_%C5%BDeljezni%C4%8Dar_Sarajevo).[^cite_note-MFCHistBk1-2]

The club experienced severe financial difficulties during the mid-1980s.[^cite_note-26] Middlesbrough were dropping down the table, and finished 19th in the [1984–85 season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1984%E2%80%9385_in_English_football).[^cite_note-finish_table-16] In April 1986, the club had to borrow £30,000 from the [Professional Footballers' Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professional_Footballers'_Association) (PFA) to pay wages.[^cite_note-MFCHistBk1-2] The final game of the season saw Middlesbrough relegated to the Third Division again.[^cite_note-MFCHistBk1-2] That summer, the club called in the [Provisional Liquidator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Official_Receiver), and, shortly afterwards, the club was wound up and the gates to Ayresome Park were padlocked.[^cite_note-MFCHistBk1-2] Without the £350,000 capital required for [Football League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Football_League) registration, a new rule, the club risked folding permanently.[^cite_note-27] [Steve Gibson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Gibson_\(businessman\)), however, a member of the board at the time, brought together a consortium, and with 10 minutes to spare before the deadline they completed their registration with the Football League for the [1986–87 season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1986%E2%80%9387_in_English_football).[^cite_note-liquidation-28] Following the registration came both a change of club crest and a change of the official company name to Middlesbrough Football and Athletic Company (1986) Ltd.[^cite_note-Hist86-Pres-29][^cite_note-2026-crest-30]

Over the next two seasons, Middlesbrough gained successive promotions into Division Two and then into Division One,[^cite_note-finish_table-16] the latter being the first and only time a second-tier side [directly relegated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_Football_League_Second_Division_play-off_final) a first tier side through the [English Football League play-offs](6043445.md). The next season however, they came straight back down to the Second Division, despite having being seventh in the league in November and outside the relegation zone until the day after the season. Early in the 1989-90 campaign, defender [Gary Pallister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Pallister) was sold to [Manchester United](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_United_F.C.) for £2.3 million, one of the highest fees in English football at the time.[^cite_note-31] Following another promotion in 1992, Middlesbrough became one of the founding members of the [FA Premier League](11250.md) when it was launched in the [1992–93 season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1992%E2%80%9393_FA_Premier_League), but were relegated after just one season.[^cite_note-PremierLeagueSite-3]

### Bryan Robson years (1994–2001)

Player-manager [Bryan Robson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryan_Robson), from Manchester United, took charge in 1994.[^cite_note-Bryan_Robson-32] Following promotion to the Premier League Middlesbrough made high-profile purchases like Brazilian international [Juninho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juninho_Paulista)[^cite_note-PremierLeagueSite-3] and previous season's [Champions League final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_UEFA_Champions_League_final) goalscorer and Italian international [Fabrizio Ravanelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fabrizio_Ravanelli). A difficult [1996–97 season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996%E2%80%9397_FA_Premier_League), however, was compounded by a deduction of three points imposed just after Christmas as punishment for the club's failure to fulfil a fixture against [Blackburn Rovers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackburn_Rovers_F.C.), which ultimately resulted in relegation, despite the club taking guidance from the Premier League itself regarding the integrity of a match that would have included a number of debutants had Middlesbrough played due to illness in the Boro squad.[^cite_note-PremierLeague96/97-33] At the same time, the club reached both the [League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997_Football_League_Cup_final) and [FA Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997_FA_Cup_final) finals for the first time, but lost both games. Despite being in the second tier, they were again runners-up in the [League Cup final the next year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1998_Football_League_Cup_final).[^cite_note-Hist86-Pres-29]

Despite losing high-profile players Ravanelli and Juninho due to relegation, Middlesbrough were promoted back to the Premier League at the first attempt, in 1998. The following season saw them settle well and they had a 12-game unbeaten run midway through [1998–99](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1998%E2%80%9399_FA_Premier_League), including a 3–2 win at [Old Trafford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Trafford) In December 1998 during which they took a 3–0 lead; it was Manchester United's only home defeat during their [treble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treble_\(association_football\))-winning [season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1998%E2%80%9399_Manchester_United_F.C._season). Middlesbrough continued to stay secure in mid-table the following season, due mainly to the goals of [Hamilton Ricard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton_Ricard) and the signings of prominent players such as [Paul Ince](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Ince) and [Christian Ziege](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Ziege). In [2000–01](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2000%E2%80%9301_FA_Premier_League), they had a brief relegation scare that was solved with the arrival of [Terry Venables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terry_Venables) as co-manager, and a 3–0 win away at [Arsenal](2174.md) in April was the team's best result. The trend of buying high profile European-based players continued with the acquisitions of [Christian Karembeu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Karembeu) and [Alen Bokšić](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alen_Bok%C5%A1i%C4%87). Bryan Robson left the club before the start of [2001–02 season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001%E2%80%9302_FA_Premier_League), having served as manager for seven years.[^cite_note-34]

### Venture into Europe (2001–2009)



The [2004 League Cup final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Football_League_Cup_final) at the [Millennium Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennium_Stadium)

After Venables decided not to take on the role of full time manager, in June 2001 Manchester United assistant coach [Steve McClaren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_McClaren) was appointed to replace Robson.[^cite_note-35]

In his first season, McClaren led Middlesbrough to a respectable 12th place league finish and an FA Cup semi final, narrowly losing 1–0 to Arsenal. A slight improvement in the league saw the club finish 11th the following season. The [2003–04 season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003%E2%80%9304_Middlesbrough_F.C._season) saw the club again finish 11th in the league, but much more significantly win a first major trophy by beating [Bolton Wanderers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolton_Wanderers_F.C.) 2–1 in the [League Cup final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Football_League_Cup_final).[^cite_note-LgCpFinal-36] The League Cup win also ensured that Middlesbrough would qualify for Europe – the [UEFA Cup](232175.md) – for the first time – the following season, where they reached the [last 16 of the competition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004%E2%80%9305_UEFA_Cup_final_phase#Round_of_16).[^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38] UEFA Cup qualification was achieved for the [second consecutive year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005%E2%80%9306_UEFA_Cup) after a 1–1 away draw with [Manchester City](165813.md) on the final game of the season. The match concluded with a dramatic last minute late penalty save by goalkeeper [Mark Schwarzer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Schwarzer) which saw Middlesbrough finish above City in 7th place and qualify for Europe again.[^cite_note-Results0405-39]

Middlesbrough enjoyed one of its most successful cup campaigns to date in the [2005–2006 season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005%E2%80%9306_Middlesbrough_F.C._season). Domestically the club reached the League Cup quarter final and the FA Cup semi final, losing out to a late goal in a 1–0 loss to [West Ham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Ham_United) at [Villa Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villa_Park). The club also reached the [2006 UEFA Cup final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_UEFA_Cup_final) following two miraculous last minute comebacks from 3–0 down on aggregate in the quarter and semi finals against [FC Basel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FC_Basel) and [Steaua Bucharest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FCSB) respectively,[^cite_note-UEFACpQt-40][^cite_note-UEFACpSm-41] however ultimately fell short losing 4–0 to [Sevilla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sevilla_FC) in the final in [Eindhoven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eindhoven).[^cite_note-Results0506-42] McClaren's teams featured local youth players such as [Stewart Downing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stewart_Downing), [Adam Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Johnson_\(footballer\)) and [James Morrison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Morrison_\(footballer\))[^cite_note-43] as well as experienced international players such as forward trio [Yakubu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakubu_\(footballer\)), [Mark Viduka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Viduka) and [Jimmy Floyd Hasselbaink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Floyd_Hasselbaink),[^cite_note-44] and midfielder [Gaizka Mendieta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaizka_Mendieta). Middlesbrough fielded the youngest starting 11 (average age 20) in the final Premier League game in the 2005–06 season, against [Fulham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fulham_F.C.). Nine players were teenagers – all English – and two more came on as substitutes.[^cite_note-45]

Following the European Cup final loss, McClaren left to manage the [England national team](9904.md), and captain [Gareth Southgate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gareth_Southgate) took over as manager. Despite not having the coaching qualifications, he was allowed by the Premier League board to continue after receiving special dispensation.[^cite_note-GateCoachingBadges-46] Southgate led the club to a 12th-placed finish and an FA Cup quarter final in his [first season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006%E2%80%9307_Middlesbrough_F.C._season) as manager. In the subsequent [2007–2008 season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007%E2%80%9308_Middlesbrough_F.C._season), Middlesbrough again reached the [FA Cup quarter final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007%E2%80%9308_FA_Cup), but lost to Championship side [Cardiff City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_City). Middlesbrough eventually finished 13th in the league, defeating Manchester City 8–1 on the final day.

In January 2008, the club broke its record transfer fee, paying £13.6 million for [Brazilian international](149286.md) striker [Afonso Alves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afonso_Alves).[^cite_note-47] The club was relegated to the [Championship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EFL_Championship) at the end of the [2008–09 season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008%E2%80%9309_Middlesbrough_F.C._season)[^cite_note-48] in 19th place.

### Decline, brief revival and relegation (2009–2017)

Middlesbrough sacked [Gareth Southgate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gareth_Southgate) as manager in October 2009, when Southgate's team were one point from leading the Championship, and replaced him with [Gordon Strachan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Strachan).[^cite_note-49] At the time of Southgate's dismissal, Boro were fourth in the Championship but their form under Strachan declined and they finished mid-table.[^cite_note-50][^cite_note-51] On 18 October 2010, Strachan resigned and was later replaced by [Tony Mowbray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Mowbray).[^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53] Following a poor run of form at the start of the [2013–14 campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2013%E2%80%9314_Middlesbrough_F.C._season), Mowbray left the club with immediate effect on 21 October.[^cite_note-54]

[Aitor Karanka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aitor_Karanka), a former assistant coach at [Real Madrid](26413.md) to [José Mourinho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Mourinho), became the new Middlesbrough manager.[^cite_note-AKNOV13-55] He became the first non-British manager at the club,[^cite_note-AKNOV13-55] and led Boro to a 12th-place finish.[^cite_note-FCHD-56] In Karanka's first full season in charge, Middlesbrough finished fourth and thus qualified for the [2015 Football League play-offs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_Football_League_play-offs). After defeating [Brentford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brentford_F.C.) 5–1 on aggregate in the semi-final, the club lost 2–0 to [Norwich City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwich_City_F.C.) at [Wembley Stadium](8913012.md) in the [final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_Football_League_Championship_play-off_final).[^cite_note-57][^cite_note-58] The following [2015–16](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015%E2%80%9316_Football_League_Championship) season ended in dramatic fashion. The final match of the ordinary season was a head to head between 2nd placed Boro and 3rd placed [Brighton & Hove Albion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brighton_&_Hove_Albion_F.C.) at The Riverside. Boro required 1 point from the match to finish above Brighton in second place and secure automatic promotion. After taking a first half lead, they were pegged back, but ultimately held on for the draw, securing promotion back to the Premier League.[^cite_note-59]

Middlesbrough dismissed Karanka in March 2017 following a poor run of form, and the team were relegated in 19th place, after just one season back in the top flight. The team won only 5 league games, and scored 27 goals, the lowest in the league.[^cite_note-60]

### Return to the Championship (2017–present)

The club appointed former [Leeds United](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leeds_United_F.C.) manager [Garry Monk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garry_Monk) as manager in the off-season. Expectations at the club were high, having spent close to £50 million in the transfer window on player purchases, in order to mount an immediate promotion challenge back to the Premier League.[^cite_note-61] Monk left in December, with Middlesbrough ninth in the Championship,[^cite_note-62][^cite_note-BBC23dec17-63] and [Tony Pulis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Pulis) was appointed as his replacement.[^cite_note-64] Pulis led the side to finish 5th in the table, however, they lost in the play-off semi-finals to Aston Villa.[^cite_note-65][^cite_note-FCHD-56] In the [following season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018%E2%80%9319_Middlesbrough_F.C._season), a poor finish to the season caused them to finish 7th and miss out on the play-offs by one point.[^cite_note-66]

When Pulis's contract was not extended,[^cite_note-67] he was replaced by former Middlesbrough defender and first team coach, [Jonathan Woodgate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Woodgate) on 14 June 2019 on a three-year contract.[^cite_note-68] Woodgate was dismissed with the club only outside of the relegation zone on goal difference with eight games left of the 2019–20 season,[^cite_note-69] and [Neil Warnock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neil_Warnock) was appointed as his replacement on the same day.[^cite_note-70] Warnock ensured survival from relegation, securing safety on the final day of the season and a 17th-place finish.[^cite_note-FCHD-56][^cite_note-71] On 6 November 2021, Middlesbrough parted company with Warnock, who was replaced by [Chris Wilder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chris_Wilder) the following day.[^cite_note-72][^cite_note-73] After 11 months in charge, Wilder was dismissed with the club in 22nd position.[^cite_note-74]

Former Manchester United midfielder [Michael Carrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Carrick) was appointed as his successor and led Boro to a fourth-place finish, but lost in the play-off semi-finals against Coventry City.[^cite_note-75][^cite_note-76][^cite_note-77] On 26 May 2023, the club officially became affiliated with the women's team.[^cite_note-Women's_team_affiliation-78] In the [2023–24](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023%E2%80%9324_EFL_Cup) season, Middlesbrough reached the semi-finals of the League Cup for the first time since 2004.[^cite_note-79] Despite defeating Chelsea in the first leg, Middlesbrough lost 6–2 on aggregate.[^cite_note-80] Carrick was dismissed in June 2025 with [Rob Edwards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rob_Edwards_\(footballer,_born_1982\)) replacing him.[^cite_note-81] However, after a promising start to the season that saw the club enter the automatic promotion spaces, Edwards left in November 2025 to become the head coach of [Wolverhampton Wanderers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolverhampton_Wanderers_F.C.).

On 24 November 2025 [Kim Hellberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kim_Hellberg) was named as the successor to [Rob Edwards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rob_Edwards_\(footballer,_born_1982\)).[^cite_note-82]

In May 2026, [Southampton](81576.md) defeated Middlesbrough 2–1 on aggregate (after extra time) in the [EFL Championship play-off](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EFL_Championship_play-offs) semi-final. Two days before the first leg of the semi-final, Middlesbrough complained that a member of Southampton's staff had been observed attempting to watch one of their training sessions in the build-up to the tie, and on the day before the first leg, Southampton were charged with breaching EFL regulations.[^cite_note-spygate1-83] An Independent Disciplinary Commission ruled that Southampton be expelled from the play-offs and receive a four-point deduction for the [2026–27](82934740.md) Championship season.[^cite_note-spygate2-84] Middlesbrough were reinstated to the [play-off final](83148672.md), where they were defeated 1–0 by [Hull City](451358.md) at Wembley.[^cite_note-85] The incident was widely referred to in the media as ["Spygate"](83228613.md).[^cite_note-spygate2-84]

Middlesbrough's original home kit upon election to the Football League in 1899 was a white home shirt with red shorts, and they did not adopt their colours of blue and white until later that season.[^cite_note-hist-kit-87] Previous kits included a white shirt with a red and white [polka dotted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polka_dot) collar from around 1889.[^cite_note-hist-kit-87] The Middlesbrough kit has remained broadly the same since 1899; a red shirt with white detailing, with shorts and socks of either red or white. The distinctive broad white stripe across the chest was introduced by Jack Charlton in 1973 (following an attempt to change the home shirt to a Leeds United-style white shirt), and brought back for a one-off in 1997–98, and, then again, for the 2000–01 and 2004–05 seasons due to popular demand.[^cite_note-chestband-88] The club subsequently announced in December 2007 that the club would allow fans to decide via an online and text vote whether the white band should return for the [following season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008%E2%80%9309_Middlesbrough_F.C._season).[^cite_note-chestband-88] On 8 January 2008, the club announced that, with 77.4% of voters voting in its favour, the white band would return to the home kit, and that fans would choose the final shirt appearance from a selection of three designs,[^cite_note-whiteband-89] of which the winner was announced on 7 May 2008.[^cite_note-90]

The Middlesbrough crest has gone through four changes since the formation of the club. Initially, the badge was simply the town of Middlesbrough's crest with a red lion instead of a blue lion in order to fit in with the club's colours. Following the adoption of the white band on the shirts in 1973, only the red lion remained with the letters "M.F.C" underneath in red. This was further adapted following the reformation of the club in 1986 to a circular crest with the lion in the middle and the words "Middlesbrough Football Club 1986" around the circle in order to reflect this new era. In 2007, Middlesbrough changed their crest again, this time with the lion inside a shield and the words "Middlesbrough Football Club 1876" underneath.[^cite_note-newcrest-10] The club's chairman Steve Gibson stated that the intention was to reflect the club's long history and not just their post-liquidation status.[^cite_note-newcrest-10] On 16 October 2025, Middlesbrough announced a new crest to be used from the 2026–27 season onwards, signalling a return to the all-familiar roundel shape after 19 years and tie in with the club's 150th anniversary celebrations, with a special version of the new crest to be used during that anniversary season.[^cite_note-2026-crest-30]

## Stadiums



The Riverside Stadium in 2006, with the old gates to Ayresome Park in the foreground

Main articles: [Ayresome Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayresome_Park) and [Riverside Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riverside_Stadium)

After formation in 1876, and with the club still amateurs, Middlesbrough's first two years of football were played at [Albert Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Park,_Middlesbrough) in Middlesbrough. After seeing the damage being caused by players and supporters, the Park Committee ordered the club to find an alternate venue. The club moved to Breckon Hill, behind the former [Middlesbrough College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middlesbrough_College) longlands site, after agreeing to rent the land from its owner. However, two years later in 1880, the owner increased the rent and the club decided to move. They moved into the [Linthorpe Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linthorpe_Road) ground in 1882, home at the time of [Middlesbrough Cricket Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middlesbrough_Cricket_Club). The cricket club departed in 1893–94 to move to the Breckon Hill field, and Middlesbrough Football Club became sole users of the ground.[^cite_note-GroundsHistory-98]

With the club's growing size, and entry to [the Football League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Football_League), they had to move to a new ground in 1903, [Ayresome Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayresome_Park).[^cite_note-PremierLeagueSite-3] It was designed by [Archibald Leitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archibald_Leitch) and would be the club's home for the next 92 years, having also been chosen as one of the stadia for the [1966 FIFA World Cup](61629.md). Following the [Taylor Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_Report) in 1990, the ground either needed modernising or the club needed a new stadium. The club decided on the latter, and moved out at the end of the 1994–95 season. Ayresome Park was used as a training ground during 1995–96, before it was demolished in 1997 and a housing estate built in its place.[^cite_note-GroundsHistory-98] Since the 1990s, the club trains at a £7 million complex at [Rockliffe Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockliffe_Park_\(Hurworth\)), in [Hurworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurworth-on-Tees), on the outskirts of [Darlington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darlington).[^cite_note-99]

The [Riverside Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riverside_Stadium), named by the supporters of the club after a vote, became the club's home in 1995. It was the first stadium to be built in line with the Taylor Report's recommendations on all-seater stadiums for clubs in the top two divisions of the [English football league system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_football_league_system).[^cite_note-Riverside_Stadium-100] It was originally a 30,000-seater stadium, constructed at a cost of £16 million,[^cite_note-RSStadium-1] before it was expanded in 1998 to a capacity of 35,100 for an extra £5 million. The current capacity of the Riverside Stadium is 33,746 due to updates to the stadium, including the movement of the away section and the addition of a large screen.[^cite_note-101][^cite_note-102][^cite_note-RSStadium-1]

Since then, several reorganisations of the Riverside Stadium have taken place. At the start of the 2013–14 season, away fans were moved from behind the goal in the South stand to the South East corner, while home fans were situated behind both goals to help create a better atmosphere inside the stadium. A giant TV screen was also installed at the back of the South-East corner, replacing the older style scoreboards attached to the North and South stand roofs.[^cite_note-103] For the start of the [2016–17 season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016%E2%80%9317_Middlesbrough_F.C._season) (and a return to the Premier League), the club had to improve the stadium's broadcasting facilities and floodlighting in order to meet Premier League requirements. The club also took this opportunity to move the main camera gantry to the back of the East stand, so that it faced the main West stand. As of the 2016–17 season, the stadium capacity is 33,746 due to the updates undertaken when the club was last in the Premier League.[^cite_note-104][^cite_note-105][^cite_note-Gazette_Live_Website-106]

## Supporters

Middlesbrough's average historic attendance is the 16th highest of all the clubs in England and Wales.[^cite_note-107]

Traditionally, supporters come from Middlesbrough itself and towns in the immediate area. As of May 2001, Middlesbrough had one of the highest proportions in Britain of locally-born season ticket holders at 80%, and one of the highest proportions of female fans at 20%.[^cite_note-Football'sRich_Pickings-108] A survey at the start of the 2007–08 season found Middlesbrough supporters were the seventh-loudest set of fans in the Premier League.[^cite_note-109] Middlesbrough Official Supporters Club, which features its own team in the local football league,[^cite_note-SupportersClub-110] has links with supporters' clubs across the globe. The largest supporters' clubs include the Official Supporters' Club, the Middlesbrough Disabled Supporters' Association, Yarm Reds, Red Faction and Middlesbrough Supporters South.[^cite_note-SupportersClub-110]

For Middlesbrough supporters, their main rivals are [Newcastle United](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newcastle_United_F.C.) (with whom they contest the [Tyne–Tees derby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyne%E2%80%93Tees_derby)), followed by [Sunderland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunderland_A.F.C.) (with whom they contest the [Tees–Wear derby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tees%E2%80%93Wear_derby)), and also, according to a planetfootball.com's 2004 survey, [Leeds United](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leeds_United_F.C.).[^cite_note-fan_survey-8]

&#x20;The nickname *[Smoggies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoggie)* was first used as a derogatory term by opposing supporters;[^cite_note-111] it relates to the industrial air pollution – smog – that used to hang over the town, but it was later used by Middlesbrough fans in a somewhat self-deprecating manner, before finally being adopted as a badge of pride by supporters of the club. An example of this can be seen on the banners carried to away games stating "Smoggies on Tour".[^cite_note-banners-112] Middlesbrough fans received praise from [UEFA](32332.md) Chief Executive [Lars-Christer Olsson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lars-Christer_Olsson) after their behaviour during the [2005–06 UEFA Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005%E2%80%9306_UEFA_Cup) campaign.[^cite_note-113] He commented that:&#x20;

> You have the satisfaction of knowing that, although your team did not win the game, your supporters present in Eindhoven proved to the world that football fans can turn a match into a friendly, violence-free celebration.

&#x20;Middlesbrough fans also received praise from [Cleveland Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleveland_Police) for their behaviour in previous rounds, particularly in the light of aggravation prior to and during the match at [Roma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AS_Roma).[^cite_note-114]

## Media relations

Middlesbrough was the first football club in the world to launch its own TV channel – Boro TV. The first broadcasts were tied to the club's first ever major cup final appearance in 1997, a full year ahead of Manchester United's MUTV, which still claims to be the first in the world. The channel was the brainchild of then NTL marketing director, Peter Wilcock. The programme became synonymous with former Middlesbrough player Bernie Slaven and radio commentator Alastair Brownlee.[^cite_note-115] Its programmes were not live initially but were pre-recorded and hosted by [Dave Roberts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dave_Roberts_\(sports_broadcaster\)).

In August 2001, Boro TV claimed another first when it became the first English football club to broadcast time-delayed full-match footage of their league games on its own channel.[^cite_note-116] Boro TV ran through [NTL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgin_Media) cable television until July 2005.[^cite_note-117] The club then began to show match highlights through a subscription-based scheme on its official website.[^cite_note-118]

Middlesbrough's official [matchday programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matchday_programme), *Redsquare*, was *Programme Monthly's* 2006–07 Programme of the Year.[^cite_note-119] There are numerous other [fanzines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fanzine) available, most notably *Fly Me to the Moon*, formed in September 1988 following [Bruce Rioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Rioch)'s quote to [Tony Mowbray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Mowbray), stating "If I had to go to the [moon](19331.md) I'd want him by my side".[^cite_note-120] In 2025, it became the football fanzine with the most issues, overtaking previous record holders [*Oatcake* from Stoke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Oatcake), which stopped printing in 2019.[^cite_note-121]

## Community

**Middlesbrough Football Club in the Community** (MFCIC) was founded in 1996 by club chairman [Steve Gibson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Gibson_\(businessman\))[^cite_note-122] and is one of the largest community-based football schemes in the United Kingdom.[^cite_note-123] It is run separately from the football club, but receives support from both the club in terms of providing players, staff, stadium facilities and [PR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_relations) for articles in the matchday programme and other publications, as well as support from other local organisations.[^cite_note-124] In 2012, MFCIC was relaunched as MFC Foundation. The Foundation aims to use the club's profile to deliver sport, health, education and inclusion projects in vulnerable and disadvantaged communities across Teesside. Since 1996, the Foundation has delivered 20,000 qualifications, engaged over 500,000 people and invested £25 million in local communities to tackle inequality and disadvantage.

Since 2002, the club and MFCIC have also run the Middlesbrough Enterprise Academy, a scheme which helps local children. In March 2008, plans were announced by the Premier League to roll out the scheme nationally amongst all Premier League clubs.[^cite_note-125]

It was announced in December 2007 that Middlesbrough football club had carried out more community work during [2006–07](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006%E2%80%9307_in_English_football) than any other [Premier League](11250.md) club, rising from second place the previous year, with the club making 318 appearances – almost twice the Premier League average of 162.[^cite_note-126] They were in the top two for community appearances again in 2007–08, with 374 – a 17% increase on the previous season.[^cite_note-127]

Middlesbrough's mascot is Roary the Lion. The club runs Roary's Children's Charity Fund, which purchases items for local children's charities.[^cite_note-128]

In 2009, steel producer [Corus Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corus_Group) announced the possibility that it would mothball its Teesside plant after a consortium of steel magnates walked away from a 10-year deal. Middlesbrough Football Club helped with the "Save Our Steel" campaign through various methods.[^cite_note-SaveOurSteel1-129] Chairman Steve Gibson said:

> "Middlesbrough Football Club exists for the community, for the people of Teesside—and the closure of the steel plants threatens to rip the heart out of our community. We cannot stand by and allow that to happen. We want the steelworkers and their families to know that we are behind them and will help their campaign in any way we can ... We like to think that the football club is the flagship of Teesside. Well this is our town and these are our people and we have to do what we can to help them."[^cite_note-SaveOurSteel1-129]

## Middlesbrough Women

Main article: [Middlesbrough F.C. Women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middlesbrough_F.C._Women)

[Middlesbrough Women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middlesbrough_F.C._Women) is the women's team affiliated to Middlesbrough. Founded as Cleveland Spartans in 1976, they became officially affiliated with the men's team in 2023,[^cite_note-Women's_team_affiliation-78] and currently play in the [FA Women's National League North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women's_National_League_North), the third level of [English women's football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women's_football_in_England#Pyramid).
