# Offshore drilling



Holstein, an oil drilling platform at Green Canyon in the [Gulf of Mexico](21076367.md), approximately 100 miles from land.

**Offshore drilling** is a mechanical process where a [wellbore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellbore) is drilled below the seabed. It is typically carried out in order to explore for and subsequently extract [petroleum](23195.md) that lies in rock formations beneath the seabed. Most commonly, the term is used to describe drilling activities on the [continental shelf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_shelf), though the term can also be applied to drilling in [lakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakes), [inshore waters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inshore_waters) and [inland seas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inland_sea).

Offshore drilling presents all environmental challenges, both offshore and onshore from the produced [hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbons) and the materials used during the drilling operation. Controversies include the ongoing [US offshore drilling debate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_offshore_drilling_debate).[^cite_note-1]

There are many different types of facilities from which offshore drilling operations take place. These include bottom founded drilling rigs ([jackup barges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackup_rig) and swamp [barges](4177.md)), combined drilling and production facilities either bottom founded or floating platforms, and deepwater mobile offshore drilling units (MODU) including [semi-submersibles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-submersible_platform) or [drillships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drillship). These are capable of operating in water depths up to 3,000 metres (9,800 ft). In shallower waters the mobile units are anchored to the seabed; however, in water deeper than 1,500 metres (4,900 ft), the semi-submersibles and drillships are maintained at the required drilling location using [dynamic positioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_positioning).

## History



Offshore oil well drilling platform, Continental Oil Co., Gulf of Mexico, 1955.

Around 1891, the first submerged oil wells were drilled from platforms built on piles in the fresh waters of the [Grand Lake St. Marys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Lake_St._Marys) in [Ohio](22199.md). The wells were developed by small local companies such as Bryson, Riley Oil, German-American and Banker's Oil.[^cite_note-2]

Around 1896, the first submerged oil wells in salt water were drilled in the portion of the [Summerland field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summerland_Oil_Field) extending under the [Santa Barbara Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Barbara_Channel) in [California](5407.md). The wells were drilled from piers extending from land out into the channel.[^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4]

Other notable early submerged drilling activities occurred on the Canadian side of [Lake Erie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Erie) in the 1900s and [Caddo Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caddo_Lake) in [Louisiana](18130.md) in the 1910s. Shortly thereafter wells were drilled in tidal zones along the [Texas](29810.md) and Louisiana [gulf coast](11969.md). The [Goose Creek Oil Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goose_Creek_Oil_Field) near [Baytown, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baytown,_Texas) is one such example. In the 1920s drilling activities occurred from concrete platforms in [Venezuela](32374.md)'s [Lake Maracaibo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Maracaibo).[^cite_note-5]

One of the oldest subsea wells is the [Bibi Eibat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibiheyb%C9%99t) well, which came on stream in 1923 in [Azerbaijan](746.md).[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7] The well was located on an artificial island in a shallow portion of the [Caspian Sea](19653787.md). In the early 1930s, the [Texas Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texaco) developed the first mobile steel barges for drilling in the [brackish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brackish_water) coastal areas of the [Gulf of Mexico](21076367.md).

In 1937, [Pure Oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_Oil) and its partner [Superior Oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superior_Oil) used a fixed platform to develop a field 1 mile (1.6 km) offshore of [Calcasieu Parish, Louisiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcasieu_Parish,_Louisiana) in 14 feet (4.3 m) of water.

In 1938, [Humble Oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humble_Oil) built a mile-long wooden trestle with railway tracks into the sea at McFadden Beach on the Gulf of Mexico, placing a derrick at its end – this was later destroyed by a hurricane.[^cite_note-8]



Worker on an offshore drilling rig.

In 1945, concern for American control of its offshore oil reserves caused President [Harry Truman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Truman) to issue an Executive Order unilaterally extending American territory to the edge of its continental shelf, an act that effectively ended the [3-mile limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3-mile_limit) "[freedom of the seas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_the_seas)" regime.[^cite_note-9]

In 1946, [Magnolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnolia_Petroleum_Company) drilled at a site 18 miles (29 km) off the coast, erecting a platform in 18 feet (5.5 m) of water off [St. Mary Parish, Louisiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Mary_Parish,_Louisiana).[^cite_note-10]

In early 1947, [Superior Oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superior_Oil) erected a drilling and production platform in 20 feet (6.1 m) of water some 18 miles (29 km) off Vermilion Parish, La. But it was [Kerr-Magee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerr-McGee_Oil_Industries), as operator for partners [Phillips Petroleum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phillips_Petroleum) and [Stanolind Oil & Gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanolind_Oil_&_Gas) that completed its historic Ship Shoal Block 32 well in October 1947, months before Superior actually drilled a discovery from their Vermilion platform farther offshore. In any case, that made Kerr-McGee's well the first oil discovery drilled out of sight of land. When offshore drilling moved into deeper waters of up to 30 metres (98 ft), fixed platform rigs were built, until demands for drilling [equipment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equipment) was needed in the 100 feet (30 m) to 120 metres (390 ft) depth of the Gulf of Mexico, the first [jack-up rigs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackup_rig) began appearing from specialized offshore drilling contractors.[^cite_note-11]



Offshore drilling rig, c. 1968.

The first [semi-submersible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-submersible_platform) resulted from an unexpected observation in 1961.[^cite_note-12] Blue Water Drilling Company owned and operated the four-column submersible Blue Water Rig No. 1 in the Gulf of Mexico for [Shell Oil Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shell_Oil_Company). As the pontoons were not sufficiently buoyant to support the weight of the rig and its consumables, it was towed between locations at a draught midway between the top of the pontoons and the underside of the deck.

It was noticed that the motions at this draught were very small, and Blue Water Drilling and Shell jointly decided to try operating the rig in the floating mode. The concept of an anchored, stable floating deep-sea platform had been designed and tested back in the 1920s by [Edward Robert Armstrong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Robert_Armstrong) for the purpose of operating aircraft with an invention known as the 'seadrome'. The first purpose-built drilling [semi-submersible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-submersible_platform) *Ocean Driller* was launched in 1963 by [ODECO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ODECO). Since then, many semi-submersibles have been purpose-designed for the drilling industry mobile offshore fleet.



Comparison of deepwater [semi-submersible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-submersible_platform) and [drillship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drillship).

The first offshore [drillship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drillship) was the *CUSS 1* developed for the [Mohole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohole) project to drill into the Earth's crust.[^cite_note-13]

As of June 2010, there were over 620 mobile offshore drilling rigs (jackups, semisubs, drillships, barges, etc.) available for service in the worldwide offshore rig fleet.[^cite_note-14]

One of the world's deepest hubs is currently the [Perdido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perdido_oil_platform) in the Gulf of Mexico, floating in 2,438 meters (7,999 ft) of water. It is operated by [Royal Dutch Shell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Dutch_Shell) and was built at a cost of \$3 billion.[^cite_note-15] The deepest operational platform is the Petrobras America Cascade FPSO in the Walker Ridge 249 field in 2,600 meters (8,500 ft) of water.[^cite_note-16]

## Drilling platforms

Main article: [Oil platform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_platform)



Types of offshore oil and gas structures.

See also: [Fixed platform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_platform), [Compliant tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compliant_tower), [Tension-leg platform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tension-leg_platform), and [Spar (platform)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spar_\(platform\))

Offshore drilling is usually done from platforms generically known as mobile offshore drilling units (MODU), which can be of one of several formats, depending on the water depth:

- [Jackup rig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackup_rig)

- [Submersible drilling rig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submersible_drilling_rig)

- [Semi-submersible platform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-submersible_platform)

- [Drillship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drillship)

Beyond the MODUs mentioned above, offshore developments also employ a range of [fixed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_platform) and floating [drilling platforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drilling_platform) that also support drilling, production, or both. Key types include:

1. [Fixed platforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_platform) - These are rigid structures whose legs are anchored directly into the [seabed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seabed), supporting a deck which holds [drilling rigs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drilling_rig), [production facilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drilling_and_production_platform), and even living quarters. These platforms are generally constructed from [steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel), creating “jacket” platforms, or [concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concrete), creating “gravity-based structures.” Steel-jacket platforms consist of tubular steel members piled into the seabed, while concrete [caisson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caisson_\(engineering\)) platforms may incorporate large concrete bases and internal [buoyancy tanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buoyancy_tank).

   [^cite_note-\:0-17]

   &#x20;Fixed platforms are cost-effective for shallow to moderate water depths, as their practical depth limit is usually a few hundred meters.

   [^cite_note-\:1-18]

   [^cite_note-\:2-19]

2. [Compliant Towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compliant_tower) - Compliant Towers or CTs are tall, slender, and flexible tower structures anchored to the seabed by piles, but designed to withstand significant lateral deflection under environmental loads like [wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind), [waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_wave), and [currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_current).

   [^cite_note-20]

   &#x20;Their flexibility allows them to operate in much deeper waters than conventional fixed jackets, commonly in the range of several hundred to about 900m.

   [^cite_note-\:0-17]

3. [Tension-Leg Platforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tension-leg_platform) - This type of platform is a floating structure stabilized by vertical, tensioned tendons, also known as tethers, that anchor it to the seabed. Because the tendons are very stiff vertically, they eliminate most vertical motion, enabling [wellheads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellhead) and production equipment to be located on the deck rather than [subsea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subseafloor_biosphere).

   [^cite_note-\:1-18]

   [^cite_note-\:3-21]

   &#x20;TLPs are well suited for deep-water environments, often hundreds of thousands of meters, and “mini-TLP” variants like Seastar platforms have been developed for smaller [fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_fields).

   [^cite_note-\:1-18]

   [^cite_note-\:2-19]

4. [Spar Platforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spar_\(platform\)) - Spar platforms consist of a large-diameter, vertical cylindrical [hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hull_\(watercraft\)) or hard tank that provides [buoyancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buoyancy), with a deck on top, and is [moored](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mooring) to the seabed using conventional mooring lines like through chains or wires.

   [^cite_note-\:4-22]

   &#x20;There are several types of this platform, notably classic, [truss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truss), and cell spars, which vary in hull configuration and internal [ballast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballast) arrangements.

   [^cite_note-23]

   &#x20;The [deep-draft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Draft_\(hull\)) design of spar platforms gives them excellent stability in very deep waters, making them suitable for ultra-deep production and drilling.

   [^cite_note-\:1-18]

5. Floating Production Systems (FPS) - FPS are floating platforms like [semi-submersibles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-submersible_platform) that are equipped for both production and, occasionally, drilling.

   [^cite_note-\:4-22]

   &#x20;These units are anchored by either [mooring lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mooring_line), usually cables and chains, or maintained in position by [dynamic positioning systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_Positioning_Systems).

   [^cite_note-\:5-24]

   &#x20;FPS units can operate in ultra-deep waters, which is around thousands of feet.

   [^cite_note-\:5-24]

6. [Floating Production, Storage and Offloading (FPSO) Units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating_production_storage_and_offloading) - An FPSO is a ship-shaped vessel that processes [hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon) from subsea wells and stores its oil in its [hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hull_\(watercraft\)).

   [^cite_note-\:1-18]

   &#x20;It is moored to the seabed, often via a turret mooring system, allowing it to “weathervane” in response to environmental forces.

   [^cite_note-\:3-21]

   &#x20;Periodically, [shuttle tankers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shuttle_tanker) offload stored oil, which is advantageous in remote or frontier deepwater areas where pipeline infrastructure is limited or absent.

   [^cite_note-\:1-18]

## Challenges



Being far from land can create many challenges, from logistics to safety concerns.

Offshore oil and gas production is more challenging than land-based installations due to the remote and harsher environment. Much of the innovation in the offshore petroleum sector concerns overcoming these challenges, including the need to provide very large production facilities. Production and drilling facilities may be very large and a large investment, such as the [Troll A platform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troll_A_platform) standing on a depth of 300 meters (980 ft).[^cite_note-28]

Another type of offshore platform may float with a [mooring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mooring) system to maintain it on location. While a floating system may be lower cost in deeper waters than a fixed platform, the dynamic nature of the platforms introduces many challenges for the drilling and production facilities.

The ocean can add several thousand meters or more to the fluid column. The addition increases the equivalent circulating density and downhole pressures in drilling wells, as well as the energy needed to lift produced fluids for separation on the platform.

The trend today is to conduct more of the production operations [subsea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsea), by separating water from oil and re-injecting it rather than pumping it up to a platform, or by flowing to onshore, with no installations visible above the sea. Subsea installations help to exploit resources at progressively deeper waters—locations which had been inaccessible—and overcome challenges posed by sea ice such as in the [Barents Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barents_Sea). One such challenge in shallower environments is [seabed gouging by drifting ice features](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seabed_gouging_by_ice) (means of protecting offshore installations against ice action includes burial in the seabed).

Offshore manned facilities also present logistics and human resources challenges. An offshore oil platform is a small community in itself with cafeteria, sleeping quarters, management and other support functions. In the North Sea, staff members are transported by helicopter for a two-week shift. They usually receive higher salary than onshore workers do. Supplies and waste are transported by ship, and the supply deliveries need to be carefully planned because storage space on the platform is limited. Today, much effort goes into relocating as many of the personnel as possible onshore, where management and technical experts are in touch with the platform by video conferencing. An onshore job is also more attractive for the aging workforce in the [petroleum industry](994887.md), at least in the western world. These efforts among others are contained in the established term [integrated operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_operations). The increased use of subsea facilities helps achieve the objective of keeping more workers onshore. Subsea facilities are also easier to expand, with new separators or different modules for different oil types, and are not limited by the fixed floor space of an above-water installation.

## Effects on the environment

See also: [Ecological effects of oil platforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_platform#Ecological_effects)

Offshore oil production involves environmental risks, most notably [oil spills](158705.md) from oil tankers or pipelines transporting oil from the platform to onshore facilities, and from leaks and accidents on the platform (e.g. [Deepwater Horizon oil spill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deepwater_Horizon_oil_spill) and [Ixtoc I oil spill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ixtoc_I_oil_spill)).[^cite_note-29] [Produced water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Produced_water) is also generated, which is water brought to the surface along with the oil and gas; it is usually highly [saline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saline_water) and may include dissolved or unseparated hydrocarbons.
