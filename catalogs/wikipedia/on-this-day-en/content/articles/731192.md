The [International Olympic Committee](15147.md) (IOC) uses icons, flags, and symbols to represent and enhance the [Olympic Games](22576.md). These symbols include those commonly used during Olympic competitions such as the flame, fanfare, and theme as well as those used both during and outside competition, such as the Olympic flag.

The Olympic flag was created in 1914 under the guidance of [Baron de Coubertin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_de_Coubertin) of France. It was first hoisted in [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria), Egypt, in Old [Shatby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shatby) Stadium which is now located in [Al Ittihad Alexandria Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Ittihad_Alexandria_Club), at the 1914 Pan-Egyptian Games.[^cite_note-1] The five rings on the flag represent the inhabited continents of the world (the [Americas](29833.md) considered as one continent). It contains the colours blue, black, red, yellow, and green, which are common on national flags globally.[^cite_note-2]

## Motto and creed



The motto on a [1948 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1948_Summer_Olympics) medal

The original Olympic motto is the [hendiatris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendiatris) "*Citius, Altius, Fortius*,"[^cite_note-3] [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) for "Faster, Higher, Stronger" (also "Swifter, Higher, Stronger.")[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5] The motto was proposed by [Pierre de Coubertin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_de_Coubertin) upon the creation of the IOC. Coubertin borrowed it from his friend [Henri Didon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Didon), a [Dominican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominican_Order) priest who was an athletics enthusiast.[^cite_note-motto-6] Coubertin said that "these three words represent a programme of moral beauty. The aesthetics of sport are intangible".[^cite_note-motto-6] The motto was introduced at the [1924 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1924_Summer_Olympics) in Paris.[^cite_note-7] Coubertin's Olympic ideals are expressed in the Olympic creed:

> *L'essentiel est de participer. L'important n'est pas de triompher, mais de lutter. L'essentiel n'est pas d'avoir vaincu, mais d'avoir bien combattu.*
>
> \[The most important thing in the Olympic Games is not to win but to take part, just as the most important thing in life is not the triumph but the struggle. The essential thing is not to have conquered but to have fought well.[^cite_note-olympicsymbols-8]]

— Pierre de Coubertin[^cite_note-9]

Coubertin got this text from a sermon by [Bishop of Central Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episcopal_Diocese_of_Central_Pennsylvania) [Ethelbert Talbot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethelbert_Talbot), during the [1908 London Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1908_Summer_Olympics).[^cite_note-10]

In 2021, the IOC approved the addition of the word "together", after an [en dash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dash), to the motto. It now reads "*Citius, Altius, Fortius – Communiter*", Latin for "Faster, Higher, Stronger – Together" ([French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language): *"Plus vite, Plus haut, Plus fort – Ensemble"*).[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12] According to some [Italian](385155.md) Latinists, such as professor Mario De Nonno and professor Giorgio Piras, the concept of acting together is not properly conveyed by *communis* nor *communiter*. De Nonno suggested an adverb such as *Una* meaning "as one".[^cite_note-13][^cite_note-14]

The Olympic rings consist of five interlocking rings, coloured blue, yellow, black, green, and red on a white field. The symbol was originally created in 1913 by [Coubertin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_de_Coubertin).[^cite_note-15] Although the colors of the rings were later said to be representations of individual continents, Coubertin originally only meant the number of rings to "represent the five parts of the world now won over to Olympism."[^cite_note-KLennartz-16] According to Coubertin, the colours of the rings, along with the white background, represented the colours of every competing country's flag at the time. Upon its initial introduction, Coubertin stated the following in the August 1913 edition of *Olympique*:[^cite_note-17]

> ... the six colours \[including the flag's white background] combined in this way reproduce the colours of every country without exception. The blue and yellow of Sweden, the blue and white of Greece, the tricolour flags of France, the United Kingdom, the United States, Germany, Belgium, Italy, and Hungary, and the yellow and red of Spain are included, as are the innovative flags of Brazil and Australia, and those of ancient Japan and modern China. This, truly, is an international emblem.



USFSA logo



Competition cap of the [Union des sociétés françaises de sports athlétiques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_des_Soci%C3%A9t%C3%A9s_Fran%C3%A7aises_de_Sports_Athl%C3%A9tiques). The two interlocking rings inspired the Olympic rings.

In his article published in November 1992 in the *Olympic Revue,* the official magazine of the IOC, American historian Robert Barney explained that the idea of the interlaced rings came to Coubertin when he was in charge of the [USFSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_des_Soci%C3%A9t%C3%A9s_Fran%C3%A7aises_de_Sports_Athl%C3%A9tiques). This association was founded by the union of two French sports associations and, until 1925, was responsible for representing the IOC in France. The emblem of the USFSA was two interlaced rings. Barney noted that Swiss psychiatrist [Carl Jung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Jung) considered circles to symbolise wholeness and that their linkage symbolized continuity.[^cite_note-AAFLA-18]

The 1914 [Olympic Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Congress) was suspended due to the outbreak of [World War I](4764461.md), so the symbol and flag officially debuted at the [1920 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1920_Summer_Olympics) in [Antwerp](32149462.md), Belgium.[^cite_note-19]

The symbol's popularity and widespread use began during the lead-up to the [1936 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1936_Summer_Olympics) in Berlin. [Carl Diem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Diem), president of the organizing committee for the 1936 Summer Olympics, wanted to hold a torchbearers's ceremony in the stadium at [Delphi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delphi), the site of the famous oracle where the [Pythian Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythian_Games) were also held. For this reason, he ordered the construction of a milestone with the Olympic rings carved in the sides and that a torchbearer should carry the flame along with an escort of three others from there to Berlin. The ceremony was celebrated, but the stone was never removed. Later, American authors [Lynn and Gray Poole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynn_Poole), when visiting Delphi in the late 1950s, saw the stone and reported in their *History of the Ancient Games*[^cite_note-20] that the Olympic rings design came from ancient Greece. This has become known as "Carl Diem's Stone".[^cite_note-aldaver-21] This created a myth that the symbol had an ancient Greek origin.

The current view of the IOC is that the symbol "reinforces the idea" that the Olympic Movement is international and welcomes all countries of the world to join.[^cite_note-IOC-22] As can be read in the [Olympic Charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Charter), the Olympic symbol represents the union of the [five continents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_continents) of the world and the meeting of athletes from throughout the world at the Olympic Games. However, no continent is represented by any specific ring. The 1949–50 edition of the IOC's "Green Booklet" stated that each colour corresponded to a particular continent except for [Antarctica](18959138.md): "blue for [Europe](9239.md), yellow for [Asia](689.md), black for [Africa](5334607.md), green for [Australia](22621.md), and red for [North America](21139.md) and [South America](26769.md).[^cite_note-23] This assertion was reversed in 1951 because there was no evidence that Coubertin had intended it: "at the very most he might perhaps have admitted it afterwards".[^cite_note-24] Nevertheless, the pre–2014 logo of the [Association of National Olympic Committees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_of_National_Olympic_Committees) placed the logo of each of its five continental associations inside the ring of the corresponding colour.[^cite_note-25]

An Olympic Rings emoji was added to [WhatsApp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WhatsApp) on 24 July 2016 in version 2.16.7, it was later removed on 15 August 2016 in version 2.16.9.[^cite_note-26] It consisted of five U+25EF ◯ LARGE CIRCLE characters joined with U+200D  [ZERO WIDTH JOINER](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-width_joiner)s, forming a [joined character sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-width_joiner). This was presumably part of a temporary agreement with the International Olympic Committee.[^cite_note-Emojipedia_Blog_-_WhatsApp_Adds_Olympic_Rings_Emoji-27]

### Specific flags used

There are specific Olympic flags that are displayed by cities that will be hosting the next Olympic games. During each Olympic closing ceremony, in what is traditionally known as the Antwerp or Oslo Ceremony,[^cite_note-OCprotocol2-29] the flag is passed from the mayor of one host city to the next host, where it will then be taken to the new host city and displayed at its city hall. These special flags should not be confused with the larger Olympic flags manufactured specifically for each Games, which are flown over the stadium during the duration of the Games and then retired. As there is no specific flag for this purpose, the flags flown over the stadiums generally have subtle differences. This includes minor colour variations, and more noticeably the presence (or lack) of white outlines around each ring.

#### Antwerp flag

During the opening ceremony of the [1920 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1920_Summer_Olympics) in [Antwerp](32149462.md), Belgium, the Olympic flag with the five rings signifying the universality of the Olympic Games was raised for the first time at an Olympic Games.[^cite_note-Antwerp1920-30] At the end of the Games, the flag could not be found and a new Olympic flag had to be made for the handover ceremony to the officials of the [1924 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1924_Summer_Olympics) in Paris. Despite it being a replacement, the IOC officially calls this the "Antwerp Flag" instead of the "Paris Flag".[^cite_note-Vancouver2010Symbols-31] It was passed on to the next organising city of the [Summer Olympics](27566.md). The 1924 flag continued to be used at the Summer Olympics until the [1988 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_Summer_Olympics) in [Seoul](19159283.md) when it was retired and replaced by the Seoul flag.

In 1997, at a banquet hosted by the U.S. Olympic Committee, a reporter was interviewing [Hal Haig Prieste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hal_Haig_Prieste) who had won a bronze medal in platform diving as a member of the 1920 U.S. Olympic team. The reporter mentioned that the IOC had not been able to find out what had happened to the original Olympic flag. "I can help you with that," Prieste said, "It's in my suitcase." At the end of the Antwerp Olympics, spurred on by teammate [Duke Kahanamoku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duke_Kahanamoku), he climbed a flagpole and stole the Olympic flag. For 77 years the flag was stored away in the bottom of his suitcase. The flag was returned to the IOC by Prieste, by then 103 years old, in a special ceremony held at the 2000 Games in Sydney.[^cite_note-32] The original flag raised at the Antwerp opening ceremony was placed on display at the Olympic Museum in [Lausanne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lausanne), [Switzerland](26748.md), with a plaque thanking Prieste for donating it.[^cite_note-33] The flag returned to Antwerp in 2004 and since 2013, the year that Antwerp bore the title of European Sports Capital,[^cite_note-34] the flag was displayed in the entrance hall of Antwerp's city hall. In 2017, however, the flag was stored in the collection of the MAS due to the renovation of the town hall.

While the flag is recognized by the IOC, critics and historians note that the returned flag is not the one that was used in the 1920 opening ceremony, as the original flag was much larger than the one returned by Prieste.[^cite_note-KLennartz-16]

#### Oslo flag

The Oslo flag was presented to the IOC by the mayor of [Oslo](22309.md), Norway, during the [1952 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1952_Winter_Olympics). Between 1952 and 2014, the flag was passed to the next organising city for the [Winter Olympics](34069.md), with its last appearance in Sochi at the [2014 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Winter_Olympics). For the [2010 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Winter_Olympics), the actual Oslo flag was kept in a special box that has brass plaques from all host cities attached to the inside of the beautifully decorated lid and available for public viewing at the Vancouver City Hall.[^cite_note-35] It was replaced by the Pyeongchang flag.

#### Seoul flag



[Flag of South Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_South_Korea) alongside an Olympic Flag in [Olympic Park, Seoul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Park,_Seoul)

As a successor to the Antwerp Flag,[^cite_note-36] the Seoul flag was presented to the IOC at the [1988 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_Summer_Olympics) by the city of [Seoul](19159283.md), South Korea. It was passed on to the next organising cities of the [Summer Olympics](27566.md)[^cite_note-37] between 1988 and 2012. Its last appearance was in London at the [2012 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Summer_Olympics). It was replaced by the Rio de Janeiro flag.

#### Rio de Janeiro flag

As a successor to the Seoul Flag,[^cite_note-38] the Rio de Janeiro flag was presented to the IOC at the [2016 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Summer_Olympics) by the city of [Rio de Janeiro](25936.md), Brazil. It has since been passed to the next organising cities of the [Summer Olympics](27566.md).[^cite_note-imbo-39]

#### Pyeongchang flag

As a successor to the Oslo Flag,[^cite_note-40] the Pyeongchang flag was presented to the IOC at the [2018 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Winter_Olympics) by the city of [Pyeongchang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyeongchang), South Korea. It has since been passed to the [next organizing city](38416091.md) of the [Winter Olympics](34069.md).[^cite_note-imbo-39]

#### Singapore flag

For the inaugural [Youth Olympic Games](11315968.md), an Olympic flag was created for the junior version of the Games. The flag is similar to the Olympic flag, but has the host city and year on it and was first presented to Singapore by [IOC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IOC) president [Jacques Rogge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Rogge).[^cite_note-singapore-41][^cite_note-singapore2-42] During the closing ceremony on 26 August 2010, Singapore officials presented it to the next organising committee, [Nanjing 2014](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanjing_2014).[^cite_note-43]

#### Innsbruck flag

For the inaugural winter [Youth Olympic Games](11315968.md), an Olympic flag was presented to the IOC at the [2012 Winter Youth Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Winter_Youth_Olympics) by the city of [Innsbruck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Innsbruck), Austria.[^cite_note-\:0-44] It has since been passed on to the next organising city of the Winter Youth Olympics.[^cite_note-\:0-44]

## Flame and torch relay

Main article: [Olympic flame](74217.md)



The Olympic flame at [Athens 2004](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Summer_Olympics) during the opening ceremony.

The modern tradition of moving the Olympic flame via a relay system from Greece to the Olympic venue began with the Berlin Games in 1936. The organizers in Nazi Germany thought to use the Olympic torch relay as a propaganda tool to link ancient Greek ideals with the modern German state.[^cite_note-45] Today, the relay symbolizes peace and unity, with the International Olympic Committee overseeing its organization.[^cite_note-46] Months before the Games are held, the Olympic flame is lit on a torch, with the rays of the sun concentrated by a [parabolic reflector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabolic_reflector), at the site of the Ancient Olympics in [Olympia, Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympia,_Greece). The torch is then taken out of Greece, most often to be taken around the country or continent where the Games are held. The Olympic torch is carried by athletes, leaders, celebrities, and ordinary people alike, and at times in unusual conditions, such as being electronically transmitted via satellite for [Montreal 1976](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montreal_1976), submerged underwater without being extinguished for [Sydney 2000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2000_Summer_Olympics), or in space and at the [North Pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Pole) for [Sochi 2014](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sochi_2014). On the final day of the torch relay, the day of the [opening ceremony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Games_ceremony#Opening), the flame reaches the main stadium and is used to light a cauldron situated in a prominent part of the venue to signify the beginning of the Games.

## Medals and diplomas

Main articles: [Olympic medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_medal) and [Olympic diploma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_diploma)

Olympic medals and diplomas are awarded to the highest-finishing competitors in each event at the Olympic Games. The medals are made of [gold-plated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold-plated) silver (for the [gold medals](526488.md)), silver, or [bronze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze), and are awarded to the top three finishers in a particular event. Each medal for an Olympiad has a common design, decided upon by the organisers for the particular games. For medals awarded at Summer Games, the obverse side has an image of [Nike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nike_\(mythology\)), the Greek goddess of victory, holding a palm in her left hand and a winner's crown in her right, and the [Colosseum](49603.md) in Rome in the background. In contrast, at Winter Games the creators of the medals have complete freedom to choose their designs.

The original medal design, created by [Giuseppe Cassioli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giuseppe_Cassioli), was used from the [1928 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1928_Summer_Olympics) to the [2000 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2000_Summer_Olympics). The design underwent a drastic change for the [2004 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Summer_Olympics), when Cassioli's design was replaced by a more modern design connected to Greece. The obverse side was changed to make more explicit references to the Greek goddess and traditions. In this design, the goddess Nike flies into the grounds of the [Panathenaic Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panathenaic_Stadium), symbolizing the renewal of the Games. The new design was created by Greek jewelry designer [Elena Votsi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elena_Votsi).[^cite_note-47]

For each Summer Olympics from 2004 onwards, the reverse side, as well as the labels for each Olympiad, were changed to reflect scenic images or locations from the host city.

[Olympic diplomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_diploma) are given to competitors placing fourth, fifth, and sixth since 1949, and to competitors placing seventh and eighth since 1981.

## Anthems

Main articles: [Olympic Hymn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Hymn) and [List of Olympic songs and anthems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Olympic_songs_and_anthems)

The "[Olympic Hymn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Hymn)", officially known as the "Olympic Anthem", is played when the Olympic flag is raised. It was composed by [Spyridon Samaras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spyridon_Samaras) with words from a poem by the Greek poet and writer [Kostis Palamas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kostis_Palamas). Both the poet and the composer were the choice of [Demetrius Vikelas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demetrius_Vikelas), a Greek [Pro-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pro-European) and the first president of the IOC. The anthem was performed for the first time for the opening ceremony of the [1896 Athens Olympic Games](48644.md) but was not declared the official hymn by the IOC until 1958. In the following years, every hosting location commissioned the composition of a specific Olympic hymn for their edition of the Games until the [1960 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1960_Winter_Olympics) in Squaw Valley.

## Kotinos

Main article: [Olive wreath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olive_wreath)

The *kotinos* ([Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language): κότινος),[^cite_note-53] is an [olive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olive) branch, originally of wild olive-tree, intertwined to form a circle or a horse-shoe, introduced by [Heracles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heracles).[^cite_note-54] In the [ancient Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Olympic_Games) there were no [gold](526488.md#Olympic_Games), silver, or bronze medals. There was only one winner per event, crowned with an olive wreath made of wild olive leaves from a sacred tree near the temple of Zeus at Olympia. The victorious athletes were honored, feted, and praised. Their deeds were heralded and chronicled so that future generations could appreciate their accomplishments.

According to the Greek historian [Herodotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotus), the Persian King [Xerxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerxes_I_of_Persia) was interrogating some [Arcadians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regions_of_ancient_Greece#Arcadia) after the [Battle of Thermopylae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Thermopylae). He inquired why there were so few Greek men defending Thermopylae. The answer was "All other men are participating in the Olympic Games". When asked "What is the prize for the winner?", he was told "an olive-wreath". [Tigranes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigranes), one of his generals, replied "Good heavens! [Mardonius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mardonius_\(general\)), what kind of men are these against whom you have brought us to fight? Men who do not compete for possessions, but for honour."[^cite_note-55]

In his play [Plutus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutus_\(play\)), the Greek playwright [Aristophanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristophanes) suggested that the wreath being made of wild olive instead of gold signified that Zeus hoards his gold for himself.[^cite_note-56]

In later times, athletes were rewarded with a generous sum of money as well as the *kotinos*. The *kotinos* tradition was renewed specifically for the Athens 2004 Games, although in this case it was bestowed together with the gold medal. Apart from its use in the awards ceremonies, the *kotinos* was chosen as the [2004 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Summer_Olympics) emblem.

## Olympic salute



*The Olympic Salute* sculpted by [Gra Rueb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gra_Rueb), sculpted for the [1928 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1928_Summer_Olympics) in Amsterdam

The Olympic salute is a variant of the [Roman salute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_salute), with the right arm and hand stretched and pointing upward, the palm outward and downward, with the fingers touching. However, unlike the Roman salute, the arm is raised higher and at an angle to the right from the shoulder. The greeting is visible on the official posters of the [Paris 1924](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1924_Summer_Olympics)[^cite_note-1924poster-57] and [Berlin 1936](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1936_Summer_Olympics) games.[^cite_note-58]

The Olympic salute has fallen out of use since [World War II](32927.md) because of its resemblance to the [Nazi salute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_salute).[^cite_note-59] It was used by the French team at the opening ceremony of the [1948 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1948_Winter_Olympics).[^cite_note-60]

## Mascots

See also: [List of Olympic mascots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Olympic_mascots)

Since the [1968 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1968_Winter_Olympics) in [Grenoble](50972.md), France, the [Olympic Games](22576.md) have had a [mascot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mascot). This is usually an animal native to the area, or occasionally human figures representing the cultural heritage. The first major mascot in the Olympic Games was [Misha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misha) at the [1980 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980_Summer_Olympics) in Moscow. Misha was used extensively during the opening and closing ceremonies, as well as having a TV animated cartoon and appearing on several merchandise products. Nowadays, most of the merchandise aimed at young people focuses on the mascots, rather than the Olympic flag or organization logos.

## Intellectual property

The Olympic movement is very protective of its symbols. Many jurisdictions have given the movement exclusive trademark rights to any interlocking arrangement of five rings and usage of the word "Olympic". The rings are not eligible for copyright protection, both because of their date of creation and because five circles arranged in a pattern do not reach the [threshold of originality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threshold_of_originality) required to be copyrighted.

The movement has taken action against numerous groups alleged to have violated their trademarks, including the [Gay Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gay_Games); the [Minneapolis](6097240.md)-based band [The Hopefuls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hopefuls), formerly The Olympic Hopefuls; the Redneck Olympics or [Redneck Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redneck_Games); [Awana Clubs International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Awana), a Christian youth ministry who used the term for its competitive games; [Wizards of the Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wizards_of_the_Coast), publisher at the time of the card game *[Legend of the Five Rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legend_of_the_Five_Rings)*; and Philippine sporting good chain Olympic Village.[^cite_note-61]

In 1938, the Norwegian brewery Frydenlund patented a label for its root beer which featured the five Olympic rings. In 1952, when Norway was to host the Winter Olympics, the Olympic Committee was notified by Norway's Patent Office that it was Frydenlund that owned the rights to the rings in that country. Today, the successor company [Ringnes AS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ringnes) owns the rights to use the patented five rings on its root beer.[^cite_note-62] In addition, a few other companies have been successful in using the Olympic name, such as Olympic Paint, which has a paintbrush in the form of a torch as its logo, and the former Greek passenger carrier [Olympic Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_Airlines). The airline was, however, obliged to distinguish its logo from the Olympic rings by adding a sixth ring.[^cite_note-63]

Certain other sporting organisations and events have been granted permission by the IOC to use the word "Olympics" in their name, such as the [Special Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Olympics), an international sporting event held every four years for people with intellectual disabilities.

The IOC maintains exclusive ownership and control of the use of Olympic symbols through an international treaty and the IOC's demand for the enactment of laws in hosting locations to grant special trademark protection to Olympic symbols. In 1981, the Nairobi Treaty on the Protection of the Olympic Symbol, a treaty administered by the [World Intellectual Property Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Intellectual_Property_Organization), was signed by fifty-two states.[^cite_note-64] All fifty-two signatories to the Nairobi Treaty became obligated under the treaty to protect Olympic symbols against use for commercial purposes without authorization of the IOC. Under the Nairobi Treaty, if the IOC authorizes use of an Olympic symbol in a country that is a party to the treaty, then that country's National Olympic Committee is entitled to a portion of any revenue generated from the IOC's authorisation of use.[^cite_note-65] The Nairobi Treaty provides for a framework of international intellectual property protection of the licensure of all Olympic symbols.[^cite_note-66][^cite_note-67]

In recent years, organising committees have also demanded the passing of laws to combat [ambush marketing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambush_marketing) by non-official sponsors during the Games. This was seen with the [London Olympic Games and Paralympic Games Act 2006](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Olympic_Games_and_Paralympic_Games_Act_2006), which put heavy restrictions on using any term or imagery that could constitute an unauthorized association with the games, including mere mentioning of the host city and the year.[^cite_note-independent-180712-68][^cite_note-atalantic-130712-69]

[Bob Barney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Barney) co-authored the book *Selling the Five Rings* (2002), with Stephen Wenn and Scott Martyn, which discussed the history of corporate sponsorships and television rights for the Olympic Games.[^cite_note-70][^cite_note-71] Barney argued that the Olympic torch had been commercialised since its inception in 1936, and that sponsors of the torch relay benefit from brand awareness. In contrast, the medal [podium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Podium) ceremonies, which began in 1932, had not become commercialized since no advertising is allowed inside Olympic venues.[^cite_note-72]
