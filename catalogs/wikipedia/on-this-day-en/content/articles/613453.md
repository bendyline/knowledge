A **covert operation,** or **undercover operation** is a military or police operation involving a [covert agent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covert_agent) or troops acting under an assumed [cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cover_\(intelligence_gathering\)) to conceal the identity of the party responsible.[^cite_note-Carson2018-1]<sup>: 5–6 </sup>

Covert actions come with both benefits and risks. In terms of benefits, covert action might have operational advantages, such as precise targeting, while also enabling escalation control, and reduced political [backlash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backlash_\(sociology\)). In terms of risks, covert action might have logistical constraints, undermine messaging, create exposure risks, and lead to long-term blowback.[^cite_note-2]

## US law

Under US law, the [Central Intelligence Agency](5183633.md) (CIA) must lead covert operations unless the president finds that another agency should do so and informs Congress.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDaugherty200425-3] The CIA's authority to conduct covert action comes from the [National Security Act of 1947](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Security_Act_of_1947).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDaugherty2004-4] President [Ronald Reagan](25433.md) issued [Executive Order 12333](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_12333) titled *United States Intelligence Activities* in 1984. This order defined covert action as "special activities", both political and military, that the US Government could legally deny. The CIA was also designated as the sole authority under the 1991 [Intelligence Authorization Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence_Authorization_Act) and in [Title 50 of the United States Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title_50_of_the_United_States_Code) Section 413(e).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDaugherty2004-4][^cite_note-5] The CIA must have a "Presidential Finding" issued by the President in order to conduct these activities under the [Hughes-Ryan amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hughes%E2%80%93Ryan_Amendment) to the 1991 Intelligence Authorization Act.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDaugherty200425-3] These findings are then monitored by the oversight committees in both the [US Senate](24909346.md) and [House of Representatives](19468510.md).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDaugherty200428-6] As a result of this framework, the CIA "receives more oversight from the Congress than any other agency in the federal government", according to one author.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDaugherty200429-7] The [Special Activities Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Activities_Division) (SAD) is a division of the CIA's [Directorate of Operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directorate_of_Operations_\(CIA\)), responsible for Covert Action and "Special Activities". These special activities include covert political influence and paramilitary operations.

### Impact

According to a 2018 study by [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago) political scientist Austin Carson, covert operations may have the beneficial effect of preventing escalation of disputes into full-blown wars.[^cite_note-Carson2018-1] He argues that keeping military operations secret can limit escalation dynamics, as well as insulate leaders from domestic pressures while simultaneously allowing them communicating their interest to the adversary in keeping a war contained.[^cite_note-Carson2018-1] He finds that covert operations are frequently detected by other major powers.[^cite_note-Carson2018-1]

A 2024 study found that state denials of covert actions, even when the covert actions were obvious, could have a de-escalatory effect (compared to making the covert action public).[^cite_note-8]

## Domestic settings

To go "**undercover**" (that is, to go on an **undercover operation**) is to avoid detection by the object of one's observation, and especially to [disguise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deception) one's own identity (or use an [assumed identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assumed_identity)) for the purposes of gaining the trust of an individual or organization in order to learn or confirm confidential information, or to gain the trust of targeted individuals to gather information or [evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidence_\(law\)). Undercover operations are traditionally employed by [law enforcement agencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_enforcement_agencies) and [private investigators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_investigators); those in such roles are commonly referred to as undercover agents.

## Black operations

"Black ops" redirects here. For other uses, see [Black Ops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Ops_\(disambiguation\)).

A black operation or "black op" is a covert operation by a [government agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_agency), a [military unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_unit) or a [paramilitary organization](146730.md) in which the operation itself is at least partially hidden from the organization or government's own scrutiny.[^cite_note-Smith-9] For example, in the United States, some activities by military and [intelligence agencies](81014.md) are funded by a classified "[black budget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_budget)", of which the details, and sometimes even the total, are hidden from the public and from most congressional oversight.[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11]

A single such activity may be called a [black bag operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_bag_operation);[^cite_note-Smith-9] that term is primarily used for surreptitious entries into structures to obtain information for [human intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HUMINT) operations.[^cite_note-intelnews-12] Such operations have been carried out by the [FBI](11127.md),[^cite_note-13] [CIA](5183633.md),[^cite_note-wald-14] and the intelligence services of other states.[^cite_note-intelnews-12]

## History

Law enforcement has carried out undercover work in a variety of ways throughout the course of history, but [Eugène François Vidocq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eug%C3%A8ne_Fran%C3%A7ois_Vidocq) (1775–1857) developed the first organized (though informal) undercover program in France in the early 19th century, from the late [First Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_French_Empire) through most of the [Bourbon Restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourbon_Restoration_in_France) period of 1814 to 1830. At the end of 1811, Vidocq set up an informal plainclothes unit, the *[Brigade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brigade) de la [Sûreté](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%BBret%C3%A9)* ("Security Brigade"), which was later converted to a security police unit under the [Prefecture of Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefecture_of_Police). The Sûreté initially had eight, then twelve, and, in 1823, twenty employees. One year later, it expanded again, to 28 secret agents. In addition, there were eight people who worked secretly for the Sûreté, but instead of a salary, they received licences for gambling halls. A major portion of Vidocq's subordinates comprised ex-criminals like himself.[^cite_note-15]

Vidocq personally trained his agents, for example, in selecting the correct disguise based on the kind of job. He himself went out hunting for criminals too. His [memoirs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memoir) are full of stories about how he outsmarted crooks by pretending to be a beggar or an old [cuckold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuckold). At one point, he even simulated his own death.[^cite_note-16]

In England, the first modern [police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police) force was established in 1829 by Sir [Robert Peel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Peel) as the [Metropolitan Police](192450.md) of London. From the start, the force occasionally employed plainclothes undercover detectives, but there was much public anxiety that its powers were being used for the purpose of [political repression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_repression). In part due to these concerns, the 1845 official *Police Orders* required all undercover operations to be specifically authorized by the [superintendent](3627099.md). It was only in 1869 that Police commissioner [Edmund Henderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Henderson) established a formal plainclothes detective division.[^cite_note-17]



[Special Branch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Branch) detectives on an undercover operation at the [London Docks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Docks), 1911

The first [Special Branch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Branch) of police was the [Special Irish Branch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Irish_Branch), formed as a section of the [Criminal Investigation Department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_Investigation_Department) of the [MPS](192450.md) in London in 1883, initially to combat the bombing campaign that the [Irish Republican Brotherhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Republican_Brotherhood) had begun a few years earlier. This pioneering branch became the first to receive training in [counter-terrorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counter-terrorism) techniques.

Its name was changed to [Special Branch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Branch_\(Metropolitan_Police\)) as it had its remit gradually expanded to incorporate a general role in counter terrorism, combating foreign subversion and infiltrating [organized crime](22625.md). [Law enforcement agencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_enforcement_agency) elsewhere established similar branches.[^cite_note-18]

In the United States, a similar route was taken when the [New York City Police Department](255413.md) under [police commissioner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City_Police_Commissioner) [William McAdoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_McAdoo_\(New_Jersey\)) established the Italian Squad in 1906 to combat rampant crime and intimidation in the poor Italian neighborhoods.[^cite_note-19] Various federal agencies began their own undercover programs shortly afterwards – [Charles Joseph Bonaparte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Joseph_Bonaparte) founded the Bureau of Investigation, the forerunner of the [Federal Bureau of Investigation](11127.md), in 1908.[^cite_note-20][^cite_note-Romano2010-21]

[Secret police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secret_police) forces in the [Eastern Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Bloc) also used undercover operatives.[^cite_note-22]

## Participation in criminal activities

Undercover agents may engage in criminal activities as part of their investigation. Joh defined the term *authorized criminality* to describe this phenomenon, which she restricts primarily to undercover [law enforcement officers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_enforcement_officer), excluding [confidential informants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Informant).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJoh2009157-23] These criminal activities are primarily used to "provide opportunities for the suspect to engage in the target crime" and to maintain or bolster their cover identity. However, these crimes must be necessary to advance the investigation otherwise they may be prosecutable like any other crime.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJoh2009165-24] The FBI requires that such activities must be sanctioned and necessary for the investigation; they also stipulate that agents may not instigate criminal activity (to avoid [entrapment](216694.md)) or participate in violence except for [self-defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-defense) or the defense of others.[^cite_note-25] Most other legislation surrounding authorized criminality is not uniform and is a patchwork of federal and state laws.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJoh2009168-26]

## Risks

There are two principal problems that can affect agents working in undercover roles. The first is the maintenance of identity and the second is the reintegration back into normal duty.

Living a double life in a new environment presents many problems. Undercover work is one of the most stressful jobs a special agent can undertake.[^cite_note-27] The largest cause of stress identified is the separation of an undercover officer from friends, family and his normal environment. This simple isolation can lead to [depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depression_\(mood\)) and [anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety). There is no data on the [divorce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divorce) rates of agents, but strain on relationships does occur. This can be a result of a need for secrecy and an inability to share work problems, and the unpredictable work schedule, personality and lifestyle changes and the length of separation can all result in problems for relationships.[^cite_note-Marx,_G._1988-28]

[Stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_\(biology\)) can also result from an apparent lack of direction of the investigation or not knowing when it will end. The amount of elaborate planning, risk, and expenditure can pressure an agent to succeed, which can cause considerable stress.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-29] The stress that an undercover agent faces is considerably different from his counterparts on regular duties, whose main source of stress is the administration and bureaucracy.[^cite_note-30] As undercover agents are removed from the bureaucracy, it may result in another problem. The lack of the usual controls of a uniform, badge, constant supervision, a fixed place of work, or (often) a set assignment could, combined with their continual contact with the [organized crime](22625.md), increase the likelihood for corruption.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-29]

This stress may be instrumental in the development of [drug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substance_abuse) or [alcohol abuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcohol_abuse) in some agents. They are more prone to the development of an [addiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substance_dependence) as they suffer greater stress than other police, they are isolated, and drugs are often very accessible.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-29] Police, in general, have very high [alcoholism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcoholism) rates compared to most occupational groups, and stress is cited as a likely factor.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-29] The environment that agents work in often involves a very liberal exposure to the consumption of alcohol,[^cite_note-31] which in conjunction with the stress and isolation could result in alcoholism.

There can be some [guilt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guilt_\(emotion\)) associated with going undercover due to betraying those who have come to [trust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trust_\(social_science\)) the officer. This can cause anxiety or even, in very rare cases, sympathy with those being targeted. This is especially true with the infiltration of political groups, as often the agent will share similar characteristics with those they are infiltrating like class, age, ethnicity or religion. This could even result in the conversion of some agents.[^cite_note-Marx,_G._1988-28]

The lifestyle led by undercover agents is very different compared to other areas in law enforcement, and it can be quite difficult to reintegrate back into normal duties. Agents work their own hours, they are removed from direct supervisory monitoring, and they can ignore dress and etiquette rules.[^cite_note-32] Resettling into the normal police role requires the shedding of old habits, language and dress. After working such free lifestyles, agents may have discipline problems or exhibit neurotic responses. They may feel uncomfortable and take a cynical, suspicious or even paranoid world view and feel continually on guard.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-29]

## Plainclothes law enforcement

Not to be confused with [Plain dress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plain_dress).

Undercover agents should not be confused with law enforcement officers who wear **plainclothes**—that is, to wear [civilian clothing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mufti_\(dress\)), instead of wearing a [uniform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform), to avoid detection or identification as a law enforcement officer. However, plainclothes police officers typically carry normal police equipment and normal identification. Police detectives are assigned to wear plainclothes by not wearing the uniform typically worn by their peers. Police officers in plainclothes must identify themselves when using their police powers; however, they are not always required to identify themselves on demand and may lie about their status as a police officer in some situations (see [sting operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sting_operation)).

Sometimes, police might drive an unmarked vehicle or a vehicle which looks like a taxi.[^cite_note-33]

## Examples

Bush](3414021.md) had authorized the [Central Intelligence Agency](5183633.md) (CIA) to undertake "black operations" in [Iran](14653.md) in order to promote [regime change](154074.md) as well as to sabotage [Iran's nuclear program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran's_nuclear_program).

Bush administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Bush_administration) knew of their plans to publish the information and raised no objections.

CIA Director General [Michael Hayden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Hayden_\(general\)) explained why he released the documents, saying that they provided a "glimpse of a very different time and a very different agency".
