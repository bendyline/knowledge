# Cetacean stranding

"Beached whale" redirects here; not to be confused with [Beaked whale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaked_whale).

Photo of dozens of whales

A mass stranding of [pilot whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilot_whale) on the shore of [Cape Cod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Cod), 1902

**Cetacean stranding**, commonly known as **beaching**, is a phenomenon in which [whales](33777.md), [dolphins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolphin) and [porpoises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porpoise) strand themselves on land, usually on a [beach](40630.md). Beached whales often die due to [dehydration](155056.md), collapsing under their own weight, or drowning when high tide covers the [blowhole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blowhole_\(anatomy\)).[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-2] [Cetacean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cetacea) stranding has occurred since before [recorded history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recorded_history).[^cite_note-Álvarez-FernándezCarriol2014-3]

Several explanations for why cetaceans strand themselves have been proposed, including changes in water temperatures,[^cite_note-abc-climate-4] peculiarities of whales' [echolocation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_echolocation) in certain surroundings,[^cite_note-uwa-acoustics-5] and geomagnetic disturbances,[^cite_note-McGrath2017-6] but none have so far been universally accepted as a definitive reason for the behavior. However, a link between the mass beaching of [beaked whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaked_whale) and use of mid-frequency active [sonar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonar) has been found.[^cite_note-Commerce2000-7]

Whales that die due to stranding can subsequently decay and bloat to the point where they can [explode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exploding_whale), causing gas and their internal organs to fly out.

## Species

See also: [List of sperm whale strandings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_sperm_whale_strandings)



Beached [false killer whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_killer_whale) at [Flinders Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flinders_Bay), Western Australia, 1986

Every year, up to 2,000 animals beach themselves.[^cite_note-Martin-8] Although the majority of strandings result in death, they pose no threat to any species as a whole. Only about ten [cetacean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cetacea) species frequently display mass beachings, with ten more rarely doing so.

All frequently involved species are [toothed whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toothed_whale) (Odontoceti), rather than [baleen whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baleen_whale) (Mysticeti). These species share some characteristics which may explain why they beach.

Body size does not normally affect the frequency, but both the animals' normal habitat and social organization do appear to influence their chances of coming ashore in large numbers. Odontocetes that normally inhabit deep waters and live in large, tightly knit groups are the most susceptible. This includes the [sperm whale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sperm_whale), oceanic dolphins, [pilot whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilot_whale) and [orcas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orca), and a few [beaked whale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaked_whale) species. The most common species to strand in the United Kingdom is the [harbour porpoise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harbour_porpoise); the common dolphin (*[Delphinus delphis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delphinus_delphis)*) is second-most common, and after that long-finned pilot whales (*[Globicephala melas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globicephala_melas)*).[^cite_note-9]

Solitary species naturally do not strand en masse. Cetaceans that spend most of their time in shallow, coastal waters almost never mass strand.

## Causes

Strandings can be grouped into several types. The most obvious distinction is between single and multiple strandings. Many theories, some of them controversial, have been proposed to explain beaching, but the question remains unresolved.

Natural deaths at sea

The carcasses of deceased cetaceans are likely to float to the surface at some point; during this time, [currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_current) or [winds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind) may carry them to a [coastline](5236.md). Since thousands of cetaceans die every year, many become stranded posthumously. Offshore deaths of multiple whales are unlikely to lead to multiple strandings, since winds and currents are variable and will scatter a group of corpses. Most carcasses never reach the coast, and are [scavenged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scavenge), or [decompose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decompose) enough to sink to the ocean bottom, where the carcass forms the basis of a unique local ecosystem called a *[whale fall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whale_fall)*.

Individual strandings

Single live strandings are often the result of individual illness or injury; in the absence of human intervention these almost inevitably end in death.

Multiple strandings

Multiple strandings in one place are rare, and often attract media coverage as well as rescue efforts. The strong social cohesion of toothed whale pods appears to be a key factor in many cases of multiple stranding: If one gets into trouble, its distress calls may prompt the rest of the pod to follow and beach themselves alongside.

### Environmental



*Three Beached Whales*, a 1577 engraving by the Flemish artist [Jan Wierix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Wierix), depicts stranded sperm whales. Note the incorrectly recorded "nostril" and [death erection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_erection).



"The Whale beached between [Scheveningen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheveningen) and [Katwijk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katwijk), with elegant sightseers", by [Esaias van de Velde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esaias_van_de_Velde), c. 1617

[Whales](33777.md) have beached throughout human history, with evidence of humans salvaging from stranded [sperm whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sperm_whale) in southern Spain during the Upper [Magdalenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magdalenian) era some 14,000 years before the present.[^cite_note-Álvarez-FernándezCarriol2014-3] Some strandings can be attributed to natural and environmental factors, such as rough weather, weakness due to old age or infection, difficulty giving birth,[^cite_note-abc-mystery-10] hunting too close to shore, or navigation errors.

In 2004, scientists at the [University of Tasmania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tasmania) linked whale strandings and weather, hypothesizing that when cool [Antarctic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctic) waters rich in [squid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squid) and [fish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish) flow north, whales follow their prey closer towards land.[^cite_note-abc-climate-4] In some cases predators (such as killer whales) have been known to panic other whales, herding them towards the shoreline.[^cite_note-abc-climate-4]

Their [echolocation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_echolocation) system can have difficulty picking up very gently-sloping coastlines.[^cite_note-fatal_shore-11] This theory accounts for mass beaching hot spots such as [Ocean Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_Beach_\(Tasmania\)), Tasmania and [Geographe Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographe_Bay), Western Australia, where the slope is about half a degree (approximately 8 m \[26 ft] deep one kilometre \[0.62 mi] out to sea). The [University of Western Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Western_Australia) [Bioacoustics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioacoustics) group proposes that repeated reflections between the surface and ocean bottom in gently sloping shallow water may [attenuate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attenuate) sound so much that the echo is inaudible to the whales.[^cite_note-uwa-acoustics-5] Stirred up sand as well as long-lived microbubbles formed by rain may further exacerbate the effect.

A 2017 study by scientists from [Germany](11867.md)'s [Kiel University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiel_University) suggests that large geomagnetic disruptions of the [Earth's magnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_magnetic_field), brought on through [solar storms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_wind), could be another cause for whale beachings.[^cite_note-McGrath2017-6] The authors hypothesize that whales [navigate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigation) using the Earth's magnetic field by detecting differences in the field's strength to find their way. The solar storms cause [anomalies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_anomaly) in the field, which may disturb the whales' ability to navigate, sending them into shallow waters where they get trapped.[^cite_note-McGrath2017-6] The study is based on the mass beachings of 29 sperm whales along the coasts of Germany, the [Netherlands](21148.md), the [UK](31717.md) and [France](5843419.md) in 2016.[^cite_note-McGrath2017-6]

### "Follow-me" strandings



Mass stranding of dolphins, Nova Scotia (1918)

Some strandings may be caused by larger cetaceans following [dolphins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolphin) and [porpoises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porpoise) into shallow coastal waters. The larger animals may habituate to following faster-moving dolphins. If they encounter an adverse combination of [tidal flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_flow) and [seabed topography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seabed_topography), the larger species may become trapped.

Sometimes following a dolphin can help lead a whale out of danger: In 2008, a local dolphin was followed out to open water by two [pygmy sperm whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pygmy_sperm_whale) that had become lost behind a sandbar at [Mahia Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahia_Beach), New Zealand.[^cite_note-12]

### Orcas' intentional, temporary strandings

Pods of [killer whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killer_whale) – predators of dolphins and porpoises – very rarely strand. It might be that killer whales have learned to stay away from shallow waters, and that heading to the shallows offers the smaller animals some protection from predators. However, killer whales in [Península Valdés](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pen%C3%ADnsula_Vald%C3%A9s), Argentina, and the [Crozet Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crozet_Islands) in the Indian Ocean *have* learned how to operate in shallow waters, particularly in their pursuit of seals. The killer whales regularly demonstrate their competence by chasing seals up shelving gravel beaches, up to the edge of the water. The pursuing whales are occasionally partially thrust out of the sea by a combination of their own impetus and retreating water, and have to wait for the next wave to re-float them and carry them back to sea.[^cite_note-Baird-2002-p24-13]



A [killer whale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killer_whale) hunting sea lions at [Valdes Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valdes_Peninsula), Argentina, deliberately stranding itself in the process

In Argentina, killer whales are known to hunt on the shore by intentionally beaching themselves and then lunging at nearby seals before riding the next wave safely back into deeper waters. This was first observed in the early 1970s, then hundreds times more since within this pod. This behavior seems to be taught from one generation to the next, evidenced by older individuals nudging juveniles towards the shore, and can sometimes also be a play activity.[^cite_note-Baird-2002-p24-13][^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15]

### Sonar

See also: [Noise pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noise_pollution) and [Marine mammals and sonar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_mammals_and_sonar)

There is evidence that [active sonar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_sonar) leads to beaching. On some occasions cetaceans have stranded shortly after military sonar was active in the area, suggesting a link.[^cite_note-Commerce2000-7] Theories describing how sonar may cause whale deaths have also been advanced after [necropsies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necropsies) found internal injuries in stranded cetaceans. In contrast, some who strand themselves due to seemingly natural causes are usually healthy prior to beaching:

> The low frequency active sonar (LFA sonar) used by the military to detect submarines is the loudest sound ever put into the seas. Yet the U.S. Navy is planning to deploy LFA sonar across 80 percent of the world ocean. At an amplitude of two hundred forty decibels, it is loud enough to kill whales and dolphins and has already caused mass strandings and deaths in areas where U.S. and/or NATO forces have conducted exercises.&#x20;

— [Whitty 2007](#CITEREFWhitty2007), p. 50

#### Direct injury

The large and rapid pressure changes made by loud sonar can cause [hemorrhaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemorrhaging). Evidence emerged after 17 cetaceans were hauled out in [the Bahamas](3451.md) in March 2000 following a [United States Navy](20518076.md) sonar exercise. The Navy accepted blame agreeing that the dead whales experienced acoustically induced hemorrhages around the ears.[^cite_note-Commerce2000-7] The resulting disorientation probably led to the stranding. Ken Balcomb, a [cetologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cetologist), specializes in the [killer whale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killer_whale) populations that inhabit the [Strait of Juan de Fuca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strait_of_Juan_de_Fuca) between [Washington](13015878.md) and [Vancouver Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vancouver_Island).[^cite_note-16] He investigated these beachings and argues that the powerful sonar pulses [resonated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resonance) with airspaces in the dolphins, tearing tissue around the ears and brain.[^cite_note-17] Apparently not all species are affected by sonar.[^cite_note-18]

#### Injury at a vulnerable moment

Another means by which sonar could be hurting cetaceans is a form of [decompression sickness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decompression_sickness). This was first raised by necrological examinations of 14 [beaked whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaked_whale) stranded in the [Canary Islands](5717.md). The stranding happened on 24 September 2002, close to the operating area of Neo Tapon, an international naval exercise, about four hours after the activation of mid-frequency sonar.[^cite_note-jepson-2003-19] The team of scientists found acute tissue damage from gas-bubble lesions, which are indicative of decompression sickness.[^cite_note-jepson-2003-19]

The precise mechanism of how sonar causes bubble formation is not known. It could be due to cetaceans panicking and surfacing too rapidly in an attempt to escape the sonar pulses. There is also a theoretical basis by which sonar vibrations can cause supersaturated gas to [nucleate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleate), forming bubbles, which are responsible for decompression sickness.[^cite_note-20]

#### Diving patterns of Cuvier's beaked whales

The overwhelming majority of the cetaceans involved in sonar-associated beachings are [Cuvier's beaked whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuvier's_beaked_whale) (*Ziphius cavirostrus*). Individuals of this species strand frequently, but mass strandings are rare.

Cuvier's beaked whales are an open-ocean species that rarely approach the shore, making them difficult to study in the wild. Prior to the interest raised by the sonar controversy, most of the information about them came from stranded animals. The first to publish research linking beachings with naval activity were Simmonds and Lopez-Jurado in 1991. They noted that over the past decade there had been a number of mass strandings of beaked whales in the Canary Islands, and each time the [Spanish Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Navy) was conducting exercises. Conversely, there were no mass strandings at other times. They did not propose a theory for the strandings. Fernández *et al.* in a 2013 letter to *[Nature](43427.md)* reported that there had been no further mass strandings in that area, following a 2004 ban by the Spanish government on military exercises in that region.[^cite_note-21]

In May 1996, there was another mass stranding in West [Peloponnese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peloponnese), Greece. At the time, it was noted as "atypical" both because mass strandings of beaked whales are rare, and also because the stranded whales were spread over such a long stretch of coast, with each individual whale spatially separated from the next stranding. At the time of the incident, there was no connection made with active sonar; A. Frantzis, the marine biologist investigating the incident, made the connection to sonar because he discovered a notice to mariners concerning the test. His report was published in March 1998.[^cite_note-22]

Peter Tyack, of [Woods Hole Oceanographic Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woods_Hole_Oceanographic_Institute), has been researching noise's effects on marine mammals since the 1970s. He has led much of the recent research on beaked whales (Cuvier's beaked whales in particular). Data tags have shown that Cuvier's dive considerably deeper than previously thought, and are in fact the deepest-diving species of marine mammal yet known.

At shallow depths Cuvier's stop vocalizing, either because of fear of predators, or because they don't need vocalization to track each other at shallow depths, where they have light adequate to see each other.

Their surfacing behavior is highly unusual, because they exert considerable physical effort to surface by a controlled ascent, rather than passively floating to the surface as [sperm whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sperm_whale) do. Every deep dive is followed by three or four shallow dives. The elaborate dive patterns are assumed to be necessary to control the diffusion of gases in the bloodstream. No data show a beaked whale making an uncontrolled ascent, or failing to do successive shallow dives. This behavior suggests that the Cuvier's are in a vulnerable state after a deep dive – presumably on the verge of [decompression sickness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decompression_sickness#Other_animals) – and require time and perhaps the [shallower dives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decompression_sickness#Treatment) to recover.

#### Summary review

De Quirós *et al.* (2019)[^cite_note-de-Quirós-etal-2019-01-30-23] published a review of evidence on the mass strandings of beaked whale linked to naval exercises where sonar was used. It concluded that the effects of mid-frequency active sonar are strongest on Cuvier's beaked whales but vary among individuals or populations. The review suggested the strength of response of individual animals may depend on whether they had prior exposure to sonar, and that symptoms of decompression sickness have been found in stranded whales that may be a result of such response to sonar. It noted that no more mass strandings had occurred in the Canary Islands once naval exercises where sonar was used were banned, and recommended that the ban be extended to other areas where mass strandings continue to occur.[^cite_note-de-Quirós-etal-2019-01-30-23][^cite_note-24]

The first detailed ecological study of the decomposition of a stranded whale was carried out with a [minke whale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minke_whale) (*Balaenoptera acutorostrata*) beached on the Dutch island of [Rottumerplaat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rottumerplaat) on 25 November 2020. The carcass was examined at regular intervals over the following two years, recording the scavengers and other species that used it.[^cite_note-25]

## Rescue



Beached whales being kept wet, [New Zealand](4913064.md)



Whale carcass, [Olympic National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic_National_Park), U.S.

Many stranded whales have been rescued; methods include pouring water onto the whale to keep it cool and its skin wet while waiting for a high tide.[^cite_note-26] Whales are large, making rescue difficult without training; wildlife charities like [British Divers Marine Life Rescue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Divers_Marine_Life_Rescue) have run training courses for local volunteers in some areas where beaching is common.[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28]

## Disposal

See also: [Exploding whale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exploding_whale) and [Whale fall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whale_fall)



A [beachcomber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beachcombing) inspects the carcass of a whale. The bite marks on the whale were made by a [great white shark](43619.md).



Memorial to beached whales outside [Florence, Oregon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence,_Oregon)

If a whale is beached near an inhabited locality, the rotting carcass can pose a nuisance as well as a health risk. Such very large carcasses are difficult to move. The whales are often towed back out to sea away from shipping lanes, allowing them to decompose naturally, or they are towed out to sea and blown up with explosives. Government-sanctioned explosions have occurred in South Africa, Iceland, Australia and United States.[^cite_note-29][^cite_note-mbl-30][^cite_note-ABC1-31] If the carcass is older, it is buried.

In New Zealand, the site of many whale strandings, treaties with the indigenous [Māori people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ori_people) allow the tribal gathering and traditional use of [whalebone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whalebone) from any animal which has died as a result of stranding. Whales are regarded as *[taonga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taonga)* (spiritual treasures), descendants of the ocean god [Tangaroa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangaroa), and are as such held in very high respect. Sites of whale strandings and any whale carcasses from strandings are treated as *[tapu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tapu_\(Polynesian_culture\))* sites or sacred ground. Stranded whales are disposed of in consultation with local *[iwi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iwi)* (tribes), scientists, and the [Department of Conservation](368208.md).[^cite_note-32]

## Health risks

A beached whale carcass should not be consumed. In 2002, fourteen Alaskans ate *[muktuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muktuk)* (whale blubber) from a beached whale, resulting in eight of them developing [botulism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botulism), with two of the affected requiring [mechanical ventilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_ventilation).[^cite_note-33] This is a possibility for any meat taken from an unpreserved carcass.

### Others

On June 23, 2015, 337 dead whales were discovered in a remote [fjord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiord) in [Patagonia](65433.md), southern [Chile](5489.md), the largest stranding of [baleen whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baleen_whale) to date.[^cite_note-53] Three hundred and five bodies and 32 skeletons were identified by aerial and satellite photography between the [Gulf of Penas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Penas) and [Puerto Natales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puerto_Natales), near the southern tip of [South America](26769.md). They may have been [sei whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sei_whale).[^cite_note-54] This is one of only two or three such baleen mass stranding events in the last hundred years. It is highly unusual for baleen whales to strand other than singly, and these Patagonia strandings are tentatively attributed to an unusual cause such as ingestion of poisonous algae.

In November 2018, over 140 whales were witnessed stranded on a remote beach in New Zealand and had to be euthanised because of their declining health condition.[^cite_note-55] In July 2019, nearly 50 long-finned pilot whales were found stranded on Snaefellsnes Peninsula in Iceland. However, they were already dead when spotted.[^cite_note-56]

On the evening of November 2, 2020, over 100 short-finned pilot whales were stranded on the [Panadura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panadura) Beach in western coast of [Sri Lanka](26750.md).[^cite_note-57] Four deaths were reported, and all other whales were rescued.[^cite_note-58]
