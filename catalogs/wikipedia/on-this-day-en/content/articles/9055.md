**Derry**,[^cite_note-8] officially **Londonderry**,[^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10] is a [cathedral city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_status_in_the_United_Kingdom) in [Northern Ireland](21265.md). Located in [County Londonderry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Londonderry), the city now covers both banks of the [River Foyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Foyle). Cityside and the old [walled city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walled_city) are on the west bank and [Waterside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterside,_Derry) is on the east, with two road bridges and one footbridge crossing the river in between.

The population of the city was 85,279 in the 2021 census,[^cite_note-11] making it the [second-largest city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_localities_in_Northern_Ireland_by_population) in Northern Ireland after [Belfast](5046.md).[^cite_note-12] The [Derry Urban Area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derry_Urban_Area) had a population of 105,066 in 2011.[^cite_note-13] The district administered by Derry City and Strabane District Council contains both [Londonderry Port](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Londonderry_Port) and [City of Derry Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Derry_Airport). Derry is close to the [border](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_border) with [County Donegal](45576.md), with which it has had a close link for many centuries. The person traditionally seen as the founder of the original Derry is Saint [Colmcille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columba), a holy man from [Tír Chonaill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C3%ADr_Chonaill), the old name for almost all of modern County Donegal, of which the west bank of the Foyle was part before 1610.[^cite_note-Lacy_1983_p._1-14]

In 2013, Derry was the inaugural [UK City of Culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_City_of_Culture), having been awarded the title in 2010.[^cite_note-Derry_wins_City_of_Culture-15][^cite_note-DCS_–_UK_City_of_Culture-16]

## Name

Main article: [Derry/Londonderry name dispute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derry/Londonderry_name_dispute)



Road sign in Northern Ireland with the reference to *London* obscured



Road signs in the [Republic of Ireland](14560.md) use *Derry* and the Irish *Doire*.

Despite the official name, the city is commonly known as *Derry*,[^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18] which is an [anglicisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglicisation) of the Irish *Daire* or *Doire*, which translates as '[oak](39696.md)-grove/oak-wood'. The name derives from the settlement's earliest references, *Daire Calgaich* ('oak-grove of Calgach').[^cite_note-19][^cite_note-20][^cite_note-21] The name was changed from Derry in 1613 during the [Plantation of Ulster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plantation_of_Ulster) to reflect the establishment of the city by the [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_London) [guilds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Livery_company).[^cite_note-22][^cite_note-Curl-23]

*Derry* has been used in the names of the [local government district and council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_government_in_Northern_Ireland) since 1984, when the council changed its name from *Londonderry City Council* to *Derry City Council*.[^cite_note-bbc-2006-court-24] This also changed the name of the district, which had been created in 1973 and included both the city and surrounding rural areas. In the [2015 local government reform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reform_of_local_government_in_Northern_Ireland), the district was merged with the Strabane district to form the [Derry City and Strabane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derry_City_and_Strabane) district, with the councils likewise merged.

According to the city's [royal charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_charter) of 10 April 1662, the official name is *Londonderry*. This was reaffirmed in a [High Court decision in 2007](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derry_City_Council,_Re_Application_for_Judicial_Review).[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26]

The 2007 court case arose because Derry City Council wanted clarification on whether the 1984 name change of the council and district had changed the official name of the city and what the procedure would be to effect a name change.[^cite_note-bbc-2006-court-24] The court clarified that Londonderry remained the official name and that the correct procedure to change the name would be via a petition to the [Privy Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privy_Council).[^cite_note-27] Derry City Council afterwards began this process and was involved in conducting an [equality impact assessment report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derry-Londonderry_name_dispute#Equality_impact_assessment) (EQIA).[^cite_note-28] Firstly it held an opinion poll of district residents in 2009, which reported that 75% of Catholics and 77% of Nationalists found the proposed change acceptable, compared to 6% of Protestants and 8% of Unionists.[^cite_note-29] The EQIA then held two consultative forums and solicited comments from the general public on whether or not the city should have its name changed to Derry.[^cite_note-TCR-30] A total of 12,136 comments were received, of which 3,108 were broadly in favour of the proposal, and 9,028 opposed it.[^cite_note-TCR-30] On 23 July 2015, the council voted in favour of a motion to change the official name of the city to Derry and to write to [Mark H. Durkan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_H._Durkan), the Northern Irish [Minister for the Environment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_the_Environment_\(Northern_Ireland\)), to ask how the change could be effected.[^cite_note-31]

The name *Derry* is preferred by [nationalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_nationalism) and it is broadly used throughout Northern Ireland's Catholic community,[^cite_note-CainName-32] as well as that of the Republic of Ireland, whereas many [unionists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unionists_\(Ireland\)) prefer *Londonderry*;[^cite_note-Kuusisto-Arponen-33] however, in everyday conversation *Derry* is used by most Protestant residents of the city.[^cite_note-34] Linguist Kevin McCafferty argues that "It is not, strictly speaking, correct that Northern Ireland Catholics call it Derry, while Protestants use the Londonderry form, although this pattern has become more common locally since the mid-1980s, when the city council changed its name by dropping the prefix". In McCafferty's survey of language use in the city, "only very few interviewees—all Protestants—use the official form".[^cite_note-McCafferty-35]



Unionist graffiti c. 1920 showing the short version of the name.

Apart from the name of the local council, the city is usually[^cite_note-CainName-32] known as *Londonderry* in official use within the UK. In the Republic of Ireland, the city and county are almost always referred to as *Derry*, on maps, in the media and in conversation.[^cite_note-36] In April 2009, however, the Republic of Ireland's Minister for Foreign Affairs, [Micheál Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miche%C3%A1l_Martin), announced that Irish passport holders who were born there could record either *Derry* or *Londonderry* as their place of birth.[^cite_note-37] Whereas official road signs in the Republic use the name *Derry*, those in Northern Ireland bear *Londonderry* (sometimes abbreviated to *L'derry*), although some of these have been defaced with the reference to London obscured.[^cite_note-38] Usage varies among local organisations, with both names being used. Examples are [City of Derry Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Derry_Airport), [City of Derry Rugby Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Derry_Rugby_Club), [Derry City FC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derry_City_FC) and the Protestant [Apprentice Boys of Derry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apprentice_Boys_of_Derry), as opposed to [Londonderry Port](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Londonderry_Port), Londonderry YMCA Rugby Club and Londonderry Chamber of Commerce.[^cite_note-39] The bishopric has always remained that of Derry, both in the (Protestant, formerly-established) [Church of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Ireland) (now combined with the bishopric of Raphoe) and in the Roman Catholic Church. Most companies within the city choose local area names such as Pennyburn, Rosemount or *Foyle* from the [River Foyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Foyle) to avoid alienating the other community. [Derry\~Londonderry railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derry_~_Londonderry_railway_station) is often referred to as Waterside railway station within the city, but is called Derry/Londonderry at other stations. The council changed the name of the local government district covering the city to Derry on 7 May 1984, consequently renaming itself Derry City Council.[^cite_note-40] This did not change the name of the city, although the city is coterminous with the district, and in law the city council is also the *Corporation of Londonderry* or, more formally, the *Mayor, Aldermen and Citizens of the City of Londonderry*.[^cite_note-41] The form *Londonderry* is used for the [post town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post_town) by the [Royal Mail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Mail);[^cite_note-McCafferty-35] however, use of *Derry* will still ensure delivery.

The city is also nicknamed "the Maiden City" by virtue of the fact that its walls were never breached despite being besieged on three separate occasions in the 17th century, the most notable being the [Siege of Derry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Derry) of 1688–1689.[^cite_note-42] It was also nicknamed "[Stroke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slash_\(punctuation\)) City" by local broadcaster [Gerry Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerry_Anderson_\(broadcaster\)), owing to the use by some of the dual name *Derry/Londonderry*[^cite_note-CainName-32] (which has itself been used by [BBC Television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Television)).[^cite_note-43] A later addition to the landscape has been the erection of several large stone columns on main roads into the city welcoming drivers, euphemistically, to 'the Walled City'.[^cite_note-44]

*Derry* is a common place name in Ireland, with at least six towns bearing that name and at least a further 79 places. The word *Derry* often forms part of the place name, for example, Derrybeg, Derryboy, Derrylea and Derrymore.

[Londonderry, Yorkshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Londonderry,_Yorkshire), near the [Yorkshire Dales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yorkshire_Dales), was named for the [Marquesses of Londonderry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marquess_of_Londonderry),[^cite_note-45] as is [Londonderry Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Londonderry_Island) off [Tierra del Fuego](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tierra_del_Fuego) in Chile.[^cite_note-46] In the United States, twin towns in [New Hampshire](21134.md) called [Derry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derry,_New_Hampshire) and [Londonderry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Londonderry,_New_Hampshire) lie about 75 miles from [Londonderry, Vermont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Londonderry,_Vermont), with additional namesakes in [Derry, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derry,_Pennsylvania), [Londonderry, Ohio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Londonderry,_Guernsey_County,_Ohio), and in Canada [Londonderry, Nova Scotia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Londonderry,_Nova_Scotia) and [Londonderry, Edmonton, Alberta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Londonderry,_Edmonton,_Alberta). There is also [Londonderry, New South Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Londonderry,_New_South_Wales), and the associated [Londonderry electorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_district_of_Londonderry).[^cite_note-47]

## City walls

Main article: [Derry city walls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derry_city_walls)



A portion of the city walls of Derry



Bishops Street Gate

Derry is the only remaining city in Ireland with completely intact city walls, some of the finest in Europe[^cite_note-48][^cite_note-49][^cite_note-50] The walls constitute the largest monument in State care in Northern Ireland and, as part of the last walled city to be built in Europe, stand as the most complete and spectacular.[^cite_note-51]

The Walls were built in 1613–1619 by [The Honourable The Irish Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Honourable_The_Irish_Society) as defences for early 17th-century settlers from England and Scotland. The Walls, which are approximately one mile (1.5 kilometres) in circumference and which vary in height and width between 3.7 and 10.7 metres (12 and 35 feet), are completely intact and form a walkway around the inner city. They provide a unique promenade to view the layout of the original town which still preserves its Renaissance-style street plan. The four original gates to the Walled City are Bishop's Gate, Ferryquay Gate, Butcher Gate and Shipquay Gate. Three further gates were added later, Magazine Gate, Castle Gate and New Gate, making seven gates in total. The architect was [Peter Benson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Benson_\(architect\)), a London-born builder, who was rewarded with several grants of land.

It is one of the few cities in Europe that never saw its fortifications breached, withstanding several sieges, including the famous [Siege of Derry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Derry) in 1689 which lasted 105 days; hence the city's nickname, *The Maiden City*.[^cite_note-52]

## History

Main article: [History of Derry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Derry)



[St Columb's Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Columb's_Cathedral)

### Early history

Derry is one of the oldest continuously inhabited places in Ireland.[^cite_note-Lacey-53] The earliest historical references date to the 6th century when a [monastery](45856.md) was founded there by St [Columba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columba) or Colmcille, a famous saint from what is now [County Donegal](45576.md), but for thousands of years before that people had been living in the vicinity.

Before leaving Ireland to spread Christianity elsewhere, Colmcille founded a monastery at Derry (which was then called *Doire Calgach*), on the west bank of the Foyle. According to oral and documented history, the site was granted to Colmcille by a local king.[^cite_note-54] The monastery then remained in the hands of the federation of Columban churches who regarded Colmcille as their spiritual mentor. The year 546 is often referred to as the date that the original settlement was founded. However, it is now accepted by historians that this was an erroneous date assigned by medieval chroniclers.[^cite_note-Lacey-53] It is accepted that between the 6th century and the 11th century, Derry was known primarily as a monastic settlement.[^cite_note-Lacey-53]

The town became strategically more significant during the [Tudor conquest of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tudor_conquest_of_Ireland) and came under frequent attack. During [O'Doherty's Rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O'Doherty's_Rebellion) in 1608 it was attacked by Sir [Cahir O'Doherty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cahir_O'Doherty), Irish chieftain of [Inishowen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inishowen), who [burnt much of the town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burning_of_Derry) and killed the governor [George Paulet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Paulet_\(1553%E2%80%931608\)).[^cite_note-55] The soldier and statesman [Sir Henry Docwra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Docwra,_1st_Baron_Docwra_of_Culmore) made vigorous efforts to develop the town, earning the reputation of being "the founder of Derry"; but he was accused of failing to prevent the O'Doherty attack and returned to England.

### Plantation

What became the City of Derry was part of the relatively new [County Donegal](45576.md) up until 1610.[^cite_note-Lacy_1983_p._1-14] In that year, the west bank of the future city was transferred by the [English Crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Crown) to [The Honourable The Irish Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Honourable_The_Irish_Society)[^cite_note-Lacy_1983_p._1-14] and was combined with [County Coleraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Coleraine), part of [County Antrim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Antrim) and a large portion of [County Tyrone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Tyrone) to form [County Londonderry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Londonderry). Planters organised by London [livery companies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Livery_companies) through The Honourable The Irish Society arrived in the 17th century as part of the [Plantation of Ulster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plantation_of_Ulster) and rebuilt the town with high walls to defend it from Irish insurgents who opposed the plantation. The aim was to settle Ulster with a population supportive of the Crown.[^cite_note-Curl-23] It was then renamed "Londonderry".

This city was the first [planned city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planned_city) in Ireland: it was begun in 1613, with the walls being completed in 1619, at a cost of £10,757.[^cite_note-56] The central diamond within a walled city with four gates was thought to be a good design for defence. The grid pattern chosen was subsequently much copied in the colonies of British North America.[^cite_note-57] The charter initially defined the city as extending three [Irish miles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_mile) (about 6.1 km) from the centre.

The modern city preserves the 17th-century layout of four main streets radiating from a central Diamond to four gateways  – Bishop's Gate, Ferryquay Gate, Shipquay Gate and Butcher's Gate. The city's oldest surviving building was also constructed at this time: the 1633 Plantation Gothic [cathedral of St Columb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Columb's_Cathedral). In the porch of the cathedral is a stone that records completion with the inscription: "If stones could speake, then London's prayse should sound, Who built this church and cittie from the grounde."[^cite_note-58]

### 17th-century upheavals

During the 1640s, the city suffered in the [Wars of the Three Kingdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wars_of_the_Three_Kingdoms), which began with the [Irish Rebellion of 1641](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Rebellion_of_1641), when the Gaelic Irish insurgents made a failed attack on the city. In 1649 the city and its garrison, which supported the republican [Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_England) in London, were besieged by Scottish [Presbyterian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyterian) forces loyal to King [Charles I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_I_of_England). The Parliamentarians besieged in Derry were relieved by a strange alliance of [Roundhead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roundhead) troops under [George Monck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Monck) and the Irish Catholic general [Owen Roe O'Neill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Owen_Roe_O'Neill). These temporary allies were soon fighting each other again however, after the landing in Ireland of the [New Model Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Model_Army) in 1649. The war in Ulster was finally brought to an end when the Parliamentarians crushed the Irish Catholic Ulster army at the [Battle of Scarrifholis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Scarrifholis), near [Letterkenny](620333.md) in nearby [County Donegal](45576.md), in 1650.

During the [Glorious Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glorious_Revolution), only Derry and nearby [Enniskillen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enniskillen) had a Protestant garrison by November 1688. An army of around 1,200 men, mostly "*Redshanks*" ([Highlanders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Highlands)), under [Alexander MacDonnell, 3rd Earl of Antrim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_MacDonnell,_3rd_Earl_of_Antrim), was slowly organised (they set out on the week William of Orange landed in England). When they arrived on 7 December 1688 the gates were closed against them and the [Siege of Derry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Derry) began. In April 1689, King James came to the city and summoned it to surrender. The King was rebuffed and the siege lasted until the end of July with the arrival of a relief ship.

### 18th and 19th centuries



Map of County Londonderry, 1837

The city was rebuilt in the 18th century with many of its fine [Georgian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_architecture) style houses still surviving. The city's first bridge across the River Foyle was built in 1790. During the 18th and 19th centuries, the port became an important embarkation point for Irish emigrants setting out for North America.

Also during the 19th century, it became a destination for migrants fleeing areas more severely affected by the [Great Famine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Famine_\(Ireland\)).[^cite_note-Johnson-59][^cite_note-60] One of the most notable shipping lines was the [McCorkell Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McCorkell_Line) operated by Wm. McCorkell & Co. Ltd. from 1778.[^cite_note-McCorkell-61] The McCorkell's most famous ship was the *Minnehaha*, which was known as the "Green Yacht from Derry".[^cite_note-McCorkell-61]

### Early 20th century

#### World War I

During [World War I](4764461.md), the city contributed over 5,000 men to the [British Army](4887.md) from both Catholic and Protestant families.

#### Partition



The war memorial in The Diamond, erected 1927

[^cite_note-62]

During the [Irish War of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_War_of_Independence), the area was rocked by sectarian violence, partly prompted by the guerilla war raging between the [Irish Republican Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Republican_Army_\(1917%E2%80%9322\)) and British forces, but also influenced by economic and social pressures. By mid-1920 there was severe sectarian rioting in the city.[^cite_note-63][^cite_note-64] Many people died and in addition, many Catholics and Protestants were expelled from their homes during this communal unrest. After a week's violence, a truce was negotiated by local politicians on both unionist and republican sides. (See: [The Troubles in Ulster (1920–1922)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Troubles_in_Ulster_\(1920%E2%80%931922\))).

In 1921, following the [Anglo-Irish Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Irish_Treaty) and the [Partition of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_of_Ireland), it unexpectedly became a 'border city', separated from much of its traditional economic hinterland in [County Donegal](45576.md).

#### World War II

During [World War II](32927.md), the city played an important part in the [Battle of the Atlantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Atlantic).[^cite_note-65] Ships from the [Royal Navy](26061.md), the [Royal Canadian Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Canadian_Navy) and other Allied navies were stationed in the city and the United States military established a base. Over 20,000 [Royal Navy](26061.md), 10,000 [Royal Canadian Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Canadian_Navy) and 6,000 [United States Navy](20518076.md) personnel were stationed in the city during the war.[^cite_note-66] The establishment of the American presence in the city was the result of a secret agreement between the Americans and the British before the Americans entered the war.[^cite_note-culturenorthernireland1-67][^cite_note-68] It was the first American naval base in Europe and the terminal for American convoys en route to Europe.

The reason for such a high degree of military and naval activity was self-evident: Derry was the United Kingdom's westernmost port; indeed, the city was the westernmost Allied port in Europe: thus, Derry was a crucial jumping-off point, together with Glasgow and Liverpool, for the shipping convoys that ran between Europe and North America. The large numbers of military personnel in Derry substantially altered the character of the city, bringing in some outside colour to the local area, as well as some cosmopolitan and economic buoyancy during these years. Several airfields were built in the outlying regions of the city at this time, Maydown, Eglinton and Ballykelly. RAF Eglinton went on to become [City of Derry Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Derry_Airport).

The city contributed a significant number of men to the war effort throughout the services, most notably the 500 men in the 9th (Londonderry) Heavy Anti-Aircraft Regiment, known as the 'Derry Boys'. This regiment served in [North Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Africa_campaign), the Sudan, Italy and mainland UK. Many others served in the Merchant Navy taking part in the convoys that supplied the UK and Russia during the war.

The border location of the city and the influx of trade from the military convoys allowed for significant smuggling operations to develop in the city.

At the conclusion of the Second World War, eventually some 60 U-boats of the German [Kriegsmarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kriegsmarine) ended in the city's harbour at [Lisahally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisahally) after their surrender.[^cite_note-69] The initial surrender was attended by Admiral Sir [Max Horton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Kennedy_Horton), Commander-in-Chief of the [Western Approaches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Approaches), and Sir [Basil Brooke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basil_Brooke,_1st_Viscount_Brookeborough), third [Prime Minister of Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_Northern_Ireland).[^cite_note-culturenorthernireland1-67]

### Late 20th century

#### 1950s and 1960s

The city languished after the Second World War, with employment and development stagnating. A large campaign, led by the [University for Derry Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_for_Derry_Committee), to have Northern Ireland's second university located in the city ended in failure.

#### The civil rights movement

Derry was a focal point for the nascent civil rights movement in Northern Ireland.



Bogside area viewed from the walls

Catholics were discriminated against under Unionist government in Northern Ireland, both politically and economically.[^cite_note-70][^cite_note-71][^cite_note-72][^cite_note-DiscrimChron-73] In the late 1960s the city became the flashpoint of disputes about institutional [gerrymandering](12987.md). [Political scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_science) [John Whyte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Henry_Whyte) explains that:[^cite_note-74]

> &#x20;All the accusations of gerrymandering, practically all the complaints about housing and regional policy and a disproportionate amount of the charges about public and private employment come from this area. The area – which consisted of Counties Tyrone and Fermanagh, Londonderry County Borough and portions of Counties Londonderry and Armagh – had less than a quarter of the total population of Northern Ireland yet generated not far short of three-quarters of the complaints of discrimination...The unionist government must bear its share of responsibility. It put through the original gerrymander, which underpinned so many of the subsequent malpractices, and then, despite repeated protests, did nothing to stop those malpractices continuing. The most serious charge against the Northern Ireland government is not that it was directly responsible for widespread discrimination, but that it allowed discrimination on such a scale over a substantial segment of Northern Ireland.

A [civil rights](37071.md) demonstration in 1968 led by the [Northern Ireland Civil Rights Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Civil_Rights_Association) was banned by the Government and blocked using force by the [Royal Ulster Constabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Ulster_Constabulary).[^cite_note-DiscrimChron-73] The events that followed the August 1969 [Apprentice Boys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apprentice_Boys) parade resulted in the [Battle of the Bogside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Bogside), when Catholic rioters fought the police, leading to widespread civil disorder in Northern Ireland and is often dated as the starting point of [the Troubles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Troubles).

On Sunday 30 January 1972, 13 unarmed civilians were shot dead by British paratroopers during a civil rights march in the [Bogside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogside) area. Another 13 were wounded and one further man later died of his wounds. This event came to be known as [Bloody Sunday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloody_Sunday_\(Northern_Ireland_1972\)).

### The Troubles

Main article: [The Troubles in Derry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Troubles_in_Derry)



"[Free Derry Corner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Derry_Corner)" at the corner of Lecky Road and Fahan Street in the Bogside. The slogan was first painted in January 1969 by John Casey.

The conflict which became known as [the Troubles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Troubles) is widely regarded as having started in Derry with the Battle of the Bogside. The Civil Rights Movement had also been very active in the city. In the early 1970s, the city was heavily militarised and there was widespread civil unrest. Several districts in the city constructed barricades to control access and prevent the forces of the state from entering.

Violence eased towards the end of the Troubles in the late 1980s and early 1990s. Irish journalist [Ed Maloney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ed_Moloney) claims in *The Secret History of the IRA* that republican leaders there negotiated a *de facto* ceasefire in the city as early as 1991. Whether this is true or not, the city did see less bloodshed by this time than [Belfast](5046.md) or other localities.

The city was visited by an [orca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orca) in November 1977 at the height of the Troubles; it was dubbed Dopey Dick by the thousands who came from miles around to see him.[^cite_note-75]

## Governance



City Council Chamber for the city of Derry, taken in July of 2017

From 1613 the city was governed by the Londonderry Corporation. In 1898, this became Londonderry County Borough Council, until 1969 when administration passed to the unelected Londonderry Development Commission. In 1973 a new district council with boundaries extending to the rural south-west was established under the name Londonderry City Council, renamed in 1984 to [Derry City Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derry_City_Council), consisting of five electoral areas: Cityside, Northland, Rural, [Shantallow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shantallow) and [Waterside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterside,_Derry). The council of 30 members was re-elected every four years. The council merged with [Strabane District Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strabane_District_Council) in April 2015 under local government reorganisation to become [Derry and Strabane District Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derry_and_Strabane_District_Council).

### Coat of arms and motto



Derry's arms on an old fire station

The devices on the city's arms are a skeleton and a three-towered castle on a black field, with the "[chief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_\(heraldry\))" or top third of the shield showing the arms of the City of London: a red cross and sword on white. In the centre of the cross is a gold harp.[^cite_note-76][^cite_note-LP2003-77] In unofficial use the harp sometimes appears above the arms as a [crest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crest_\(heraldry\)).[^cite_note-vinycomb-78]

The arms were confirmed by Daniel Molyneux, the [Ulster King of Arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_King_of_Arms), in 1613, following the town's incorporation.[^cite_note-vinycomb-78] Molyneux's notes state that the original arms of Derry were "the picture of death (or a skeleton) sitting on a mossie ston and in the dexter point a castle". To this design he added, at the request of the new mayor, "a chief, the armes of London". Molyneux goes on to state that the skeleton is symbolic of Derry's ruin at the hands of the Irish rebel [Cahir O'Doherty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cahir_O'Doherty) and that the silver castle represents its renewal through the efforts of the London guilds: "\[Derry] hath since bene (as it were) raysed from the dead by the worthy undertakinge of the Ho'ble Cittie of London, in memorie whereof it is hence forth called and knowen by the name of London Derrie."[^cite_note-vinycomb-78]

Local legend offers different theories as to the origin of the skeleton. One identifies it as [Walter de Burgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Liath_de_Burgh), who was starved to death in the [Earl of Ulster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Donn_de_Burgh,_3rd_Earl_of_Ulster)'s dungeons in 1332.[^cite_note-79] Another identifies it as Cahir O'Doherty himself, who was killed in a skirmish near [Kilmacrennan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilmacrennan) in 1608 (but was popularly believed to have wasted away while sequestered in his castle at [Buncrana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buncrana)).[^cite_note-vinycomb-78] In the days of gerrymandering and anti-Catholic discrimination, Derry's Catholics often claimed in dark wit that the skeleton was a Catholic waiting for a job and a council house.[^cite_note-Lacey-53] However, a report commissioned by the city council in 1979 established that there was no basis for any of the popular theories and that the skeleton "\[is] purely symbolic and does not refer to any identifiable person".[^cite_note-80]

The 1613 arms depicted a harp in the centre of the cross, but this was omitted from later depictions of the city arms, and in the 1952 [letters patent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letters_patent) confirming the arms to the Londonderry Corporation.[^cite_note-81] In 2002 Derry City Council applied to the College of Arms to have the harp restored. [Garter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garter_Principal_King_of_Arms) and [Norroy & Ulster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norroy_and_Ulster_King_of_Arms) Kings of Arms issued letters patent to that effect in 2003, having accepted the 17th-century evidence.[^cite_note-LP2003-77]

The motto attached to the coat of arms reads in Latin, "Vita, Veritas, Victoria". This translates into English as "Life, Truth, Victory".[^cite_note-Lacey-53]

## Geography



Derry map provided by [OpenStreetMap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenStreetMap)



The [Craigavon Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Craigavon_Bridge)

Derry is characterised by its distinctively hilly topography.[^cite_note-82] The [River Foyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Foyle) forms a deep valley as it flows through the city, giving it a steep topography. The original [walled city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derry_city_walls) of Londonderry lies on a hill on the west bank of the River Foyle. In the past, the river branched and enclosed this hill as an island; over the centuries, however, the western branch of the river dried up and became a low-lying and boggy district that is now called the Bogside.[^cite_note-83]

Today, modern Derry extends considerably north and west of the city walls and east of the river. The half of the city on the west of the Foyle is known as the Cityside and the area east is called the [Waterside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterside,_Derry). The Cityside and Waterside are connected by the [Craigavon Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Craigavon_Bridge) and [Foyle Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foyle_Bridge) and by a footbridge in the centre of the city called [Peace Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_Bridge_\(Foyle\)). The district also extends into rural areas to the southeast of the city.

This much larger city, however, remains characterised by the often extremely steep hills that form much of its terrain on both sides of the river. A notable exception to this lies on the northeastern edge of the city, on the shores of [Lough Foyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lough_Foyle), where large expanses of sea and mudflats were reclaimed in the middle of the 19th century. Today, these [sloblands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slobland) are protected from the sea by miles of sea walls and dikes. The area is an internationally important bird sanctuary, ranked among the top 30 wetland sites in the UK.[^cite_note-84]

Other important nature reserves lie at Ness Country Park,[^cite_note-85] 10 miles (16 kilometres) east of Derry; and at Prehen Wood,[^cite_note-86] within the city's south-eastern suburbs.

### Climate

Derry has, like most of Ireland, a [temperate maritime climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maritime_climate)[^cite_note-87] (Cfb) according to the [Köppen climate classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification) system. The nearest official Met Office Weather Station for which climate data is available is [Carmoney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnmoney),[^cite_note-88] just west of [City of Derry Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Derry_Airport) and about five miles (eight kilometres) northeast of the city centre. However, observations ceased in 2004 and the nearest Weather Station is currently [Ballykelly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballykelly,_County_Londonderry), due 12 miles (19 kilometres) east-northeast.[^cite_note-89] Typically, 27 nights of the year will report an air frost at Ballykelly, while at least 1 mm of precipitation will be reported on 170 days (1981–2010 averages).

The lowest temperature recorded at Carmoney was −11.0 °C (12.2 °F) on 27 December 1995.[^cite_note-90]

### Protestant minority



"No Surrender" [mural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mural) outside city wall, taken in 2004

Concerns have been raised by both communities over the increasingly divided nature of the city. There were about 17,000 Protestants on the west bank of the River Foyle in 1971.[^cite_note-110] The proportion rapidly declined during the 1970s;[^cite_note-111] the 2011 census recorded 3,169 Protestants on the west bank, compared to 54,976 Catholics,[^cite_note-112] and it is feared that the city could become permanently divided.[^cite_note-113][^cite_note-114]

However, concerted efforts have been made by the local community, church and political leaders from both traditions to redress the problem. A conference to bring together key actors and promote tolerance was held in October 2006.[^cite_note-Alienation_conference-115] [Ken Good](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Good), the [Church of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Ireland) Bishop of [Derry and Raphoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_Derry_and_Raphoe), said he was happy living on the cityside. "I feel part of it. It is my city and I want to encourage other Protestants to feel exactly the same", he said.[^cite_note-Alienation_conference-115]

Support for Protestants in the district has been strong from the SDLP politician [Helen Quigley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_Quigley), who formerly served as the mayor of Derry. She made inclusion and tolerance key themes of her mayoralty. Cllr. Quigley said it was time for "everyone to take a stand to stop the scourge of sectarian and other assaults in the city."[^cite_note-116]

## Economy



Du Pont facility at Maydown

### History

The economy of the district was based significantly on the textile industry until relatively recently. For many years women were commonly the sole wage earners working in the shirt factories while the men in comparison had high levels of unemployment.[^cite_note-117] This led to significant male emigration.[^cite_note-118] Shirt-making in the city dates back to 1831, and is said to have been begun by William Scott and his family who first exported shirts to [Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glasgow).[^cite_note-119] Within 50 years shirt-making in the city was the most prolific in the UK, and garments were exported all over the world. It was known so well that the industry received a mention in *[Das Kapital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Das_Kapital)* by [Karl Marx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Marx), when discussing the factory system:[^cite_note-120]

> The shirt factory of Messrs. Tille at Londonderry, which employs 1,000 operatives in the factory itself, and 9,000 people spread up and down the country and working in their own houses.

The industry reached its peak in the 1920s employing around 18,000 people.[^cite_note-Lacey-53] In modern times, however, the textile industry declined due largely to lower Asian wages.[^cite_note-121]

A long-term foreign employer in the area is [Du Pont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DuPont_\(1802%E2%80%932017\)), which has been based at Maydown since 1958, its first European production facility.[^cite_note-122] Originally [Neoprene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoprene) was manufactured at Maydown and subsequently followed by [Hypalon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypalon). More recently [Lycra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycra)[^cite_note-123] and [Kevlar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevlar) production units were active.[^cite_note-DuPontClosure-124] In 2004, the facility undertook a £40 million upgrade to expand its global Kevlar production.[^cite_note-125] A closure of the plant was announced in late 2025, pending the sale of subsidiaries to [materials science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materials_science) company Arclin.[^cite_note-DuPontClosure-124][^cite_note-126][^cite_note-127]

### Inward investment



Seagate production facility

As of 2002, the three largest private-sector employers were American firms.[^cite_note-128] Economic successes have included call centres and a large investment by [Seagate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seagate_Technology), which has operated a factory in the Springtown Industrial Estate since 1993. As of 2019, Seagate was employing approximately 1,400 people in Derry.[^cite_note-129]

A controversial new employer in the area was [Raytheon Systems Limited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raytheon), a software division of the American defence contractor, which was set up in Derry in 1999.[^cite_note-130] Although some of the local people welcomed the jobs boost, others in the area objected to the jobs being provided by a firm involved heavily in the [arms trade](608296.md).[^cite_note-131] Following four years of protest by the Foyle Ethical Investment Campaign, in 2004 Derry City Council passed a motion declaring the district "a 'no – go' area for the arms trade",[^cite_note-132] and in 2006 its offices were briefly occupied by anti-war protestors who became known as the [Raytheon 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raytheon_9).[^cite_note-133] In 2009, the company announced that it was not renewing its lease when it expired in 2010 and was looking for a new location for its operations.[^cite_note-134]

Other significant multinational employers in the region include Firstsource of India, [INVISTA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/INVISTA), Stream International, Perfecseal, [NTL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arqiva), [Northbrook Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northbrook_Technology) of the United States, Arntz Belting and Invision Software of Germany and Homeloan Management of the UK. Major local business employers include Desmonds, Northern Ireland's largest privately owned company, manufacturing and sourcing garments, E\&I Engineering, [St. Brendan's Irish Cream Liqueur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Brendan's) and McCambridge Duffy, one of the largest insolvency practices in the UK.[^cite_note-135]

Even though the city provides cheap labour by standards in Western Europe, critics have noted that the grants offered by the Northern Ireland Industrial Development Board have helped land jobs for the area that only last as long as the funding lasts.[^cite_note-136] This was reflected in questions to the [Parliamentary Under-Secretary of State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_Under-Secretary_of_State) for Northern Ireland, [Richard Needham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Needham), in 1990.[^cite_note-137] It was noted that it cost £30,000 to create one job in an American firm in Northern Ireland.

Critics of investment decisions affecting the district often point to the decision to build a new university building in nearby (predominantly Protestant) [Coleraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coleraine) rather than developing the Ulster University [Magee Campus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magee_Campus). Another major government decision affecting the city was the decision to create the new town of [Craigavon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Craigavon_\(planned_town\)) outside Belfast, which again was detrimental to the development of the city. Even in October 2005, there was perceived bias against the comparatively impoverished North West of the province, with a major civil service job contract going to Belfast. [Mark Durkan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Durkan), the [Social Democratic and Labour Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_Democratic_and_Labour_Party) (SDLP) leader and Member of Parliament (MP) for Foyle was quoted in the *[Belfast Telegraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belfast_Telegraph)* as saying:[^cite_note-138]

> The fact is there has been consistent under-investment in the North West and a reluctance on the part of the Civil Service to see or support anything west of the Bann, except when it comes to rate increases, then they treat us equally.

In July 2005, the Irish Minister for Finance, [Brian Cowen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Cowen), called for a joint task force to drive economic growth in the cross-border region.[^cite_note-139]

### Shopping



[Austins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austins_of_Derry) department store

The city is the north-west's foremost shopping district, housing two large shopping centres along with numerous shop-packed streets serving much of the greater county, as well as [Tyrone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Tyrone) and [Donegal](45576.md).

The city centre has two main shopping centres; the [Foyleside Shopping Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foyleside_Shopping_Centre), which has 45 stores and 1,430 parking spaces, and the [Richmond Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richmond_Centre_\(Derry\)), which has 39 retail units. The Quayside Shopping Centre also serves the city side and there is also Lisnagelvin Shopping Centre on the Waterside. These centres, as well as local-run businesses, feature numerous national and international stores. [Crescent Link Retail Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crescent_Link_Retail_Park), located in the Waterside, has several chain stores and has become the second largest retail park in Northern Ireland (second only to Sprucefield in Lisburn).[^cite_note-140] Plans have also been approved for Derry's first Asda store, which will be located at the retail park sharing a unit with Homebase.[^cite_note-141] Sainsbury's also applied for planning permission for a store at Crescent Link, but Environment Minister [Alex Attwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alex_Attwood) turned it down.[^cite_note-142]

Until the store's closure in March 2016, the city was also home to the world's oldest independent department store, [Austins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austins_of_Derry). Established in 1830, Austins predates [Jenners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jenners) of [Edinburgh](9602.md) by 5 years, [Harrods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harrods) of London by 15 years and [Macy's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macy's) of New York by 25 years.[^cite_note-143] The store's five-story [Edwardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwardian) building is located within the walled city in the area known as The Diamond.

## Landmarks



St Eugene's Cathedral



[Bishop Street Courthouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_Street_Courthouse)



Long Tower Church



[Derry Guildhall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derry_Guildhall)

Derry is renowned for its architecture. This can be primarily ascribed to the formal planning of the historic walled city of Derry at the core of the modern city. This is centred on the Diamond with a collection of late [Georgian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_architecture), [Victorian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_architecture) and [Edwardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwardian_architecture) buildings maintaining the gridlines of the main thoroughfares (Shipquay Street, Ferryquay Street, Butcher Street and Bishop Street) to the City Gates. [St Columb's Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Columb's_Cathedral) does not follow the grid pattern reinforcing its civic status. This [Church of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Ireland) Cathedral was the first post-[Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Reformation) Cathedral built for an [Anglican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican) church. The construction of the [Roman Catholic](606848.md) [St Eugene's Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Eugene's_Cathedral) in the Bogside in the 19th century was another major architectural addition to the city. The Townscape Heritage Initiative has funded restoration works to key listed buildings and other older structures.

In the three centuries since their construction, the [city walls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derry_city_walls) have been adapted to meet the needs of a changing city. The best example of this adaptation is the insertion of three additional gates – Castle Gate, New Gate and Magazine Gate – into the walls in the course of the 19th century. Today, the fortifications form a continuous promenade around the city centre, complete with cannon, avenues of mature trees and views across Derry. Historic buildings within the city walls include St Augustine's Church, which sits on the city walls close to the site of the original monastic settlement; the copper-domed Austin's department store, which claims to be the oldest such store in the world; and the imposing Greek Revival [Courthouse on Bishop Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_Street_Courthouse). The red-brick late-Victorian [Guildhall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guildhall,_Derry), also crowned by a copper dome, stands just beyond Shipquay Gate and close to the riverfront.

There are many museums and sites of interest in and around the city, including the Foyle Valley Railway Centre, the [Amelia Earhart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amelia_Earhart) Centre And Wildlife Sanctuary, the [Apprentice Boys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apprentice_Boys_of_Derry) Memorial Hall, [Ballyoan Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballyoan_Cemetery), The Bogside, numerous [murals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murals#Politics) by the [Bogside Artists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogside_Artists), Derry Craft Village, [Free Derry Corner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Derry_Corner), O'Doherty Tower (now home to part of the [Tower Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_Museum)), the Harbour Museum, the [Museum of Free Derry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Free_Derry), Chapter House Museum, the Workhouse Museum, the [Nerve Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nerve_Centre), [St. Columb's Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Columb's_Park) and Leisure Centre, [Creggan Country Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creggan_Country_Park?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Brooke Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooke_Park), [The Millennium Forum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Millennium_Forum), the [Void Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Void_Gallery),[^cite_note-144] and the [Foyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foyle_Bridge) and [Craigavon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Craigavon_Bridge) bridges.

Attractions include museums, a vibrant shopping centre and trips to the [Giant's Causeway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giant's_Causeway), which is approximately 50 miles (80 kilometres) away, though poorly connected by public transport. [Lonely Planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lonely_Planet) called Derry the fourth best city in the world to see in 2013.[^cite_note-145]

On 25 June 2011, the [Peace Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_Bridge_\(Foyle\)) opened. It is a cycle and footbridge that begins from the Guild Hall in the city centre of Derry City to Ebrington Square and St Columb's Park on the far side of the River Foyle. It was funded jointly by the Department for Social Development (NI), the Department of the Environment, Community and Local Government along with matching funding, totalling £14 million, from the [SEUPB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_EU_Programmes_Body) Peace III programme.[^cite_note-146]

Future projects include the Walled City Signature Project, which intends to ensure that the city's walls become a world-class tourist experience.[^cite_note-147]

## Transport



The [Foyle Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foyle_Bridge) showing Derry-to-Belfast rail link

The transport network is built out of a complex array of old and modern roads and railways throughout the city and county. The city's road network also makes use of two bridges to cross the [River Foyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Foyle), the [Craigavon Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Craigavon_Bridge) and the [Foyle Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foyle_Bridge), the longest bridge in Ireland. Derry also serves as a major transport hub for travel throughout nearby [County Donegal](45576.md).

In spite of it being the second city of Northern Ireland (and it being the second-largest city in all of [Ulster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster)), road and rail links to other cities are below par for its standing. Many business leaders claim that government investment in the city and infrastructure has been badly lacking. Some have stated that this is due to its outlying border location whilst others have cited a [sectarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sectarian) bias against the region west of the [River Bann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Bann) due to its high proportion of Catholics.[^cite_note-148][^cite_note-149] There is no direct motorway link with [Dublin](8504.md) or [Belfast](5046.md). The rail link to Belfast has been downgraded over the years so that, presently, it is not a viable alternative to the roads for industry to rely on. As of 2008, there were plans for £1 billion worth of transport infrastructure investment in and around the district.[^cite_note-eGov-150] Planned upgrades to the [A5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A5_road_\(Northern_Ireland\)) [Dublin](8504.md) road agreed as part of the [Good Friday Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_Friday_Agreement) and St Andrews Talks fell through when the government of the Republic of Ireland reneged on its funding citing the [post-2008 economic downturn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-2008_Irish_economic_downturn).[^cite_note-151]

### Buses

Most public transport in Northern Ireland is operated by the subsidiaries of [Translink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translink_\(Northern_Ireland\)). Originally the city's internal bus network was run by [Ulsterbus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulsterbus), which still provides the city's connections with other towns in Northern Ireland. The city's buses are now run by [Ulsterbus Foyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulsterbus_Foyle),[^cite_note-152] just as [Translink Metro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metro_\(Belfast\)) now provides the bus service in Belfast. The Ulsterbus Foyle network offers 13 routes across the city into the suburban areas, excluding an Easibus link which connects to the Waterside and [Drumahoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drumahoe),[^cite_note-153] and a free Rail Link Bus runs from the Waterside Railway Station to the city centre. All buses leave from the Foyle Street Bus Station in the city centre.

Long-distance buses depart from Foyle Street Bus Station to destinations throughout Ireland. Buses are operated by both Ulsterbus and [Bus Éireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bus_%C3%89ireann) on cross-border routes. [Lough Swilly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Londonderry_and_Lough_Swilly_Railway) formerly operated buses to [County Donegal](45576.md), but the company entered liquidation and is no longer in operation. There is a half-hourly service to [Belfast](5046.md) every day, called the Maiden City Flyer, which is the Goldline Express flagship route. There are hourly services to [Strabane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strabane), [Omagh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omagh), [Coleraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coleraine), [Letterkenny](620333.md) and [Buncrana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buncrana), and up to twelve services a day to [Dublin](8504.md). Bus Éireann provides Expressway service to [Galway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galway), via [Sligo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sligo) and [Ireland West Airport Knock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireland_West_Airport_Knock).[^cite_note-154]

[TFI Local Link](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TFI_Local_Link) provides additional cross-border public transport routes, with route **244** ([Moville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moville)/Derry),[^cite_note-Carndonagh_to_Derry-155] **245** ([Greencastle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greencastle,_County_Donegal)/Derry),[^cite_note-156] **288** ([Ballybofey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballybofey)/Derry),[^cite_note-Stranorlar-to-Derry-157] **952** ([Carndonagh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carndonagh)/Derry),[^cite_note-Carndonagh_to_Derry-155] **957** ([Shrove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shrove,_County_Donegal)/Derry, via Moville),[^cite_note-158]**1426** ([Stranorlar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stranorlar)/Derry)[^cite_note-Stranorlar-to-Derry-157] all servicing the city.

Private coach operator, Patrick Gallagher Coaches, also runs two routes during the week that service the city. The first goes from [Crolly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crolly) in County Donegal to Belfast[^cite_note-159] (to the Leonardo Hotel in Belfast city centre, formerly [Jurys Inn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurys_Inn)),[^cite_note-160] and another that runs from County Donegal to the city.[^cite_note-161]

[City of Derry Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Derry_Airport), the council-owned airport near [Eglinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eglinton,_County_Londonderry), has grown during the early 21st century, with new investment in extending the runway and plans to redevelop the terminal.[^cite_note-162]

The [A2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A2_road_\(Northern_Ireland\)) (a [dual carriageway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_carriageway)) from [Maydown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maydown) to [Eglinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eglinton,_County_Londonderry), serves the airport.[^cite_note-163] City of Derry airport is the main regional airport for [County Donegal](45576.md), [County Londonderry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Londonderry) and west [County Tyrone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Tyrone) as well as Derry City itself.

[EasyJet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EasyJet) is scheduled to begin flights to [Birmingham Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_Airport) on 1 September 2025.[^cite_note-166]

### Railways

The city is served by a single rail link terminating at [Derry \~ Londonderry railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derry_~_Londonderry_railway_station) in Waterside that is subsidised, alongside much of Northern Ireland's railways, by [Northern Ireland Railways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Railways) (N.I.R.). The link primarily provides passenger services from the city to [Belfast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belfast_Central_railway_station), via several stops that include [Coleraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coleraine_railway_station), [Ballymoney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballymoney_railway_station) and [Antrim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antrim_railway_station), and connections to links with other parts of Northern Ireland. The route itself is the only remaining rail link used by trains; most of the lines developed in the mid-19th century fell into decline towards the mid-20th century from competition by new road networks. The original rail network that served the city included four different railways that, between them, linked the city with much of the province of Ulster, plus a harbour railway network that linked the other four lines, and a tramway on the City side of the Foyle. Usage of the rail link between Derry and Belfast remains questionable for commuters, due to the journey time of over two hours making it slower centre-to-centre than the 100-minute Ulsterbus Goldline Express service.[^cite_note-167]

#### Railway history

##### 19th century – early 20th century growth



Ireland's railway network in 1906

Several railways began operation around the city of Derry within the middle of the 19th century. The companies that set up links helped to provide key links for the city towards other towns and cities across Ireland, for the transportation of passengers and freight. The lines that were constructed featured a mixture of Irish gauge and narrow gauge railways. Companies that operated them included:

- The [Londonderry and Enniskillen Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Londonderry_and_Enniskillen_Railway) (L\&ER) – The rail company constructed Derry's first railway in 1845 with [Irish gauge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_gauge) (5 ft 3 in (1,600 mm)) track. The line operated from a temporary station at [Cow Market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Londonderry_Cow_Market_railway_station) on the City side of the Foyle, reaching [Strabane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strabane) in 1847,

  [^cite_note-Hajducki3-168]

  &#x20;before being extended from [Cow Market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Londonderry_Cow_Market_railway_station) to its permanent terminus at Foyle Road in 1850.

  [^cite_note-Hajducki2-169]

  &#x20;The L\&ER reached [Omagh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omagh) in 1852 and [Enniskillen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enniskillen) in 1854,

  [^cite_note-Hajducki2-169]

  &#x20;and was absorbed into the [Great Northern Railway (Ireland)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Northern_Railway_\(Ireland\)) in 1883.

  [^cite_note-170]

- The [Londonderry and Coleraine Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Londonderry_and_Coleraine_Railway) (L\&CR) – The rail company constructed an Irish gauge line to the city in 1852, opening a terminus at Waterside.

  [^cite_note-Hajducki2-169]

  &#x20;The [Belfast and Northern Counties Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belfast_and_Northern_Counties_Railway) leased the line from 1861, before taking it over in 1871.

- The [Londonderry and Lough Swilly Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Londonderry_and_Lough_Swilly_Railway) (L\&LSR) – The rail company opened a line between Farland Point on [Lough Swilly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lough_Swilly) and a temporary terminus at Pennyburn in 1863,

  [^cite_note-Hajducki2-169]

  &#x20;before extending the line in 1866 to a more permanent terminus at Graving Dock.

  [^cite_note-Hajducki2-169]

  &#x20;The L\&LSR was conceived to operate on Irish gauge track when it was constructed, but was converted in 1885 to 3 ft (914 mm) [narrow gauge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narrow_gauge) to link it with the Letterkenny Railway.

- The [Londonderry Port and Harbour Commissioners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Londonderry_Port) (LPHC) – The rail company established a line that linked Graving Dock and Foyle Road stations through Middle Quay in 1867, before extending the line to create a rail connection with Waterside station, via the newly constructed Carlisle Bridge, in 1868.

  [^cite_note-Hajducki2-169]

  &#x20;When the bridge was replaced in 1933 with the double-deck [Craigavon Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Craigavon_Bridge), the LPHC was assigned to operate on the lower deck.

In 1900, the 3 ft (914 mm) gauge [Donegal Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Donegal_Railways_Joint_Committee) was extended to the city from Strabane, with construction establishing the [Londonderry Victoria Road railway terminus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Londonderry_Victoria_Road_railway_station) and creating a junction with the LPHC railway.[^cite_note-Hajducki2-169] The LPHC line was altered to [dual gauge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_gauge) which allowed 3 ft (914 mm) gauge traffic between the Donegal Railway and L\&LSR as well as Irish gauge traffic between the GNR and B\&NCR. By 1905, the government of the United Kingdom offered subsidies to both the L\&LSR and the Donegal Railway to build extensions to their railway networks into remote parts of [County Donegal](45576.md), which soon developed Derry (alongside Strabane) into becoming a key rail hub by 1905 for the county and surrounding regions.[^cite_note-171] In 1906 the [Northern Counties Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Counties_Committee) (NCC, successor to the B\&NCR) and the GNR jointly took over the Donegal Railway, making it the [County Donegal Railways Joint Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Donegal_Railways_Joint_Committee) (CDRJC).

Alongside the railways, the city was served by a [standard gauge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_gauge) (1,435 mm (4 ft 8+1⁄2 in)) tramway, the [City of Derry Tramways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Derry_Tramways).[^cite_note-HajduckiXVII-172] The tramway was opened in 1897 and consisted of [horse trams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horsecar) that operated along a single line, 1+1⁄2 miles (2.5 kilometres) long, which ran along the City side of the Foyle parallel to the LPHC's line on that side of the river.[^cite_note-Hajducki3-168] The line never converted to electrically operated trams,[^cite_note-HajduckiXVII-172] and was closed in 1919.[^cite_note-HajduckiXVII-172]

##### 20th century decline

In 1922, the [partition of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_of_Ireland) dramatically caused disruptions to the city's rail links, except for the NNC route to [Coleraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coleraine_railway_station).[^cite_note-Hajducki3-168] The creation of an international frontier with County Donegal changed trade patterns to the detriment of the railways affected by the partition, placing border posts on every line to and from Derry, causing great delays to trains and disrupting timekeeping from custom inspections – the L\&LSR faced inspections between Pennyburn and Bridge End; the CDRJC faced inspections beyond Strabane; and the GNR line faced inspections between Derry and Strabane.[^cite_note-Hajducki3-168] Custom agreements negotiated over the next few years between Britain and Ireland enabled GNR trains to travel to and from Derry – such trains would be allowed to pass without inspection through the Free State, unless they served local stations on the west bank of the Foyle – while goods transported by all railways between different parts of the Free State would be allowed to pass through Northern Ireland under [customs bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonded_warehouse). Despite these agreements, local passenger and goods traffic continued to be delayed by customs examinations.

The decline of most of Derry's rail links took place after the Second World War, due to increasing competition by road links. The L\&LSR closed its line in 1953, followed by the CDRJC in 1954.[^cite_note-Hajducki39-173] The [Ulster Transport Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_Transport_Authority), who took over the NCC in 1949 and the GNR's lines in Northern Ireland in 1958, took control of the LPHC railway before closing it in 1962,[^cite_note-Baker209-174] before eventually shutting down the former GNR line to Derry in 1965, after the submission of [The Benson Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_Transport_Authority#The_Benson_Report) to the Northern Ireland Government two years prior to the closure.[^cite_note-Hajducki39-173][^cite_note-Baker209-174][^cite_note-175] This left the former L\&CR line to Coleraine as the sole railway link for the city, providing a passenger service to Belfast, alongside [CIÉ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CI%C3%89) freight services to Donegal. By the 1990s, the service began to deteriorate.

##### 21st century regeneration

In 2008, the Department for Regional Development announced plans to relay the track between Derry and Coleraine. The plan, aimed at being completed by 2013, included adding a [passing loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passing_loop) to increase traffic capacity and increasing the number of trains with two additional [diesel multiple units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diesel_multiple_unit).[^cite_note-RailUpgrade-176] Additional phases of the plan also included improvements to existing stations along the line and the restoration of the former Victoria Road terminus building to prepare for the relocation of the city's existing terminus station to the site.[^cite_note-NorthWestTransportHub-177] Costing around £86 million, the improvements were aimed at reducing the journey time to Belfast by 30 minutes and allowing commuter trains to arrive before 9 a.m. for the first time.[^cite_note-RailUpgrade-176] [Derry \~ Londonderry railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derry_~_Londonderry_railway_station) opened in October 2019.[^cite_note-NorthWestTransportHub-177]

### Road network

The largest road investment in the north west's history took place during 2010, with the building of the 'A2 Broadbridge Maydown to City of Derry Airport dualling' project[^cite_note-178] and announcement of the 'A6 Londonderry to Dungiven Dualling Scheme'[^cite_note-179] with the intention to reduce the travel time to Belfast.[^cite_note-180] The latter project brings a dual-carriageway link between Northern Ireland's two largest cities one step closer. The project is costing £320 million and is expected to be completed in 2016.

In October 2006 the [Government of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Ireland) announced that it was to invest €1 billion in Northern Ireland;[^cite_note-181] with the planned projects including 'the A5 Western Transport Corridor',[^cite_note-182] the complete upgrade of the A5 Derry – Omagh – Aughnacloy (– Dublin) road, around 90 kilometres (55 miles) long, to [dual carriageway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_carriageway) standard.[^cite_note-183]

In June 2008 [Conor Murphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conor_Murphy), Minister for Regional Development, announced that there will be a study into the feasibility of connecting the A5 and A6.[^cite_note-eGov-150] Should it proceed, the scheme would most likely run from Drumahoe to south of Prehen along the south east of the city.[^cite_note-RailUpgrade-176]

### Sea



A mass of surrendered German U-boats at their mooring at Lisahally

[Londonderry Port](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Londonderry_Port) at [Lisahally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisahally) is the United Kingdom's most westerly port and has capacity for 30,000-ton vessels. The [Londonderry Port and Harbour Commissioners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Londonderry_Port) (LPHC) announced record turnover, record profits and record tonnage figures for the year ended March 2008. The figures are the result of a significant capital expenditure programme for the period 2000 to 2007 of about £22 million. Tonnage handled by LPHC increased by almost 65% between 2000 and 2007.[^cite_note-184]

The port gave vital [Allied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II) service in the longest-running campaign of the Second World War, the Battle of the Atlantic, and saw the surrender of the German [U-boat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U-boat) fleet at Lisahally on 8 May 1945.[^cite_note-185]

### Inland waterways

The tidal [River Foyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Foyle) is navigable from the coast at Derry to approximately 10 miles (16 km) inland. In 1796, the [Strabane Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strabane_Canal) was opened, continuing the navigation a further 4 miles (6 km) southwards to [Strabane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strabane). The canal was closed in 1962.

## Education



[Magee College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magee_College) became a campus of [Ulster University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_University) in 1969.

Derry is home to the [Magee Campus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magee_College) of [Ulster University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_University), formerly Magee College. However, Lockwood's[^cite_note-186] 1960s decision to locate Northern Ireland's second university in Coleraine rather than Derry helped contribute to the formation of the civil rights movement that ultimately led to [The Troubles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Troubles). Derry was the town more closely associated with higher learning, with Magee College already more than a century old by that time.[^cite_note-187][^cite_note-188] In the mid-1980s an attempt was made at address this by forming Magee College as a campus of the [Ulster University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_University), but this failed to stifle calls for the establishment of an independent University in Derry.[^cite_note-189] As of 2021, the Magee campus reportedly accommodated approximately 4,400 students,[^cite_note-190] out of a total [Ulster University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_University) student population of approximately 24,000, of which 15,000 are in the Belfast campus.

The [North West Regional College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_West_Regional_College) is also based in the city, and accommodates over 10,000 student enrolments annually.[^cite_note-191][^cite_note-192]

One of the two oldest secondary schools in Northern Ireland, [Foyle College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foyle_College), is located in Derry. It was founded in 1616 by the [Merchant Taylors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merchant_Taylors'_Company). Other secondary schools include [St. Columb's College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Columb's_College), [Oakgrove Integrated College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oakgrove_Integrated_College), [St Cecilia's College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Cecilia's_College), [St Mary's College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Mary's_College,_Derry), [St. Joseph's Boys' School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Joseph's_Boys'_School), [Lisneal College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisneal_College), [Thornhill College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thornhill_College), [Lumen Christi College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumen_Christi_College_\(Derry,_Northern_Ireland\)) and St. Brigid's College. There are also numerous primary schools.

## Sports



The [Derry GAA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derry_GAA) team ahead of the [2009 National League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Football_League_\(Ireland\)_2009) final

The city is home to sports clubs and teams. Both association football and [Gaelic football](37688.md) are popular in the area.

### Association football

In association football, the city's most prominent clubs include [Derry City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derry_City_F.C.) who play in the [national league](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Ireland) of the [Republic of Ireland](14560.md); [Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_F.C.) of the [NIFL Championship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NIFL_Championship) as well as [Maiden City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maiden_City_F.C.) and [Trojans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trojans_F.C.), both of the [Northern Ireland Intermediate League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland_Intermediate_League).

In addition to these clubs, which all play in national leagues, other clubs are based in the city. The local football league governed by the [IFA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Football_Association) is the North-West Junior League, which contains many clubs from the city, such as BBOB (Boys Brigade Old Boys) and Lincoln Courts. The city's other junior league is the [Derry and District League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derry_and_District_League) and teams from the city and surrounding areas participate, including Don Boscos and Creggan Swifts. The [Foyle Cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foyle_Cup) youth soccer tournament is held annually in the city. It has attracted many notable teams in the past, including [Werder Bremen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SV_Werder_Bremen), [IFK Göteborg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IFK_G%C3%B6teborg) and [Ferencváros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferencv%C3%A1rosi_TC).

### Gaelic football

In Gaelic football [Derry GAA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derry_GAA) are the county team and play in the [Gaelic Athletic Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_Athletic_Association)'s [National Football League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Football_League_\(Ireland\)), [Ulster Senior Football Championship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_Senior_Football_Championship) and [All-Ireland Senior Football Championship](885732.md). They also field [hurling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurling) teams in the equivalent tournaments. There are many Gaelic games clubs in and around the city, for example [Na Magha CLG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Na_Magha_CLG), [Steelstown GAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steelstown_GAC), [Doire Colmcille CLG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doire_Colmcille_CLG), [Seán Dolans GAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Se%C3%A1n_Dolans_GAC), Na Piarsaigh CLG Doire Trasna and [Slaughtmanus GAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slaughtmanus_GAC).

### Boxing

There are many boxing clubs. The best-known is the Ring Amateur Boxing Club, which is based on the City side and is associated with boxers [Charlie Nash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlie_Nash_\(boxer\)) and [John Duddy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Duddy).[^cite_note-193][^cite_note-194] Rochester's Amateur Boxing club is a club in the city's Waterside area.[^cite_note-195]

### Rugby union

[Rugby union](25405.md) is also quite popular in the city, with the [City of Derry Rugby Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Derry_Rugby_Club) situated not far from the city centre.[^cite_note-196] City of Derry won both the Ulster Towns Cup and the Ulster Junior Cup in 2009. Londonderry YMCA RFC is another rugby club and is based in the village of [Drumahoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drumahoe) which is on the outskirts of the city.

### Basketball

The city's only basketball club is North Star Basketball Club which has teams in the [Basketball Northern Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basketball_Northern_Ireland) senior and junior Leagues.[^cite_note-197]

### Cricket

[Cricket](25675557.md) is also played in the city, particularly in the Waterside. The city is home to two cricket clubs, [Brigade Cricket Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brigade_Cricket_Club) and [Glendermott Cricket Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glendermott_Cricket_Club), both of whom play in the [North West Senior League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_West_Senior_League).

### Golf

There are two golf clubs situated in the city, City of Derry Golf Club and Foyle International Golf Centre.

## Culture



*Hands Across the Divide* sculpture, by [Maurice Harron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Harron)

Artists and writers associated with the city and surrounding countryside include the [Nobel Prize](21201.md)-winning poet [Seamus Heaney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seamus_Heaney),[^cite_note-198] poet [Seamus Deane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seamus_Deane), playwright [Brian Friel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Friel),[^cite_note-199] writer and music critic [Nik Cohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nik_Cohn), artist [Willie Doherty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willie_Doherty), socio-political commentator and activist [Eamonn McCann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eamonn_McCann)[^cite_note-200] and bands such as [the Undertones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Undertones). The large political gable-wall murals of Bogside Artists, Free Derry Corner, the Foyle Film Festival, the Derry Walls, St Eugene's and St Columb's Cathedrals and the annual Halloween street carnival[^cite_note-201] are popular tourist attractions. In 2010, Derry was named the UK's tenth 'most musical' city by [PRS for Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PRS_for_Music).[^cite_note-202]



Effigy of Lundy the Traitor, Shutting of the Gates

The [Apprentice Boys of Derry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apprentice_Boys_of_Derry) hold two major annual [parades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange_walk) in the city, the [Relief of Derry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relief_of_Derry) and the [Shutting of the Gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shutting_of_the_Gates) (dubbed Lundy's Day).[^cite_note-203][^cite_note-204] The Relief of Derry commemorates the Siege of Derry, the longest [siege](26905.md) in British and Irish military history.[^cite_note-205] The parade takes place on the second Saturday in August.[^cite_note-206] The Shutting of the Gates commemorates the thirteen Apprentice Boys that locked the gates of [Derry city walls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derry_city_walls) on the approaching [Jacobites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobitism).[^cite_note-207] An effigy of Robert Lundy "The Traitor" is burned in the evening. The event takes place on the first Saturday in December.[^cite_note-208]



Peace Flame Monument, unveiled in May 2013

In May 2013 a perpetual Peace Flame Monument was unveiled by [Martin Luther King III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Luther_King_III) and Presbyterian minister Rev. David Latimer. The flame was lit by children from both traditions in the city and is one of only 15 such flames across the world.[^cite_note-209][^cite_note-210]

### Media

The local newspapers, the *[Derry Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derry_Journal)* (known as the *Londonderry Journal* until 1880) and the *[Londonderry Sentinel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Londonderry_Sentinel)*, reflect the divided history of the city: the *Journal* was founded in 1772 and is Ireland's second oldest newspaper;[^cite_note-Lacey-53] the *Sentinel* newspaper was formed in 1829 when new owners of the *Journal* embraced [Catholic emancipation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_emancipation) and the editor left the paper to set up the *Sentinel*.

There are numerous radio stations receivable: the largest stations based in the city are [BBC Radio Foyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_Foyle)[^cite_note-211] and the commercial station [Q102.9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q102.9).[^cite_note-212]

There was a locally based television station, [C9TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C9TV), one of only two local or 'restricted' television services in Northern Ireland, which ceased broadcasts in 2007.

### Nightlife

The city's nightlife is mainly focused on the weekends, with several bars and clubs providing "student nights" during the weekdays. Waterloo Street and Strand Road provide the main venues. Waterloo Street, a steep street lined with both Irish traditional and modern pubs, frequently has live rock and traditional music at night.
