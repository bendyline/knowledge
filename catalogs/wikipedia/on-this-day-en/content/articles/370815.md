# Coffeehouse

"Cafe" redirects here; not to be confused with [Cafeteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cafeteria) or [Cafe (British)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cafe_\(British\)).

For other uses, see [Cafe (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cafe_\(disambiguation\)) and [Coffeehouse (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffeehouse_\(disambiguation\)).



The [Café de Flore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caf%C3%A9_de_Flore) on the [Rive Gauche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rive_Gauche) in Paris is one of the oldest coffeehouses in the city. Its famous clientele included writers and philosophers.

A **coffeehouse**, **coffee shop**, or **café**, is an establishment that serves various types of [coffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffee) drinks like [espresso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Espresso), [latte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latte), [americano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caff%C3%A8_americano) and [cappuccino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cappuccino), as well as other beverages. An [espresso bar](#Espresso_bar) specializes in serving espresso and espresso-based drinks. Some coffeehouses may serve [iced coffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iced_coffee) among other cold drinks, as well as non-caffeinated drinks. A coffeehouse may also serve food, such as light snacks, [sandwiches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandwich), [muffins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muffin), [cakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cake), [breads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bread), [pastries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastries) or [doughnuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doughnut). Many [doughnut shops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doughnut_shop) in Canada and the U.S. serve coffee to accompany doughnuts, so these can also be classified as coffee shops, although doughnut shops tend to be more casual and serve cheaper fare (suiting [take-out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Take-out) and [drive-through](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drive-through), popular in those countries).[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-2] In continental Europe, some cafés even serve alcoholic drinks, and in West Asia, coffeehouses may offer a flavored tobacco smoked through a [hookah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hookah), called *shisha* in most varieties of [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic) or *nargile* in [Levantine Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levantine_Arabic), [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language), and [Turkish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_language).

While *café* may mean a coffeehouse, it tends to have [a different meaning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cafe_\(British\)) in Britain:[^cite_note-3] a [diner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diner) or "[greasy spoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greasy_spoon)"; it is also used for a [teahouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teahouse) or other casual eating and drinking place.[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6] A coffeehouse may resemble a bar or restaurant, but differs from a [cafeteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cafeteria) (a canteen, a restaurant without table service). Coffeehouse operation ranges from management of an independent venue by its owner to franchises of a large [multinational corporation](214491.md).

From a cultural standpoint, a coffeehouse largely serves as a center of social interaction: it provides patrons with a place to meet, talk, read, write, entertain one another, or pass the time, whether individually or in small groups. A coffeehouse can serve as an informal [social club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_club) for its regular members.[^cite_note-7] From as early as the 1950s [Beatnik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatnik) era and the 1960s [folk music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_music) scene, coffeehouses have hosted singer–songwriter performances, typically in the evening.[^cite_note-Groce_Coffeehouses-8] The digital age saw the rise of the [Internet café](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_caf%C3%A9) along similar principles.

The word for coffee in various European languages

[^cite_note-9]

The Arabic word *qahwa* (قهوة) is the origin of the Italian *caffè* (first attested as *caveé* in [Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venice) in 1570[^cite_note-10]), from which the English *coffee* and French *café* both derive.[^cite_note-auto-11][^cite_note-12] The Arabic term originally meant a type of wine, but after the wine ban by [Islam](6037917.md), the name was transferred to coffee, thought to have a similar rousing effect.[^cite_note-13] European knowledge of coffee (the plant, its seeds, and the drink made from the seeds) came through European contact with [Turkey](11125639.md), likely via Venetian–[Ottoman](22278.md) trade relations.

*Café* (French: [\[kafe\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/French) [//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/1/11/LL-Q150\_%28fra%29-GrandCelinien-caf%C3%A9.wav/LL-Q150\_%28fra%29-GrandCelinien-caf%C3%A9.wav.mp3](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/1/11/LL-Q150_%28fra%29-GrandCelinien-caf%C3%A9.wav/LL-Q150_%28fra%29-GrandCelinien-caf%C3%A9.wav.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup>) is the French word for both *coffee* and *coffeehouse;*[^cite_note-auto-11][^cite_note-14] it was adopted (as /ˈkæfeɪ/) by English-speaking countries in the late 19th century.[^cite_note-15] The Italian spelling, *caffè*, is also sometimes used in English.[^cite_note-16] In [Southern England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_England), especially around London in the 1950s, the French pronunciation was often facetiously altered to [/kæf/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) and spelt *caff*.[^cite_note-17][^cite_note-19]

The English word *café* to describe a *place* that serves coffee and snacks (rather than the drink itself) is derived from the French *café.* The first café in France is believed to have opened in 1660.[^cite_note-auto-11] Meanwhile, the first café in Europe is believed to have been opened in [Belgrade](55904.md), [Ottoman Serbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Serbia) in 1522 as a [Kafana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kafana) (Serbian coffeehouse).[^cite_note-20]

The [translingual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translingual) word root /kafe/ appears in many European languages with various naturalized spellings, including [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_language) and [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language) (*café*); [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language) (*Kaffeehaus* or *Café*); [Swedish](26689.md) (*kafé* or *fik*); [Finnish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finnish_language) (*kahvila*); [Spanish](26825.md) (*cafetería*); [Italian](14708.md) (*caffè* or *caffetteria*); [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_language) (*kawa*); [Serbian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbian_language) (*кафа / kafa*); [Ukrainian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_language) (*кава* (kava)); [Turkish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_language) (*kahvehane).*

## Early history

Main article: [Coffeehouses in Arabic culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffeehouses_in_Arabic_culture)



Palestinian women grinding coffee beans

Early forms of the beverage now known as coffee were consumed in Yemen, where [Sufi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism) orders used [*qahwa*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qahwah) to maintain wakefulness during nighttime *[dhikr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhikr)* recitations.[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-\:10-22] This early coffee-based beverage was named after the port city of [Mokha](2082636.md), from which coffee beans were first exported to the other parts of the Arab world.[^cite_note-23][^cite_note-24] Yemen was once one of the world's major producers and exporters, with "Mocha" coffee. Yemeni traders played a crucial role in spreading both the beverage and its associated rituals, contributing to its growing popularity across the Islamic world.

In the 15th century, coffee cultivation and consumption began in Yemen, where merchants transformed it into a lucrative and commercial product. The beans, often sourced from the [Ethiopian highlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Highlands), were roasted, ground, and brewed in a method that closely resembles modern coffee preparation. By the early 16th century, coffee spread northward to Mecca, Cairo, and Damascus, where coffeehouses — known as *maqāhī* (مقاهي) became popular meeting places.[^cite_note-Albanese2017-25]

### Ottoman Empire

Main article: [Ottoman coffeehouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_coffeehouse)



[Ottoman miniature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_miniature) of a [meddah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meddah) performing at a coffeehouse



Storyteller (*[meddah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_the_Ottoman_Empire)*) at a coffeehouse in the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md). The first coffeehouses appeared in the [Muslim world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_world) in the 15th century.

The first coffeehouses appeared in [Damascus](8914.md). These coffeehouses also made their way to [Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecca), in the [Arabian Peninsula](47858.md) in the 15th century, then spread to the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md)'s capital of [Istanbul](3391396.md), when two Arab merchants, Hakem of Aleppo and Shems of Damascus, opened the first coffeehouse in the Tahtakale district in the 16th century and to [Baghdad](4492.md).[^cite_note-Ayvazoglu-26][^cite_note-Iranica2-27][^cite_note-OzkaymakBas-28][^cite_note-Karhan-29]

Coffeehouses became popular meeting places where people gathered to drink coffee, have conversations, play board games such as [chess](5134.md) and [backgammon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backgammon), listen to stories and music, and discuss news and politics. They became known as "schools of wisdom" for the type of clientele they attracted, and their free and frank discourse.[^cite_note-britannica-30][^cite_note-31]

Coffeehouses in Mecca became a concern of [imams](15240.md), who viewed them as places for political gatherings and drinking, leading to bans between 1512 and 1524.[^cite_note-32] However, these bans could not be maintained, as coffee became ingrained in daily ritual and culture among Arabs and neighboring peoples.[^cite_note-britannica-30] The [Ottoman](22278.md) chronicler [İbrahim Peçevi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%B0brahim_Pe%C3%A7evi) reports in his writings (1642–49) about the opening of the first coffeehouse (*kiva han*) in Constantinople:

> Until the year 962 \[1555], in the High, God-Guarded city of Constantinople, as well as in Ottoman lands generally, coffee and coffeehouses did not exist. About that year, a fellow called Hakam from [Aleppo](159244.md) and a wag called Shams from Damascus came to the city; they each opened a large shop in the district called Tahtakale, and began to purvey coffee.[^cite_note-33]



A coffeehouse in [Cairo](6293.md), 18th century

### Iran

The 17th-century French traveler and writer [Jean Chardin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Chardin) gave a lively description of the [Persian](14653.md) coffeehouse (*qahveh khaneh* in [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language)):

> People engage in conversation, for it is there that news is communicated and where those interested in politics criticize the government in all freedom and without being fearful, since the government does not heed what the people say. Innocent games ... resembling [checkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Checkers), hopscotch, and chess, are played. In addition, [mollas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mullah), [dervishes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dervish), and poets take turns telling stories in verse or in prose. The narrations by the mollas and the dervishes are moral lessons, like our sermons, but it is not considered scandalous not to pay attention to them. No one is forced to give up his game or his conversation because of it. A molla will stand up in the middle, or at one end of the qahveh-khaneh, and begin to preach in a loud voice, or a dervish enters all of a sudden, and chastises the assembled on the vanity of the world and its material goods. It often happens that two or three people talk at the same time, one on one side, the other on the opposite, and sometimes one will be a preacher and the other a storyteller.[^cite_note-34]

### Mughal Empire

Consumption of Turkish coffee is attested to in the [Mughal court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire), and appears in [Mughal art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_art) from the 16th century, as is the existence of *qahwakhanas* (coffeehouses) in [Shahjahanabad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shahjahanabad) (Old Delhi).[^cite_note-GuptaH-35][^cite_note-BiswasN-36]

## Modern history

### Europe



"Discussing the War in a Paris Café", *[The Illustrated London News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Illustrated_London_News)*, 17 September 1870, during the [Franco-Prussian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franco-Prussian_War)

In the 17th century, coffee appeared for the first time in Europe outside the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md), and coffeehouses were established, soon becoming increasingly popular. The first public coffeehouse in Europe proper was opened in [Belgrade](55904.md), year 1521 and shortly after the city fell to the Ottomans. It was followed by coffeehouse in [Sarajevo](26786.md), at then lively part of the town called [Bentbaša](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bentba%C5%A1a?action=edit\&redlink=1), around year 1532. [Bentbaša](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bentba%C5%A1a?action=edit\&redlink=1) was on the route of caravans and was thus a popular meeting point. Outside Ottoman Empire in Europe, first coffeehouse is said to have appeared in 1632 in [Livorno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Livorno), Italy, founded by a Jewish merchant,[^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38] or in 1640, in Venice.[^cite_note-39] In the 19th and 20th centuries in Europe, coffeehouses were very often meeting places for writers and artists.

#### Austria

Main article: [Viennese coffee house culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viennese_coffee_house_culture)



A [Viennese café](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viennese_coffee_house)

The traditional tale of the origins of the [Viennese café](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viennese_caf%C3%A9) begins with the mysterious sacks of green beans left behind when the Turks were defeated in the [Battle of Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Vienna) in 1683. All the sacks of coffee were granted to the victorious Polish king, [Jan III Sobieski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_III_Sobieski), who in turn gave them to one of his officers, [Jerzy Franciszek Kulczycki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerzy_Franciszek_Kulczycki) (also known as Georg Kolschitzky), a Ukrainian Cossack and Polish diplomat of Ruthenian descent. Kulczycki, according to the tale, then began the first coffeehouse in [Vienna](55866.md) with the hoard, also being the first to serve coffee with milk.[^cite_note-Weinberg2002-75-40]

However, it is now widely accepted that the first Viennese coffeehouse was actually opened by an Armenian merchant named Johannes Diodato (also known as Johannes Theodat) in 1685.[^cite_note-Teply_1980-41][^cite_note-Weinberg2002-77-42] Fifteen years later, four other Armenians owned coffeehouses. The [culture of drinking coffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffee_culture) was itself widespread in the country in the second half of the 18th century.

Over time, a [Viennese coffeehouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viennese_coffeehouse) culture developed. Writers, artists, musicians, intellectuals, bon vivants and their financiers met, and new coffee varieties were served. People played cards or chess, worked, read, thought, composed, discussed, argued, observed and just chatted. Much information was also obtained, because local and foreign newspapers were freely available to all customers. This form of coffeehouse culture spread throughout the [Habsburg Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habsburg_Empire) in the 19th century.[^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44]

Scientific theories, political plans and artistic projects were worked out and discussed in Viennese coffeehouses all over Central Europe. [James Joyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joyce) enjoyed his coffee in a Viennese coffeehouse on the [Adriatic Sea](23275478.md) in [Trieste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trieste), then and now the main port for coffee and coffee processing in Italy and Central Europe. From there, the Viennese *Kapuziner* coffee developed into today's [cappuccino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cappuccino). This special multicultural atmosphere of the Habsburg coffeehouses was largely destroyed by the later Nazism and communism and today can only be found in a few places that have long been in the slipstream of history, such as Vienna or Trieste.[^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46][^cite_note-47][^cite_note-48]

#### England

Main article: [English coffeehouses in the 17th and 18th centuries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_coffeehouses_in_the_17th_and_18th_centuries)









Various cafés in England

The first coffeehouse in England was opened on the High Street (on the site of the later Grand Café) in [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford) in 1650[^cite_note-49] or 1651[^cite_note-CBH-50] by "Jacob the Jew". A second, competing coffeehouse was opened across the street (on the site of the later [Queen's Lane Coffee House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen's_Lane_Coffee_House)) in 1654, by "Cirques Jobson, the Jew".[^cite_note-51] In [London](17867.md), the earliest coffeehouse was established by [Pasqua Rosée](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasqua_Ros%C3%A9e) (described as Greek,[^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53] Armenian,[^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55] or Turkish[^cite_note-56]) in 1652.[^cite_note-57] [Anthony Wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Wood_\(antiquary\)) observed of the coffeehouses of Oxford in 1674 "The decay of study, and consequently of learning, are coffy houses, to which most scholars retire and spend much of the day in hearing and speaking of news, in speaking vilely  of their superiors."[^cite_note-58] Pasqua Rosée was the servant of a trader in goods from the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md) named Daniel Edwards, who imported the coffee and assisted Rosée in setting up the establishment there.[^cite_note-Weinberg_2002-59][^cite_note-60]

From 1670 to 1685, London coffeehouses began to increase in number, and also in political importance due to their popularity as places of debate.[^cite_note-61] For the first several years, London's coffeehouses were the preserve of "a well-educated and commercial elite",[^cite_note-WhiteM-62] but from the 1660s their popularity increased. By 1675, there were more than 3,000 coffeehouses in England;[^cite_note-63] and in London alone there were perhaps 550 at their 18th-century peak.[^cite_note-64] Many men found a coffeehouse a convenient place for doing business, holding consultations there and having mail for them sent there, as well as keeping up with news. The coffeehouses were great social levelers, open to all men and indifferent to social status, and as a result associated with equality and republicanism. Entry gave access to books or print news. The rich intellectual atmosphere of early London coffeehouses was available to anyone who could pay the sometimes one penny entry fee, giving them the name "[penny universities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_coffeehouses_in_the_17th_and_18th_centuries#Early_Oxford_coffeehouses_\(%22penny_universities%22\))".[^cite_note-WhiteM-62]

Though [Charles II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_II_of_England) tried to suppress coffeehouses[^cite_note-65] as places where the "Idle and disaffected persons" met, and where "divers False, Malitious and Scandalous Reports are devised and spread abroad, to the Defamation of His Majesties  Government",[^cite_note-66] the public still flocked to them. For several decades following the [Restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restoration_England), the wits gathered around [John Dryden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dryden) at [Will's Coffee House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will's_Coffee_House), in [Bow Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bow_Street) (although [Jonathan Swift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Swift) was unimpressed).[^cite_note-WhiteM-62] As coffeehouses were believed to be areas where anti-government gossip could easily spread, [Queen Mary II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_II) and the London City magistrates tried to prosecute people who frequented coffeehouses as they were liable to "spread false and seditious reports". [William III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_III_of_England)'s privy council also suppressed [Jacobite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobitism) sympathizers in the 1680s and 1690s in coffeehouses as places that they believed harbored plotters against the regime.[^cite_note-67]

By the early 18th century, different coffeehouses attracted different clienteles, divided by occupation or opinion, such as [Tories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tory) and [Whigs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whigs_\(British_political_party\)), wits and [stockjobbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockjobber), merchants and lawyers, booksellers and authors, men of fashion or the "cits" of [the City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_London). According to one French visitor, [Abbé Prévost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abb%C3%A9_Pr%C3%A9vost), "You read \[in coffeehouses] for two-pence all the papers for or against the administration", and they were "the seats of English liberty".[^cite_note-68]

Coffeehouses not only boosted the popularity of print news culture, they also helped the growth of various financial markets including insurance and stocks. [Lloyd's Coffee House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lloyd's_Coffee_House) was where underwriters of ship insurance met to do business, leading to the establishment of [Lloyd's of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lloyd's_of_London) insurance market and other related businesses.[^cite_note-WhiteM-62] In 1773 the stockbrokers who had been meeting at [New Jonathan's Coffee-house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan's_Coffee-House) renamed it "[The Stock Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Stock_Exchange)".[^cite_note-69]

By the 1750s, the English consumption of tea had overtaken that of coffee. As tea could be easily prepared at home, newspapers were cheap, and there was a greater variety of places for leisure and entertainment, there was no obvious demand for publicly available teahouses. Later in the century, coffeehouses tended, via pricing and memberships, to cater for only a richer clientele, and "the death of coffee-house culture was assured".[^cite_note-WhiteM-62]

In [Victorian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_era) England, the [temperance movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperance_movement) set up coffeehouses (also known as coffee taverns) for the working classes, as a place of relaxation free of [alcohol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcohol_\(drug\)), an alternative to the [public house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_house).[^cite_note-70][^cite_note-71]

#### Finland



[Café Ekberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caf%C3%A9_Ekberg) in [Helsinki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helsinki) in 2024

Finland's first coffeehouse, Kaffehus, was founded in [Turku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turku) in 1778.[^cite_note-72] The oldest coffeehouse still operating in [Helsinki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helsinki), [Café Ekberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caf%C3%A9_Ekberg), was founded in 1852.[^cite_note-73]

#### France

When [Soliman Aga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suleiman_Aga) (sent to Versailles in 1669 by Sultan [Mehmed IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehmed_IV)) returned home, a member of his retinue, Pascal, remained and sold coffee from a stall at the market of Saint-Germain-des-Prés. He soon thereafter opened a coffeehouse in Paris, on the Quai de l'École near the [Pont Neuf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pont_Neuf), and others from the Near East rivaled him. But the fad for Turquerie soon ended.

"\[I]t was not until the establishment of the [Café de Procope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caf%C3%A9_Procope) in 1689 that coffee found a truly Parisian expression".[^cite_note-74] Owned by the Sicilian Procopio Cutò (François Procope), this was a popular meeting place of the French [Enlightenment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment); the *[Encyclopédie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclop%C3%A9die)* (1751–1772) of [Diderot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denis_Diderot) and [D'Alembert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_le_Rond_d'Alembert) is said to have had its start in conversations there between the two.[^cite_note-fitch43-75]

> The issue of slavery had a profound effect on the pre-Revolutionary thinkers who gathered at the Café Procope in Paris, including [Montesquieu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montesquieu), [Rousseau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_Rousseau), and Diderot. The ethics of the [slave trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nantes_slave_trade) were discussed whilst its very fruits were consumed, a dark liquid produced by dark skins in the depths of dark despair. Diderot wrote that the trade "is a business which violates religion, morality, natural law, and all human rights", whilst Rousseau railed against the supposed right to enslave as "absurd and meaningless".[^cite_note-76]

In its decor too, Café Procope had a lasting influence. Procopio had installed wall mirrors, marble-topped tables, and more that he had had removed from a bath-house that he had bought, thereby establishing what has become a convention for many other European cafés.[^cite_note-fitch43-75]

#### Hungary

The first known cafés in [Pest](36787.md) date back to 1714 when a house intended to serve as a café (Balázs Kávéfőző) was purchased. Minutes of the Pest City Council from 1729 mention complaints by the Balázs café and Franz Reschfellner café against the Italian-originated café of Francesco Bellieno for selling underpriced coffee.[^cite_note-77]

#### Ireland

In the 18th century, [Dublin](8504.md) coffeehouses functioned as early reading centers and the emergence of circulation and subscription libraries that provided greater access to printed material for the public. The connection of the coffeehouse with virtually every aspect of the print trade was evidenced by the incorporation of printing, publishing, selling, and viewing of newspapers, pamphlets and books on the premises, most notably for [Dick's Coffee House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dick's_Coffee_House), owned by [Richard Pue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Pue); thus contributing to a culture of reading and increased literacy.[^cite_note-Abbas_2014-78] These coffeehouses were social magnets where different strata of society joined to discuss topics covered by the newspapers and pamphlets. Most coffeehouses of the 18th century would eventually be equipped with their own printing presses or incorporate a bookshop.

#### Italy



[Caffè Florian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caff%C3%A8_Florian) in [Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venice)

The first café in Venice opened in 1683 under the [Procuratie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procuratie) Nuove of [Piazza San Marco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piazza_San_Marco).[^cite_note-79] The concept was popular:

> On \[Piazza San Marco] alone in the eighteenth century there were, under the Procuratie Vecchie, the following cafés: the *Re di Francia*, the *Abbondanza*, *Pitt l'eroe*, the *Regina d'Ungheria*, the *Orfeo*, the *Redentore*, the *Coraggio*, the *Speranza*, the *Arco Celeste* and *[Quadri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caff%C3%A8_Quadri)* opened in 1775 by Giorgio Quadri of [Corfù](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corfu), who was the first to serve genuine [Turkish coffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_coffee). Under the Procuratie Nuove were the *Angelo Custode*, the *Duca di Toscana*, the *Buon genio*, the *Doge*, the *Imperatore*, the *Imperatrice della Russia*, the *Tamerlano*, the *Fontana di Diana*, the *Dame Venete*, the *Aurora*, the *Piante d'oro*, the *Arabo*, the *Piastrelle*, the *Pace*, the *Venezia trionfante*, and *[Florian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caff%C3%A8_Florian)*, opened in 1720 by Floriano Francesconi.[^cite_note-80]

During the 18th century, the oldest extant coffeehouses in Italy were established. Venice aside, these included [Antico Caffè Greco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antico_Caff%C3%A8_Greco) (circa 1760) in [Rome](25458.md),[^cite_note-81] [Caffè Pedrocchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedrocchi_Caf%C3%A9) in [Padua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Padua), [Caffè dell'Ussero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palazzo_Agostini,_Pisa) in [Pisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisa), and [Caffè Fiorio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caff%C3%A8_Fiorio) in [Turin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turin).

#### Netherlands

Not to be confused with retail outlets for [cannabis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis), which are named [coffeeshop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffeeshop_\(Netherlands\)) in the Netherlands.

Coffeehouses (koffiehuizen) emerged in the [Dutch Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Republic) in the mid-17th century following the introduction of coffee through expanding trade networks with the [Middle East](19323.md) and the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md). The earliest documented references to coffee-related establishments in the Netherlands date to the early 1660s, particularly in Amsterdam. One of the earliest known coffeehouses in the country is recorded in The Hague, where a coffeehouse reportedly opened on the Korte Voorhout in 1664. At this time, coffee was a luxury commodity, primarily consumed by wealthier groups, while traditional beverages such as beer remained dominant. By the late 17th century, coffeehouses had become established in major Dutch cities, including Amsterdam, where contemporary sources indicate the presence of multiple coffee sellers and dedicated establishments. These venues also functioned as social spaces for conversation, gaming, and the exchange of news and information. Coffeehouses in the Dutch Republic formed part of a broader European development in which such establishments contributed to urban sociability and the circulation of public discourse.[^cite_note-82][^cite_note-83][^cite_note-84]

[Statue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statue) of [Fernando Pessoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fernando_Pessoa) by Lagoa Henriques, next to the [*A Brasileira*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caf%C3%A9_A_Brasileira) café, in [Chiado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiado), Lisbon

The history of coffee in [Portugal](23033.md) is usually said to have begun during the reign of king [John V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_V_of_Portugal), when Portuguese agent Francisco de Melo Palheta supposedly managed to steal coffee beans from French Guiana and introduce it to Brazil.[^cite_note-Ukers1922-85] From Brazil, coffee was taken to [Cape Verde](18962637.md) and [São Tomé and Príncipe](20026306.md), which were also Portuguese colonies at the time. Despite this story, coffee already existed in [Angola](701.md), having been introduced by [Portuguese missionaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missionaries).[^cite_note-ClarenceSmith1985-86] During the 18th century, the first public *cafés* appeared, inspired by French gatherings from the 17th century, becoming spaces for cultural and artistic entertainment.

Several cafés emerged in [Lisbon](18091.md) such as: *Martinho da Arcada* (the oldest café still operating, having opened in 1782), *Café Tavares*, and *Botequim Parras*. Of these, several became famous for harboring poets and artists, such as [Manuel du Bocage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuel_Maria_Barbosa_du_Bocage) with his visits to *Café Nicola,* opened in 1796 by the Italian Nicola Breteiro; and [Fernando Pessoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fernando_Pessoa) with his visits to [*A Brasileira*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caf%C3%A9_A_Brasileira), opened in 1905 by Adriano Teles. The most famous was the *Café Marrare*, opened by the Neapolitan Antonio Marrare, in 1820, and frequently visited by Júlio Castilho, Raimundo de Bulhão Pato, [Almeida Garrett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almeida_Garrett), [Alexandre Herculano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandre_Herculano) and other members of the Portuguese government and the [intelligentsia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligentsia). It began its saying: *Lisboa era [Chiado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiado), o Chiado era o Marrare e o Marrare ditava a lei* (English: 'Lisbon was the Chiado, the Chiado was the Marrare and the Marrare dictated the law').

Other coffeehouses soon opened across the country, such as *Café Vianna,* opened in [Braga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braga), in 1858, by Manoel José da Costa Vianna, and visited by important Portuguese writers such as [Camilo Castelo Branco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camilo_Castelo_Branco) and [Eça de Queirós](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E%C3%A7a_de_Queiroz). During the 1930s, a surge in coffeehouses happened in [Porto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porto) with the opening of several that still exist, such as *Café Guarany*, opened in 1933, and *A Regaleira*, opened in 1934.

#### Romania

In 1667, Kara Hamie, a former Ottoman [Janissary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janissary) from [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople), opened the first coffee shop in the center of [Bucharest](36877.md) (then the capital of the [Principality of Wallachia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallachia)). On its site today stands the main building of the [National Bank of Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bank_of_Romania).[^cite_note-87]

#### Switzerland

In 1761 the [Turm Kaffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turm_Kaffee?action=edit\&redlink=1), a shop for exported goods, was opened in [St. Gallen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Gallen).[^cite_note-88]

#### United States

The first coffeehouse in the United States opened in [Boston](24437894.md), in 1676.[^cite_note-94] However, Americans did not start choosing coffee over tea until the [Boston Tea Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston_Tea_Party) and the [Revolutionary War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War). After the Revolutionary War, Americans briefly went back to drinking tea until after the [War of 1812](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_1812) when they began importing high-quality coffee from Latin America and expensive inferior-quality tea from American shippers instead of Great Britain.[^cite_note-Wolf_WhatWeEat-95][^cite_note-96] Whether they were drinking coffee or tea, coffeehouses, like those in Great Britain, were places where business was done. In the 1780s, Merchant's Coffee House on Wall Street in New York City was home to the organization of the [Bank of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_New_York_Mellon_v._Realogy_Corp.) and the [New York Chamber of Commerce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamber_of_Commerce_of_the_State_of_New_York).[^cite_note-97]

Coffeehouses in the United States arose from the [espresso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Espresso)- and pastry-centered Italian coffeehouses of the Italian American immigrant communities in the major U.S. cities, notably [New York City](645042.md)'s [Little Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Italy,_Manhattan) and [Greenwich Village](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenwich_Village), Boston's [North End](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_End,_Boston), and San Francisco's [North Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Beach,_San_Francisco). From the late 1950s onward, coffeehouses also hosted entertainment, most commonly [folk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_music) performers during the [American folk music revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_folk_music_revival).[^cite_note-Groce_Coffeehouses-8][^cite_note-98] Both Greenwich Village and North Beach became major haunts of the [Beats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beat_Generation), who were highly identified with these coffeehouses. As the youth culture of the 1960s evolved, non-Italians consciously copied these coffeehouses. The political nature of much of 1960s folk music made the music a natural tie-in with coffeehouses with their association with political action. A number of well-known performers like [Joan Baez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_Baez) and [Bob Dylan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Dylan) began their careers performing in coffeehouses. [Blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blues) singer [Lightnin' Hopkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lightnin'_Hopkins) bemoaned his woman's overindulgence in coffeehouse socializing in his 1969 song "Coffeehouse Blues".

In 1966, [Alfred Peet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Peet) began applying the dark roast style to high quality beans and opened up a small shop in [Berkeley, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berkeley,_California) to educate customers on the virtues of good coffee.[^cite_note-Wolf_WhatWeEat-95] Starting in 1967 with the opening of the [Last Exit on Brooklyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_Exit_on_Brooklyn) coffeehouse, [Seattle](11388236.md) became known for its thriving [countercultural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterculture) coffeehouse scene; the [Starbucks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starbucks) chain later standardized and mainstreamed this [espresso bar](#Espresso_bar) model, now prevalent throughout the country.[^cite_note-99][^cite_note-100][^cite_note-101]

In the 21st century, North American usage has increasingly distinguished between the terms "coffee shop" and "café" based on their operational models and revenue streams. While often used interchangeably in casual speech, a **coffee shop** is typically characterized by a focus on "[extraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffee_extraction)" (beverages), [counter service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counter_service), and high-volume throughput. In contrast, a **café** often functions as a hybrid between a restaurant and a traditional coffeehouse, prioritizing seated dining and a higher average transaction value. This distinction is further defined by physical infrastructure; cafés often require full commercial kitchen licensing, including [grease traps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grease_trap) and high-capacity [ventilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ventilation_\(architecture\)) for meal preparation, whereas coffee shops frequently operate under "light food" licenses that prioritize [the espresso machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Espresso_machine) as the central focal point of the space.[^cite_note-102]

From the 1960s through the mid-1980s, churches and individuals in the United States used the coffeehouse concept for outreach. They were often storefronts and had names like *The Lost Coin* ([Greenwich Village](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenwich_Village)), *The Gathering Place* ([Riverside, CA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riverside,_California)), *Catacomb Chapel* (New York City), and *Jesus for You* ([Buffalo, NY](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buffalo,_New_York)). Christian music (often guitar-based) was performed, coffee and food provided, and [Bible studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible_study_\(Christianity\)) convened as people of varying backgrounds gathered in a casual setting that was purposefully different from traditional churches.

## Contemporary history

See also: [List of coffeehouse chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_coffeehouse_chains)



Coffeehouses often sell [pastries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastries) or other food items.

A café may have an outdoor section (on a terrace, pavement or [sidewalk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidewalk_cafe)) with seats, tables and parasols. This is especially common in Europe. Cafés offer a more open public space than many of the traditional pubs they have replaced, which were more male-dominated with a focus on alcohol.

One of the original uses of the café, as a place for information exchange and communication, was reintroduced in the 1990s with the [Internet café](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_caf%C3%A9) or [hotspot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotspot_\(Wi-Fi\)).[^cite_note-103] The spread of modern-style cafés to urban and rural areas went hand-in-hand with the rising use of mobile computers. Computers and Internet access and contemporary decor help to create a youthful, modern place, compared to the traditional pubs or old-fashioned [diners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diner) that they replaced.

### Africa

#### Egypt

Coffeehouses in Egypt are colloquially called *ʿahwah* /ʔhwa/, the dialectal pronunciation of قَهْوة *qahwah* (literally "coffee")[^cite_note-105] These were named after what was then the most popular drink that they served,[^cite_note-Gezim-106] though also commonly served in *ʿahwah* are tea (*shāy*) and [herbal teas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbal_tea), especially the highly popular [hibiscus blend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hibiscus_tea) ([Egyptian Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_Arabic): *karkadeh* or *ennab*).

The first *ʿahwah* opened around the 1850s but for a long time were patronized mostly by older people, with youths frequenting but not always ordering. However:

> By the 1920s, there were many coffee shops throughout Egypt. They could be classified into three major kinds: the [Alexandrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria) *bursa*, the [Cairo](6293.md) club, and the rural *gharza* (inn). ... \[All three] gradually became significant centres where Egyptians sipped the spirit of integration and unity.[^cite_note-Gezim-106]

#### Ethiopia

In [Addis Ababa](52593.md), the capital of Ethiopia, independent coffeehouses that struggled before 1991 have become popular with young professionals who do not have time for traditional coffee roasting at home.[^cite_note-107] One that has become well known is [Tomoca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomoca_Coffee), which opened in 1953.[^cite_note-108]

### Asia

#### India

In India, coffee culture has expanded in the past twenty years. Chains like [Indian Coffee House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Coffee_House), [Café Coffee Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caf%C3%A9_Coffee_Day), and [Barista Lavazza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barista_Lavazza) have become very popular. Cafés are considered good places to conduct office meetings and for friends to meet.[^cite_note-109]

#### China

In China, a growing number of domestic coffeehouse chains have emerged since the 1990s. Among them, [Luckin Coffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luckin_Coffee), founded in Beijing in 2017, has become a notable homegrown challenger to Western brands that have dominated China's market such as [Starbucks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starbucks). By leveraging a digital-first, app-based model and aggressive pricing, Luckin rapidly expanded to over 20,000 stores in China by 2024, overtaking Starbucks as the country's largest coffee chain by revenue and store count. Starbucks' market share in China fell from a peak of 42% in 2017 to 14% in 2024, while Luckin posted strong revenue and profit growth. In 2025, Luckin took its rivalry global by opening its first U.S. stores in [New York City](645042.md), signaling the rise of a domestic Chinese coffee brand on the world stage. Coffee culture continues to expand in major cities, though tea remains the dominant and historical daily beverage for most of the population.[^cite_note-LuckinCampaign-110][^cite_note-LuckinFoodTalks-111][^cite_note-LuckinYicai-112][^cite_note-LuckinInvestor-113]

In Malaysia and Singapore, traditional breakfast and coffee shops are called *[kopi tiam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kopi_tiam)*. The word is a [compound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compound_\(linguistics\)) of the [Malay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malay_language) word for coffee (as borrowed and altered from English) and the [Hokkien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Min_Nan) dialect word for shop (店; [POJ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pe%CC%8Dh-%C5%8De-j%C4%AB): tiàm). Menus typically offer a variety of simple dishes based on [egg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eggs_as_food), [toast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toast_\(food\)), and [coconut jam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coconut_jam), plus coffee, tea, and [Milo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milo_\(drink\)), a popular malted chocolate drink.

#### Indonesia

In Indonesia, traditional coffeehouses are called *kedai kopi*, *rumah kopi*, or *warung kopi* which is often abbreviated as *warkop*. *[Kopi tubruk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kopi_tubruk)* (resembling [Turkish coffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_coffee)) is a common drink in small *warkop*. To accompany this, traditional *[kue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kue)* is also served. The first coffeehouse in Indonesia was founded in 1878 in [Jakarta](16275.md) and named *Warung Tinggi Tek Sun Ho*.[^cite_note-114]

#### Philippines



A coffeehouse stand in [Angeles City](842728.md), [Philippines](23440.md)

In the Philippines, coffee shop chains like [Starbucks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starbucks) have become the prevalent hangouts for upper- and middle-class professionals in such districts as the [Makati CBD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makati_Central_Business_District). However, *carinderias* (small eateries) continue to serve coffee alongside breakfast and snack dishes. A number of places often called "cafés" serve not just coffee and pastries but full meals, often international cuisine adapted to Filipino tastes.[^cite_note-115]

#### Thailand

In Thailand, the term "café" not only is a coffeehouse as understood elsewhere, but in the past was considered a [bar](272207.md) serving [alcoholic drinks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcoholic_drink) during a comedy show on stage. This type of business flourished in the 1990s, before the [1997 financial crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997_Asian_financial_crisis).[^cite_note-116]

The first real coffeehouse in Thailand opened in 1917 at the [Si Kak Phraya Si](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Si_Kak_Phraya_Si) in the area of [Rattanakosin Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rattanakosin_Island), by [Madam Cole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edna_Sarah_Cole), an American woman then living in Thailand. Later, [Chao Phraya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phraya) Ram Rakop (เจ้าพระยารามราฆพ), a Thai aristocrat, opened a coffeehouse named "Café de Norasingha" (คาเฟ่นรสิงห์) at Sanam Suea Pa (สนามเสือป่า), next to the [Royal Plaza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Plaza_\(Thailand\)).[^cite_note-117] Café de Norasingha has been renovated and moved within [Phayathai Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phayathai_Palace).[^cite_note-118] In the [southern region](1453076.md), a traditional coffeehouse or *kopi tiam* is popular with locals, like many countries in the [Malay Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malay_Peninsula).[^cite_note-119]

### Australia



The [Federal Coffee Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Coffee_Palace), built on Collins Street, Melbourne, in 1888, was the largest and grandest "coffee palace" ever built. It was demolished in 1973.



[Centre Place, Melbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre_Place,_Melbourne). Australia and New Zealand have competing claims to being the birthplace of the "[flat white](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_white)".

In the 19th century, coffeehouses such as the [Federal Coffee Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Coffee_Palace) in the center of [Melbourne](17306237.md) were part of the [temperance movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperance_movement).[^cite_note-120]

In modern Australia, coffee shops are commonly called cafés. Since the post-[World War II](32927.md) influx of Italian and Greek immigrants introduced the first espresso machines to Australia in the 1950s, there was initially a slow rise in café culture, particularly in Melbourne, until a nationwide boom in locally-owned cafés began in the 1990s.[^cite_note-121] Alongside the rise in the number of cafés has been a rise in demand for locally (or on-site) roasted specialty coffee, particularly in [Sydney](27862.md) and Melbourne. A local favorite is the "[flat white](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_white)".[^cite_note-122]

### Europe



A [sidewalk cafe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidewalk_cafe) in Spain

In most European countries, such as [Spain](26667.md), [Austria](26964606.md), [Denmark](76972.md), Germany, [Norway](21241.md), [Sweden](5058739.md), [Portugal](23033.md), and others, the term *café* means a restaurant primarily serving coffee, as well as [pastries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastry) such as [cakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cake), [tarts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tart), [pies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pie), and [buns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bun). Many cafés also serve light meals such as sandwiches. European cafés often have tables on the pavement (sidewalk) as well as indoors. Some, particularly in Southern Europe, also serve alcoholic drinks (e.g., wine). In the Netherlands and Belgium, a *café* is the equivalent of a bar, and also sells alcoholic drinks. In the Netherlands a *koffiehuis* serves coffee, while a "[coffee shop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis_coffee_shops)" (the English term) sells "soft" drugs ([cannabis](1481886.md) and [hashish](14415.md)) and is generally not allowed to sell alcoholic drinks. In France, most cafés serve as lunch restaurants in the day, and bars in the evening. They generally do not have pastries except in the mornings, when a [croissant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croissant) or [pain au chocolat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pain_au_chocolat) can be bought with breakfast coffee.

In Italy, cafés are similar to those found in France and known as *bar*. They typically serve a variety of espresso coffee, cakes and alcoholic drinks. Bars in city centers usually have different prices for consumption at the bar and consumption at a table.[^cite_note-123]

#### Ireland

Today, the word *café* – also spelled *cafe*,[^cite_note-124] but always pronounced as two syllables – is used for most coffeehouses. It has also come to be used for a type of diner that offers cooked meals (again, without alcoholic beverages) which can be standalone or operating within shopping centres or department stores.

#### United Kingdom

The patrons of the early English coffeehouses were far removed from those of modern Britain. Haunts for teenagers in particular, [Italian-run](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italians_in_the_United_Kingdom) espresso bars and their [formica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formica_\(plastic\))-topped tables were a feature of 1950s [Soho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soho) that provided a backdrop as well as a title for [Cliff Richard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cliff_Richard)'s 1960 film *[Expresso Bongo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expresso_Bongo)*. The first of these was the Moka in [Frith Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frith_Street), opened by [Gina Lollobrigida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gina_Lollobrigida) in 1953.[^cite_note-ClassicCafes-125] The late 50s Soho cafes in the film have "\[an] exotic [Gaggia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaggia) [coffee machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Espresso_machine) ... \[and] [Coke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coca-Cola), [Pepsi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pepsi), weak frothy coffee and a Suncrush orange fountain";[^cite_note-Perry-126] they spread to other urban centers during the 1960s, providing affordable, warm places for young people to congregate and an ambience far removed from the global coffee bar standard set in the final decades of the century by chains such as [Starbucks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starbucks) and [Pret a Manger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pret_a_Manger).[^cite_note-Perry-126][^cite_note-ClassicCafes-125]

### Espresso bar

Interior of an espresso bar in [Baliwag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baliwag), Philippines

The **espresso bar** is a type of coffeehouse that specializes in coffee drinks made from [espresso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Espresso). Originating in Italy, it has spread throughout the world in various forms. International chains include [Starbucks Coffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starbucks), based in [Seattle](11388236.md), U.S., and [Costa Coffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Costa_Coffee), based in [Loudwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loudwater,_Buckinghamshire), U.K. (the first and second largest coffeehouse chains respectively), although the espresso bar exists in some form throughout much of the world.

The espresso bar typically has a long counter with a high-yield [espresso machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Espresso_machine) (usually bean to cup machines, automatic or semiautomatic pump-type machine, although occasionally a manually operated lever-and-piston system) and a display case containing pastries and occasionally savory items such as sandwiches. In the traditional Italian bar, customers either order at the bar and consume their drinks standing or, if they wish to sit down and be served, are usually charged a higher price. In some bars there is an additional charge for drinks served at an outside table. In other countries, especially the United States, seating areas for customers to relax and work are provided free of charge. Some espresso bars also sell coffee paraphernalia, candy, and even music. North American espresso bars were also at the forefront of the proliferation of public [Wi-Fi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wi-Fi) access points to provide Internet services to people working on laptop computers.

The offerings at the typical espresso bar are generally quite Italianate in inspiration; [biscotti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biscotti), [cannoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannoli) and [pizzelle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pizzelle) are common traditional accompaniments to a [caffè latte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latte) or [cappuccino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cappuccino). Some espresso bars even offer alcoholic drinks such as grappa and sambuca. Nevertheless, typical pastries are not always strictly Italianate and common additions include [scones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scones), [muffins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muffins), [croissants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croissant), and even [doughnuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doughnut). There is usually a large selection of teas as well, and the North American espresso bar culture is responsible for the popularization of the Indian spiced tea drink [masala chai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masala_chai). Iced drinks are also popular in some countries, including both iced tea and iced coffee as well as blended drinks such as Starbucks' [Frappucino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frappucino).

A worker in an espresso bar is called a [barista](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barista). This is a skilled position that requires familiarity with the drinks being made (often very elaborate, especially in North American-style espresso bars) and a reasonable facility with some equipment, as well as the usual customer service skills.
