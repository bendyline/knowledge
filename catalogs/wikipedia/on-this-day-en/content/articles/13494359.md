**Sudanese Arabs** ([Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language): عرب سودانيون, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Arabic): *ʿarab sūdāniyyūn*) are the inhabitants of [Sudan](27421.md) who identify as [Arabs](2185.md) and speak [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudanese_Arabic) as their mother tongue.[^cite_note-SN-3] Sudanese Arabs make up 70% of the population of [Sudan](27421.md);[^cite_note-Sudan-1] however, prior to the independence of [South Sudan](32350676.md) in 2011, Sudanese Arabs made up 40% of the population.[^cite_note-4] They are [Sunni Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam) and speak [Sudanese Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudanese_Arabic).

## History

The Sudanese Arab ethnic group finds its origins in the centuries-long admixture of indigenous African populations with Arab immigrants as well as from cultural and [linguistic shifts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_shift) to an [Arab](2185.md) identity, culture, and language leading to a unique cultural identity. Prior to [Arabization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabization), [Sudan](27421.md) was mainly inhabited by [Cushitic-speaking groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cushitic-speaking_peoples) like the [Beja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beja_people) and [Nilo-Saharan peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilo-Saharan_languages) such as the [Nubians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nubians), whose civilizations, including the ancient kingdoms of [Kush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kush) and [Meroe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mero%C3%AB), left their mark on the region's early history. Particularly famous is the developmentally high architectural, artistic, and political achievements of the Nubians along the [Nile](21244.md).[^cite_note-minorityrights.org-5][^cite_note-Encyclopedia_Britannica-6]

The transformation toward an Arab identity accelerated with the arrival of Arab tribes during the 15th–19th centuries. These tribes, such as the [Juhaynah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juhaynah) and [Rufa'a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rufa'a_people), brought [Islam](6037917.md) and the [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic) language, which became widely spread across Sudan. This interaction was characterized by marriage, trade, and assimilation of the indigenous people into Arab-Islamic culture. The Arabic language became the primary medium of communication and a symbol of social and religious prestige that contributed to cultural change.[^cite_note-minorityrights.org-5][^cite_note-Encyclopedia_Britannica-6]

This [Arabization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabization) was not, however, uniformly experienced. Whereas the northern and central regions became identified with Arabism, the [southern](767653.md) and [western parts](605837.md) of the Sudan retained very distinct [languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Sudan), traditions, and [religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_Sudan), more often than not resisting assimilation. This complex ethnic and cultural history informs the Sudanese Arab group today, which is the single largest ethnic bloc and enjoys the greatest influence over political, social, and cultural development in the country.[^cite_note-minorityrights.org-5][^cite_note-Encyclopedia_Britannica-6]

## Regional variation

Arab tribes arrived in Sudan in three main waves, beginning with the [Ja'alin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ja'alin_tribe) in the 12th century. The Ja'alin trace their lineage to [Abbas ibn Abd al-Muttalib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbas_ibn_Abd_al-Muttalib) and their culture was closely linked with that of the [Bedouin](61770.md) in [Arabia](47858.md). The second main wave was the migration of the [Juhaynah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juhaynah) before the 17th century in two main subgroups, the [Baggara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baggara_Arabs) and [Kabbabish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kababish_tribe). The final main wave was the migration of [Bani Rashid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashaida_people) in the mid-19th century.[^cite_note-SN-3]

The great majority of the Sudanese Arabs tribes are part of larger tribal confederations: the [Ja'alin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ja'alin_tribe), who primarily live along the [Nile river](21244.md) basin between [Khartoum](17320.md) and [Abu Hamad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Hamad); the [Shaigiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaigiya_tribe), who live along the Nile between [Korti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korti) and [Jabal al-Dajer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jebel_Dair), and parts of the [Bayuda Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayuda_Desert); the [Juhaynah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juhaynah), who live east and west of the Nile, and include the [Rufaa people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rufaa_people), the [Shukria clan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shukria_clan) and the [Kababish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kababish); the [Banu Fazara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banu_Fazara) or [Fezara people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fezara_people) who live in [Northern Kordofan](767669.md); the [Kawahla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kawahla_people), who inhabit [eastern Sudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Sudan), [Northern Kordofan](767669.md), and [White Nile State](767622.md); and the [Baggara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baggara_Arabs), who inhabit [South Kordofan](767653.md) and extend to [Lake Chad](59314.md). There are numerous smaller tribal units that do not conform to the above groups, such as the Messelemiya, the Rikabia, the [Hawawir people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawawir_people), the Magharba, the [Awadia and Fadnia tribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Awadia_and_Fadnia_tribes), the Kerriat, the Kenana people, the Kerrarish, the Hamran, amongst others.[^cite_note-7]

[Sudan](27421.md) also houses non-Sudanese Arab populations such as the [Rashaida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashaida) that only recently settled in Sudan in 1846, after migrating from the [Hejaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hejaz) region of the [Arabian Peninsula](47858.md).[^cite_note-8] Additionally, other smaller Sudanese groups who have also been [Arabized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabization), or partially Arabized, but retain a separate, non-[Arab identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_identity), include the [Nubians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nubians), [Copts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copts), and [Beja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beja_people). Most Sudanese Arabs speak modern [Sudanese Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudanese_Arabic), with western Sudanese tribes bordering [Chad](5488.md), like the [Darfurians](605837.md), generally speaking [Chadian Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chadian_Arabic). Sudanese Arabs have large variations in culture and genealogy because of their descent from a combination of various population groups.[^cite_note-9] Other Arab populations in Sudan that are not Sudanese Arab, (i.e. those that are recent arrivals to the region exist), most of them such as the [Awadia and Fadnia tribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Awadia_and_Fadnia_tribes), the [Bani Hassan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beni_%E1%B8%A4ass%C4%81n), Al-Ashraf and [Rashaida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashaida) tribes generally speak [Hejazi Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hejazi_Arabic) instead of the more widespread [Sudanese Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudanese_Arabic).

## Sudanese Arabic

Main article: [Sudanese Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudanese_Arabic)

The variety of Arabic spoken by Sudanese Arabs has evolved from the [varieties of Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varieties_of_Arabic) brought by [Arabs](2185.md) who migrated to the region after the signing of the [Treaty of Baqt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baqt), a 7th-century treaty between the Muslim rulers of Egypt and the Nubian kingdom of [Makuria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makuria). Testimonies by travelers to the areas that would become modern-day Sudan, like [Ibn Battuta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Battuta), indicate that Arabic coexisted alongside indigenous Sudanese languages, with multilingualism in Arabic and non-Arabic Sudanese languages being well attested by travelers to the region up until the 19th century.[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11] Sudanese Arabic has characteristics similar to [Egyptian Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_Arabic). As a point of difference, though, the Sudanese dialect retains some archaic pronunciation patterns, such as the letter ج, and it also exhibits characteristics of the ancient [Nobiin language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobiin_language) that once covered the region.[^cite_note-12] Accordingly, linguists have identified a variety of influences from [Nubian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nubian_languages), [Beja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beja_language), [Fur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fur_language), [Nilotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilotic_languages), and other Sudanese languages on the vocabulary and phonology of Sudanese Arabic.[^cite_note-SCD-13]

Sudanese Arabic is highly diverse. Famed Sudanese linguist [Awn ash-Sharif Gasim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Awn_Alsharif_Qasim) noted that "it is difficult to speak of a 'Sudanese colloquial language' in general, simply because there is not a single dialect used simultaneously in all the regions where Arabic is the mother tongue. Every region, and almost every tribe, has its own brand of Arabic."[^cite_note-14] However, [Gasim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Awn_Alsharif_Qasim) broadly distinguishes between the varieties spoken by sedentary groups along the Nile (such as the [Ja'aliyyin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ja'alin_tribe)) and pastoralist groups (such as [the Baggara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baggara_Arabs) groups of west Sudan).[^cite_note-SCD-13] The most widely-spoken variety of Sudanese is variably referred to as Central Sudanese Arabic, Central Urban Sudanese Arabic, or Khartoum Arabic,[^cite_note-15] which more closely resembles varieties spoken by sedentary groups.

In 1889, the Journal of the Royal Anthropological Institute of Great Britain claimed that the Arabic spoken in Sudan was "a pure but archaic Arabic". The pronunciation of certain letters was like Hijazi, and not Egyptian, such as g being the pronunciation for the Arabic letter Qāf and J being the pronunciation for Jeem.[^cite_note-16]

## Genetics

In 2007, the mtDNA haplotype diversity for 102 individuals in Northern Sudan was analysed. The haplogroup distribution was 22.5% of Eurasian ancestry, 4.9% of the East African M1 lineage, and 72.5% of sub-Saharan affiliation.[^cite_note-17]

According to [Y-DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y-DNA) analysis by Hassan et al. (2008), among Sudanese Arabs, 67% of Arakien, 43% of [Messiria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messiria), and 40% of [Ja'alin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ja'alin) individuals carry the [Haplogroup J](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_J_\(Y-DNA\)). The remainder mainly belong to the [E1b1b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_E-M215_\(Y-DNA\)) clade, which is borne by 18% of Ja'aliyeen, 17% of Arakien, and 14% of Messiria. The next most frequently observed haplogroups among Sudanese Arabs are the European-associated [R1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_R1) clade (25% Messiria, 16% Ja'alin, 8% Arakien), followed by the Eurasian lineage [F](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_F_\(Y-DNA\)) (11% Messiria, 10% Ja'alin, 8% Arakien), the Europe-associated [I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_I-M170) clade (7% Messiria, 4% Ja'alin), and the African [A3b2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_A_\(Y-DNA\)) haplogroup (6% Ja'alin).[^cite_note-Hassan2008-18]

Maternally, Hassan (2009) observed that over 90% of the Sudanese Arabs samples carried various subclades of the [Macrohaplogroup L](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macro-haplogroup_L_\(mtDNA\)). Of these [mtDNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MtDNA) lineages, the most frequently borne clade was [L3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_L3_\(mtDNA\)) (68% Ja'alin, 40% Messiria, 24% Arakien), followed by the [L2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_L2_\(mtDNA\)) (53% Arakien, 33% Messiria, 9% Ja'alin), [L0a1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_L0) (13% Messiria), [L1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_L1_\(mtDNA\)) (7% Messiria, 5% Ja'alin), and [L5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_L5_\(mtDNA\)) (9% Ja'alin, 6% Arakien) haplogroups. The remaining \~10% of Sudanese Arabs belonged to sublineages of the Eurasian macrohaplogroup [N](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_N_\(mtDNA\)) (Arakien: 6% preHV1, 6% [N1a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_N1a_\(mtDNA\)), 6% N/[J1b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_J_\(mtDNA\)); Ja'alin: 9% [preHV1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haplogroup_R0_\(mtDNA\)); Messiria: 7% N/J1b).[^cite_note-Hassan2009-19]

Dobon et al. (2015) identified an ancestral autosomal component of West Eurasian origin that is common to many modern Sudanese Arabs (as well as other Northern Sudanese populations). Known as the *Coptic* component, it peaks among Egyptian [Copts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copts) who settled in Sudan over the past two centuries. The scientists associate the Coptic component with Ancient Egyptian ancestry, without the later Arabian influence that is present among other Egyptians.[^cite_note-Dobon2015-20]

Hollfelder et al. (2017) analysed various populations in Sudan, and observed Sudanese Arabs showed signs of also having younger admixture dates, from the fall of Dongola in 1315-1316 AD. With the authors noting:

> The Eurasian admixture proportion in the Arab populations is high, ranging between \~40%–48%. The presence of a northeast African genetic signature similar to Nilotic populations and the recent admixture signal from Eurasia indicates that the populations in central Sudan that self-identify as Arab were originally a local northeast African population (similar to the Nubians and the Beja) that mixed with a Eurasian population during the Arab expansion, or possibly earlier. However, the mixed groups kept the language and culture of the incoming migrants.[^cite_note-Hollfelder_et_al-21]
