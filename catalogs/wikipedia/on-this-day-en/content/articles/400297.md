**County Durham**,[^cite_note-3] also called simply **Durham**, is a [ceremonial county](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceremonial_county) in [North East England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_East_England).[^cite_note-lieutenancies19972-4] The county borders [Northumberland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northumberland) and [Tyne and Wear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyne_and_Wear) to the north, the [North Sea](21179.md) to the east, [North Yorkshire](143759.md) to the south, and [Cumbria](73021.md) to the west. The largest settlement is [Darlington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darlington).

The county has an area of 2,676 square kilometres (1,033 sq mi) and had an estimated population of 894,025 in 2024. The centre and east of the county are more densely populated than the west, and the major settlements include [Hartlepool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartlepool) on the south-east coast, [Stockton-on-Tees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockton-on-Tees) in the south-east, Darlington in the south, and the city of [Durham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durham,_England) in the north-centre. Stockton-on-Tees is part of the [Teesside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teesside) conurbation, which extends into North Yorkshire. [Barnard Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard_Castle) is the largest town in the west of the county. For [local government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_government_in_England) purposes the county comprises the [unitary authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_authorities_of_England) areas of [County Durham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Durham_\(district\)), [Darlington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borough_of_Darlington), [Hartlepool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borough_of_Hartlepool), and part of [Stockton-on-Tees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borough_of_Stockton-on-Tees). [Durham County Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durham_County_Council) is part of the [North East Combined Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_East_Combined_Authority), and the councils of the other three areas are part of the [Tees Valley Combined Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tees_Valley_Combined_Authority). The part of Tyne and Wear south of the [River Tyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Tyne) was historically part of County Durham, and the part of Durham south of the River Tees was historically part of the [North Riding of Yorkshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Riding_of_Yorkshire).

The west of the county contains part of the [North Pennines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Pennines) uplands, a [national landscape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Landscape). The hills are the source of the rivers [Tees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Tees) and [Wear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Wear), which flow east and form the valleys of [Teesdale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teesdale) and [Weardale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weardale) respectively. The east of the county is flatter, and the two rivers meander through it; the Tees forms the boundary with North Yorkshire in its lower reaches, and the Wear exits the county near [Chester-le-Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chester-le-Street) in the north-east. The [county's coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durham_Coast) is characterised by tall limestone and dolomite cliffs and is a [site of special scientific interest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Site_of_special_scientific_interest).

What is now County Durham was on the border of [Roman Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Britain), and contains survivals of this era at sites such as [Binchester Roman Fort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binchester_Roman_Fort). In the [Anglo-Saxon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_settlement_of_Britain) period the region was part of the [Kingdom of Northumbria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Northumbria). In 995 the city of Durham was founded by monks seeking a place safe from [Viking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viking_Age) raids to house the relics of St [Cuthbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuthbert). [Durham Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durham_Cathedral) was rebuilt after the [Norman Conquest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Conquest), and together with [Durham Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durham_Castle) is now a [World Heritage Site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durham_Castle_and_Cathedral). By the [late Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Middle_Ages) the county was [governed semi-independently](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Palatine_of_Durham) by the [bishops of Durham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishops_of_Durham) and was also a buffer zone between England and Scotland. County Durham became heavily industrialised in the nineteenth century, when many [collieries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collieries) opened on the [Durham coalfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durham_coalfield). The [Stockton and Darlington Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockton_and_Darlington_Railway), the world's first public railway to use steam locomotives, opened in 1825.[^cite_note-_heritage2-5] Most collieries closed during the last quarter of the twentieth century, but the county's coal mining heritage is remembered in the annual [Durham Miners' Gala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durham_Miners'_Gala).

## Toponymy

The ceremonial county is officially named *Durham*,[^cite_note-lieutenancies-6] but the county has long been commonly known as *County* Durham and it is the only [English county](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_county) name prefixed with "County" in common usage (a practice common in [Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counties_of_Ireland)). Its unusual naming (for an English shire) is explained to some extent by the relationship with the [Bishops of Durham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishops_of_Durham), who for centuries governed Durham as a [county palatine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_palatine) (the [County Palatine of Durham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Palatine_of_Durham)) outside the usual structure of county administration in England.

Early attestations of the county name include *Dunelmensischira* (Durhamshire) from c. 1100 and *Comitatus Dunelmensis* (County Durham) from 1344.[^cite_note-UNottsurvey-7]

The situation regarding the formal name in modern local government is less clear:

- The 2009 structural change legislation

  [^cite_note-cdsc2008-8]

  &#x20;created the present [unitary council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_authorities_of_England) (that covers a large part – but not all – of the ceremonial county) refers to "the [county](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-metropolitan_county) of County Durham" and names the new unitary [district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-metropolitan_district) "County Durham" too.&#x20;

  - A later amendment to that legislation

    [^cite_note-9]

    &#x20;refers to the "county of Durham" and the amendment allows for the unitary council to name itself "The Durham Council".

- The council retains the name as [Durham County Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durham_County_Council). With either option, the name does not include *County* Durham.

- The [former postal county](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Former_postal_county) was named "County Durham" to distinguish it from the [post town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post_town) of Durham.

## History

Main article: [History of County Durham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_County_Durham)

### Anglian Kingdom of Bernicia

Main article: [Bernicia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernicia)

Around 547 CE, an Angle named [Ida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ida_of_Bernicia) founded the kingdom of Bernicia after spotting the defensive potential of a large rock at Bamburgh, upon which many a fortification was thenceforth built.[^cite_note-Gething-10] Ida was able to forge, hold and consolidate the kingdom; although the native British tried to take back their land, the Angles triumphed and the kingdom endured.

### Kingdom of Northumbria

Main article: [Kingdom of Northumbria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Northumbria)

In 604 CE, Ida's grandson [Æthelfrith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%86thelfrith) forcibly merged Bernicia (ruled from [Bamburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bamburgh)) and Deira (ruled from [York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/York), which was known as *Eforwic* at the time) to create the [Kingdom of Northumbria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Northumbria). In time, the realm was expanded, primarily through warfare and conquest; at its height, the kingdom stretched from the River Humber (from which the kingdom drew its name) to the Forth. Eventually, factional fighting and the rejuvenated strength of neighbouring kingdoms, most notably Mercia, led to Northumbria's decline.[^cite_note-Gething-10] The arrival of the Vikings hastened this decline, and the Scandinavian raiders eventually claimed the Deiran part of the kingdom in 867 (which became [Jórvík](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%B3rv%C3%ADk)). The land that would become County Durham now sat on the border with the [Great Heathen Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Heathen_Army), a border which today still forms the boundaries between the historic counties of Yorkshire and County Durham.



The Church of [St Mary and St Cuthbert, Chester-le-Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Mary_and_St_Cuthbert,_Chester-le-Street), on the site of the wooden ninth century church built by the Lindisfarne monks

Viking settlement petered out at the Tees and the lands north thereof lay outside of the Danelaw. The House of Bamburgh, successors of the Kings of Bernicia and Northumbria, retained control north of the Tyne; between the Tyne and the Tees emerged the Community of St. Cuthbert or *Haliwerfolc*, successors of the See of Lindisfarne. The House of Wessex absorbed the Kingdom of York into the unified English Kingdom in 954 and claimed overlordship over Northumbria. However, the lands north of the Tees remained outside the governmental frontiers of the English realm, thus Bamburgh and the Haliwerfolc went on to become contested buffer states between England and Scotland.[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12]

### City of Durham founded

Main article: [Durham, England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durham,_England)

In 995 CE, St Cuthbert's community, who had been transporting Cuthbert's remains around, partly in an attempt to avoid them falling into the hands of Viking raiders, settled at Dunholm (Durham) on a site that was defensively favourable due to the horseshoe-like course of the River Wear.[^cite_note-13] St Cuthbert's remains were placed in a shrine in the White Church, which was originally a wooden structure but was eventually fortified into a stone building.

Once the City of Durham had been founded, the Bishops of Durham gradually acquired the lands that would become County Durham. Bishop Aldhun began this process by procuring land in the Tees and Wear valleys, including Norton, Stockton, Escomb and Aucklandshire in 1018. In 1031, [King Canute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Canute) gave Staindrop to the Bishops. This territory continued to expand, and was eventually given the status of a [liberty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberty_\(division\)). Under the control of the Bishops of Durham, the land had various names: the "Liberty of Durham", "Liberty of St Cuthbert's Land" "the lands of St Cuthbert between Tyne and Tees" or "the Liberty of Haliwerfolc" (holy [Wear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Wear) folk).[^cite_note-scammell-14]

The bishops' special jurisdiction rested on claims that King [Ecgfrith of Northumbria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecgfrith_of_Northumbria) had granted a substantial territory to [St Cuthbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Cuthbert) on his election to the [see](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episcopal_see) of [Lindisfarne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindisfarne) in 684. In about 883 a cathedral housing the saint's remains was established at [Chester-le-Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chester-le-Street) and [Guthfrith, King of York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guthfrith,_King_of_York) granted the community of St Cuthbert the area between the [Tyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Tyne) and the [Wear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Wear), before the community reached its final destination in 995, in Durham.

Following the [Norman invasion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_conquest_of_England), the administrative machinery of government extended only slowly into northern England. Northumberland's first recorded [Sheriff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Sheriff_of_Northumberland) was Gilebert from 1076 until 1080 and a 12th-century record records Durham regarded as within the shire.[^cite_note-15] However, the bishops disputed the authority of the sheriff of Northumberland and his officials, despite the second sheriff, for example, being the reputed slayer of [Malcolm Canmore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malcolm_Canmore), King of Scots. The crown regarded Durham as falling within Northumberland until the late thirteenth century.

### County Palatine of Durham

Main article: [County Palatine of Durham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Palatine_of_Durham)

[Map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special\:Map/8/54.7/-1.68/en) Matters regarding the bishopric of Durham came to a head in 1293 when the bishop and his steward failed to attend proceedings of *[quo warranto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quo_warranto)* held by the justices of Northumberland. The bishop's case went before parliament, where he stated that Durham lay outside the bounds of any English shire and that *"from time immemorial it had been widely known that the sheriff of Northumberland was not sheriff of Durham nor entered within that liberty as sheriff. . . nor made there proclamations or attachments"*.[^cite_note-16] The arguments appear to have prevailed, as by the fourteenth century Durham was accepted as a liberty which received royal mandates direct. In effect it was a private shire, with the bishop appointing his own sheriff.[^cite_note-scammell-14] The area eventually became known as the "[County Palatine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Palatine) of Durham".

[Sadberge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sadberge_\(wapentake\)) was a liberty, sometimes referred to as a county, within Northumberland. In 1189 it was purchased for the see but continued with a separate [sheriff](89516.md), [coroner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coroner) and court of pleas. In the 14th century Sadberge was included in [Stockton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockton-on-Tees) ward and was itself divided into two wards. The division into the four wards of Chester-le-Street, [Darlington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darlington), [Easington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easington_District) and Stockton existed in the 13th century, each ward having its own coroner and a three-weekly court corresponding to the [hundred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hundred_\(division\)) court. The [diocese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese) was divided into the [archdeaconries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archdeacon) of Durham and Northumberland. The former is mentioned in 1072, and in 1291 included the deaneries of Chester-le-Street, Auckland, [Lanchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanchester,_County_Durham) and Darlington.

The term *palatinus* is applied to the bishop in 1293 and, from the 13th century onwards, the bishops frequently claimed the same rights in their lands as the king enjoyed in his kingdom.

### Early administration

#### Overview



The [historic boundaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historic_counties_of_England) of the county shown in [John Speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Speed)'s map of the county in his *Theatre of the Empire of Great Britaine*, c. 1611. These boundaries remained in use for administrative purposes until the local government reforms starting in the 1960s. A depiction of the city of Durham is inset in the top right.

The [historic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historic_counties_of_England) boundaries of County Durham included a main body covering the [catchment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catchment_area_\(human_geography\)) of the [Pennines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennines) in the west, the [River Tees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Tees) in the south, the [North Sea](21179.md) in the east and the Rivers [Tyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Tyne) and [Derwent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Derwent,_North_East_England) in the north.[^cite_note-vision_map-17][^cite_note-18] The county palatinate also had a number of [liberties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberty_\(division\)): the [Bedlingtonshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bedlingtonshire), [Islandshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islandshire)[^cite_note-vision_islandshire-19] and [Norhamshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norhamshire)[^cite_note-vision_norhamshire-20] [exclaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exclaves) within Northumberland, and the [Craikshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Craikshire) exclave within the North Riding of Yorkshire. In 1831 the county covered an area of 679,530 acres (2,750.0 km<sup>2</sup>)[^cite_note-vision_acres-21] and had a population of 253,910.[^cite_note-ns_durham-22] These exclaves were included as part of the county for parliamentary electoral purposes until 1832, and for judicial and local-government purposes until the coming into force of the [Counties (Detached Parts) Act 1844](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counties_\(Detached_Parts\)_Act_1844), which merged most remaining exclaves with their surrounding county. The boundaries of the county proper remained in use for administrative and ceremonial purposes until the [Local Government Act 1972](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_Government_Act_1972).

#### Early English and Norman period

Following the Battle of Hastings, [William the Conqueror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_the_Conqueror) appointed Copsig as Earl of Northumbria, thereby bringing what would become County Durham under Copsig's control. Copsig was, just a few weeks later, killed in Newburn.[^cite_note-Dodds-23] Having already being previously offended by the appointment of a non-Northumbrian as Bishop of Durham in 1042, the people of the region became increasingly rebellious.[^cite_note-Dodds-23] In response, in January 1069, William despatched a large [Norman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normans) army, under the command of [Robert de Comines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_de_Comines), to [Durham City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durham,_England). The army, believed to consist of 700 cavalry (about one-third of the number of Norman knights who had participated in the Battle of Hastings),[^cite_note-Dodds-23] entered the city, whereupon they were attacked, and defeated, by a Northumbrian assault force. The Northumbrians wiped out the entire Norman army, including Comines,[^cite_note-Dodds-23] all except for one survivor, who was allowed to take the news of this defeat back.

Following the Norman slaughter at the hands of the Northumbrians, resistance to Norman rule spread throughout Northern England, including a similar uprising in York.[^cite_note-Dodds-23] [William the Conqueror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_the_Conqueror) subsequently (and successfully) attempted to halt the northern rebellions by unleashing the notorious [Harrying of the North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harrying_of_the_North) (1069–1070).[^cite_note-24] Because William's main focus during the harrying was on Yorkshire,[^cite_note-Dodds-23] County Durham was largely spared the Harrying.[^cite_note-douglas-25] The best remains of the Norman period include [Durham Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durham_Cathedral) and [Durham Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durham_Castle), and several parish churches, such as St Laurence Church in [Pittington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittington). The [Early English period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) has left the eastern portion of the cathedral, the churches of Darlington, Hartlepool, and St Andrew, Auckland, Sedgefield, and portions of a few other churches.

#### Palatinate

Until the 15th century, the most important administrative officer in the [Palatinate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palatinate_of_Durham) was the [steward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steward_\(office\)). Other officers included the sheriff, the coroners, the [Chamberlain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamberlain_\(office\)) and the [chancellor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancellor). The palatine exchequer originated in the 12th century. The palatine assembly represented the whole county, and dealt chiefly with fiscal questions. The bishop's council, consisting of the clergy, the sheriff and the [barons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baron), regulated judicial affairs, and later produced the [Chancery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_equity) and the courts of [Admiralty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Admiralty_court) and [Marshalsea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshalsea_Court).



The entrance to [Durham Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durham_Castle), the bishops' palace until 1832 when it moved to Auckland Castle

The [prior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prior_\(ecclesiastical\)) of Durham ranked first among the bishop's barons. He had his own court, and almost exclusive jurisdiction over his men. A UNESCO site describes the role of the bishops in Durham, the "buffer state between England and Scotland":[^cite_note-26]

> From 1075, the Bishop of Durham became a Prince-Bishop, with the right to raise an army, mint his own coins, and levy taxes. As long as he remained loyal to the king of England, he could govern as a virtually autonomous ruler, reaping the revenue from his territory, but also remaining mindful of his role of protecting England's northern frontier.

A report states that the Bishops also had the authority to appoint judges and barons and to offer pardons.[^cite_note-27]

There were ten palatinate barons in the 12th century, most importantly the [Hyltons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baron_Hylton) of [Hylton Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hylton_Castle), the Bulmers of [Brancepeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brancepeth,_County_Durham), the Conyers of Sockburne, the Hansards of Evenwood, and the [Lumleys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earl_of_Scarbrough) of [Lumley Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumley_Castle). The Nevilles owned large estates in the county. [John Neville, 3rd Baron Neville de Raby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Neville,_3rd_Baron_Neville_de_Raby) rebuilt [Raby Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raby_Castle), their principal seat, in 1377.

[Edward I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_I)'s *[quo warranto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quo_warranto)* proceedings of 1293 showed twelve lords enjoying more or less extensive franchises under the bishop. The repeated efforts of [the Crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Crown) to check the powers of the palatinate bishops culminated in 1536 in the [Jurisdiction in Liberties Act 1535](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisdiction_in_Liberties_Act_1535), which deprived the bishop of the power to pardon offences against the law or to appoint judicial officers. Moreover, indictments and legal processes were in future to run in the name of the king, and offences to be described as against the peace of the king, rather than that of the bishop. In 1596 restrictions were imposed on the powers of the chancery, and in 1646 the palatinate was formally abolished. It was revived, however, after the [Restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Restoration), and continued with much the same power until 5 July 1836, when the [Durham (County Palatine) Act 1836](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durham_\(County_Palatine\)_Act_1836) ([6 & 7 Will. 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/6_&_7_Will._4). c. 19) provided that the palatine jurisdiction should in future be vested in the Crown.[^cite_note-28]



Hand-drawn map of Durham by Christopher Saxton in 1576

#### Wars

During the 15th-century [Wars of the Roses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wars_of_the_Roses), [Henry VI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_VI_of_England) passed through Durham. On the outbreak of the [Great Rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Civil_War) in 1642 Durham inclined to support the cause of [Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_England), and in 1640 the high sheriff of the palatinate guaranteed to supply the Scottish army with provisions during their stay in the county. In 1642 the [Earl of Newcastle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Cavendish,_1st_Duke_of_Newcastle-upon-Tyne) formed the western counties into an association for the King's service, but in 1644 the palatinate was again overrun by a Scottish army, and after the [Battle of Marston Moor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Marston_Moor) (2 July 1644) fell entirely into the hands of Parliament.

#### Parliamentary representation and secular powers

In 1614, a [bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_\(law\)) was introduced in Parliament for securing representation to the county and city of Durham and the borough of [Barnard Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard_Castle). The bishop strongly opposed the proposal as an infringement of his palatinate rights, and the county was first summoned to return members to Parliament in 1654. After the Restoration of 1660 the county and city returned two members each. In the wake of the [Reform Act 1832](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reform_Act_1832) the county returned two members for two divisions, and the boroughs of [Gateshead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gateshead), [South Shields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Shields) and [Sunderland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunderland) acquired representation. The bishops lost their secular powers in 1836.[^cite_note-29] The boroughs of Darlington, Stockton and [Hartlepool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartlepool) returned one member each from 1868 until the [Redistribution of Seats Act 1885](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redistribution_of_Seats_Act_1885).

## Geography

### Urban

The historic county excludes the village of [Startforth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Startforth), the ceremonial county excludes the city of [Sunderland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunderland) and the non-metropolitan county excludes the town of [Stockton-on-Tees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockton-on-Tees). The ceremonial county includes the non-metropolitan county with the addition of the boroughs of [Darlington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borough_of_Darlington), [Hartlepool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borough_of_Hartlepool), and [Stockton-on-Tees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borough_of_Stockton-on-Tees).[^cite_note-lieutenancies-6] The historic county's boundaries stretched from the rivers [Tyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Tyne) to [Tees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Tees) and it is covered by the ceremonial county, the [city of Sunderland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Sunderland), the boroughs of [Gateshead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Borough_of_Gateshead) and [South Tyneside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Tyneside), while the non-metropolitan county includes part of [Yorkshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yorkshire).

County Durham contains a small area of the [North East Green Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_East_Green_Belt) in the county's north; surrounding the city of Durham, Chester-le-Street and other communities along the shared county border with Tyne and Wear, avoiding [Wearside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wearside)'s spread west. A smaller [green belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_belt_\(United_Kingdom\)) separates [Urpeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urpeth), [Ouston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouston,_County_Durham), [Pelton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelton,_County_Durham), and [Perkinsville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perkinsville,_County_Durham) from [Birtley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birtley,_Tyne_and_Wear) in Tyne and Wear. A further small segment by the coast separates Seaham from the Sunderland settlements of Beckwith Green and [Ryhope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ryhope). It was first drawn up in the 1990s.[^cite_note-30]

The moors above [Weardale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weardale)

County Durham is underlain by [Carboniferous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carboniferous) rocks in the west. [Permian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permian) and [Triassic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triassic) strata overlie these older rocks in the east. These [sedimentary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedimentary) sequences have been cut by [igneous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igneous) [dykes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyke_\(geology\)) and [sills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sill_\(geology\)).[^cite_note-31]

The county contains a sizeable area of the [North Pennines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Pennines), designated an [Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Area_of_Outstanding_Natural_Beauty), primarily west of [Tow Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tow_Law) and [Barnard Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard_Castle).[^cite_note-32] The highest point ([county top](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_counties_of_England_and_Wales_in_1964_by_highest_point)) of historic County Durham is the [trig point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trig_point) (not the summit) of [Burnhope Seat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burnhope_Seat), height 746 metres (2,448 ft), between Weardale and Teesdale on the border with historic [Cumberland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumberland) in the far west of the county. The local government reorganisation of 1974 placed the higher [Mickle Fell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mickle_Fell) south of Teesdale (the county top of [Yorkshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yorkshire)) within the administrative borders of Durham (where it remains within the ceremonial county). However, it is not generally recognised as the highest point in Durham.

The two main dales of County Durham (Teesdale and Weardale) and the surrounding fells, many of which exceed 2,000 feet (610 m) in height, are excellent [hillwalking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hillwalking) country, although not nearly as popular as the nearby [Yorkshire Dales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yorkshire_Dales) and [Lake District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_District) national parks. The scenery is rugged and remote, and the high fells have a landscape typical of the [Pennines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennines) with extensive areas of [tussock grass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tussock_grass) and blanket [peat bog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peat_bog) in the west, with [heather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ericaceae) [moorland](553383.md) on the lower slopes descending to the east.

## Governance

Main article: [History of local government districts in Durham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_local_government_districts_in_Durham)

### Local

Since 2009, County Durham has comprised the unitary authority areas of [County Durham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Durham_\(district\)), [Darlington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borough_of_Darlington), [Hartlepool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borough_of_Hartlepool), and the part of [Stockton-on-Tees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borough_of_Stockton-on-Tees) that is north of the [River Tees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Tees).[^cite_note-40][^cite_note-41] The councils of the four areas are [unitary authorities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_authorities_of_England), meaning they combine the functions of a [non-metropolitan county](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-metropolitan_county) council and a [non-metropolitan district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-metropolitan_district) council in order to provide most local government services in their area. These include education, libraries, roads, social care, waste, environment, and housing.[^cite_note-42]

The county is partially [parished](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_civil_parishes_in_County_Durham). The [city of Durham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durham,_England) is the most populous settlement in the county to have a parish. Multiple parishes are styled as having [town councils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Town_council): [Billingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billingham) (in Stockton Borough), [Barnard Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard_Castle), [Bishop Auckland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_Auckland), [Chilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chilton,_County_Durham), [Ferryhill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferryhill), [Great Aycliffe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Aycliffe), [Newton Aycliffe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton_Aycliffe), [Greater Willington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Willington), [Peterlee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peterlee), [Seaham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seaham), [Sedgefield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedgefield), [Shildon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shildon), [Spennymoor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spennymoor), [Stanley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley,_County_Durham) and [Tow Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tow_Law).[^cite_note-43]

The ceremonial county itself has only a minor administrative function, being the area to which the [Lord Lieutenant of Durham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Lieutenant_of_Durham) is appointed; the [shrieval county](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shrieval_county) has the same boundaries and is the area to which the [High Sheriff of Durham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Sheriff_of_Durham) is appointed.[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45]

#### History



A map of England in 1971, including County Durham, showing the administrative boundaries before the Local Government Act 1972.

The [Local Government Act 1888](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_Government_Act_1888) reformed English local government by creating [administrative counties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Administrative_counties_of_England), [county boroughs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_borough), and elected county councils and county borough councils to govern them. County boroughs largely consisted of those settlements with a population over 50,000, and administrative counties covered smaller towns and rural areas.[^cite_note-46] Under the Act, County Durham was reconstituted as an administrative county and [Gateshead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gateshead), [South Shields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Shields), and [Sunderland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunderland) became county boroughs. West Hartlepool became a county borough in 1902, and [Darlington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darlington) in 1915.[^cite_note-47][^cite_note-48] In 1967, the county borough of Middlesbrough, which was part of [County of York, North Riding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_of_York,_North_Riding), was abolished and replaced with the larger [county borough of Teesside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Borough_of_Teesside), which included [Stockton-on-Tees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockton-on-Tees) and adjacent areas.[^cite_note-49]

The [Local Government Act 1972](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_Government_Act_1972), which came into effect in 1974, reformed English local government again. It introduced [metropolitan counties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_county), which covered the largest urban areas, and [non-metropolitan counties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-metropolitan_county) elsewhere. Both types of county were divided into districts, with local government functions divided between a county council and district councils.[^cite_note-\:2-50][^cite_note-51] The north-east of Durham, including Gateshead, South Shields, and Sunderland, became part of the metropolitan county of [Tyne and Wear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyne_and_Wear). The south-east, including West Hartlepool and Teesside, became part of the non-metropolitan county of [Cleveland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleveland_\(county\)).[^cite_note-\:042-52] The remaining parts of the former administrative county, together with Darlington and the [Startforth Rural District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Startforth_Rural_District) from the North Riding of Yorkshire (which comprised southern [Teesdale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teesdale) and the area to the south), became part of the new non-metropolitan county of Durham.[^cite_note-\:04-53]

From 1974 until 1996, the non-metropolitan county contained eight non-metropolitan districts, with the provision of local government services divided between [Lancashire County Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lancashire_County_Council) and the district councils. The districts were [Chester-le-Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chester-le-Street_\(district\)), [Derwentside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derwentside), Darlington, [Durham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durham_\(non-metropolitan_district\)), [Easington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easington_District), [Sedgefield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedgefield_\(borough\)), [Teesdale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teesdale_\(district\)), and [Wear Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wear_Valley).[^cite_note-54] In 1996, Darlington became a [unitary authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_authorities_of_England) area.[^cite_note-55] In the same year, Cleveland was abolished and its former districts became unitary authority areas.[^cite_note-56] Hartlepool and the part of Stockton-on-Tees south of the River Tees became part of County Durham for ceremonial purposes.[^cite_note-57][^cite_note-58] In 2009, the remaining seven districts of County Durham were abolished and [Durham County Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durham_County_Council) was reconstituted as a unitary authority.[^cite_note-59]

### Parliament

Main article: [Parliamentary constituencies in County Durham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_constituencies_in_County_Durham)

The county boundaries used for parliamentary constituencies were originally those used between 1974 and 1996, consisting of the County Durham district and the Darlington Borough, until boundary changes in 2024. This area elects eight Members of Parliament. County Durham itself elects six of them, and as of the 2024 general election, all of these MPs are [Labour](19279158.md). The rest of the ceremonial county is included in the [Cleveland parliamentary constituency area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_constituencies_in_Cleveland).

### Employment

The proportion of the population working in agriculture fell from around 6% in 1851 to 1% in 1951; currently less than 1% of the population work in agriculture.[^cite_note-ns_durham-22] There were 15,202 people employed in coal mining in 1841, rising to a peak of 157,837 in 1921.[^cite_note-ns_durham-22]

## Culture

### Mining and heavy industry



John Wilson Carmichael *A View of Murton Colliery near Seaham, County Durham*, 1843

A substantial number of colliery villages were built throughout the county in the nineteenth century to house the growing workforce, which included large numbers of migrant workers from the rest of the UK.[^cite_note-durhamrecordoffice.org.uk-99] Sometimes the migrants were brought in to augment the local workforce but, in other cases, they were brought in as strike breakers, or "blacklegs". Tens of thousands of people migrated to County Durham from Cornwall (partly due to their previous experience of [tin mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tin_mining)) between 1815 and the outbreak of the First World War, so much so that the miners' cottages in east Durham called "Greenhill" were also known locally as "Cornwall", and Easington Colliery still has a Cornish Street.[^cite_note-100] Other migrants included people from Northumberland, Cumberland, South Wales, Scotland and Ireland.[^cite_note-co-curate.ncl.ac.uk-101][^cite_note-102] Coal mining had a profound effect on trade unionism, public health and housing, as well as creating a related culture, language, folklore and sense of identity that still survives today.[^cite_note-durhamintime.org.uk-103]

The migrants also were employed in the railway, ship building, iron, steel and roadworking industries, and the pattern of migration continued, to a lesser extent, up until the 1950s and 1960s. Gateshead was once home to the fourth-largest Irish settlement in England,[^cite_note-co-curate.ncl.ac.uk-101] Consett's population was 22% Irish[^cite_note-104] and significant numbers of Irish people moved to Sunderland, resulting in the city hosting numerous events on [St. Patrick's Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Patrick's_Day) due to the Irish heritage.[^cite_note-105]



Durham Miners' Gala 2008

The culture of coal mining found expression in the [Durham Miners' Gala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durham_Miners'_Gala), which was first held in 1871,[^cite_note-advice-106] developed around the culture of trade unionism. Coal mining continued to decline and pits closed. The [1984–1985 United Kingdom miners' strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1984%E2%80%931985_United_Kingdom_miners'_strike) involved many miners across the county. Today no deep-coal mines exist in the county and numbers attending the Miners' Gala decreased over the period between the end of the strike and the 21st century. However recent years have seen numbers significantly grow, and more banners return to the Gala each year as former colliery communities restore or replicate former banners to march at the Gala parade.[^cite_note-advice-106][^cite_note-heritage-107]

### Art

In 1930, the [Spennymoor Settlement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spennymoor_Settlement) (otherwise known as the Pitman's Academy) opened. The settlement, initially funded by the [Pilgrim Trust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilgrim_Trust), aimed to encourage people to be neighbourly and participate in voluntary social service.[^cite_note-spennymoorsettlement.co.uk-108] The settlement operated during the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression), when unemployment was widespread and economic deprivation rife; Spennymoor was economically underprivileged. The settlement provided educational and social work, as well as hope; this included providing unemployed miners with on outlet for their creativity, a poor person's lawyer service, the town's first library and the Everyman Theatre. The output included paintings, sewing, socially-significant plays, woodwork and sculptures. Several members went on to win adult scholarships at Oxford University[^cite_note-spennymoorsettlement.co.uk-108] when such a route would normally be closed to the underprivileged. Former members include artists [Norman Cornish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Cornish) and [Tom McGuinness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_McGuinness_\(artist\)), writer [Sid Chaplin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sid_Chaplin) and journalist Arnold Hadwin (1929–2011). The Spennymoor Settlement at its home in the Everyman Theatre (Grade II listed) is still operating, administered by the current trustees, offering community events and activities, including Youth Theatre Group, an Art Group and various classes, as well as offering community accommodation facilities.

Several Durham miners have been able to turn their former mining careers into careers in art. For example, [Tom Lamb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Lamb_\(artist\)), as well as the aforementioned Tom McGuinness and Norman Cornish. Their artworks depict scenes of life underground, the streets in which they lived and the people they loved.&#x20;

In 2017, The Mining Art Gallery opened in Bishop Auckland in a building that was once a bank.[^cite_note-109] Part of the Auckland Project, the gallery includes the work of artists from within County Durham and beyond, including such other North-Eastern mining artists as [Robert Olley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Olley), as well as contributions from outside the region. It features three permanent areas and a temporary exhibition area; the gallery's Gemini Collection includes 420 pieces of mining art. [^cite_note-110] Much of the artwork was donated, by Dr Robert McManners and Gillian Wales, for example.[^cite_note-111]

In 2019, 100 years after his birth, a permanent tribute to the work of the artist Norman Stansfield Cornish MBE was opened within the Town Hall, and a Cornish Trail around the town was established to include areas of the town depicted in Cornish's artwork.

### Music

As with neighbouring Northumberland, County Durham has a rich heritage of [Northumbrian music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northumbrian_music), dating back from the Northumbrian Golden Age of the 7th and 8th centuries. [Bede](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bede) made references to harp-playing, and abundant archeological evidence has been found of wooden flutes, bone flutes, panpipes, wooden drums and lyres (a six-string form of harp).[^cite_note-Gething-10] North-East England has a distinctive folk music style that has drawn from many other regions, including southern Scotland, Ireland and the rest of northern England, that has endured stably since the 18th century.[^cite_note-112] Instruments played include, in common with most folk music styles, stringed instruments such as the guitar and fiddle, but also the Northumbrian smallpipe, which is played and promoted by people including the [Northumbrian Pipers' Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northumbrian_Pipers'_Society) throughout the North East, including County Durham, with the society having an active group in Sedgefield.[^cite_note-113] Contemporary folk musicians include [Jez Lowe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jez_Lowe) and Ged Foley.

In 2018, The [Arts Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arts_Council_England) funded the Stories of Sanctuary project in the [city of Durham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durham,_England). The project aims to assist people living in the city to share their stories about seeking sanctuary in the North East through photography, stories, poetry and music. The art is based on a history of sanctuary in Durham, from St Cuthbert's exile, through to the miners' strike of 1984, and to refugees escaping civil war in the Middle East. The music produced as part of the project includes contributions from singer-songwriter Sam Slatcher and viola player Raghad Haddad from the National Syrian Orchestra.[^cite_note-114]

Other notable performers/songwriters who were born or raised in the county include [Paddy McAloon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paddy_McAloon), [Eric Boswell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Boswell_\(songwriter\)), [Jeremy Spencer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Spencer), [Alan Clark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Clark_\(keyboardist\)), [Martin Brammer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Brammer), [Robert Blamire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Blamire), [Thomas Allen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Allen_\(baritone\)), [Zoe Birkett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoe_Birkett), [John O'Neill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_O'Neill_\(musician\),_born_1926?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Karen Harding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karen_Harding) and [Courtney Hadwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courtney_Hadwin).

### Flag

Main article: [Flag of County Durham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_County_Durham)



County Durham flag

County Durham has an unofficial [flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_County_Durham), registered with the [vexillological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vexillological) charity the [Flag Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_Institute) on 21 November 2013.[^cite_note-115] The flag consists of St Cuthbert's cross counterchanged with the county's blue and gold colours.
