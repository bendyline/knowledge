***Louisiana v. Callais***, consolidated with ***Robinson v. Callais***, [608](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_United_States_Supreme_Court_cases,_volume_608) [U.S.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Reports) \_\_\_ (2026), is a [landmark decision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_landmark_court_decisions_in_the_United_States) of the [Supreme Court of the United States](31737.md) concerning [racial gerrymandering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_gerrymandering) and redistricting in the state of Louisiana following the [2020 United States census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_United_States_census). After the Supreme Court's decision in *[Allen v. Milligan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allen_v._Milligan)*, the Louisiana legislature produced a new map under court orders, allocating two of the state's six districts as [majority-minority districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_majority-minority_United_States_congressional_districts) using the state's 2020 census demographics and in compliance with Section 2 of the [Voting Rights Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_Rights_Act). The new maps were challenged as racially-gerrymandered and a constitutional violation of the [Fourteenth](31666.md) and [Fifteenth Amendments](31667.md). In a 6–3 decision, the Court ruled along ideological lines that affirmed the federal district panel's judgement that the new redistricting map was an unconstitutional racial gerrymander under the Fifteenth Amendment.

While the Court declined to find Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act unconstitutional, it established additional requirements for vote dilution claims to succeed, beyond the ones set out in *[Thornburg v. Gingles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thornburg_v._Gingles)* (1986). Justice [Samuel Alito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Alito), writing for the majority, held that plaintiffs in Section 2 challenges must demonstrate that a state intentionally redistricted to diminish the opportunity for minority voters, by the plaintiffs offering their own, alternative maps that meet the state's legitimate goals while correcting the minority vote. Absent such proof, the Court indicated that such challenges would be deemed not an illegal gerrymander, under the precedent of *[Rucho v. Common Cause](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rucho_v._Common_Cause)* (2019).

## Background

President Lyndon B. Johnson signs the Voting Rights Act in 1965

President [Lyndon B. Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson) signs the [Voting Rights Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_Rights_Act) in 1965.

### Voting Rights Act of 1965 and redistricting

The [Voting Rights Act of 1965](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_Rights_Act) (VRA) was signed into law under [Lyndon B. Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson). Prior to that, many of the southern United States had [Jim Crow laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Crow_laws) that promoted racial segregation despite the guarantees of the [Fourteenth](31666.md) and [Fifteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution](31667.md), particularly limiting the ability of Black Americans to vote with [poll taxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poll_tax). After organized protests, including the [Selma to Montgomery marches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selma_to_Montgomery_marches) in 1965 and other violence, Johnson urged Congress to pass laws to assure that minorities had the right to vote, leading to the passage of the VRA and other civil rights laws. As enacted, the VRA prevented state laws that deliberately restricted minorities' ability to vote.[^cite_note-nytimes_vra_history-1]

Among [other amendments to the VRA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amendments_to_the_Voting_Rights_Act_of_1965), a 1982 amendment was made following the Supreme Court ruling in the redistricting case *[Mobile v. Bolden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_v._Bolden)* (1980), which had ruled that exclusionary voting practices were unlawful under the VRA "only if motivated by a discriminatory purpose" under the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments.[^cite_note-scotus_analysis_p1-2] The 1982 amendment added language to the VRA to screen for voting practices that would cause "a class of \[protected] citizens ... have less opportunity than other members of the electorate to participate in the political process and to elect representatives of their choice."[^cite_note-scotus_analysis_p1-2] The new language did not require exact proportional representation, but implied a minimum limit to meet the VRA.[^cite_note-scotus_analysis_p1-2]

The 1982 amendment was central to the Supreme Court case *[Thornburg v. Gingles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thornburg_v._Gingles)* (1986). The decision in *Gingles* gave a set of three preconditions to determine if a redistricting map was racially discriminatory under the 1982 VRA amendment:[^cite_note-scotus_analysis_p1-2]

- The plaintiffs (challenging the redistricting) can show the minority group make up a sufficiently large group in a "reasonably configured district" without violating "traditional districting criteria";

- The minority group is "politically cohesive", typically voting for the same issues;

- And whether the majority group vote "sufficiently as a bloc usually to defeat the minority's preferred candidates".

If all three preconditions are met, then the judiciary can evaluate the "totality of the circumstances" if the minority group's ability to vote for their preferred candidate is invalidated.[^cite_note-scotus_analysis_p1-2]

The Supreme Court under Chief Justice [John Roberts](1928850.md) has issued several decisions prior to *Callais* placing limits on the VRA. These cases include *[Shelby County v. Holder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shelby_County_v._Holder)* (2013) which struck down the preclearance requirements for any voting-related state laws for nine states, which had previously been justified by Section 5 of the VRA. These states then instituted new redistricting maps from the [2020 United States census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_United_States_census).[^cite_note-nytimes_vra_history-1]

### Case background

Following the 2020 census, Louisiana was assigned six seats in the [United States House of Representatives](19468510.md). The census found that about one-third of the people in the state were [Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_people). The [Louisiana State Legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_State_Legislature), where the [Republican Party](32070.md) held a majority in both chambers, approved a new district map that was largely unchanged, giving the state five districts with White majorities and one with a Black majority.[^cite_note-3]

Lawyers challenged the new map in court, alleging it violated Section 2 of the VRA. In the ensuing case, *Robinson v. Ardoin*, U.S. District Judge [Shelly Dick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shelly_Dick) ruled in June 2022 that the map violated the VRA by not including a second majority-Black district, and issued an order enjoining the state from using its map.[^cite_note-4] Louisiana's Secretary of State [Kyle Ardoin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyle_Ardoin) sought a stay of the order from the [Fifth Circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth_Circuit), which was denied. Ardoin then sought relief from the Supreme Court, which stayed the order pending its decision in *[Allen v. Milligan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allen_v._Milligan)*, a similar VRA case from Alabama.[^cite_note-5]

In June 2023, the Supreme Court ruled in *Allen* that Alabama's district map violated the VRA.[^cite_note-6] Consequently, the Court lifted its stay of Dick's ruling, returning the case to the Fifth Circuit. In declining to stay the order again, the Fifth Circuit told the legislature that unless it passed a map with two majority-Black districts by January 15, 2024, Judge Dick could draw and impose a new map.[^cite_note-7] In order to prevent this, the legislature held a special session and in January 2024 passed a new map of its own in which its [6th congressional district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana's_6th_congressional_district) became the state's second majority-Black district.[^cite_note-laill_feb12024-8]

A lawsuit over the state's new map was then filed by a group of plaintiffs calling themselves "non–African-American voters", led by Phillip "Bert" Callais, who lived in an area near [Baton Rouge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baton_Rouge,_Louisiana) that had been brought into the new majority-Black district.[^cite_note-9] The suit claimed the 6th district was racially gerrymandered and violated the [Fourteenth](31666.md) and [Fifteenth Amendments](31667.md).[^cite_note-laill_feb12024-8] Because it was a constitutional rather than a statutory challenge, the case was heard by a panel of three federal district judges from the [Western District of Louisiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_Western_District_of_Louisiana), rather than a single judge. In May 2024, the panel ruled 2–1 that the new map was unconstitutionally gerrymandered and blocked its use. The state sought a stay from the Supreme Court, arguing it was too close to the election to create a new map. The Supreme Court ordered the state to use, due to the timing constraints, the January 2024 map for the 2024 elections, without ruling on the merits of the panel's decision.[^cite_note-10]

## Supreme Court

Two separate petitions were filed with the Supreme Court to hear the challenge on the district panel's ruling: one from the state itself (*Louisiana v. Callais*) and another from Black voters and civil rights organizations such as the [NAACP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAACP) (*Robinson v. Callais*). The Supreme Court agreed to hear both cases[^cite_note-12] and [consolidated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consolidation_\(law\)) them in November 2024.[^cite_note-13]

The first oral argument was held on March 24, 2025.[^cite_note-14] On June 27, 2025, instead of issuing a ruling, the Court ordered reargument for the 2025 term, with only Justice [Clarence Thomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarence_Thomas) dissenting.[^cite_note-15]

During the summer recess before its 2025 term, the Supreme Court directed all parties to submit supplemental [briefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brief_\(law\)) on a new question: whether the new maps violated the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments. There were rumors that the reargument and added question were signs that the Court was preparing to make major changes in its jurisprudence (as it had done in *[Citizens United v. FEC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizens_United_v._FEC)*),[^cite_note-16] perhaps by ruling that compliance with Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act would be unconstitutional.[^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19] In its supplemental brief, Louisiana said it would no longer defend its position in the case at the Supreme Court, and instead argued that the new maps violated the 14th and 15th Amendments.[^cite_note-20]

The second oral session for the case took place on October 15, 2025. There were rumors that the Court would limit the use of the VRA for redistricting by suggesting that the Act's mandates have a time limit.

### Decision

The court issued its decision on April 29, 2026. In a 6–3 decision,[^cite_note-limits-21][^cite_note-strikes-22][^cite_note-door-23] the court affirmed the three-judge district court's ruling that the state's new map was an illegal racial gerrymander. The majority opinion was delivered by Justice [Samuel Alito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Alito).[^cite_note-ap_decision-24] Alito wrote: "Allowing race to play any part in government decision-making represents a departure from the constitutional rule that applies in almost every other context. Compliance with section 2 \[of the VRA] thus could not justify the state's use of race-based redistricting here. The state's attempt to satisfy the Middle District's ruling, although understandable, was an unconstitutional racial gerrymander."[^cite_note-guardian_decision-25]

The decision did not hold Section 2 of the VRA to be unconstitutional, nor did it overturn cases such as *[Allen v. Milligan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allen_v._Milligan)* (2023), though Alito wrote that "major developments" since one of the last major tests of Section 2, *Gingles*, required "additional considerations." *Gingles* provided for three pre-conditions: that the minority voters were "sufficiently numerous and compact to constitute a majority in a reasonably configured district"; that they voted in a "politically cohesive" manner; and that the majority voted in a bloc as to defeat the minority's preferred candidate.[^cite_note-scotusblog_decision-26] Alito said of the first *Gingles* precondition, computer-aided redistricting tools have advanced enough for plaintiffs challenging maps to demonstrate a map that "fully achieves all the State’s legitimate goals" including [incumbency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incumbency) protection by "a specific margin of victory" while creating the majority-minority district.[^cite_note-scotusblog_decision-26][^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28] For the second and third *Gingles* preconditions, Alito said that under the combination of the US having a two-party system coupled with the ruling in *[Rucho v. Common Cause](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rucho_v._Common_Cause)* that partisan gerrymandering was non-[justiciable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justiciability) within federal courts, that "a litigant can easily exploit \[Section 2] for partisan purposes by 'repackag\[ing] a partisan-gerrymandering claim as a racial-gerrymandering claim.'"[^cite_note-scotusblog_decision-26] Finally, Alito said, in regards to the final part of the *Gingles* test, that "things have changed dramatically" in the southern states since the VRA was passed, and "black voters now participate in elections at similar rates as the rest of the electorate." As such, the addition of a second majority-Black district in the challenged Louisiana maps was racially motivated and not required.[^cite_note-scotusblog_decision-26][^cite_note-limits-21] He concluded that a successful challenger must now prove that a state "intentionally drew its districts to afford minority voters less opportunity because of their race." Failing to "disentangle race from the state's race-neutral considerations, including politics" will now likely lead to an unsuccessful suit.[^cite_note-strikes-22]



The 2025 [congressional district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana's_congressional_districts) map of Louisiana upheld by lower courts before the decision (left) and a [proportional representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proportional_representation_in_the_United_States) map under the [Fair Representation Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fair_Representation_Act_\(United_States\)) (right)

In a concurring opinion, Justice [Clarence Thomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarence_Thomas) (joined by Justice [Neil Gorsuch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neil_Gorsuch)) contended that Section 2 of the VRA should not apply to districting at all, writing that the court "should never have interpreted" it "to effectively give racial groups 'an entitlement to roughly [proportional representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proportional_representation_in_the_United_States)'."[^cite_note-scotusblog_decision-26]

Justice [Elena Kagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elena_Kagan) wrote the dissent, joined by Justices [Sonia Sotomayor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonia_Sotomayor) and [Ketanji Brown Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ketanji_Brown_Jackson). Kagan wrote: "The consequences \[of the Court's decision] are likely to be far-reaching and grave. Today's decision renders Section 2 all but a dead letter."[^cite_note-ap_decision-24] She said that the Voting Rights Act "is —or, now more accurately, was— 'one of the most consequential, efficacious, and amply justified exercises of federal legislative power in our Nation's history.' It was born of the literal blood of Union soldiers and civil rights marchers. It ushered in awe-inspiring change, bringing this Nation closer to fulfilling the ideals of democracy and racial equality." Kagan accused the majority of reviving the intent-based standard from *[Mobile v. Bolden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_v._Bolden)* that Congress had repudiated in [its 1982 amendments to the VRA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amendments_to_the_Voting_Rights_Act_of_1965#1982), which, she said, would make vote dilution cases nearly unwinnable. She stressed that Congress was the only authority to determine whether the VRA is no longer needed.[^cite_note-scotusblog_decision-26]

### Analysis

Analysts had predicted that a decision in *Callais* reducing the scope of the VRA could allow Republicans to gain a significant number of seats in the U.S. House of Representatives from redistricting in the Southeast, where the VRA has generally prevented Republican-controlled legislatures from maximizing their numbers of "safe" districts. *The New York Times* estimated that, if the Court weakened the VRA, up to twelve seats could shift from Democratic to Republican control.[^cite_note-nytimes_impact-29][^cite_note-30]

When the decision in *Callais* was announced, some media outlets characterized it as a "landmark" decision, predicting that the additional restrictions on Section 2 of the VRA would likely make challenges to redistricting significantly more difficult. Some analysts expect it will allow southern states to eliminate [majority-minority districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_majority-minority_United_States_congressional_districts), possibly changing election outcomes.[^cite_note-guardian_decision-25][^cite_note-ap_decision-24][^cite_note-31][^cite_note-32] The decision was seen by some lawyers as the culmination of a long-time grudge of Roberts against the VRA,[^cite_note-cnn_roberts_callais-33][^cite_note-34][^cite_note-35] while media outlets considered the decision as rendering the VRA all but dead and [harming the country's democratic process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_backsliding_in_the_United_States).[^cite_note-36][^cite_note-37] Other commentators considered the ruling to start a new era of [Jim Crow laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Crow_laws), allowing for legal discrimination of minorities.[^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39] The court's opinion was expected to affect all levels of government including state and local governments.[^cite_note-40][^cite_note-41][^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43]

An analysis from *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)* found that Justice Alito's claim that Black voter turnout had surpassed white voter turnout in two of the five most recent presidential elections, both nationally and in Louisiana, was based on misleading data and questionable methodology. Alito cited the brief from the [Department of Justice](52563.md) which used "a proportion of the total population of each racial group over the age of 18"; however, that approach is "not favored by experts", who instead use "voter turnout as a proportion of the citizen voting age population or the voter eligible population." Following the latter analysis, the gap in voter turnout has actually widened, with Black voter turnout dropping since 2012, particularly after the Supreme Court's opinion in *[Shelby County v. Holder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shelby_County_v._Holder)* which struck down the preclearance formula defined in Section 4(b) of the VRA for states with historical discrimination concerns including Louisiana.[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-\:13-45]

The [editorial board of *The Wall Street Journal*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Editorial_board_at_The_Wall_Street_Journal) praised the ruling, which in its view restored the intent of the VRA to prevent discrimination at the poll, and not to be used for districting.[^cite_note-46] President [Donald Trump](4848272.md) thanked "brilliant Justice" Alito for "authoring this important and appropriate Opinion".[^cite_note-47]

## Aftermath

### In Louisiana

The day the decision was announced, the non-Black voter plaintiffs who had prevailed asked the Court to make its decision effective immediately, skipping the usual 32-day window, to give the state the option to draw a new map for [its 2026 congressional elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_United_States_House_of_Representatives_elections_in_Louisiana). The intervening minority voters asked the court not to do so, arguing that they should have the usual opportunity to seek rehearing and that the [*Purcell* principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purcell_principle) (which limits court decisions about election law that come too close before an affected election) should apply. Concurrently, the district court ordered that its injunction on the 2024 map would remain in place and the state be "afforded the opportunity to enact a Constitutionally compliant map."[^cite_note-49] Louisiana Governor [Jeff Landry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeff_Landry) and Attorney General [Liz Murrill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liz_Murrill) announced that they would suspend the state's May 16 primary to allow the legislature to draw a new, compliant map, even though mail-in ballots had already been sent to overseas and early-voting residents.[^cite_note-50] On May 4, the Supreme Court granted the request, making the decision effective immediately and allowing the state to proceed to redistrict before the election. While the order was unsigned, Alito wrote a concurring statement joined by Thomas and Gorsuch, and Jackson dissented.[^cite_note-51]

### In other states

See also: [2025-2026 United States redistricting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025-2026_United_States_redistricting)

Two challenges to maps drawn from the 2020 census in Mississippi and North Dakota, which had been on the Supreme Court's docket, were added via order on May 18, 2026, with the Supreme Court vacating the current rulings and remanding the cases back to their lower courts to review in light of the *Callais* decision. Both cases were related to standing relative to the VRA; while both suits were brought by private individuals, the states had argued that challenges to district maps under VRA Section 2 could only be brought by a state attorney general.[^cite_note-52]

When *Callais* was decided, efforts by several states to perform [mid-decade redistricting](81439539.md) had been underway for months. At President Trump's urging, [Texas](80621571.md) with other Republican-controlled states redistricted in 2025 to increase the number of their Republican representatives in the House.[^cite_note-nytimes_impact-29] Democratic-controlled states, including California, retaliated with their own redistricting.[^cite_note-53] Over the winter of 2025–26, the Court allowed the Texas and then the California maps to take effect, rejecting legal challenges on the grounds that the new maps were driven by "pure partisanship" and thus did not implicate the VRA.[^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55] The Court also blocked a lower court mandate for New York to redraw a congressional district in [Staten Island](127062.md) following a challenge by Black voters in the district.[^cite_note-56]

At first, *Callais* appeared unlikely to cause further redistricting ahead of the [2026 general election](70357296.md) beyond the change to Louisiana's map, because states' primary elections had begun or would soon.[^cite_note-57] But despite expectations, several southern states initiated redistricting in the weeks after the decision was issued, reasoning that their existing maps with majority-minority districts previously mandated by the VRA were unconstitutional under the Court's new standard.[^cite_note-58][^cite_note-59] Tennessee Republicans [passed a new map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Tennessee_redistricting) that eliminated the sole majority-minority House district in their state.[^cite_note-60] Florida legislators had been debating a [redistricting bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Florida_redistricting) to increase the number of Republican-controlled districts in time for primaries, and passed it the day *Callais* was decided.[^cite_note-61]

Alabama, whose legislature-drawn maps had been replaced with ones drawn by a special master under *[Allen v. Milligan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allen_v._Milligan)*, filed an emergency motion for the Supreme Court to allow its legislature's map to be reinstated. The state argued that *Callais* overrode *Allen*, justifying the use of legislature-drawn maps for the 2026 election even though early voting had already begun for the state primaries. At the same time, Governor [Kay Ivey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kay_Ivey) ordered a special legislative session for a new House district map to be drawn.[^cite_note-62][^cite_note-63] The Court granted the state's request, instructing the district court which had appointed the special master to reconsider the matter in light of *Callais*. Sotomayor dissented, joined by Kagan and Jackson.[^cite_note-64]

While states such as [South Carolina](27956.md), [Georgia](48830.md) and [Mississippi](16949861.md) opted against redistricting for the 2026 elections, they are considering [redistricting for the 2028 elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2027-2028_United_States_redistricting).[^cite_note-65][^cite_note-66]
