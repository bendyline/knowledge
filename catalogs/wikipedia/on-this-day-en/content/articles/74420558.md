# Social media in the Russo-Ukrainian war

Social media has played a prominent role during the [Russo-Ukrainian War](42085878.md), and especially the full-scale [Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine). It has made the war one of the most "meticulously documented" in recent history.[^cite_note-time-1]

## Outreach by Ukraine

[Ukrainian President](20688479.md) [Volodymyr Zelenskyy](59210523.md) has personally used a "unique, media-friendly dynamic style" in how he communicates with the public on social media during the full-scale invasion.[^cite_note-firstTikTokWar-2] On 25 February 2022, shortly after the beginning of the full-scale Russian invasion, as the [battle of Kyiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Kyiv_\(2022\)) began with Russian forces approaching the city and false rumors spread of Zelensky fleeing the city, Zelenskyy posted a "defiant" video of himself with other top government leaders on the streets of Kyiv, saying "We are all here. We're in here. We are in Kyiv. We defend Ukraine."[^cite_note-3][^cite_note-grim-4] Throughout the war, Zelensky has continually posted low-production-value "selfie videos" on social media giving updates and speeches as an everyman, furthering his perceived authenticity and boosting Ukrainian morale.[^cite_note-grim-4][^cite_note-5]

In July 2022, Ukraine launched [United24 Media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United24_Media) (separate from the [United24](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United24) fundraising platform), a cross-platform, mostly English-language outlet "to promote Ukrainian culture and debunk Russian propaganda".[^cite_note-firstTikTokWar-2] Valentyn Paniuta, head of the organization, described Ukraine's public outreach as a "question of \[Ukrainian] survival, noting that since [support for Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_military_aid_to_Ukraine_during_the_Russo-Ukrainian_War) came from Western democracies, Ukraine needed to "appeal to ordinary people" from those countries and "make them feel some kind of empathy for the Ukrainian people".[^cite_note-firstTikTokWar-2]

Paniuta noted that "Ukraine faced a situation where there was powerful [Russian propaganda](45060755.md) in different countries, but we didn't have any international media. We had to create it immediately, and our only weapon was [viral content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viral_content) on social media."[^cite_note-firstTikTokWar-2] Marketing expert Srulik Einhorn, writing in the *[Jerusalem Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem_Post)*, called the social media campaign conducted by Ukraine during the full-scale invasion "one of the most brilliant campaigns orchestrated by countries in recent decades".[^cite_note-6]

Ukrainian soldier [Roman Trokhymets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Trokhymets) has gained a significant social media following after sharing his experiences in the war online, one of his videos, ″Thoughts and experiences under fire at the front″ gaining over 1.2 million views.[^cite_note-Schumacher_2023_u311-7]

## Military impact

Further information: [Open-source intelligence in the Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-source_intelligence_in_the_Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine)

In the [leadup to Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prelude_to_the_Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine), footage of Russian military vehicles posted to the video-sharing platform [TikTok](56822861.md) helped corroborate widespread Western claims of an impending invasion.[^cite_note-econOSINT-8] During the invasion itself, [open-source intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-source_intelligence) (OSINT) derived from social media footage has continued to play a large role in the conflict. *The Economist* wrote that this poses a "nightmare" for militaries trying to preserve [operational security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operational_security).[^cite_note-econOSINT-8]

On the Ukrainian side, civilians have been able to report information about Russian troop movements through [Telegram](41501305.md) [bots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_bot) that channel the data back to Ukrainian military authorities. The [Security Service of Ukraine](709222.md) have said that at least once, a tip-off had allowed them to successfully strike Russian vehicles during the [Battle of Kyiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Kyiv_\(2022\)) in 2022.[^cite_note-time-1]

## Disinformation and propaganda

For broader coverage of this topic, see [Disinformation in the Russian invasion of Ukraine](70144390.md).

### Russian information war against Ukraine

Main article: [Russian information war against Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_information_war_against_Ukraine)

Russia [has used online disinformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_information_war_against_Ukraine) and propaganda to justify its war aims on social media for years, even before its [2014 annexation of Crimea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annexation_of_Crimea_by_the_Russian_Federation) and the beginning of the [Russo-Ukrainian War](42085878.md).[^cite_note-9][^cite_note-hrw-10] According to a report by NATO's [Cooperative Cyber Defence Centre of Excellence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_Cyber_Defence_Centre_of_Excellence), during the early stages of the war, pro-Russian social media accounts "systemically cultivated fear, anxiety, and hate" among [ethnically Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russians_in_Ukraine) and [other minority populations of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_groups_in_Ukraine) by distributing and manipulating images of supposed atrocities by Ukrainian forces.[^cite_note-Lange108-11] For example, during the [2014 Odesa clashes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Odesa_clashes) in the wider [2014 pro-Russian unrest in Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_pro-Russian_unrest_in_Ukraine), there was a viral post originally posted to Facebook - now widely considered to be a hoax - by a supposed "Igor Rosovskiy" claiming to have witnessed Ukrainian nationalists beating up other residents of the city and burning them alive, and making [antisemitic](1078.md) threats towards Rosovskiy, an alleged emergency physician, when he tried to help the victims. Analysts later discovered that Rosovskiy's profile picture "was actually that of a [dentist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dentist) from the [North Caucasus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Caucasus)", leading them to conclude the story was fake.[^cite_note-Lange108-11] In another prominent example, the fake [crucified boy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crucified_boy) story, a piece of [atrocity propaganda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrocity_propaganda) about Ukrainian forces supposedly publicly [crucifying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crucifixion) a three-year-old boy in the eastern Ukrainian city of [Sloviansk](2533448.md), was originally spread on Russian state TV, but spread widely on social media afterward.[^cite_note-12]

In May 2014, evidence emerged about the creation of the [Internet Research Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Research_Agency) (IRA), which hired Russians to post 100 posts a day in support of the [Russian information war against Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_information_war_against_Ukraine).[^cite_note-13] The founder of the organization was Russian oligarch [Yevgeny Prigozhin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yevgeny_Prigozhin), better known as the leader of the government-affiliated paramilitary [Wagner Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wagner_Group).[^cite_note-Prigozhin-14] It was eventually announced in July 2023 that the IRA would be dissolved, as part of larger reprisals against Prigozhin in the wake of his largely unsuccessful [Wagner Group rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wagner_Group_rebellion).[^cite_note-15]

### Misinformation

Social media has also been the source of largely spontaneous myths about the war, such as the mythical [Ghost of Kyiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost_of_Kyiv), a supposed Ukrainian [MiG-29 Fulcrum](185653.md) [flying ace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flying_ace) credited with shooting down six [Russian](25391.md) planes over [Kyiv](585629.md) during the [Kyiv offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyiv_offensive_\(2022\)) on 24 February 2022.[^cite_note-Bubola-16][^cite_note-17]

### Countermeasures



Logo of [NAFO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAFO_\(group\))

Since the beginning of the Russo-Ukrainian War in 2014, Ukraine has repeatedly urged social media corporations to do more work in countering Russian disinformation.[^cite_note-hrw-10] Since the escalation in 2022, the companies have increased their efforts in this area, but some reports have indicated that these steps fell short.[^cite_note-hrw-10]

The pro-Ukrainian [meme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_meme) group and loose social media movement [NAFO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAFO_\(group\)) ("North Atlantic Fella Organization", a play on [NATO](21133.md)) uses online memes mocking Russian forces and propaganda to further their stated goal of countering Russian disinformation.[^cite_note-vice-18][^cite_note-economist-19]

## Censorship

For broader coverage of this topic, see [Internet censorship in Russia](37529240.md).

Since the beginning of the full-scale invasion of Ukraine, social media has been subject to increased restriction and censorship in Russia. The Russian government fully blocked [Facebook](7529378.md) on 4 March 2022, then [Instagram](31591547.md) on 11 March, after [Meta](62420226.md), the parent company of both websites, introduced an exception to its violent speech policy to allow calls for violence against Russian soldiers in Ukraine.[^cite_note-hrw-10] Russians have been investigated and sometimes sent to prison for criticizing Kremlin policies in social media posts, enabled by [Russian 2022 war censorship laws](70646883.md).[^cite_note-20][^cite_note-21]

## Telegram

In particular, the messaging service [Telegram](41501305.md) has played a highly important role in the information space of the war since 2022, used by both sides for messaging and information.[^cite_note-time-1][^cite_note-atlantic-22][^cite_note-23] Telegram has been popular in Russia for a long time, and was used by Volodymyr Zelensky's campaign in the [2019 Ukrainian presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Ukrainian_presidential_election).[^cite_note-time-1] Telegram has been controversial in the past due to its lack of moderation in oversight, and this has also allowed it to be used a lot by [Russian government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_government) as well as [Russian opposition](38212364.md) sources who "\[found] themselves cut off from most mainstream social media."[^cite_note-time-1]

As a result, it has become the center of new information about the war. Aric Toler, a journalist for the [open-source intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-source_intelligence) investigation organization [Bellingcat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bellingcat), told *The Atlantic* that "Almost every bit of information about the war on [Twitter](9988187.md), \[[Instagram](31591547.md), [Facebook](7529378.md), and others] is downstream of Telegram."[^cite_note-atlantic-22] [Yevgeny Prigozhin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yevgeny_Prigozhin), leader of the [Wagner Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wagner_Group), used Telegram extensively to organize his June 2023 [rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wagner_Group_rebellion) against the Russian Ministry of Defense.[^cite_note-atlantic-22] Telegram also played an "essential" role in helping [Ukrainian refugees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_refugee_crisis_\(2022%E2%80%93present\)) escape the country and get aid.[^cite_note-time-1]
