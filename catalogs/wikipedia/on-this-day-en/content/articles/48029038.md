The **Arba'in pilgrimage** is the world's largest annual public gathering.[^cite_note-IBtimes-22-1][^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3] It is an annual pilgrimage to the [Shrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imam_Husayn_Shrine) of [Husayn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Husayn_ibn_Ali), grandson of the [Islamic prophet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophets_and_messengers_in_Islam) [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad) and the third [Shia Imam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imamate_in_Shia_doctrine). Every year, on the twentieth of [Safar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safar), also known as [Arba'in](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arba'in), millions of pilgrims flock to [Karbala](210957.md) in [Iraq](7515928.md), often arriving there on foot. It marks forty days after the tenth of [Muharram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muharram), known as [Ashura](488563.md). Up to 40 million visit the city during Arba'in, the largest annual public gathering on earth. Most of the pilgrims travel on foot and come from all around Iraq and nearly 60 countries.[^cite_note-4]

On this day in 61 [AH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_calendar) (680 [CE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Era)), Husayn was martyred, alongside most of his relatives and his small retinue, in the [Battle of Karbala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Karbala) by the army of the [Umayyad caliph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Caliphate) [Yazid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yazid_I). Husayn's suffering and martyrdom became a symbol of sacrifice in the struggle for right against wrong, and for justice and truth against injustice and falsehood. He is a universal, borderless, interfaith and [meta-religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meta-religion) symbol. Husayn and his companions are widely regarded as [martyrs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martyr) by all Muslims.

## Significance of Arba'in

Main article: [Arba'in](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arba'in)

See also: [Battle of Karbala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Karbala), [Mourning of Muharram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mourning_of_Muharram), [Ashura](488563.md), and [Muharram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muharram)

In the [Islamic calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_calendar), twentieth of [Safar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safar) is known as Arba'in,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEChelkowski1988-5][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMomen1985239-6] which marks forty days after [Ashura](488563.md), tenth of [Muharram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muharram).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAyoub1986-7] In turn, Ashura is the death anniversary of [Husayn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Husayn_ibn_Ali), grandson of the [Islamic prophet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophets_and_messengers_in_Islam) [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad) and the third [Shia Imam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imamate_in_Shia_doctrine).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAghaie2013-8] Husayn was killed on 10 Muharram 61 AH (10 October 680 [CE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Era)), alongside most of his male relatives and his small retinue, in the [Battle of Karbala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Karbala) against the army of the [Umayyad caliph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Caliphate) [Yazid ibn Mu'awiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yazid_I) (r. 680–683), having been surrounded for some days and deprived of the drinking water of the nearby [Euphrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphrates) river. After the battle, women and children in Husayn's camp were taken prisoner and marched to the Umayyad capital [Damascus](8914.md) in [Syria](7515849.md). The battle followed failed negotiations and Husayn's refusal to pledge his allegiance to Yazid, who is often portrayed by [Muslim](19541.md) historians as impious and immoral.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMomen198528-9][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPinault200070-10][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAghaie2007117-11] The fight took place in the desert land of [Karbala](210957.md), en route to the nearby [Kufa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kufa), whose residents had earlier invited Husayn to lead them against Yazid.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMadelung2004-12]

Karbala symbolizes the eternal struggle between good and evil,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAghaie20049-13][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAghaie2007112-14] the pinnacle of self-sacrifice,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEChelkowski198519-15] and the ultimate sabotage of Muhammad's prophetic mission.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECrow2016-16] Historically, the event served to crystallize the Shia community into a distinct sect and remains an integral part of their religious identity to date.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKennedy201677-17][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHyder20069-18] Ashura to Arba'in is thus a period of mourning for Shia Muslims,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPinault1992188-19] particularly the first ten days of Muharram and Arba'in.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEOsman2014133-20][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAyoub1986-7][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPakatchi2021-21] On the one hand, Shia mourners hope to share in the pain of Husayn to benefit from his intercession on the [Day of Judgement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judgement_Day_in_Islam).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlank200184-22][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMunson198824-23] On the other, the Shia minority views mourning for Husayn as an act of protest against oppression, and as such a struggle for God (*[jihad](16203.md)*).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAyoub1978142–143-24][^cite_note-FOOTNOTENakash1993165-25]

## Origins

Forty is a sacred number in [Islam](6037917.md),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAyoub1986-7] and commemorating the dead forty days after their death is a long-standing Islamic tradition,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHaj_Manouchehri2008-26][^cite_note-FOOTNOTECalmard1987-27][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAyoub1986-7] dating back to the early Islamic period.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHaj_Manouchehri2008-26] Shia tradition attaches a similar significance to Arba'in, the fortieth of Husayn.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHaj_Manouchehri2008-26] Probably by combining the accounts available to him,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHaj_Manouchehri2008-26] the Shia scholar [Ibn Tawus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayyed_Ibn_Tawus) (d. 1266) reports that Husayn's relatives returned via Karbala to their hometown of [Medina](36636.md) when they were freed from captivity in Damascus. Upon arrival in Karbala on Arba'in,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAyoub1978152-28][^cite_note-FOOTNOTECalmard2004-29] they met [Jabir ibn Abd Allah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jabir_ibn_Abd_Allah) (d. 697), a [companion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Companions_of_the_Prophet) of Muhammad,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHaj_Manouchehri2008-26] who had learned about the death of Husayn through a divine sign.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAyoub1986-7] This origin story was repeated by many authors after Ibn Tawus, even though several scholars before Ibn Tawus report only the Arba'in pilgrimage of Jabir.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHaj_Manouchehri2008-26] The veracity of Ibn Tawus' account has therefore been questioned by some, including the Shia scholar [Husain Noori Tabarsi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirza_Husain_Noori_Tabarsi) (d. 1902) and the Islamicist [Mahmoud M. Ayoub](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahmoud_M._Ayoub) (d. 2021).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHaj_Manouchehri2008-26][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAyoub1986-7]

Ayoub adds that Arba'in is not mentioned in *[Kamil al-ziyarat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamil_al-Ziyarat)*, an early and authoritative hadith collection by the Shia traditionist [Ibn Qulawayh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Qulawayh) (d. c. 978).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAyoub1986-7] Whatever the case, such narratives may have helped establish Arba'in in Shia culture.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHaj_Manouchehri2008-26] Risking the Umayyads' wrath, the commemoration of Karbala was initially small and private.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHussain200581-30][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHyder200620-31] In particular, pilgrimage to Karbala remained limited and precarious during the Umayyad period.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENakash1993167-32] Soon after the Umayyads fell, however, Shia imams worked to institutionalize the Ashura and Arba'in pilgrimages to the tomb of Husayn, as reflected in some of the traditions ascribed to the imams. For instance, the Shia imam [Hasan al-Askari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasan_al-Askari) (d. c. 874) is reported to have listed the Arba'in pilgrimage among the five signs of a true believer.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHaj_Manouchehri2008-26]

## The world's largest annual gathering

Arba'in is a day of pilgrimage to the [shrine of Husayn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imam_Husayn_Shrine) in Karbala, [Iraq](7515928.md). Pilgrims arrive there in large numbers, often on foot. The most popular route is [Najaf](200512.md) to Karbala, as many pilgrims first travel to Najaf and then walk from there to Karbala, some eighty kilometers away, which takes about three days on foot. Along the way, volunteers provide the pilgrims with free meals and services.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESzanto201814-33] In Karbala alone, seven thousand of such hospitality units (*mawakib*, sing. *mawkib*) were set up in 2014.[^cite_note-\:5-34] Indeed, this generosity and hospitality are said to characterize the Arba'in pilgrimage. When the pilgrims finally reach the shrine of Husayn in Karbala, they recite the [*ziyara* of Arba'in](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ziyarat_of_Arba'een), a supplication for this occasion.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESzanto201814-33]

As with other Shia rituals of Karbala,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGordon_Melton2010211–212-35] the Arba'in pilgrimage was banned by the Iraqi president [Saddam Hussein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saddam_Hussein) (r. 1979–2003),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENasr200718–19-36] who favored the [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam) community in Iraq,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGordon_Melton2010211–212-35] and viewed large Shia rituals as a political threat.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENasr200718–19-36] The pilgrimage was revived immediately after the deposal of Saddam in 2003,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENasr200718–19-36] with the total attendance numbers for this multi-day event growing from two million participants in that year to nine million in 2008,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENasr200718–19-36][^cite_note-37] and around twenty million in 2014,[^cite_note-IBtimes-22-1][^cite_note-political-38] making that year's pilgrimage the second largest gathering in history.[^cite_note-largest-39] The figure reached twenty-two million in 2015, according to Iraq's state-run media.[^cite_note-\:3-40] In 2016, [al-Khoei Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imam_Al-Khoei_Benevolent_Foundation) estimated around twenty-two million pilgrims.[^cite_note-41] Even though the Hindu festival [Kumbh Mela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kumbh_Mela) draws a larger crowd, it is held once every three years, which makes the Arba'in pilgrimage "the world's largest annual gathering in one place."[^cite_note-IBtimes-22-1]

### Comparison to Hajj

Unlike the voluntary Arba'in pilgrimage, performing [Hajj](23487999.md) once is obligatory for every Muslim who can afford it and is physically capable. Nevertheless, tight regulations on Hajj by [Saudi](349303.md) authorities have driven up its costs, making pilgrimage to Shia shrines an affordable alternative for Shia Muslims. In recent years, Karbala has consistently received ten to twenty million pilgrims, compared to fewer than three million Hajj pilgrims in 2018.[^cite_note-42]

### Interfaith participation

As Hussein is regarded as a universal, borderless, and [meta-religious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meta-religion) symbol,[^cite_note-43][^cite_note-ibtimes.co.uk-44] the Arba'een pilgrimage, while rooted in Shia Islam, has emerged as a symbol of interfaith engagement. It increasingly attracts participants from various religious backgrounds, including [Sunnis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam), [Christians](18337522.md), [Jews](25955086.md),[^cite_note-45] and non-Abrahamic faiths such as [Hindus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindus), [Yazidis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yazidis), and [Zoroastrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism), who come to commemorate and mourn the death of Husayn.[^cite_note-auto-46][^cite_note-47][^cite_note-ibtimes.co.uk-44] Each year, millions of participants, including Sunnis and people of other faiths, join the pilgrimage to Karbala, both to attend and to serve the devotees.[^cite_note-auto-46][^cite_note-48][^cite_note-49]

### Free services

During the pilgrimage "copious supplies of food, small clinics and even dentists are available for pilgrims and they all work for free. The care of pilgrims is regarded as a religious duty."[^cite_note-\:0-50] Along the roads to Karbala, *mawakib* provide "accommodation, food and beverage and medical services," and practically anything else the pilgrims need for free.[^cite_note-Modarresi-51]

## In the media

### False reports

In 2016, *[Asharq al-Awsat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asharq_al-Awsat)*, a Saudi-owned news site, reported from the [World Health Organization](33583.md) (WHO) that "unplanned pregnancies and \[...] disease" were seen "following the arrival of scores of unregulated Iranians to take part in the annual Shia pilgrimage to Karbala." The article added that 169 unmarried women were impregnated by Iranian pilgrims.[^cite_note-\:4-52][^cite_note-nst-53] These claims later proved to be fabricated. They were rejected by the WHO, which also condemned the use of its name for spreading false news.[^cite_note-nst-53][^cite_note-54] The [Iraqi prime minister](208879.md) [Haider al-Abadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haider_al-Abadi) and some other Shia leaders similarly condemned the report,[^cite_note-nst-53] which had further described the arrival of Iranian pilgrims as an "incursion against Iraqi sovereignty" and warned that they would spread diseases in Iraq.[^cite_note-\:4-52]

## Security aspects

As with Ashura,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGordon_Melton2010-55] Arba'in can be an occasion for Sunni violence against Shia Muslims.[^cite_note-56][^cite_note-57][^cite_note-58] For instance, a suicide bomber killed at least forty-four Arba'in pilgrims and wounded some seventy others in 2012 near [Nasiriya](33572510.md), Iraq.[^cite_note-CNN-59] In another attack in 2013, at least twenty pilgrims were killed and another fifty were wounded by a car bomb.[^cite_note-60] The pilgrimage is therefore performed under tightened security, guarded by tens of thousands of Iraqi forces,[^cite_note-Reuters-61] and supported by [Iranian](14653.md) advisers at least in 2015.[^cite_note-62] In the same year, the Iraqi police seized eighteen [booby-trapped](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booby-trap) dolls, stuffed with explosives, which were intended to be scattered on the roads leading to Karbala during the Arba'in pilgrimage.[^cite_note-63][^cite_note-64]

## Political dimensions

As with other Shia rituals of Karbala,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGordon_Melton2010211–212-35] the Arba'in pilgrimage was banned by Saddam Hussein,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENasr200718–19-36] who favored the Sunni minority in Iraq,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGordon_Melton2010211–212-35] and viewed Shia rituals as a political threat.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENasr200718–19-36] The pilgrimage was revived immediately after his deposal in 2003 and that year's march to Karbala thus symbolized Shia defiance of Sunni regional powers. The 2003 pilgrimage also set off a regional wave of Sunni violence against the Shia minority as scores of Shia mourners were killed in multiple bombings in the following Ashura.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENasr200718–20-65] Later on, as millions of Shias risked their lives by their participation, the Arba'in pilgrimage embodied their protests against the rise of [ISIL](9087364.md), a Sunni extremist militant group with aspirations for a hardline Sunni caliphate and responsible for several Shia massacres.[^cite_note-political-38][^cite_note-\:2-66] In recent years, the participation of non-Iraqi Shias in the Arba'in pilgrimage has fostered a sense of solidarity among Shias. In particular, the unprecedented Iranian attendance in recent pilgrimages could be an indication of Iran's victories against its Sunni regional rivals. Yet the Arba'in pilgrimage also exposes the rivalries between different political currents within the Shia community.[^cite_note-political-38]

The contemporary scale and political significance of the Arba'in pilgrimage has also become a subject of critical academic inquiry. Iranian sociologist Hossein Solati, employing [Foucauldian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foucault) genealogical method, has analyzed the pilgrimage not as a continuous tradition but as a "constructed" and "re-articulated" cultural phenomenon. Solati argues that the Arba'in walk, as a "new manifestation" of cultural identity, represents a discursive break from earlier identity formations in Iran. His analysis focuses on the interplay of power, knowledge, and truth in shaping this ritual, suggesting that its current form is inseparable from the power relations and political imperatives of post-revolutionary Iran.[^cite_note-67] This genealogical perspective, emphasizing "discontinuity" and the constructed nature of identity, offers a counterpoint to narratives that frame the pilgrimage solely as an expression of timeless faith or unified political will, instead revealing it as a site where contemporary Iranian identity is actively negotiated and produced.
