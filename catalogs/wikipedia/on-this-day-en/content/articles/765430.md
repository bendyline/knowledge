**Social liberalism**,[^cite_note-9] or **progressive liberalism**,[^cite_note-10] is a [political philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_philosophy) and variety of [liberalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberalism) that endorses [social justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_justice), [social services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_services), a [mixed economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_economy), and the expansion of [civil and political rights](37071.md), as slightly opposed to [classical liberalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_liberalism) which favors [limited government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limited_government) and an overall more *[laissez-faire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laissez-faire)* style of governance. While both are committed to personal freedoms, social liberalism places greater emphasis on the role of government in addressing social inequalities and ensuring public welfare. Social liberal governments address economic and social issues such as [poverty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poverty), [welfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welfare_spending), [infrastructure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrastructure), [healthcare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Healthcare), and [education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education) using government intervention, while emphasising individual rights and autonomy.[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12][^cite_note-13]

Economically, social liberalism is based on the [social market economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_market_economy) and views the [common good](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_good) as harmonious with the individual's freedom.[^cite_note-14] Social liberals overlap with [social democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_democrats) in accepting [market intervention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_intervention) more than other liberals;[^cite_note-Slomp_2000-15] its importance is considered auxiliary compared to social democrats.[^cite_note-16] Ideologies that emphasize its economic policy include **welfare liberalism**,[^cite_note-17] [New Deal liberalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Deal_liberalism) and [New Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Democrats_\(United_States\)) in the United States,[^cite_note-18] and **Keynesian liberalism**.[^cite_note-19] Almost all of the Western world has widely adopted social liberal policies.[^cite_note-Faulks-20]

Social liberal ideas and parties tend to be considered [centre](39619035.md) to [centre-left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre-left), although there are deviations from these positions to both the [political left or right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_spectrum).[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-Slomp_2000-15][^cite_note-hombachbodo-22][^cite_note-womenpower-23] In modern political discourse, social liberalism is associated with [progressivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressivism),[^cite_note-24][^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26] a left-liberalism contrasted to the right-leaning [neoliberalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoliberalism),[^cite_note-27] and combines support for a [mixed economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_economy) with cultural liberalism.[^cite_note-28]

[Cultural liberalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_liberalism) is an ideology that advocates the freedom of individuals to choose whether to conform to cultural norms. In American usage, the term *social liberalism* may also refer to [American progressive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_progressive) stances on sociocultural issues,[^cite_note-29] such as [reproductive rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reproductive_rights) and [same-sex marriage](92656.md), in contrast with [American social conservatism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_social_conservatism). Cultural liberalism is often referred to as *social liberalism* because it expresses the social dimension of liberalism; however, it is not the same as the broader political ideology known as social liberalism. In [American politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_politics), a *social liberal* may hold either [conservative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiscal_conservatism) ([economic liberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_liberal)) or [progressive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_progressive) views on [fiscal policy](132645.md).[^cite_note-30]

## Origins

See also: [History of liberalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_liberalism)

### United Kingdom



[Leonard Hobhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Hobhouse) was one of the originators of social liberalism, notably through his book *Liberalism*, published in 1911.

By the end of the 19th century, downturns in economic growth challenged the principles of [classical liberalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_liberalism), a growing awareness of poverty and unemployment present within modern industrial cities, and the agitation of [organised labour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organised_labour). A significant political reaction against the changes introduced by [industrialisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrialisation) and *[laissez-faire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laissez-faire)* [capitalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalism) came from [one-nation conservatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-nation_conservatism) concerned about social balance and the introduction of the famous [Education Act 1870](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_Act_1870). However, [socialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism) later became a more important force for change and reform. Some Victorian writers—including [Charles Dickens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Dickens), [Thomas Carlyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Carlyle) and [Matthew Arnold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Arnold)—became early influential critics of social injustice.[^cite_note-31]

[John Stuart Mill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Stuart_Mill) contributed enormously to liberal thought by combining elements of classical liberalism with what eventually became known as the new liberalism. Mill developed this philosophy by liberalising the concept of [consequentialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consequentialism) to promote a rights based system.[^cite_note-32] He also developed his liberal dogma by combining the idea of using a [utilitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utilitarianism) foundation to base upon the idea of individual rights.[^cite_note-33] The new liberals tried to adapt the old language of liberalism to confront these difficult circumstances, which they believed could only be resolved through a broader and more interventionist conception of the state. Ensuring that individuals did not physically interfere with each other or merely by impartially having formulated and applied laws could not establish an equal right to liberty. More positive and proactive measures were required to ensure that every individual would have an [equal opportunity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equal_opportunity) for success.[^cite_note-eatwell-34]

#### New Liberals

Not to be confused with [The New Liberals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TNL_\(political_party\)) in Australia.



[Thomas Hill Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hill_Green)

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, a group of British thinkers known as the New Liberals made a case against *laissez-faire* classical liberalism. It argued in favour of state intervention in social, economic and cultural life. What they proposed is now called social liberalism.[^cite_note-freeden-1] The New Liberals, including intellectuals [Thomas Hill Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hill_Green), [Leonard Hobhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Hobhouse) and [John A. Hobson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_A._Hobson), saw individual liberty achievable only under favourable social and economic circumstances.[^cite_note-adams-2] In their view, the poverty, squalor, and ignorance in which many people lived made it impossible for freedom and individuality to flourish. New Liberals believed through collective action coordinated by a strong, welfare-oriented and interventionist state could alleviate these conditions.

The [Liberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Party_\(UK\)) governments of [Henry Campbell-Bannerman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Campbell-Bannerman) and [H. H. Asquith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._H._Asquith), mainly thanks to [Chancellor of the Exchequer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancellor_of_the_Exchequer) and later Prime Minister [David Lloyd George](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Lloyd_George), established the foundations of the [welfare state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welfare_state) in the United Kingdom before World War I. The comprehensive welfare state built in the United Kingdom after World War II, although primarily accomplished by the [Labour Party](19279158.md)'s [Attlee ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attlee_ministry), was significantly designed by two Liberals, namely [John Maynard Keynes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maynard_Keynes) (who laid the foundations in economics with the [Keynesian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keynesian_Revolution)) and [William Beveridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Beveridge) (whose [Beveridge Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beveridge_Report) was used to design the welfare system).[^cite_note-adams-2]

&#x20;Historian Peter Weiler has argued:&#x20;

> Although still partially informed by older Liberal concerns for character, self-reliance, and the capitalist market, this legislation nevertheless marked a significant shift in Liberal approaches to the state and social reform, approaches that later governments would slowly expand and that would grow into the welfare state after the Second World War. What was new in these reforms was the underlying assumption that the state could be a positive force, that the measure of individual freedom ... was not how much the state left people alone, but whether he gave them the capacity to fulfill themselves as individuals.[^cite_note-35][^cite_note-36]

### Germany

The term "Social Liberalism" may have first been employed by [Max Stirner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Stirner) in his primary work, [The Ego and its Own](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ego_and_its_Own), used to ridicule [Socialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism).[^cite_note-37]

In 1860s Germany, left-liberal politicians like [Max Hirsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Hirsch_\(labor_economist\)), [Franz Duncker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Duncker), and [Hermann Schulze-Delitzsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Schulze-Delitzsch) established the [Hirsch-Duncker trade unions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hirsch-Duncker_trade_unions)—modelled on their British counterparts—to help workers improve working and economic conditions through reconciliation of interests and cooperation with their employers rather than class struggle. Schulze-Delitzsch is also the founding father of the German [cooperative movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_cooperative_movement) and the organiser of the world's first [credit unions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credit_union). Some liberal economists, such as [Lujo Brentano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lujo_Brentano) or [Gerhart von Schulze-Gävernitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerhart_von_Schulze-G%C3%A4vernitz), established the *[Verein für Socialpolitik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verein_f%C3%BCr_Socialpolitik)* (German Economic Association) in 1873 to promote social reform based on the [historical school of economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_school_of_economics) and therefore rejecting [classical economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_economics), proposing a third way between [Manchester Liberalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Liberalism) and socialist revolution in the 1871-founded [German Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Empire).

However, the German left-liberal movement fragmented into wings and new parties over the 19th century. The main objectives of the left-liberal parties—the [German Progress Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Progress_Party) and its successors—were free speech, freedom of assembly, representative government, secret and equal but obligation-tied suffrage, and protection of private property. At the same time, they were strongly opposed to creating a [welfare state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welfare_state), which they called [state socialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_socialism). The main differences between the left-liberal parties were:

- The national ambitions.

- The different substate people's goals.

- [Free trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_trade) against *Schutzzollpolitik.*

- *The* building of the national economy.

The term *social liberalism* ([German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language): *Sozialliberalismus*) was used first in 1891 by [Austria-Hungarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria-Hungary) economist and journalist [Theodor Hertzka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Hertzka).[^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39] Subsequently, in 1893, the historian and [social reformer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reform_movement) [Ignaz Jastrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignaz_Jastrow) also used this term and joined the [German Economic Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verein_f%C3%BCr_Socialpolitik). He published the [socialist democratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_socialism) manifesto "Social-liberal: Tasks for Liberalism in Prussia" to create an "action group" for the general people's welfare in the [Social Democratic Party of Germany](60585.md), which they rejected.[^cite_note-40]



[Friedrich Naumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Naumann)

The [National-Social Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National-Social_Association), founded by the Protestant pastor [Friedrich Naumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Naumann) also maintained contacts with the left liberals.[^cite_note-41] He tried to draw workers away from [Marxism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism) by proposing a mix of nationalism and Protestant-Christian-value-inflected social liberalism to overcome class antagonisms by non-revolutionary means. Naumann called this a "proletarian-bourgeois integral liberalism". Although the party could not win any seats and soon dissolved, he remained influential in theoretical German left-liberalism.

In the [Weimar Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weimar_Republic), the [German Democratic Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Democratic_Party) was founded and came into an inheritance of the left-liberal past and had a leftist social wing[^cite_note-42] and a rightist economic wing but heavily favoured the democratic constitution over a monarchist one. Its ideas of a socially balanced economy with solidarity, duty, and rights among all workers struggled due to the economic sanctions of the [Treaty of Versailles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Versailles), but it influenced local [cooperative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative) enterprises.[^cite_note-43][^cite_note-Kurlander-44]

After 1945, the [Free Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Democratic_Party_\(Germany\)) included most of the social liberals, including Naumann and Brentano disciple [Theodor Heuss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Heuss) who became the first party chairman and the first [Federal President of West Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_Germany). Other social liberals joined the [Christian Democratic Union of Germany](60584.md). Until the 1960s, [post-war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-war) [ordoliberalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordoliberalism) was the model for Germany. It had a theoretical social liberal influence based on duty and rights.[^cite_note-cis.org.au-45]

During the 1950s and 1960s, the Free Democratic Party was dominated by [conservative liberals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_liberalism), [national liberals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_liberalism) and [classical liberals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_liberalism). However, in the 1970s, the party was briefly influenced by [progressive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressivism) and social liberal ideas which culminated in the 1971 Freiburg Thesis programme.[^cite_note-Merkl1989-46] Among other things, the party committed itself to "self-determination", "democratization of society", a "reform of capitalism" and a form of [ecoliberalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eco-liberalism) which prioritized "environmental protection over profit and personal gains".[^cite_note-47] In 1977, the social liberal era came to an end with the more [economically liberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_liberalism) Kiel Thesis programme (*Kieler Thesen*) effectively setting the party back on a classical liberal course.

As the Free Democrats discarded social liberal ideas in a more conservative and economically liberal approach,[^cite_note-48] some members left the party and formed the social liberal [Liberal Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Democrats_\(Germany\)) in 1982.[^cite_note-49][^cite_note-50] Other social liberals have found a new home in Germany's Green party [Alliance 90/The Greens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alliance_90/The_Greens).[^cite_note-51]

### France

In France, [solidaristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_solidarity) thinkers, including [Alfred Fouillée](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Fouill%C3%A9e) and [Émile Durkheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89mile_Durkheim), developed the social-liberal theory in the Third Republic. [Sociology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology) inspired them, and they influenced radical politicians like [Léon Bourgeois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%A9on_Bourgeois). They explained that a more extensive division of labour caused more opportunity and [individualism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Individualism) and inspired more complex interdependence. They argued that the individual had a debt to society, promoting progressive taxation to support public works and welfare schemes. However, they wanted the state to coordinate rather than manage, encouraging cooperative insurance schemes among individuals. Their main objective was to remove barriers to social mobility rather than create a welfare state.[^cite_note-52]

### United States

Main article: [Modern liberalism in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_liberalism_in_the_United_States)



[Franklin D. Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt), the 32nd [President of the United States](24113.md), whose [New Deal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Deal) domestic policies defined [American liberalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_liberalism) for the middle third of the 20th century

Social liberalism was a term in the United States to differentiate it from [classical liberalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberalism_in_the_United_States) or *[laissez-faire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laissez-faire)*. It dominated political and economic thought for several years until the word branched off from it around the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression) and the [New Deal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Deal).[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-richardson-54] In the 1870s and the 1880s, the American economists [Richard Ely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_T._Ely), [John Bates Clark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Bates_Clark), and [Henry Carter Adams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Carter_Adams)—influenced both by [socialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism) and the [Evangelical Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelical_Protestant) movement—castigated the conditions caused by industrial factories and expressed sympathy toward [labour unions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labor_unions). However, none developed a systematic political philosophy, and they later abandoned their flirtations with socialist thinking. In 1883, [Lester Frank Ward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lester_Frank_Ward) published the two-volume *Dynamic Sociology*. He formalized the basic tenets of social liberalism while at the same time attacking the *laissez-faire* policies advocated by [Herbert Spencer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Spencer) and [William Graham Sumner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Graham_Sumner). The historian [Henry Steele Commager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Steele_Commager) ranked Ward alongside [William James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_James), [John Dewey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dewey), and [Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Wendell_Holmes_Jr.) and called him the father of the modern welfare state.[^cite_note-55] A writer from 1884 until the 1930s, John Dewey—an educator influenced by Hobhouse, Green, and Ward—advocated socialist methods to achieve liberal goals. John Dewey's expanding popularity as an economist also coincided with the greater [Georgist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgism) movement that rose in the 1910s, pinnacling with the presidency of [Woodrow Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodrow_Wilson).[^cite_note-56] America later incorporated some social liberal ideas into the New Deal,[^cite_note-57] which developed as a response to the Great Depression when Franklin D. Roosevelt came into office.

## Implementation



[David Lloyd George](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Lloyd_George), who became closely associated with this new liberalism and vigorously supported expanding social welfare

The welfare state grew gradually and unevenly from the late 19th century but fully developed following World War II, along with the [mixed market economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_market_economy) and general [welfare capitalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welfare_capitalism).[^cite_note-58] Also called [embedded liberalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embedded_liberalism), social liberal policies gained broad support across the political spectrum because they reduced society's disruptive and polarizing tendencies without challenging the capitalist economic system. Businesses accepted social liberalism in the face of widespread dissatisfaction with the [boom and bust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boom_and_bust) cycle of the earlier financial system as it seemed to them to be a lesser evil than more left-wing modes of government. Characteristics of social liberalism were cooperation between big business, government, and labour unions. Governments could assume a vital role because the wartime economy had strengthened their power, but the extent to which this occurred varied considerably among Western democracies.[^cite_note-59] Social liberalism is also a generally [internationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internationalism_\(politics\)) ideology.[^cite_note-60] Social liberalism has also historically been an advocate for [liberal feminism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_feminism) among other forms of social progress.[^cite_note-61]

Social liberals tend to find a compromise between the perceived extremes of unrestrained [capitalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalism) and [state socialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_socialism) to create an economy built on [regulated capitalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulated_capitalism).[^cite_note-62] Due to a reliance on what they believe to be a too [centralized government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centralized_government) to achieve its goals, critics have called this strain of liberalism a more authoritarian ideological position compared to the original schools of liberal thought, especially in the United States, where conservatives have called presidents [Franklin D. Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt) and [Lyndon B. Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson) authoritarians.[^cite_note-63][^cite_note-64]

### United Kingdom



British leaflet from the [Liberal Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Party_\(UK\)) expressing support for the [National Insurance Act 1911](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Insurance_Act_1911) and the legislation provided benefits to sick and unemployed workers, marking a major milestone in the development of [social welfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_welfare)

The first notable implementation of social liberal policies occurred under the [Liberal Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Party_\(United_Kingdom\)) in Britain from 1906 until 1914. These initiatives became known as the [Liberal welfare reforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_welfare_reforms). The main elements included pensions for poor older adults, and [health, sickness, and unemployment insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Insurance_Act_1911). These changes were accompanied by [progressive taxation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_income_tax), particularly in the [People's Budget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People's_Budget) of 1909. The old system of charity relying on the [Poor Laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Poor_Laws) and supplemented by private charity, public cooperatives, and private insurance companies was in crisis, giving the state added impetus for reform. The Liberal Party caucus elected in 1906 also contained more professionals, including academics and journalists, sympathetic to social liberalism. The large business owners had mostly deserted the Liberals for the [Conservatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Party_\(UK\)), the latter becoming the favourite party for commercial interests. Both business interests and trade unions regularly opposed the reforms. Liberals most identified with these reforms were Prime Minister [H. H. Asquith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._H._Asquith), [John Maynard Keynes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maynard_Keynes), [David Lloyd George](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Lloyd_George) (especially as [Chancellor of the Exchequer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancellor_of_the_Exchequer)), and [Winston Churchill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winston_Churchill) (as [President of the Board of Trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_Board_of_Trade)), in addition to the civil servant (and later Liberal MP) [William Beveridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Beveridge).[^cite_note-65]

Most of the [social democratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_democracy) parties in Europe (including the [British Labour Party](19279158.md)) have taken on strong influences of social liberal ideology. Despite Britain's two major parties coming from the traditions of socialism and conservatism, the most substantive political and economic debates of recent times were between social liberal and classical liberal concepts.[^cite_note-Vincent_54-66]

### Germany



[Alexander Rüstow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_R%C3%BCstow)

[Alexander Rüstow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_R%C3%BCstow), a German economist, first proposed the German variant of economically social liberalism. In 1932, he dubbed this kind of social liberalism [neoliberalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoliberalism) while speaking at the Social Policy Association. However, that term now carries a meaning different from the one proposed by Rüstow. Rüstow wanted an alternative to socialism and the classical liberal economics developed in the [German Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Empire). In 1938, Rüstow met with various economic thinkers—including [Ludwig Mises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Mises), [Friedrich Hayek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Hayek), and [Wilhelm Röpke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_R%C3%B6pke)—to determine how and what could renew liberalism. Rüstow advocated a powerful state to enforce free markets and state intervention to correct [market failures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_failure). However, Mises argued that monopolies and cartels operated because of state intervention and protectionism and claimed that the only legitimate role for the state was to abolish barriers to market entry. He viewed Rüstow's proposals as negating market freedom and saw them as similar to socialism.[^cite_note-cis.org.au-45]

Following World War II, the West German government adopted Rüstow's neoliberalism, now usually called [ordoliberalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordoliberalism) or the [social market economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_market_economy), under [Ludwig Erhard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Erhard). He was the Minister of Economics and later became Chancellor. Erhard lifted price controls and introduced free markets. While Germany's post-war economic recovery was due to these policies, the welfare state—which Bismarck had established—became increasingly costly.[^cite_note-cis.org.au-45]

### Turkey

Main article: [Kemalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kemalism)

The Kemalist economic model was designed by [Mustafa Kemal Atatürk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mustafa_Kemal_Atat%C3%BCrk) in 1930s, founder of the Republic of Turkey, after an unsuccessful attempt to embrace a [regulated market economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulated_market) from [İzmir Economic Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%B0zmir_Economic_Congress) until the [1929 Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression). He put the principle of "[etatism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kemalism#Etatism)" in his [Six Arrows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Six_Arrows) and stated that etatism was a unique economic system for Turkey and that it was different from [socialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism), [communism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communism), and [collectivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collectivism_and_individualism).[^cite_note-67] Atatürk explained his economic idea as follows:

> State can't take the place of individuals, but, it must take into consideration the individuals to make them improve and develop theirselves. Etatism includes the work that individuals won't do because they can't make profit or the work which are necessary for national interests. Just as it is the duty of the state to protect the freedom and independence of the country and to regulate internal affairs, the state must take care of the education and health of its citizens. The state must take care of the roads, railways, telegraphs, telephones, animals of the country, all kinds of vehicles and the general wealth of the nation to protect the peace and security of the country. During the administration and protection of the country, the things we just counted are more important than cannons, rifles and all kinds of weapons. (...) Private interests are generally the opposite of the general interests. Also, private interests are based on rivalries. But, you can't create a stable economy only with this. People who think like that are delusional and they will be a failure. (...) And, work of an individual must stay as the main basis of economic growth. Not preventing an individual's work and not obstructing the individual's freedom and enterprise with the state's own activities is the main basis of the principle of democracy.[^cite_note-68]

Moreover, Atatürk said this in his opening speech on 1 November 1937: "Unless there is an absolute necessity, the [markets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_economy) can't be intervened; also, no markets can be completely free."[^cite_note-69] Also it was said by [İsmet İnönü](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%B0smet_%C4%B0n%C3%B6n%C3%BC) that Atatürk's principle of [etatism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kemalism#Etatism) was [Keynesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keynesian_economics) and a Turkish variant of [New Deal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Deal).[^cite_note-70]

### Rest of Europe

The post-war governments of other countries in Western Europe also followed social liberal policies. These policies were implemented primarily by [Christian democrats](328969.md) and [social democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_democracy) as liberal parties in Europe declined in strength from their peak in the 19th century.[^cite_note-71]

American political discourse resisted this social turn in European liberalism. While the economic policies of the [New Deal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Deal) appeared [Keynesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keynesianism), there was no revision of liberal theory in favour of more significant state initiatives. Even though the United States lacked an effective socialist movement, New Deal policies often appeared radical and were attacked by the right. American liberalism would eventually evolve into a more [anti-communist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-communism) ideology as a result.[^cite_note-72] [American exceptionalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_exceptionalism) was likely the reason for the separate development of [modern liberalism in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_liberalism_in_the_United_States), which kept mainstream American ideology within a narrow range.[^cite_note-73]

[John Rawls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Rawls)' principal work, *[A Theory of Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Theory_of_Justice)* (1971), can be considered a flagship exposition of social liberal thinking, noted for its use of [analytic philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_philosophy) and advocating the combination of individual freedom and a fairer distribution of resources.[^cite_note-74] According to Rawls, every individual should be allowed to choose and pursue their conception of what is desirable. At the same time, the greater society must maintain a socially just distribution of goods. Rawls argued that differences in material wealth are tolerable if general economic growth and wealth also benefit the poorest.[^cite_note-75] *A Theory of Justice* countered [utilitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utilitarianism) thinking in the tradition of [Jeremy Bentham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Bentham), instead following the [Kantian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant) concept of a [social contract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_contract), picturing society as a mutual agreement between [rational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationality) citizens, producing rights and duties as well as establishing and defining roles and tasks of the state. Rawls put the equal liberty principle in the first place, providing every person with equal access to the same set of [fundamental liberties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_rights), followed by the fair equality of opportunity and difference, thus allowing social and economic inequalities under the precondition that privileged positions are accessible to everyone, that everyone has equal opportunities and that even the least advantaged members of society benefit from this framework. This framework repeated itself in the equation of *[Justice as Fairness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justice_as_Fairness)*. Rawls proposed these principles not just to adherents of liberalism but as a basis for all democratic politics, regardless of ideology. The work advanced social liberal ideas immensely within the 1970s political and philosophic academia.[^cite_note-76] Rawls may therefore be a "patron saint" of social liberalism.[^cite_note-Vincent_54-66]

## Decline

Following economic problems in the 1960s and 1970s, liberal thought underwent some transformation. Keynesian financial management faced criticism for interfering with the free market. At the same time, increased welfare spending funded by higher taxes prompted fears of lower investment, lower [consumer spending](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumer_spending), and the creation of a "dependency culture." Trade unions often caused high wages and industrial disruption, while total employment was considered unsustainable. Writers such as [Milton Friedman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milton_Friedman) and [Samuel Brittan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Brittan), whom Friedrich Hayek influenced, advocated a reversal of social liberalism. Their policies—often called [neoliberalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoliberalism)—had a significant influence on Western politics, most notably on the governments of United Kingdom Prime Minister [Margaret Thatcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Thatcher) and the United States President [Ronald Reagan](25433.md). They pursued policies of [deregulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deregulation) of the economy and reduction in spending on social services.[^cite_note-Faulks-20]

Part of the reason for the collapse of the social liberal coalition was a challenge in the 1960s and 1970s from financial interests that could operate independently of national governments. A related reason was the comparison of ideas such as socialized medicine, advocated by politicians such as Franklin D. Roosevelt, facing criticisms and being dubbed as socialist by conservatives during the midst of the [Red Scare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Scare), notably by the previously mentioned Reagan.[^cite_note-77] Another cause was the decline of organized labour which had formed part of the coalition but was also a support for left-wing ideologies challenging the liberal consensus. Related to this were the downfall of working-class consciousness and the growth of the middle class. The push by the United States and the United Kingdom, which had been least accepting of social liberalism for trade liberalization, further eroded support.[^cite_note-78]

## Contemporary revival of social liberal thought

From the end of the 20th century, at the same time that it was losing political influence, social liberalism experienced an intellectual revival with several substantial authors, including John Rawls (political philosophy), [Amartya Sen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amartya_Sen) (philosophy and economy), [Ronald Dworkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Dworkin) (philosophy of law), [Martha Nussbaum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martha_Nussbaum) (philosophy), [Bruce Ackerman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Ackerman) (constitutional law), and others.[^cite_note-79]

## Parties and organisations

In Europe, social liberal parties tend to be small or medium-sized [centrist](39619035.md) and [centre-left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre-left) parties.[^cite_note-80] Examples of successful European social liberal parties participating in government coalitions at national or regional levels include the [Liberal Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Democrats_\(UK\)) in the United Kingdom, the [Democrats 66](172352.md) in the Netherlands, and the [Danish Social Liberal Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_Social_Liberal_Party). In continental European politics, social liberal parties are integrated into the [Renew Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renew_Europe) group in the [European Parliament](9581.md), the fifth biggest group in the parliament, and includes social liberal parties, market liberal parties, and centrist parties. Other groups such as the [European People's Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_People's_Party), the [Greens–European Free Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greens%E2%80%93European_Free_Alliance), and the [Progressive Alliance of Socialists and Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_Alliance_of_Socialists_and_Democrats) also house some political parties with social-liberal factions.

In North America, social liberalism (as Europe would refer to it) tends to be the dominant form of liberalism present, so in common parlance, "liberal" refers to social liberals. In [Canada](5042916.md), social liberalism is held by the [Liberal Party of Canada](149536.md), while in the [United States](3434750.md), social liberalism is a significant force within the [Democratic Party](5043544.md).

Giving an exhaustive list of social liberal parties worldwide is difficult, mainly because political organisations are not always ideologically pure, and party ideologies often change over time. However, peers such as the [Africa Liberal Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa_Liberal_Network), the [Alliance of Liberals and Democrats for Europe Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alliance_of_Liberals_and_Democrats_for_Europe_Party), the [Council of Asian Liberals and Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Asian_Liberals_and_Democrats), the [European Liberal Forum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Liberal_Forum), the [Liberal International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_International), and the [Liberal Network for Latin America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Network_for_Latin_America) or scholars usually accept them as parties who are following social liberalism as a core ideology.
