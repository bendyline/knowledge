A **structure fire** is a fire involving the structural components of various types of residential, commercial or industrial [buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Building), such as [barn fires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barn_fire). Residential buildings range from [single-family detached homes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-family_detached_home) and [townhouses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Townhouse) to [apartments](265567.md) and [tower blocks](118767.md), or various [commercial buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_building) ranging from [offices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office) to [shopping malls](235723.md). This is in contrast to "room and contents" fires, [chimney fires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chimney_fire), [vehicle fires](5856003.md), [wildfires](56106.md) or other outdoor fires.

Structure fires typically have a similar response from the [fire department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_department) that include [engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_apparatus), ladder trucks, [rescue squads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rescue_squad), chief officers, and an [EMS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergency_medical_services) unit, each of which will have specific initial assignments. The actual response and assignments will vary between fire departments.

It is not unusual for some fire departments to have a predetermined mobilization plan for when a fire incident is reported in certain structures in their area. This plan may include mobilizing the nearest aerial [firefighting vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firefighting_apparatus) to a tower block, or a [foam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_fighting_foam)-carrying vehicle to structures known to contain certain [hazardous chemicals](1476975.md).

## Building Construction Types (United States)

In the United States, according to [NFPA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Fire_Protection_Association), structures are divided into five construction types based on the severity of the fire hazard:

| Type **I**: [Fire Resistive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passive_fire_protection) | Typically used in [high-rises](118767.md). The material comprising the structure is either inherently able to withstand significant exposure to fire (concrete), or in which a fire resistive covering is applied to steel structural members. |
| --- | --- |
| Type **II**: Non-combustible | Typically used in strip shopping center malls. Roofs are constructed out of steel rafters. |
| Type **III**: Ordinary construction | [Brick and mortar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brickwork) walls, wood frame floors. City [rowhouses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rowhouse) are where this type of construction is most often found. |
| Type **IV**: Heavy [timber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timber) | Often used in [churches](6325.md) or other community-based buildings. |
| Type **V**: [Wood frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timber_framing) | Typically used in recent construction of single-family dwellings, townhouses, garden apartments with four floors or less. |

### Canada

Information from Canada's National Fire Information Database shows these ignition sources for residential fires in 2014.

| Ignition source | Examples | Percentage |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cooking equipment | stoves, hot plates, BBQs, deep fryers | 33% |
| Smoker's material and open flame | cigarettes, lighters, matches, candles, lanterns, blow torches | 24% |
| Heating equipment | central heating, space heaters, wood stoves, fireplaces | 14% |
| Electrical distribution equipment | permanent electric wiring, extension cords, batteries | 11% |
| Appliances and household equipment | televisions, household appliances | 7% |
| Exposure | fire spread from other buildings or outdoors | 7% |
| Other electrical equipment | power tools, lamps, computers | 4% |
| No igniting object | lightning | 1% |

A 2023 update from Statistics Canada confirmed that cooking equipment and smoker's material continued to be the top causes of residential fires, at 32% and 25% of total incidents respectively.[^cite_note-2]

### United States

Data from the [U.S. Fire Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Fire_Administration)'s [National Fire Incident Reporting System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Fire_Incident_Reporting_System) shows that cooking has consistently been the leading cause of residential building fires.[^cite_note-3]

| Cause | Examples | Percentage |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cooking | stoves, ovens, cooking fires | 50.9% |
| Heating | furnaces, boilers, water heaters, portable heaters, chimneys | 9.3% |
| Other Unintentional, Careless | product misuse, discarded materials, heat source too close to combustibles | 7.4% |
| Electrical Malfunction | electrical wiring, lighting fixtures | 6.7% |
| Intentional | [deliberately set fires](184695.md) | 4.3% |
| Open Flame | candles, matches, lighters, embers | 4.3% |
| Other Heat | fireworks, heat/sparks from friction | 3.4% |
| Appliances | most electronic and electrical appliances | 3.0% |
| Equipment Misoperation, Failure | equipment malfunction or operation deficiency | 2.3% |
| Smoking | cigarettes and other smoking materials | 2.0% |
| Exposure | heat spread from another hostile fire | 1.8% |
| Natural | spontaneous ignition, chemicals, storms | 1.7% |
| Other Equipment | computer, telephone, special or unspecified equipment | 1.3% |
| Cause under investigation | cause still undetermined | 1.3% |
| Playing with Heat Source | children or others playing with fire | 0.4% |
