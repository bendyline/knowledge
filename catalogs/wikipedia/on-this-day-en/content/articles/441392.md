A **trade war** is an [economic conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_conflict) often resulting from extreme [protectionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protectionism), in which [states](23604120.md) raise or implement [tariffs](55551.md) or other [trade barriers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_barrier) against each other as part of their [commercial policies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_policy), in response to similar measures imposed by the opposing party.[^cite_note-1] If tariffs are the exclusive mechanism, then such conflicts are known as **customs wars**, *toll wars*, or *tariff wars*; as a reprisal, the latter state may also increase the tariffs. Trade war arises only if the competitive protection between states is of the same type and it is not valid in case of dumping exports.[^cite_note-2] Increased protection causes both nations' output compositions to move towards their [autarky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autarky) position.[^cite_note-3] Minor trade disagreements are often called **trade disputes** when the war [metaphor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphor) is [hyperbolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbole).

Trade wars could be escalated to full conflict between states as evidenced in the [Massacre of the Bandanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governorate_of_the_Banda_Islands#Massacre_of_the_Bandanese) after alleged violations of a new treaty. The [First Anglo-Dutch War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Anglo-Dutch_War) was triggered by disputes over trade; the war began with English attacks on Dutch merchant shipping but expanded to vast fleet actions. The [Second Anglo-Dutch War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Anglo-Dutch_War) was for control over the seas and trade routes, where England tried to end the Dutch domination of world trade during a period of intense European commercial rivalry. The [Fourth Anglo-Dutch War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Anglo-Dutch_War) started over British and Dutch disagreements on the legality and conduct of Dutch trade with Britain's enemies in that war. The [Shimonoseki Campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shimonoseki_Campaign) after unrest over the shogunate's open-door policy to foreign trade. The [First Opium War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Opium_War) which started after the [Qing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qing_dynasty) government blockaded its ports, confiscated opium contraband and confined [British](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire) traders, resulted in the dispatch of the [British Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Navy) to China and engage the Chinese Navy in the [Battle of Kowloon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Kowloon). The First Opium War eventually led to the British colony of [Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Hong_Kong), and the [Second Opium War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Opium_War), which arose from another trade war with the same underlying causes, expanded the [British possessions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_possessions) on the island.

## Examples from the 1920s

### German–Polish customs war

One example of a modern tariff war occurred in the 1920s and 1930s between the [Weimar Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weimar_Republic) (Germany) and [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Polish_Republic), in the [German–Polish customs war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German%E2%80%93Polish_customs_war) of 1925 to 1934. In the Weimar Republic, [Gustav Stresemann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Stresemann) ([Minister of Foreign Affairs](1329748.md) from 1923 to 1929) wanted to force Poland to give up territory by creating an economic crisis; to achieve this, the Germans increased tolls on coal and steel products developed there.

As a reprisal, the Poles increased toll rates for many German products. This led to the rapid development of the port of [Gdynia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gdynia), which was the only way Poland could export its goods to Western Europe without having to transport them through Germany.

### The U.S. Fordney–McCumber Tariff

U.S. President [Warren G. Harding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warren_G._Harding) signed the [Fordney–McCumber Tariff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fordney%E2%80%93McCumber_Tariff) bill (named after [Joseph Fordney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Fordney), chair of the [House Ways and Means Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_Ways_and_Means_Committee), and [Porter McCumber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porter_McCumber), chair of the [Senate Finance Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senate_Finance_Committee)) in September 1922.[^cite_note-4] In the end, the tariff law raised the average American *[ad valorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ad_valorem)* tariff rate to 38 percent.

Trading partners complained immediately. Those injured by [World War I](4764461.md) said that, without access for their exports to the American market, they would not be able to make payments to America on war-time [loans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loan). But others saw that this tariff increase would have broader deleterious effects. Democratic Representative [Cordell Hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cordell_Hull) said, "Our foreign markets depend both on the efficiency of our production and the tariffs of countries in which we would sell. Our own \[high] tariffs are an important factor in each. They injure the former and invite the latter."

Five years after the passage of the tariff, the U.S.'s trading partners had raised their own tariffs by a significant degree. [France](5843419.md) raised its tariffs on [automobiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automobile) from 45% to 100%, [Spain](26667.md) raised tariffs on American goods by 40%, and Germany and [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Italy) raised tariffs on wheat.[^cite_note-5] This customs war is sometimes cited as one of the causes of the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression).[^cite_note-6]
