# Skiff

This article is about a type of boat. For other uses, see [Skiff (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skiff_\(disambiguation\)).

A **skiff** is any of a variety of essentially unrelated styles of small boats, usually propelled by sails or oars. Traditionally, these are coastal craft or [river craft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_craft) used for work, leisure, as a utility craft, and for fishing, and have a one-person or small crew. Sailing skiffs have developed into high performance competitive classes. Many of today's skiff classes are based in Australia and New Zealand in the form of [12 ft (3.66 m)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/12ft_Skiff), 13 ft (3.96 m), [16 ft (4.88 m)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/16ft_Skiff) and [18 ft (5.49 m) skiffs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/18ft_Skiff). The [29er](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/29er_\(dinghy\)), [49er](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/49er_\(dinghy\)), SKUD and [Musto Skiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musto_Skiff) are all considered to have developed from the skiff concept, all of which are sailed internationally.

The term skiff is also used for a racing shell called [single scull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_scull) for competitive rowing.

## Etymology

The word is related to *ship* and has a complicated etymology: "skiff" comes from the [Middle English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_English) *skif*, which derives from the [Old French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_French) *esquif*, which in turn derives from the [Old Italian](14708.md) *schifo*, which is itself of [Germanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language) origin (German *Schiff*). "Ship" comes from the [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English) "scip", which has the same Germanic predecessor.[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3]

## By location

### United Kingdom



*Boulter's Lock, Sunday Afternoon* by [Edward John Gregory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_John_Gregory) shows skiffs among other craft coming out of the lock

The term has been used for a number of styles of craft round the United Kingdom, often small river and sea going craft. They varied from double ended rowing boats to small sailing boats. The poet [John Milton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Milton) refers to a "night foundered skiff" in *[Paradise Lost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise_Lost)* as early as 1670. There are references to skiffs involved in accidents on the River Thames as early as 1812,[^cite_note-4] and 1824 at Oxford.[^cite_note-5] In August 1815, the poet [Percy Bysshe Shelley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percy_Bysshe_Shelley) was taken on an expedition by skiff from [Old Windsor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Windsor) to [Lechlade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lechlade) by Charles Clairmont and [Thomas Love Peacock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Love_Peacock).[^cite_note-6] He subsequently settled at [Marlow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marlow,_Buckinghamshire), where he regularly rowed his skiff through the locks.[^cite_note-7] Shelley later drowned sailing in a skiff off the coast of Italy. A skiff was also mentioned in Sir [Walter Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Scott)'s poem *[The Lady of the Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lady_of_the_Lake_\(poem\))*.

The [Thames skiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thames_skiff) became formalised as a specific design in the early part of the 19th century. It is a round-bottomed [clinker-built](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinker-built) rowing boat that is still very common on the [River Thames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Thames) and other rivers in England. Rowing skiffs became very popular in Victorian Britain, and a skiff journey up the River Thames is described in *[Three Men in a Boat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Men_in_a_Boat)* by [Jerome K. Jerome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerome_K._Jerome).[^cite_note-8] These skiffs could carry a sail and could be used for camping. Although general usage has declined, skiffs are still used for leisure and racing. During the year, [skiffing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skiffing) regattas are held in various riverside towns in England, the major event being the [Skiff Championships Regatta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skiff_Championships_Regatta) at [Henley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henley-on-Thames).

Akin to the skiff is the [yoal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoal) or [yole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yole), which is a clinker-built boat used for fishing in the Orkney and Shetland Islands. The boat itself is a version of the Norwegian [Oselvar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oselvar) which is similar to a skiff in appearance, while the word is cognate with "[yawl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yawl)". The French *[yole](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yole)* is a leisure craft similar to the Thames Skiff and is translated as "skiff", while the French *[skiff](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skiff%20\(aviron\))* translates to a [single scull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_scull). In Dutch and German, "Skiff" also means a single scull, while Czech *[skif](https://cs.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skif)* refers to sculling boats in general.

Regattas are also held across Northern Ireland, with one of the largest being held in [Portadown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portadown), but smaller events take place throughout the year across County Down.[^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11]

### Americas



Classic flat-bottom skiff in [Maine](19977.md)

In American usage, the term is used for small sea-going fishing boats. It is referred to historically in literature in *[Moby-Dick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moby-Dick)* by [Herman Melville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Melville)[^cite_note-12] and *[The Old Man and the Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Old_Man_and_the_Sea)* by [Ernest Hemingway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Hemingway).[^cite_note-oldman-13] Boats powered by [sails](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sail) or by [oars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oar) can be referred to as skiffs.

One usage of the word refers to a typically small flat-bottomed open boat with a pointed [bow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bow_\(watercraft\)) and a flat [stern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stern) originally developed as an inexpensive and easy-to-build boat for use by inshore [fishermen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fishermen). Originally designed to be powered by rowing, their form has evolved to be powered by [outboard motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outboard_motor). The design is still in common use today for both work and pleasure craft. They can be made of wood or other materials. A similar style of craft in Central America and Mexico is generally called a [panga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panga_\(skiff\)).

## By use

### Piracy and smuggling



Captured Somalian pirates with their skiff

The term skiff has been applied to motorized boats of small size and construction used as sea-going vessels for [piracy](50715.md) or [drug smuggling](218449.md).[^cite_note-14]

### Racing



Modern [12ft Skiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/12ft_Skiff) at speed

The skiff with a sail has developed into specific sailing boats bearing the name "skiff". In Sydney, the term was used for a number of racing classes (sizes from 6 ft to 23 ft have existed). These were originally heavily crewed and canvassed boats that were relatively short for the canvas and crew carried and were developed from working boats of the time. This style of boat is still active in the form of Historical [10 foot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_10_foot_skiffs) and [18 foot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/18ft_Skiff) classes.

The skiff classes developed to become much lighter and faster with relatively smaller (but still very large by any other standards) rigs and smaller crews. [12ft Skiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/12ft_Skiff), 13 ft Skiff, [16ft Skiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/16ft_Skiff), and [18ft Skiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/18ft_Skiff) classes are raced in that form. With two crew on the 12 and 13 footer and three on the 16 and 18 these are still heavily crewed boats for their size. Modern developments began with the introduction of carbon fibre reinforced composite hulls, allowing for a significant reduction in weight, and an increase in rigidity. Following this, the use of carbon in masts and rigging allowed for more sail area, and better gust response. Moulded sails are being tested in both 12 ft and 16 ft skiffs, with most modern Australian 18 ft Skiffs utilising the new technology.

Because the modern 18s have such a high profile, the term skiff is widely used internationally to refer to other high-performance [sailing dinghy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sailing_dinghy) classes, mostly featuring [asymmetrical spinnaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinnaker) and [trapeze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trapeze_\(sailing\)) which have been strongly influenced by modern skiffs. Examples include: [Cherub Skiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherub_Sailing), [International 14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_14), [29er](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/29er_\(boat\)), and [49er](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/49er_\(dinghy\)). These boats tend to be less heavily crewed in relation to their length than the traditional Australian Skiff Classes. The term is even used for some single-handed boats like the [Musto Skiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musto_Skiff) which are far removed from the heavily crewed original boats.

The [SKUD 18](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SKUD_18) is a two-person [keelboat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keelboat) which claims strong influence from skiff development.[^cite_note-15] This made its debut in the [2008 Paralympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Paralympic_Games).

In the International [Moth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moth_\(dinghy\)) class the term skiff is used to distinguish designs that have an essentially vertical bow from [scow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scow) designs, which have a broadly horizontal bow.
