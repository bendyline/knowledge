The **Benedictines**, officially the **Order of Saint Benedict** ([Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language): *Ordo Sancti Benedicti*, abbreviated as **O.S.B.** or **OSB**), are a mainly [contemplative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_mysticism) [monastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_monasticism) [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_order_\(Catholic\)) of the [Catholic Church](606848.md) for men and for women who follow the [Rule of Saint Benedict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_Saint_Benedict). Initiated in 529, they are the oldest of all the religious orders in the [Latin Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Church).[^cite_note-1] The male religious are also sometimes called the **Black Monks**, especially in English speaking countries, after the colour of their [habits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_habit), although some, like the [Olivetans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olivetans), wear white.[^cite_note-2] They were founded by [Benedict of Nursia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedict_of_Nursia), a 6th-century Italian monk who laid the foundations of Benedictine monasticism through the formulation of his Rule. Benedict's sister [Scholastica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholastica), possibly his twin, also became religious from an early age, but chose to live as a [hermit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermit). They retained a close relationship until her death.[^cite_note-3]

Despite being called an order, the Benedictines do not operate under a single hierarchy. They are instead organized as a collection of autonomous monasteries and convents, some known as [abbeys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbey). The order is represented internationally by the [Benedictine Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedictine_Confederation), an organization set up in 1893 to represent the order's shared interests. They do not have a [superior general](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superior_general) or [motherhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motherhouse) with universal jurisdiction but elect an [Abbot Primate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbot_Primate) to represent themselves to the [Vatican](13393.md) and to the world.

In some regions, Benedictine [nuns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuns) are given the title [Dame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dame) in preference to [Sister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sister_\(religious\)).[^cite_note-4]

## Historical development

Main article: [Benedict of Nursia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedict_of_Nursia)



Saint Benedict of Nursia (c. 480–543); detail from a [fresco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresco) by [Fra Angelico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fra_Angelico) (c. 1400–1455) in the [Friary of San Marco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Marco,_Florence) [Florence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence)

The monastery at [Subiaco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subiaco,_Lazio) in Italy, established by [Benedict of Nursia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedict_of_Nursia) c. 529, was the first of the dozen monasteries he founded. He later founded the [Abbey of Monte Cassino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbey_of_Monte_Cassino). There is no evidence, however, that he intended to found an order and the [Rule of Saint Benedict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_Saint_Benedict) presupposes the autonomy of each community. When Monte Cassino was sacked by the Lombards about the year 580, the monks fled to Rome, and it seems probable that this constituted an important factor in the diffusion of a knowledge of Benedictine monasticism.[^cite_note-Catholic-5]

Copies of Benedict's Rule survived; around 594 [Pope Gregory I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Gregory_I) spoke favorably of it. The rule is subsequently found in some monasteries in southern Gaul along with other rules used by abbots.[^cite_note-6] [Gregory of Tours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregory_of_Tours) says that at [Ainay Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_of_Saint-Martin_d'Ainay#Ainay_Abbey), in the sixth century, the monks "followed the rules of Basil, Cassian, Caesarius, and other fathers, taking and using whatever seemed proper to the conditions of time and place", and doubtless the same liberty was taken with the Benedictine Rule when it reached them. In Gaul and Switzerland, it gradually supplemented the much stricter Irish or Celtic Rule introduced by [Columbanus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbanus) and others. In many monasteries it eventually entirely displaced the earlier codes.[^cite_note-Catholic-5]



Abbey of [Monte Cassino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Cassino)

By the ninth century, however, the Benedictine had become the standard form of monastic life throughout the whole of Western Europe, excepting Scotland, Wales, and Ireland, where the Celtic observance still prevailed for another century or two.[^cite_note-Catholic-5] Largely through the work of [Benedict of Aniane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedict_of_Aniane), it became the rule of choice for monasteries throughout the Carolingian empire.[^cite_note-Theisen-7]

Monastic scriptoria flourished from the ninth through the twelfth centuries. Sacred Scripture was always at the heart of every monastic scriptorium. As a general rule those of the monks who possessed skill as writers made this their chief, if not their sole, active work. An anonymous writer of the ninth or tenth century speaks of six hours a day as the usual task of a scribe, which would absorb almost all the time available for active work in the day of a medieval monk.[^cite_note-CE1913_Scriptorium-8]

In the Middle Ages, monasteries were often founded by the nobility. [Cluny Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cluny_Abbey) was founded by [William I, Duke of Aquitaine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_I,_Duke_of_Aquitaine), in 910. The abbey was noted for its strict adherence to the Rule of Saint Benedict. The abbot of Cluny was the superior of all the daughter houses, through appointed priors.[^cite_note-Theisen-7]

One of the earliest reforms of Benedictine practice was that initiated in 980 by [Romuald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romuald), who founded the [Camaldolese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camaldolese) community.[^cite_note-9] The [Cistercians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cistercians) branched off from the Benedictines in 1098; they are often called the "White monks".[^cite_note-10]

The dominance of the Benedictine monastic way of life began to decline towards the end of the twelfth century, which saw the rise of the [mendicant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mendicant) [Franciscans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franciscans) and nomadic [Dominicans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominican_Order).[^cite_note-Theisen-7] Benedictines by contrast, took a vow of "stability", which professed loyalty to a particular foundation in a particular location. Not being bound by location, the mendicants were better able to respond to an increasingly "urban" environment. This decline was further exacerbated by the practice of appointing a commendatory abbot, a lay person, appointed by a noble to oversee and to protect the assets of the monastery. Often, however, this resulted in the appropriation of the assets of monasteries at the expense of the community which they were intended to support.[^cite_note-11]

### Austria and Germany



[Melk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melk) Abbey

[Saint Blaise Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Blaise_Abbey,_Black_Forest) in the [Black Forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Forest) of [Baden-Württemberg](66401.md) is believed to have been founded around the latter part of the tenth century. Between 1070 and 1073 there seem to have been contacts between St. Blaise and the Cluniac [Abbey of Fruttuaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbey_of_Fruttuaria) in Italy, which led to St. Blaise following the Fruttuarian reforms. The [Empress Agnes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empress_Agnes) was a patron of Fruttuaria, and retired there in 1065 before moving to Rome. The Empress was instrumental in introducing Fruttuaria's Benedictine customs, as practiced at Cluny, to [Saint Blaise Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Blaise_Abbey,_Black_Forest) in [Baden-Württemberg](66401.md).[^cite_note-12] Other houses either reformed by, or founded as priories of, St. Blasien were [Muri Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muri_Abbey) (1082), [Ochsenhausen Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ochsenhausen_Abbey) (1093), [Göttweig Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B6ttweig_Abbey) (1094), [Stein am Rhein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stein_am_Rhein) Abbey (before 1123) and [Prüm Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pr%C3%BCm_Abbey) (1132). It also had significant influence on the abbeys of [Alpirsbach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpirsbach_Abbey) (1099), [Ettenheimm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ettenheim)ünster (1124) and [Sulzburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulzburg) (c. 1125), and the priories of Weitenau (now part of [Steinen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steinen,_Baden-W%C3%BCrttemberg), c. 1100), [Bürgel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%BCrgel_Abbey) (before 1130) and [Sitzenkirch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kandern#Sitzenkirch) (c. 1130).

### France



[Abbatiale Saint-Benoit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fleury_Abbey), southern aspect as in 1893



Basilica of Saint-Martin d'Ainay

[Fleury Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fleury_Abbey) in [Saint-Benoît-sur-Loire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Beno%C3%AEt-sur-Loire), [Loiret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loiret) was founded in about 640.[^cite_note-13] It is one of the most celebrated Benedictine monasteries of Western Europe, and possesses the relics of St. Benedict. Like many Benedictine abbeys it was located on the banks of a river, here the [Loire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loire).[^cite_note-14] Ainey Abbey is a ninth century foundation on the [Lyon](8638634.md) peninsula. In the twelfth century on the current site there was a [romanesque monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanesque_architecture), subsequently rebuilt.

The seventeenth century saw a number of Benedictine foundations for women, some dedicated to the indigent to save them from a life of exploitation, others dedicated to the [Perpetual Adoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpetual_Adoration) of the [Blessed Sacrament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blessed_Sacrament) such as the one established by [Catherine de Bar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechtilde_of_the_Blessed_Sacrament) (1614–1698).[^cite_note-silver-15] In 1688 Dame Mechtilde de Bar assisted [Marie Casimire Louise de La Grange d'Arquien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Casimire_Louise_de_La_Grange_d'Arquien), queen consort of Poland, to establish a Benedictine foundation in [Warsaw](32908.md).[^cite_note-16]

Abbeys were among the institutions of the Catholic Church swept away during the [French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution). Monasteries and convents were again allowed to form in the 19th century under the [Bourbon Restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourbon_Restoration_in_France). Later that century, under the [Third French Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_French_Republic), laws were enacted preventing religious teaching. The original intent was to allow secular schools. Thus in 1880 and 1882, Benedictine teaching monks were effectively exiled; this was not completed until 1901.[^cite_note-17][^cite_note-Chadwick1998-18][^cite_note-19][^cite_note-20]

In 1898 Marie-Adèle Garnier, in religion, Mother Marie de Saint-Pierre, founded a Benedictine house in [Montmartre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montmartre) (*Mount of the Martyr*), in Paris.[^cite_note-21] However, the [Waldeck-Rousseau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ren%C3%A9_Waldeck-Rousseau)'s *Law of Associations*, passed in 1901, placed severe restrictions on religious bodies which were obliged to leave France. Garnier and her community relocated to another place associated with executions, this time it was in [London](17867.md), near the site of [Tyburn tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyburn#Tyburn_gallows) where 105 Catholic martyrs—including [Saint Oliver Plunkett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Plunkett) and [Saint Edmund Campion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Campion) had been executed during the [English Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Reformation). A stone's throw from [Marble Arch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marble_Arch) (350 yards (320 m)),[^cite_note-22] the [Tyburn Convent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adorers_of_the_Sacred_Heart_of_Montmartre) is now the Mother House of the Congregation.[^cite_note-23]

### Poland and Lithuania



Benedictine church in [Warsaw](32908.md)'s New Town, depicted by [Bellotto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernardo_Bellotto)

Benedictines are thought to have arrived in the [Kingdom of Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Poland_\(1025-1031\)) in the 11th-century. One of the earliest foundations is [Tyniec Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyniec) on a promontory by the [Vistula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vistula) river. The Tyniec monks led the translation of the Bible into Polish vernacular. Other surviving Benedictine houses can be found in [Stary Kraków Village](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stary_Krak%C3%B3w), [Biskupów](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biskup%C3%B3w), [Lubiń](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lubi%C5%84,_Ko%C5%9Bcian_County). Older foundations are in [Mogilno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedictine_Monastery,_Mogilno), [Trzemeszno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trzemeszno), [Łęczyca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%81%C4%99czyca), [Łysa Góra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%81ysa_G%C3%B3ra) and in [Opactwo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opactwo), among others. In the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) the city of [Płock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C5%82ock), also on the Vistula, had a successful monastery, which played a significant role in the local economy. In the 18th-century benedictine convents were opened for women, notably in Warsaw's New Town.

A 15th-century Benedictine foundation can be found in [Senieji Trakai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senieji_Trakai), a village in Eastern [Lithuania](17675.md).

### Switzerland

[Kloster Rheinau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rheinau_Abbey) was a Benedictine monastery in Rheinau in the Canton of Zürich, Switzerland, founded in about 778.[^cite_note-24] [Einsiedeln Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einsiedeln_Abbey) is a Benedictine monastery in Einsiedeln. ### United Kingdom

The [English Benedictine Congregation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Benedictine_Congregation) is the oldest of the nineteen Benedictine congregations. Through the influence of [Wilfrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilfrid), [Benedict Biscop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedict_Biscop), and [Dunstan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunstan),[^cite_note-26] the Benedictine Rule spread rapidly, and in the North it was adopted in most of the monasteries that had been founded by the Celtic missionaries from Iona. Many of the episcopal sees of England were founded and governed by the Benedictines, and no fewer than nine of the old cathedrals were served by the black monks of the priories attached to them.[^cite_note-Catholic-5] Monasteries served as hospitals and places of refuge for the weak and homeless. The monks studied the healing properties of plants and minerals to alleviate the sufferings of the sick.[^cite_note-Hicks-27]

During the [English Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Reformation), all [monasteries were dissolved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_monasteries) and their lands confiscated by the Crown, forcing those who wished to continue in the monastic life to flee into exile on the Continent. From the middle of the 19th century, Benedictine monasteries and nunneries began to flourish again.[^cite_note-28]



The two sides of a Saint Benedict medal

[St. Mildred's Priory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minster_in_Thanet_Priory), on the [Isle of Thanet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isle_of_Thanet), [Kent](16766.md), was built in 1027 on the site of an abbey founded in 670 by the daughter of the first Christian [King of Kent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_of_Kent). Currently the priory is home to a community of Benedictine nuns. Five of the most notable English abbeys are the Basilica of St Gregory the Great at Downside, commonly known as [Downside Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downside_Abbey), The Abbey of St Edmund, King and Martyr commonly known as [Douai Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douai_Abbey) in Upper Woolhampton, Reading, Berkshire, [Ealing Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ealing_Abbey) in Ealing, West London, and [Worth Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worth_Abbey).[^cite_note-Battell-29][^cite_note-Martin-30] [Prinknash Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prinknash_Abbey), used by Henry VIII as a hunting lodge, was officially returned to the Benedictines four hundred years later, in 1928. During the next few years, so-called Prinknash Park was used as a home until it was returned to the order.[^cite_note-31]

[St. Lawrence's Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampleforth_Abbey) in Ampleforth, Yorkshire was founded in 1802. In 1955, Ampleforth set up a daughter house, a priory at St. Louis, Missouri which became independent in 1973 and became [Saint Louis Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Louis_Abbey) in its own right in 1989.[^cite_note-32]



Interior of [Stanbrook Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanbrook_Abbey) Church, Wass, Yorkshire

As of 2015, the English Congregation consists of three abbeys of nuns and ten abbeys of monks. Members of the congregation are found in England, Wales, the United States of America, Peru and Zimbabwe.[^cite_note-English-33]

In England there are also houses of the [Subiaco Cassinese Congregation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subiaco_Cassinese_Congregation#English_Province): Farnborough, Prinknash, and Chilworth: the [Solesmes Congregation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solesmes_Congregation), Quarr and St Cecilia's on the Isle of Wight, as well as a diocesan monastery following the Rule of Saint Benedict: The Community of Our Lady of Glastonbury.[^cite_note-34]

Since the [Oxford Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_Movement), there has also been a modest flourishing of Benedictine monasticism in the [Anglican Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Communion) and Protestant Churches. Anglican Benedictine Abbots are invited guests of the Benedictine Abbot Primate in Rome at Abbatial gatherings at Sant'Anselmo.[^cite_note-35]

In 1168 local Benedictine monks instigated the anti-semitic [blood libel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_libel) of [Harold of Gloucester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_of_Gloucester) as a template for explaining child deaths. According to historian Joe Hillaby, the blood libel of Harold was crucially important because for the first time an unexplained child death occurring near the Easter festival was arbitrarily linked to Jews in the vicinity by local Christian churchmen: "they established a pattern quickly taken up elsewhere. Within three years the first ritual murder charge was made in France."[^cite_note-hil-36]

#### Monastic libraries in England

The forty-eighth Rule of Saint Benedict prescribes extensive and habitual "holy reading" for the brethren.[^cite_note-37] Three primary types of reading were done by the monks in medieval times. Monks would read privately during their personal time, as well as publicly during services and at mealtimes. In addition to these three mentioned in the Rule, monks would also read in the infirmary. Monasteries were thriving centers of education, with monks and nuns actively encouraged to learn and pray according to the [Benedictine Rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedictine_Rule). Rule 38 states that 'these brothers' meals should usually be accompanied by reading, and that they were to eat and drink in silence while one read out loud.

Benedictine monks were not allowed worldly possessions, thus necessitating the preservation and collection of sacred texts in monastic libraries for communal use.[^cite_note-38] For the sake of convenience, the books in the monastery were housed in a few different places, namely the [sacristy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacristy), which contained books for the choir and other liturgical books, the [rectory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clergy_house), which housed books for public reading such as sermons and lives of the saints, and the [library](17727.md), which contained the largest collection of books and was typically in the cloister.

The first record of a monastic library in England is in [Canterbury](48274.md). To assist with [Augustine of Canterbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Canterbury)'s [English mission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorian_mission), Pope [Gregory the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Gregory_I) gave him nine books which included the Gregorian Bible in two volumes, the Psalter of Augustine, two copies of the [Gospels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospels), two [martyrologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martyrologies), an Exposition of the Gospels and Epistles, and a [Psalter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psalter).[^cite_note-Savage1912-39]<sup>: 23–25 </sup> [Theodore of Tarsus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_of_Tarsus) brought Greek books to Canterbury more than seventy years later, when he founded a school for the study of Greek.[^cite_note-Savage1912-39]<sup>: 26 </sup>

### United States

The first Benedictine to live in the United States was Pierre-Joseph Didier. He came to the United States in 1790 from Paris and served in the Ohio and St. Louis areas until his death. The first actual Benedictine monastery founded was [Saint Vincent Archabbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Vincent_Archabbey), located in [Latrobe, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latrobe,_Pennsylvania). It was founded in 1832 by [Boniface Wimmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boniface_Wimmer), a German monk, who sought to serve German immigrants in America. In 1856, Wimmer started to lay the foundations for [St. John's Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_John's_Abbey,_Collegeville) in Minnesota. In 1876, Herman Wolfe, of Saint Vincent Archabbey established [Belmont Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belmont_Abbey,_North_Carolina) in North Carolina.[^cite_note-40] By the time of his death in 1887, Wimmer had sent Benedictine monks to Kansas, New Jersey, North Carolina, Georgia, Florida, Alabama, Illinois, and Colorado.[^cite_note-RB_1980-41]

Wimmer also asked for Benedictine [sisters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nun#Distinction_between_a_nun_and_a_religious_sister) to be sent to America by St. Walburg Convent in [Eichstätt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eichst%C3%A4tt), Bavaria. In 1852, [Sister Benedicta Riepp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sister_Benedicta_Riepp?action=edit\&redlink=1) and two other sisters founded [St. Marys, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Marys,_Pennsylvania). Soon they would send sisters to Michigan, New Jersey, and Minnesota.[^cite_note-RB_1980-41]

By 1854, Swiss monks began to arrive and founded [St. Meinrad Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Meinrad_Archabbey) in Indiana, and they soon spread to Arkansas and Louisiana. They were soon followed by Swiss sisters.[^cite_note-RB_1980-41]

There are now over 100 Benedictine houses across America. Most Benedictine houses are part of one of four large Congregations: American-Cassinese, Swiss-American, St. Scholastica, and St. Benedict. The congregations mostly are made up of monasteries that share the same lineage. For instance the American-Cassinese congregation included the 22 monasteries descended from Boniface Wimmer.[^cite_note-42]

## Benedictine vows and life

Main article: [Rule of Saint Benedict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_Saint_Benedict)

A sense of community has been the defining characteristic of the order since the beginning.[^cite_note-43] To that end, section 17 in chapter 58 of the [Rule of Saint Benedict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_Saint_Benedict) specifies the solemn vows candidates joining a Benedictine community are required to make: a vow of stability (to remain in the same community), to adopt a "conversion of habits" (*conversatio morum* in Latin) and to obey to the community's superior.[^cite_note-44] The "Benedictine vows" are equivalent to the [evangelical counsels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelical_counsels) accepted by all candidates entering a [religious order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_order_\(Catholic\)). The interpretation of *conversatio morum* understood as "conversion of the habits of life" has generally been replaced by notions such as adoption of a monastic manner of life, drawing on the [Vulgate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulgate)'s use of *conversatio* as indicating "citizenship" or "local customs", see [Philippians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippians) 3\:20. The Rule enjoins monks and nuns "to live in this place as a religious, in obedience to its rule and to the abbot or abbess".

Benedictine abbots and abbesses have jurisdiction over their [abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbey) and thus canonical authority over the monks or nuns who are resident. This authority includes the power to assign duties, to decide which books may or may not be read, to regulate comings and goings, and to punish and to [excommunicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excommunicate), in the sense of an enforced isolation from the monastic community.

A tight communal timetable – the [horarium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horarium) – is meant to ensure that the time given by God is not wasted but used in God's service, whether for prayer, work, meals, spiritual reading or sleep. The order's motto is *[Ora et Labora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ora_et_Labora)*: "pray and work".

Although Benedictines do not take a vow of silence, hours of strict silence are set, and at other times silence is maintained as much as is practically possible. Social conversations tend to be limited to communal recreation times. Such details, like other aspects of the daily routine of a Benedictine house are left to the discretion of the superior, and are set out in its *customary*, the code adopted by a particular Benedictine house by adapting the Rule to local conditions.[^cite_note-45]

According to the norms of the [1983 Code of Canon Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1983_Code_of_Canon_Law), a Benedictine abbey is a "[religious institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_institute)" and its members therefore participate in [consecrated life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consecrated_life) which Canon 588 §1 explains is intrinsically "neither clerical nor lay." Males in consecrated life, however, may be ordained.

Benedictines' rules contain a reference to [ritual purification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ritual_purification), which is inspired by Benedict's encouragement of [bathing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bathing).[^cite_note-46] Benedictine monks have played a role in the development and promotion of [spas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spa).[^cite_note-ASpiritualHistory-47]

## Organization

Benedictine monasticism differs from other Christian religious orders in that as congregations sometimes with several houses, some of them in other countries, they are not bound into a unified religious order headed by a "Superior General". Each Benedictine congregation is autonomous and governed by an abbot or abbess.[^cite_note-48]

The autonomous houses are characterised by their chosen [charism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charism) or specific dedication to a particular devotion. For example, In 1313 [Bernardo Tolomei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernardo_Tolomei) established the [Order of Our Lady of Mount Olivet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olivetans). The community adopted the Rule of Saint Benedict and received canonical approval in 1344. The Olivetans are part of the Benedictine Confederation.[^cite_note-49] Other specialisms, such as [Gregorian chant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorian_chant) as at [Solesmes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solesmes_Abbey) in France, or [Perpetual Adoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpetual_Adoration) of the *Holy Sacrament* have been adopted by different houses, as at the Warsaw Convent, or the [Adorers of the Sacred Heart of Montmartre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adorers_of_the_Sacred_Heart_of_Montmartre) at Tyburn Convent in London. Other houses have dedicated themselves to books, reading, writing and printing them as at [Stanbrook Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanbrook_Abbey) in England. Others still are associated with the places where they were founded or their founders centuries ago, hence [Cassinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassinese), [Subiaco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subiaco,_Lazio), [Camaldolese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camaldolese) or [Sylvestrines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylvestrines).

All Benedictine houses became federated in the [Benedictine Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedictine_Confederation) brought into existence by [Pope Leo XIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Leo_XIII)'s [Apostolic Brief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostolic_Brief) "*Summum semper*" on 12 July 1893. Pope Leo also established the office of [Abbot Primate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbot_Primate) as the abbot elected to represent this Confederation at the [Vatican](13393.md) and to the world. The headquarters of the Benedictine Confederation and the Abbot Primate is the [Primatial Abbey of Sant'Anselmo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sant'Anselmo_all'Aventino) built by Pope Leo XIII in [Rome](25458.md).[^cite_note-50][^cite_note-51]

## Other orders

The Rule of Saint Benedict is also used by a number of religious orders that began as reforms of the Benedictine tradition such as the [Cistercians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cistercian)[^cite_note-52] and [Trappists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trappist).[^cite_note-53] These groups are separate congregations and not members of the [Benedictine Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedictine_Confederation).

Although most Benedictines are Catholic, there are also other communities that follow the Rule of Saint Benedict. For example, of an estimated 2,400 celibate Anglican religious (1,080 men and 1,320 women) in the [Anglican Communion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Communion) as a whole, some have adopted the Rule of Benedict. Likewise, such communities can be found in the [Eastern Orthodox Church](10186.md),[^cite_note-Simpson2016-54][^cite_note-55] and the [Lutheran Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lutheran_Church).[^cite_note-SAH2018-56]
