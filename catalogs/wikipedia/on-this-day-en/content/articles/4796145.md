[Christianity](5211.md) has a long and continuous history in [Lebanon](17771.md). [Biblical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblical) scriptures show that [Peter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Peter) and [Paul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_the_Apostle) evangelized the [Phoenicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenicia), leading to the dawn of the ancient [Patriarchate of Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarch_of_Antioch). As such, Christianity in Lebanon is as old as the Christian faith itself. Christianity spread slowly in Lebanon due to [pagans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paganism) who resisted conversion, but it ultimately spread throughout the country. Even after centuries of [living under Muslim empires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_conquests), Christianity remains the dominant faith of the [Mount Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Lebanon) region and has substantial communities elsewhere.

The [Maronites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maronite) and the [Druze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Druze) founded modern Lebanon in the nineteenth century, through a governing and social system known as the "[Maronite-Druze dualism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_and_Druze)" in the [Mount Lebanon Mutasarrifate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Lebanon_Mutasarrifate).[^cite_note-3] Lebanon has the second highest proportion of Christians of any [Middle Eastern](19323.md) country (after [Cyprus](5593.md)),[^cite_note-4] estimated to be between 37% and 43%; Egypt and Syria are next, at roughly 10%. Lebanese Christians constitute the majority of the [Lebanese diaspora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_diaspora) worldwide.

## History



A map of religious and ethnic communities of Syria and Lebanon (1935)

Before the Christian faith reached the territory of Lebanon, [Jesus](1095706.md) had traveled to its southern parts near [Tyre](30796.md) where the scripture tells that he cured a possessed Canaanite child.[^cite_note-5][^cite_note-SKHR-6][^cite_note-FJM-7] [Christianity](5211.md) in [Lebanon](17771.md) is as old as [gentile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gentile#Christianity) Christian faith itself. Early reports relate the possibility that [Saint Peter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Peter) himself was the one who [evangelized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelism) the [Phoenicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenicians) whom he affiliated to the ancient [Patriarchate of Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarchate_of_Antioch).[^cite_note-ASA-8] Paul also preached in Lebanon, having lingered with the early Christians in [Tyre](30796.md) and [Sidon](28366.md).[^cite_note-JB-9] Even though Christianity was introduced to Lebanon after the first century AD, its spread was very slow, particularly in the mountainous areas where [paganism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paganism) was still unyielding.[^cite_note-MMM-10]

The earliest indisputable tradition of Christianity in Lebanon can be traced back to [Saint Maron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Maron) in the 4th century AD, being of Greek/Eastern/Antiochian Orthodox origin and the founder of national and ecclesiastical Maronitism. Saint Maron adopted an ascetic and reclusive life on the banks of the Orontes river in the vicinity of [Homs](77533.md)–[Syria](7515849.md) and founded a community of monks which began to preach the gospel in the surrounding areas.[^cite_note-ASA-8] By faith, liturgy, rite, religious books and heritage, the [Maronites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maronite_Christians_in_Lebanon) were of [Eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Christianity) origin.[^cite_note-MMM-10] The Saint Maron Monastery was too close to Antioch to grant the monks their freedom and autonomy, which prompted [Saint John Maron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maron), the first Maronite patriarch-elect, to lead his monks into the [Lebanese mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Lebanon) to escape emperor [Justinian II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justinian_II)'s persecution, finally settling in the [Qadisha valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kadisha_Valley).[^cite_note-ASA-8] Nevertheless, the influence of the Maronite establishment spread throughout the Lebanese mountains and became a considerable [feudal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feudalism) force. The existence of the Maronites was largely ignored by the western world until the [Crusades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusades).[^cite_note-ASA-8] In the 16th century, the [Maronite Church](66391078.md) adopted the catechism of the [Catholic Church](606848.md) and reaffirmed its relationship with it.[^cite_note-MMM-10] Moreover, Rome dispatched [Franciscan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franciscans), [Dominican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominican_Order) and later [Jesuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesuit) [missionaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missionary) to Lebanon to [Latinise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liturgical_Latinisation) the Maronites.[^cite_note-ASA-8]

The relationship between the [Druze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Druze) and [Christians](18337522.md) has been characterized by [harmony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmony) and peaceful [coexistence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plurinationalism),[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12][^cite_note-13][^cite_note-14] with amicable relations between the two groups prevailing throughout history, with the exception of some periods, including [1860 Mount Lebanon civil war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1860_Mount_Lebanon_civil_war).[^cite_note-google-15][^cite_note-16]

Due to their turbulent history, the Maronites formed a secluded identity in the mountains and valleys of Lebanon, led by the Maronite patriarch who voiced his opinion on contemporary issues. They identify themselves as a unique community whose religion and culture is distinct from the predominantly [Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim) [Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab) world.[^cite_note-MMM-10] The Maronites played a major part in the definition of and the creation of the state of Lebanon. The modern state of Greater Lebanon was established by [France](5843419.md) in 1920 after the instigation of ambitious Maronite leaders headed by patriarch [Elias Peter Hoayek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elias_Peter_Hoayek), who presided over delegations to France following [World War I](4764461.md) and requested the re-establishment of the entity of the Principality of Lebanon (1515AD–1840AD). With the creation of the state of Lebanon, Arabism was overcome by [Lebanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_nationalism), which emphasizes Lebanon's Mediterranean and Phoenician heritage. In the [National Pact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Pact), an unwritten gentleman's agreement between the [Maronite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maronite_Christianity_in_Lebanon) President [Bshara el-Khoury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bechara_El_Khoury) and [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam_in_Lebanon) prime minister [Riad as-Solh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riad_as-Solh), the seats of presidency were distributed between the main Lebanese religious denominations. According to the pact, the President of the [Lebanese republic](17771.md) shall always be a Maronite. Furthermore, the pact also states that Lebanon is a state with an "Arab face" (not an Arab identity).[^cite_note-KBAD-17]

Lebanon has the highest proportion of Christians of any country in the Middle East, but exact size of this population has been disputed for many years. One estimate of the Christian share of Lebanon's population, as of 2012, was 40.5%.[^cite_note-CIAFFB-Leb-23] And more recently, in 2018 the CIA World Factbook estimated that Christians constituted 33.7% of Lebanon's population.[^cite_note-Lebanon:_people_and_society-24]

Half of the Lebanese Christians, around a million, dwell in districts where they constitute the majority (75-95%): Matn (governorate of Mount Lebanon), Byblos and Kesrwan (governorate of Keserwan-Jbeil), Batroun, Bsharri, Koura and Zgharta (in the North governorate). These districts form a strip with Beirut in the South, Tripoli in the North, the Mediterranean sea in the West, and the Mount Lebanon range in the East. The district of Jezzine forms a pocket with a Christian majority in the South governorate. Christians constitute important minorities in the districts of Baadba, Aley and Chouf (Mount Lebanon governorate), Zahle (Beqaa), and Beirut.&#x20;

The [Maronite Church](39667585.md), an [Eastern Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Catholic) church in [full communion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_communion) with the [Catholic Church](606848.md), is the largest and politically most active and influential denomination of Lebanon's Christians. The Catholic Church also includes other Eastern Catholic churches, such as the [Melkite Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_Melkite_Christians). The [Greek Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_Greek_Orthodox_Christians) forms the second-largest proportion of Lebanese Christians. The [Armenian Apostolic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Apostolic_Church) also forms a large portion of the Christian population in Lebanon.

The other [six smaller Christian sects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minorities_\(Lebanon\)) are considered ethnic [Assyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_people) ([Syriac Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Orthodox_Church), [Syriac Catholics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Catholic_Church), [Assyrian Church of the East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_Church_of_the_East) and [Chaldean Catholics](698821.md)).

In the [Lebanese Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_Parliament), Christians hold 64 seats in tandem with 64 seats for [Lebanese Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_Lebanon). The [Maronites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maronite_Christians_in_Lebanon) are allotted 34 seats, the [Eastern Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Christianity_in_Lebanon) 14, [Melkites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melkite_Christianity_in_Lebanon) eight, the [Armenians Apostolics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Apostolic_Church) five, [Catholic Armenians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Catholic_Church) one, [Protestants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism_in_Lebanon) one, and other [Christian minority groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minorities_\(Lebanon\)), one.

These churches serve a wide range of Christian communities and stand as enduring symbols of Lebanon's rich religious heritage. The directory documents thousands of places of worship and provides historical information on many of the nation's most significant churches, monasteries, shrines, and Christian landmarks. ## Role in politics

See also: [Maronite politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maronite_politics)

Unlike Shiites, who mostly support the [Amal Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amal_Movement) and [Hezbollah](13919.md) alliance, Christian politics is characterized by deep political fragmentation caused by the [Lebanese civil war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_Civil_War), with some aligned with the [Kataeb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kataeb) party, [Michel Aoun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Aoun)'s [Free Patriotic Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Patriotic_Movement), the [El Marada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Marada) Party headed by [Suleiman Frangieh, Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suleiman_Frangieh,_Jr.), the [Lebanese Forces](2113218.md) Movement Samir Geagea, and others within the collection of various [14 March](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_14_Alliance) Christian leaders.

The political role of Christianity in Lebanon emerged from the Ottoman millet system, which granted autonomous governance to religious communities particularly with declaration of the [Double Qaim-Maqamate of Mount Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_Qaim-Maqamate_of_Mount_Lebanon) in 1843 dividing Mount Lebanon into two administrative regions ruled by the Druze and the Christians.[^cite_note-29] During the 19th century, Maronite Christians in Mount Lebanon developed a semi-autonomous status under European protection, particularly French, culminating in the 1860 [Mount Lebanon Mutasarrifate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Lebanon_Mutasarrifate), a Christian-governed Ottoman district. This established precedents for Christian political autonomy within a Muslim-majority region.

On 27 October 1919, the Lebanese delegation led by [Maronite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maronite_Christianity_in_Lebanon) [Patriarch](175409.md) [Elias Peter Hoayek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elias_Peter_Hoayek) presented the Lebanese aspirations in a memorandum to the [Paris Peace Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Peace_Conference,_1919). This included a significant extension of the frontiers of the Lebanon Mutasarrifate,[^cite_note-Salibi26-30] arguing that the additional areas constituted natural parts of Lebanon although the Christian community would not be a clear majority in such an enlarged state.[^cite_note-Salibi26-30] The quest for the annexation of agricultural lands in the Bekaa and Akkar was fueled by existential fears following the death of nearly half of the [Mount Lebanon Mutasarrifate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Lebanon_Mutasarrifate) population in the [Great Famine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Famine_of_Mount_Lebanon); the Maronite church and the secular leaders sought a state that could better provide for its people.[^cite_note-31]

The unwritten [National Pact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Pact) of 1943, negotiated between Maronite President [Bechara El Khoury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bechara_El_Khoury) and Sunni Prime Minister [Riad Al Solh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riad_Al_Solh), established Lebanon's confessional power-sharing formula. Christians accepted Lebanon's "Arab face" and renounced Western protection, while Muslims abandoned aspirations for union with Syria. The pact reserved the presidency for a Maronite Christian, the premiership for a Sunni Muslim, and the parliamentary speakership for a Shi'a Muslim. Parliamentary seats were allocated on a 6\:5 Christian-Muslim ratio based on the 1932 census, which showed a Christian majority.[^cite_note-32]

During the Lebanese Civil War, Christian political leaders viewed the battles as an existential struggle to preserve Christian political dominance and prevent the [Palestinian Liberation Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestine_Liberation_Organization) (PLO) from transforming Lebanon into a [base for operations against Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestinian_insurgency_in_South_Lebanon).[^cite_note-33][^cite_note-LexicOrient-34] The PLO's armed presence in southern Lebanon and its alliance with Muslim and leftist factions threatened the delicate confessional balance that underpinned Christian political power. Christian militias, initially organized as neighborhood defense forces, rapidly evolved into sophisticated military organizations controlling large territories referred to as [cantons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Beirut_canton).

Rivalries between militia leaders, ideological differences, and competition for foreign patronage produced violent conflicts between Christian parties. By 1977, relations within the Lebanese Front had deteriorated significantly, particularly between the Lebanese Phalanges (Kataeb) and their former allies in the [Marada Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marada_Movement). This rift emerged after the Phalanges pursued a strategic alignment with Israel—an initiative firmly rejected by the Maradas, who were staunchly anti-Zionist and closely aligned with Syria.\[1 As tensions escalated, armed clashes broke out between forces loyal to Bashir Gemayel and the Marada Brigade. During this period, Bashir Gemayel sought to consolidate Christian militias under a unified command by forcibly integrating them into the Lebanese Forces and dismantling independent militias. This policy intensified inter-Christian violence, culminating in 1980 with the forced absorption of Dany Chamoun’s Tiger Militia into the LF.&#x20;

The [Taif Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taif_Agreement) helped establish a power-sharing system between the Christian and Muslim Lebanese political parties.[^cite_note-taif-35] The political and economic situation in Lebanon had improved greatly. Lebanon had rebuilt its infrastructure. Historical and contemporary conflicts between Hezbollah and Israel have threatened to deteriorate Lebanon's political and economic situation, with growing tension between the 8 and 14 March alliances and threatening Lebanon with renewed strife.

The constitutional remit of the president includes the role of Commander in Chief of the armed forces, as well as the sole ability to form and dissolve governments. Many Lebanese leaders, as well as global powers, continue to lobby to roll back features of the Taif Agreement that eroded the constitutional powers of the president of the republic. The role of president of the Lebanese Central bank is also a position reserved for Lebanese Christians.[^cite_note-36] This is due to the historical and contemporary influence of Lebanese Christians among the key bankers of the Middle East region.

Although Lebanon is a [secular country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secularism_in_Lebanon), family matters such as marriage, divorce, and inheritance are still handled by the religious authorities representing a person's faith. Calls for civil marriage are unanimously rejected by the religious authorities but civil marriages conducted in another country are recognized by Lebanese civil authorities.

Non-religion is not recognized by the state. But the [Minister of the Interior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Interior_and_Municipalities_\(Lebanon\)) [Ziad Baroud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ziad_Baroud) made it possible in 2009 to have religious affiliation removed from the [Lebanese identity card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_identity_card). This does not, however, deny the religious authorities' complete control over civil family issues inside the country.[^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38]

## Christian denominations among Lebanese people

### Maronite Catholic

Main articles: [Maronite Church](66391078.md), [Lebanese people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_people), and [Lebanese Maronite Christians](39667585.md)



Portrait of Saint Charbel Makhlouf who was a Maronite monk and priest.

The [Maronite Christians of Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_people_\(Maronite_Christians\)) are the largest Christian denomination among the Lebanese people, representing 21% of the Lebanese population.[^cite_note-CIAFFB-Leb-23]

The Maronite Church's full communion with the [Catholic Church](606848.md) was reaffirmed in 1182, after hundreds of years of isolation in Mount Lebanon. By the terms of union, they retain their rites and canon law and use Arabic and Aramaic in their liturgy, as well the Karshuni script with old Syriac letters. Their origins are uncertain. One version traces them to John Maron of Antioch in the seventh century A.D.; another points to [St. Maron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Maron), a monk in the late fourth and early fifth centuries (who is considered by many to be the true origin of the Maronite Church). The words "maron" or "marun" in Syriac mean "small lord."

In the late seventh century, as a result of persecutions from other Christians for the heterodox views they had adopted, the Maronites withdrew from the coastal regions into the mountainous areas of Lebanon and Syria. During the Ottoman era (1516–1914) they remained isolated and relatively independent in these areas. In 1857 and 1858 the Maronites revolted against the large landowning families. The revolt was followed by a further struggle between the Druzes and Maronites over land ownership, political power, and safe passage of community members in the territory of the other. The conflict led France to send a military expedition to the area in 1860. The disagreements diminished in intensity only after the establishment of the Mandate and a political formula whereby all denominations achieved a degree of political representation.

Besides the Beirut archdiocese, nine other archdioceses and dioceses are in the Middle East: Aleppo, Damascus, Jubayl-Al Batrun, Cyprus, Baalbek, Tripoli, Tyre, Sidon, and Cairo. Parishes and independent dioceses are situated in Argentina, Brazil, Venezuela, the United States, Canada, Mexico, Côte d'Ivoire, and Senegal. There are four minor seminaries in Lebanon (Al Batrun, Ghazir, Ayn Saadah, and Trablous) and a faculty of theology at the University of the Holy Spirit at Al Kaslik, which is run by the Maronite Monastic Order. The patriarch is elected in a secret ceremony by a synod of bishops and confirmed by the Pope.

Leaders of the Rite have considered Maronite Christianity as the "foundation of the Lebanese nation". The Maronites have been closely associated with the political system of independent Lebanon; it was estimated that in pre-Civil War Lebanon, members of this Rite held a large portion of the leading posts. However, roles were shifted due to the Taif Agreement's theoretical balancing of power.[^cite_note-Ghazi-39]

[St. George Greek Orthodox Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_George_Greek_Orthodox_Cathedral), Beirut

Main articles: [Greek Orthodox Church of Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Orthodox_Church_of_Antioch) and [Lebanese Eastern Orthodox Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_Eastern_Orthodox_Christians)

[Lebanese Greek Orthodox Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_Greek_Orthodox_Christians) is the second largest Christian denomination among the Lebanese people, representing 8% of the Lebanese population.[^cite_note-CIAFFB-Leb-23] The [Greek Orthodox Church of Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Orthodox_Church_of_Antioch) adheres to the [Eastern Orthodox Church](10186.md), which is actually a group of autocephalous churches using the [Byzantine rite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_rite) and are [the second largest Christian denomination within Christianity in Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Christianity_in_Lebanon). Historically, these churches grew out of the four Eastern Patriarchates ([Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Orthodox_Church_of_Jerusalem), [Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Orthodox_Church_of_Antioch), [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Orthodox_Church_of_Alexandria), and [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecumenical_Patriarchate_of_Constantinople)) of the original five major episcopal sees (the [Pentarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentarchy)) of the Roman Empire which included Rome. The final split took place in 1054. From that time, the Eastern Churches have continued to reject the claims of the Patriarchate of Rome (the Catholic Church) to universal supremacy and have rejected the concept of papal infallibility. Doctrinally, the main point at issue between the Eastern and Western Churches is that of the procession of the Holy Spirit and there are also divergences in ritual and discipline.

The Eastern Orthodox Christians include many free-holders, and the community is less dominated by large landowners than other Christian denominations. In present-day Lebanon, the Lebanese Greek Orthodox have become increasingly urbanized, and form a major part of the commercial and professional class of [Beirut](37428.md) and other cities. Many are found in the Southeast ([Nabatieh](4981449.md)/[Beqaa](866204.md)) and [North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Governorate), near [Tripoli](268796.md). They are highly educated and well-versed in finance. The church has often served as a bridge between Lebanese Christians and the Arab countries, because it exists in various parts of the Arab world. Members of the rite constitute 8% of the population.[^cite_note-freedom-40][^cite_note-41]

### Melkite Catholic

Main articles: [Melkite Greek Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melkite_Greek_Catholic_Church), [Lebanese people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_people), and [Lebanese Melkite Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_Melkite_Christians)

[Melkite Christianity in Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_people_\(Melkite_Christians\)) is the third-largest Christian denomination, representing 5% of the Lebanese population.[^cite_note-CIAFFB-Leb-23]

The [Melkite Catholics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melkite_Christianity_in_Lebanon) emerged as a distinct group from 1724 when they split from the Greek Orthodox Church over a disputed election of the Patriarch of Antioch. The elected man was considered too 'pro-Roman' and another faction, the larger, elected a rival who was supported by the Orthodox patriarch in Constantinople (the see of Antioch had ignored the split between the two which occurred in 1054 and was canonically in union with both in 1724). Although they fully accept Catholic doctrines as defined by the [Holy See](13393.md), they have generally remained close to the Greek Orthodox Church, retaining more of the ancient rituals and customs than have the Maronites. They employ Arabic and Greek and follow the Byzantine rite.

The highest official of the church since 1930 has been the Patriarch of Antioch, who resides at Ayn Traz, about twenty-four kilometers southeast of Beirut. The patriarch is elected by bishops in a synod and confirmed by the Pope in Rome, who sends him a pallium (a circular band of white wool worn by archbishops) in recognition of their communion. Greek Catholic churches, like those of the Greek Orthodox, contain icons but no statues. The [Melkite Greek Catholics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melkite_Christianity_in_Lebanon) live primarily in the central and eastern parts of the country, dispersed in many villages. Members of this rite are concentrated in Beirut, Zahlah, and the suburbs of Sidon. They have a relatively higher level of education than other denominations. Proud of their Arab heritage, Greek Catholics have been able to strike a balance between their openness to the Arab world and their identification with the West. Greek Catholics are estimated to constitute 5% of the population.

### Protestantism

Main articles: [Protestant Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant_Church), [Lebanese people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_people), and [Lebanese Protestant Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_Protestant_Christians)

The [Protestants of Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_people_\(Protestant_Christians\)) form the fourth-largest Christian group, representing 1% of the Lebanese population.[^cite_note-CIAFFB-Leb-23]

Most [Protestants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism) in Lebanon were converted by missionaries, primarily English and American, during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. They are divided into a number of Reformed denominations, including Presbyterian, Congregational, Anglican and Baptist. The [Lebanese Baptist Evangelical Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_Baptist_Evangelical_Convention) was founded in 1955 by various churches, they number around 1,600 people.[^cite_note-42] Lebanese Protestant Christians are perceived by some to number disproportionately highly among the professional middle class. They constitute nearly 1 percent of the population (around 40,000) and live primarily in Beirut ([Greater Beirut](42710804.md)).[^cite_note-Ghazi-39]

## Christian denominations among ethnic minorities

### Armenian Apostolic Church

Main articles: [Armenian Apostolic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Apostolic_Church), [Armenian Apostolic Diocese of Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Apostolic_Diocese_of_Lebanon), [Armenians](387816.md), and [Armenians in Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenians_in_Lebanon)

The [Armenians in Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenians_in_Lebanon) mostly descend from refugees who had fled Turkey during and after the [Armenian genocide](140376.md) during [World War I](4764461.md).[^cite_note-arm-43]

The [Armenian Apostolic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Apostolic_Church) was organized in the third century and became autocephalous as a national church in the fourth century. In the sixth century, it modified the formulations of the Council of Chalcedon of 451 that confirmed the dual nature of Christ in one person. Instead, the Armenian Apostolic Church adopted a form of [Miaphysitism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miaphysitism) that believes in the united nature of divine and human in Christ, a belief shared by the Copts and the Syrian Orthodox Church (Oriental Orthodox Churches). The Armenian Apostolic Church has two [catholicoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholicoi) (Sis and [Etchmiadzin Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etchmiadzin_Cathedral)) and two patriarchs (Constantinople and Jerusalem).

The Armenians in Lebanon reside mostly in Beirut and its northern suburbs, as well as in [Anjar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anjar,_Lebanon). During the civil war, the main stance of the Armenians was not to pick a side between Muslims or Christians and stay exempt mostly from the fighting. The largest Armenian community in Lebanon is found in [Bourj Hammoud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourj_Hammoud).[^cite_note-Ghazi-39]

### Armenian Catholic Church

Main articles: [Armenian Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Catholic_Church), [Armenian Catholic Archdiocese of Beirut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Catholic_Archdiocese_of_Beirut), [Armenians](387816.md), and [Armenians in Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenians_in_Lebanon)

Among the [Armenians in Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenians_in_Lebanon) there are some who belong to the [Armenian Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Catholic_Church). They are also refugees who had fled Turkey during and after World War I and the [Armenian genocide](140376.md).[^cite_note-arm-43]

### Latin Catholic Church

Main articles: [Catholic Church](606848.md), [Latin Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Church), [Latin Church in Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Church_in_Lebanon), [French people in Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_people_in_Lebanon), [Italians in Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italians_in_Lebanon), and [Minorities (Lebanon)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minorities_\(Lebanon\))

The [Latin Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Catholic_Church) in Lebanon consists mainly of a small group of [Latin Catholics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Catholics) who are of at least partial [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_people_in_Lebanon) or [Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italians_in_Lebanon) descent.[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45]

### Assyrian Church of the East

Main articles: [Assyrian Church of the East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_Church_of_the_East), [Assyrian people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_people), [Assyrians in Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrians_in_Lebanon), and [Minorities (Lebanon)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minorities_\(Lebanon\))

The [Assyrians in Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrians_in_Lebanon) were refugees who had fled their [native lands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_homeland) in southeastern Turkey during and after World War I due to the [Assyrian genocide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_genocide). Even today, refugees continue to flee from northern Iraq into Syria, Lebanon or Jordan due to continuous unrest in Iraq.

The Archdiocese of Lebanon and Syria of the Assyrian Church of the East is based in the Mar Gewargis Church of Sad El Bouchrieh, Beirut, Lebanon. After the recent passing of the archdiocese's late Archbishop [Mar Narsai D'Baz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mar_Narsai_D'Baz), Archbishop [Mar Meelis Zaia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mar_Meelis_Zaia) of Australia and New Zealand temporarily took over the archdiocese, handling all church related issues in Lebanon. The current bishops, the Bishop of Europe and the Bishop of Syria, oversee their individual dioceses until a new Metropolitan is appointed.

### Syriac Catholic Church

Main articles: [Syriac Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Catholic_Church), [Syriac Catholic Eparchy of Beirut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Catholic_Eparchy_of_Beirut), [Assyrian people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_people), [Assyrians in Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrians_in_Lebanon), and [Minorities (Lebanon)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minorities_\(Lebanon\))

The members of the [Syriac Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Catholic_Church) are also refugees who had fled southeastern Turkey (present day [Mardin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mardin) region) during and after World War I due to the [Assyrian/Syriac genocide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian/Syriac_genocide). Even today, refugees continue to flee from northern Iraq and northeastern Syria into Lebanon or Jordan due to continuous unrest in Iraq and Syria.

The [Syriac Catholic Eparchy of Beirut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Catholic_Eparchy_of_Beirut) is the proper archeparchy ([Eastern Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Catholic) (archdiocese) of the [Syriac Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Catholic_Church)'s ([Antiochian Rite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiochian_Rite) in [Syriac language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_language)) [Patriarch of Antioch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Catholic_Patriarch_of_Antioch) in his actual seat, Beirut, Lebanon.

### Syriac Orthodox Church

Main articles: [Syriac Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Orthodox_Church), [Assyrian people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_people), [Assyrians in Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrians_in_Lebanon), and [Minorities (Lebanon)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minorities_\(Lebanon\))

The members of the [Syriac Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Orthodox_Church) are also refugees who had fled southeastern Turkey (present day [Mardin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mardin) region) during and after World War I due to the [Assyrian/Syriac genocide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian/Syriac_genocide). Even today, refugees continue to flee from northern Iraq and northeastern Syria into Lebanon or Jordan due to continuous unrest in Iraq and Syria.

There are several archdioceses and dioceses of the Syriac Orthodox Church on the territory of Lebanon.[^cite_note-46] The church follows the Syriac liturgy of St. James and has an independent hierarchy under the Syriac Orthodox Patriarch of Antioch, whose seat was formerly at [Mardin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mardin) in [Turkey](11125639.md) and is now at [Damascus](8914.md), [Syria](7515849.md).[^cite_note-Ghazi-39]

### Chaldean Catholic Church

Main articles: [Chaldean Catholic Church](698821.md), [Chaldean Catholic Eparchy of Beirut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaldean_Catholic_Eparchy_of_Beirut), [Assyrian people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_people), [Assyrians in Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrians_in_Lebanon), and [Minorities (Lebanon)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minorities_\(Lebanon\))

The members of the [Chaldean Catholic Church](698821.md) are also refugees who had fled southeastern Turkey (present day [Mardin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mardin) region) during and after World War I due to the [Assyrian/Syriac genocide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian/Syriac_genocide). Even today, refugees continue to flee from northern Iraq and northeastern Syria into Lebanon or Jordan due to continuous unrest in Iraq and Syria.

The [Chaldean Catholic Eparchy of Beirut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaldean_Catholic_Eparchy_of_Beirut) is the sole eparchy ([Eastern Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Catholic) diocese) of the [Chaldean Catholic Church](698821.md) and is immediately dependent on the [Chaldean Catholic Patriarch of Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaldean_Catholic_Patriarch_of_Babylon) in [Baghdad](4492.md), [Iraq](7515928.md).

### Coptic Orthodox Church

Main articles: [Coptic Orthodox Church of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_Orthodox_Church_of_Alexandria), [Coptic people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_people), [Coptic diaspora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_diaspora), and [Minorities (Lebanon)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minorities_\(Lebanon\))

The [Copts in Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_diaspora) were immigrants or refugees who had fled their native lands in Egypt, Libya and Sudan.

According to tradition, the [Coptic Orthodox Church of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_Orthodox_Church_of_Alexandria) was established by Saint Mark, an apostle and evangelist, in the middle of the 1st century (approximately AD 42). The ethnic Copts in Lebanon are estimated to number 3,000–4,000,[^cite_note-state2-47] and the Coptic Orthodox Church is one of the 18 religious sects recognized by the Lebanese Constitution.

In a 'Greater Lebanon', they were bound to be outnumbered by the Muslims of the coastal towns and their hinterlands, and by those of the Bekaa valley; and all the Christian communities together, in a 'Greater Lebanon', could at best amount to a bare majority.
