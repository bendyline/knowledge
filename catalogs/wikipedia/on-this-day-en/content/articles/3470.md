**Burkina Faso**[^cite_note-14] is a [landlocked country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landlocked_country) in [West Africa](67393.md). It is bordered by [Mali](19127.md) to the northwest, [Niger](21373.md) to the northeast, [Benin](3459.md) to the southeast, [Togo](30138.md) and [Ghana](12067.md) to the south, and [Ivory Coast](57744.md) to the southwest. It covers an area of 274,223 km<sup>2</sup> (105,878 sq mi). In 2024, the country had an estimated population of approximately 23,286,000.[^cite_note-15] Its citizens are known as [Burkinabes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burkinabe_nationality_law), and its capital and largest city is [Ouagadougou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouagadougou).

The largest [ethnic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_group) in Burkina Faso is the [Mossi people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mossi_people), who settled the area in the 11th and 13th centuries. They established [kingdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mossi_Kingdoms) such as Ouagadougou, Tenkodogo, and Yatenga. In 1896, it was colonized by the [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_colonial_empire) as part of [French West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_West_Africa); in 1958, Upper Volta became a [self-governing colony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-governing_colony) within the [French Community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Community). In 1960, it gained full independence with [Maurice Yaméogo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Yam%C3%A9ogo) as [the president](378610.md). Since it gained its independence, the country has dealt with political instability, [droughts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Droughts_in_the_Sahel), famines, and corruption. There have been various coups d'état, in [1966](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1966_Upper_Voltan_coup_d'%C3%A9tat), [1980](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980_Upper_Voltan_coup_d'%C3%A9tat), [1982](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1982_Upper_Voltan_coup_d'%C3%A9tat), [1983](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1983_Upper_Voltan_coup_d'%C3%A9tat), [1987](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1987_Burkina_Faso_coup_d'%C3%A9tat), and twice in 2022, in [January](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/January_2022_Burkina_Faso_coup_d'%C3%A9tat) and [September](71882074.md). There were also unsuccessful coup attempts in [1989](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1989_Burkina_Faso_coup_d'%C3%A9tat_attempt), [2015](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_Burkina_Faso_coup_d'%C3%A9tat), and [2023](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_Burkina_Faso_coup_attempt).&#x20;

Burkina Faso remains one of the [least developed countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_developed_countries) in the world, with a GDP of \$16.226 billion in 2022. Approximately 63.8% of its population practices [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_Burkina_Faso), while 26.3% practices Christianity and 9% [animistic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_traditional_religions), especially in the [southwest of the country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sud-Ouest_Region_\(Burkina_Faso\)).[^cite_note-\:1-16] The country's official languages are all [national languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Burkina_Faso)[^cite_note-\:5-17][^cite_note-18] while [Mooré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moor%C3%A9) is spoken by more than half of the population. There are more than 60 indigenous languages, and the constitution provides for other languages to be made official by law. The former government and business language was French until January 2024 when, alongside English, its status was demoted to that of a "working language" by ratification of a constitutional amendment.[^cite_note-19][^cite_note-\:4-20]

The country's territory is geographically biodiverse, and includes plentiful reserves of gold, [manganese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manganese), copper and limestone. Due to its multicultural make-up, [Burkinabe art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_of_Burkina_Faso) has a rich and long history, and is renowned for its orthodox style.[^cite_note-21]

The country is governed as a [semi-presidential republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-presidential_system), with executive, legislative and judicial powers. It is a member of the [United Nations](31769.md) and the [Organisation of Islamic Cooperation](47725.md). Since 2025, Burkina Faso has formalised its exit from [ECOWAS](36848.md) and [La Francophonie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Francophonie), after it helped form the [Alliance of Sahel States](74840743.md) (AES). It also remains suspended from the [African Union](10634933.md).&#x20;

## Etymology

Formerly the [Republic of Upper Volta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Upper_Volta), the country was renamed "Burkina Faso" on 4 August 1984 by then-President [Thomas Sankara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Sankara). The words "Burkina" and "Faso" stem from different languages spoken in the country: "Burkina" comes from [Mooré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moor%C3%A9) and means "upright", showing how the people are proud of their integrity. "Faso" comes from the [Dyula language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyula_language), as written in [N'Ko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N'Ko_script): ߝߊ߬ߛߏ߫ *faso*, and means "fatherland", literally, "father's house". The "-bé" suffix added onto "Burkina" to form the demonym "Burkinabé" comes from the [Fula language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fula_language) and means "women or men".[^cite_note-22] The US [Central Intelligence Agency](5183633.md)'s [World Fact Book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_World_Factbook) gives the translation as "Land of the Honest (Incorruptible) Men".[^cite_note-auto1-23]

The French Colony of Upper Volta was named for its location on the upper courses of the [Volta River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volta_River), the [Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Volta), [Red](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Volta) and [White Volta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Volta).[^cite_note-24]

## History

Main article: [History of Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Burkina_Faso)

### Early history

The northwestern part of present-day Burkina Faso was populated by [hunter-gatherers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter-gatherer) from 14,000 BC to 5,000 BC. Their tools, including [scrapers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scraper_\(archaeology\)), [chisels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chisel) and [arrowheads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrowhead), were discovered in 1973 through [archaeological excavations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excavation_\(archaeology\)).[^cite_note-\:0-25] Agricultural settlements were established between 3600 and 2600 BC.[^cite_note-\:0-25] The [Bura culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bura_culture) was an [Iron-Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age) [civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civilization) centred in the southwest portion of modern-day Niger and in the southeast part of contemporary Burkina Faso.[^cite_note-whc.unesco.org-26] [Iron industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_industry), in [smelting](28716.md) and [forging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forging) for tools and weapons, had developed in [Sub-Saharan Africa](27067.md) by 1200 BC.[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28]

To date, the oldest evidence of iron smelting found in Burkina Faso dates from 800 to 700 BC and forms part of the [Ancient Ferrous Metallurgy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Ferrous_Metallurgy_Sites_of_Burkina_Faso) World Heritage Site.[^cite_note-29] From the 3rd to the 13th centuries AD, the [Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age) [Bura culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bura_culture) existed in the territory of present-day southeastern Burkina Faso and southwestern Niger. Various ethnic groups of present-day Burkina Faso, such as the [Mossi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mossi_people), [Fula](102383.md) and [Dioula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyula_people), arrived in successive waves between the 8th and 15th centuries. From the 11th century, the Mossi people established [several separate kingdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mossi_Kingdoms).



West Africa c. 1875

### 8th to 18th centuries

There is debate about the exact dates when Burkina Faso's many ethnic groups arrived to the area. The proto-[Mossi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mossi_people) arrived in the far eastern part of what is today Burkina Faso sometime between the 8th and 11th centuries, and accepted [Islam](6037917.md) as their religion in the 11th century.[^cite_note-30] The [Samo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samo_language_\(Burkina\)) arrived around the 15th century.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-31] The [Dogon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dogon_people) lived in Burkina Faso's north and northwest regions until sometime in the 15th or 16th centuries, and many of the other ethnic groups that make up the country's population arrived in the region during this time.[^cite_note-32]



The cavalry of the [Mossi Kingdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mossi_Kingdoms) were experts at raiding deep into enemy territory, even against the formidable [Mali Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mali_Empire).



Armed men prevent the French explorer [Louis-Gustave Binger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis-Gustave_Binger) from entering Sia (Bobo-Dioulasso) during his stay in April 1892.

During the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), the Mossi established several separate kingdoms including those of Tenkodogo, Yatenga, Zandoma, and Ouagadougou.[^cite_note-Nations_2014-33] Sometime between 1328 and 1338, Mossi warriors raided [Timbuktu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timbuktu) but the Mossi were defeated by [Sonni Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonni_Ali) of [Songhai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songhai_Empire) at the Battle of Kobi in Mali in 1483.[^cite_note-34]

During the early 16th century, the Songhai conducted many slave raids into what is today Burkina Faso.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-31] During the 18th century, the [Gwiriko Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwiriko) was established at [Bobo-Dioulasso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobo-Dioulasso) and ethnic groups such as the Dyan, Lobi, and Birifor settled along the [Black Volta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Volta).[^cite_note-35]

### From colony to independence (1890s–1958)

Starting in the early 1890s during the European [Scramble for Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scramble_for_Africa), a series of European military officers made attempts to claim parts of what is today Burkina Faso. At times these [colonialists](7299.md) and their armies fought the local peoples; at times they forged alliances with them and made treaties. The colonialist officers and their home governments also made treaties among themselves. The territory of Burkina Faso was invaded by [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Third_Republic), becoming a [French protectorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_colonial_empire) in 1896.[^cite_note-36]



[French West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_West_Africa) c. 1913

The eastern and western regions, where a standoff against the forces of the powerful ruler [Samori Ture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samori_Ture) complicated the situation, came under French occupation in 1897. By 1898, the majority of the territory corresponding to Burkina Faso was nominally conquered; however, French control of many parts remained uncertain.[^cite_note-\:0-25]

The [Franco-British Convention of 14 June 1898](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-French_Convention_of_1898) created the country's modern borders. In the French territory, a war of conquest against local communities and political powers continued for about five years. In 1904, the largely pacified territories of the [Volta basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volta_River) were integrated into the [Upper Senegal and Niger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Senegal_and_Niger) colony of [French West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_West_Africa) as part of the reorganization of the French West African colonial empire. The colony had its capital in [Bamako](56606.md).

The indigenous population was highly discriminated against. For example, African children were not allowed to ride bicycles or pick fruit from trees, "privileges" reserved for the children of colonists. Violating these regulations could land parents in jail.[^cite_note-37]

[Draftees](5735.md) from the territory participated in the European fronts of [World War I](4764461.md) in the battalions of the [Senegalese Rifles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirailleurs_s%C3%A9n%C3%A9galais). Between 1915 and 1916, the districts in the western part of what is now Burkina Faso and the bordering eastern fringe of Mali became the stage of one of the most important armed oppositions to colonial government: the [Volta-Bani War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volta-Bani_War).[^cite_note-38]

The French government finally suppressed the movement but only after suffering defeats.  It also had to organize its largest expeditionary force of its colonial history to send into the country to suppress the insurrection. Armed opposition wracked the Sahelian north when the [Tuareg](103054.md) and allied groups of the Dori region ended their truce with the government.



The capital, [Ouagadougou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouagadougou), in 1930

[French Upper Volta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Upper_Volta) was established on 1 March 1919. The French feared a recurrence of armed uprising and had related economic considerations. To bolster its administration, the colonial government separated the present territory of Burkina Faso from Upper Senegal and Niger.

The new colony was named *Haute Volta* for its location on the upper courses of the [Volta River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volta_River) (the [Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Volta), [Red](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Volta) and [White Volta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Volta)), and François Charles Alexis Édouard Hesling became its first [governor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_governors_of_Upper_Volta). Hesling initiated an ambitious road-making program to improve infrastructure and promoted the growth of cotton for export. The cotton policy – based on [coercion](6512.md) – failed, and revenue generated by the colony stagnated. The colony was dismantled on 5 September 1932, being split between the French colonies of [Ivory Coast](57744.md), [French Sudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Sudan) and [Niger](21373.md). Ivory Coast received the largest share, which contained most of the population as well as the cities of Ouagadougou and Bobo-Dioulasso.

France reversed this change during the period of intense anti-colonial agitation that followed the end of [World War II](32927.md). On 4 September 1947, it revived the colony of Upper Volta, with its previous boundaries, as a part of the [French Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Union). The French designated its colonies as departments of [metropolitan France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_France) on the European continent.

On 11 December 1958, the colony achieved [self-government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-governance) as the [Republic of Upper Volta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Upper_Volta); it joined the Franco-African Community. A revision in the organization of French Overseas Territories had begun with the passage of the Basic Law (Loi Cadre) of 23 July 1956. This act was followed by reorganization measures approved by the French parliament early in 1957 to ensure a large degree of self-government for individual territories. Upper Volta became an autonomous republic in the French community on 11 December 1958. Full independence from France was received in 1960.[^cite_note-WDL1-39]

### Upper Volta (1958–1984)

Main article: [Republic of Upper Volta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Upper_Volta)



[Maurice Yaméogo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Yam%C3%A9ogo), the first president of Upper Volta, examines documents pertaining to the ratification of the country's independence in 1960.

The Republic of Upper Volta (French: *République de Haute-Volta*) was established on 11 December 1958 as a [self-governing colony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-governing_colony) within the [French Community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Community). The name *Upper Volta* related to the nation's location along the upper reaches of the [Volta River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volta_River). The river's three [tributaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tributary), the [Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Volta), [White](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Volta) and [Red Volta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Volta), were expressed in the three colors of the [former national flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_Upper_Volta).

Before attaining autonomy, it had been French Upper Volta and part of the French Union. On 5 August 1960, it attained full independence from [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Fourth_Republic). The first president, [Maurice Yaméogo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Yam%C3%A9ogo), was the leader of the [Voltaic Democratic Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltaic_Democratic_Union) (UDV). The 1960 constitution provided for election by [universal suffrage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_suffrage) of a president and a national assembly. Soon after coming to power, Yaméogo banned all political parties other than the UDV.[^cite_note-40] The government lasted until 1966. After much unrest, including mass demonstrations and strikes by students, labor unions, and civil servants, the military intervened.[^cite_note-AfricanewsCoups-41]

#### Lamizana's rule and multiple coups

The [1966 military coup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1966_Upper_Voltan_coup_d'%C3%A9tat) deposed Yaméogo, suspended the constitution, dissolved the National Assembly, and placed Lt. Col. [Sangoulé Lamizana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sangoul%C3%A9_Lamizana) at the head of a government of senior army officers. The army remained in power for four years. On 14 June 1976, the Voltans ratified a new constitution that established a four-year transition period toward complete civilian rule. Lamizana remained in power throughout the 1970s as president of military or mixed civil-military governments. Lamizana's rule coincided with the beginning of the [Sahel drought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel_drought) and famine which had a devastating impact on Upper Volta and neighboring countries. After conflict over the 1976 constitution, a new constitution was written and approved in 1977. Lamizana was re-elected by open elections in 1978.

Lamizana's government faced problems with the country's traditionally powerful trade unions,[^cite_note-42] and on 25 November 1980, Col. [Saye Zerbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saye_Zerbo) overthrew President Lamizana in a [bloodless coup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980_Upper_Voltan_coup_d'%C3%A9tat). Colonel Zerbo established the Military Committee of Recovery for National Progress as the supreme governmental authority, thus eradicating the 1977 constitution.[^cite_note-Sawo2017-43]<sup>: 6–7 </sup>

Colonel Zerbo also encountered resistance from trade unions and was overthrown two years later by Maj. Dr. [Jean-Baptiste Ouédraogo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Ou%C3%A9draogo) and the Council of Popular Salvation (CSP) in the [1982 Upper Voltan coup d'état](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1982_Upper_Voltan_coup_d'%C3%A9tat). The CSP continued to ban political parties and organizations, yet promised a transition to civilian rule and a new constitution.[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45]

#### 1983 coup d'état

Infighting developed between the right and left factions of the CSP. The leader of the leftists, Capt. [Thomas Sankara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Sankara), was appointed prime minister in January 1983, but was subsequently arrested. Efforts to free him, directed by Capt. [Blaise Compaoré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blaise_Compaor%C3%A9), resulted in a military coup d'état on 4 August 1983.

The coup brought Sankara to power and his government began to implement a series of revolutionary programs which included mass-vaccinations, infrastructure improvements, the expansion of women's rights, encouragement of domestic agricultural consumption, and anti-desertification projects.[^cite_note-UprightDVD-46]

### Burkina Faso (since 1984)

On 2 August 1984, on Sankara's initiative, the country's name changed from "Upper Volta" to "Burkina Faso", or *land of the honest men*; (the literal translation is *land of the upright men*).[^cite_note-47][^cite_note-48][^cite_note-49][^cite_note-50] The presidential decree was confirmed by the National Assembly on 4 August 1984.

Sankara's government comprised the National Council for the Revolution (CNR – [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language): *Conseil national révolutionnaire*), with Sankara as its president, and established popular [Committees for the Defense of the Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committees_for_the_Defense_of_the_Revolution_\(Burkina_Faso\)) (CDRs). The [Pioneers of the Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pioneers_of_the_Revolution) youth programme was also established.

Sankara launched an ambitious socioeconomic programme for change, one of the largest ever undertaken on the African continent.[^cite_note-UprightDVD-46] His foreign policies centred on [anti-imperialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-imperialism), with his government rejecting all [foreign aid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_aid), pushing for [odious debt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odious_debt) reduction, nationalising all land and mineral wealth and averting the power and influence of the [International Monetary Fund](15251.md) (IMF) and [World Bank](36752.md). His domestic policies included a nationwide literacy campaign, land redistribution to peasants, railway and road construction and the outlawing of [female genital mutilation](11408.md), [forced marriages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forced_marriage) and [polygamy](24475.md).[^cite_note-UprightDVD-46][^cite_note-Grila2-51]

Sankara pushed for agrarian self-sufficiency and promoted public health by vaccinating 2,500,000 children against [meningitis](21009963.md), [yellow fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_fever), and [measles](58911.md).[^cite_note-Grila2-51] His national agenda also included planting more than 10,000,000 trees to halt the growing [desertification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desertification) of the [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel). Sankara called on every village to build a medical dispensary and had more than 350 communities build schools with their own labour.[^cite_note-UprightDVD-46][^cite_note-52]

In the 1980s, when ecological awareness was still very low, Sankara was one of the few African leaders to consider environmental protection a priority. He engaged in three major battles: against bush fires "which will be considered as crimes and will be punished as such"; against cattle roaming "which infringes on the rights of peoples because unattended animals destroy nature"; and against the illegitimate cutting of firewood "whose profession will have to be organized and regulated". As part of a development program involving a large part of the population, ten million trees were planted in Burkina Faso in fifteen months during the revolution. To face the advancing desert and recurrent droughts, Sankara also proposed the planting of wooded strips about fifty kilometers wide, crossing the country from east to west. Cereal production, close to 1.1 billion tons before 1983, was predicted to rise to 1.6 billion tons in 1987. Jean Ziegler, former UN special rapporteur for the right to food, said that the country "had become food self-sufficient."[^cite_note-53]

#### Compaoré presidency



[Blaise Compaoré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blaise_Compaor%C3%A9) (left), President 1987–2014, shaking hands with [George W. Bush](3414021.md)

On 15 October 1987, Sankara and twelve other government officials were assassinated in a coup d'état organized by Blaise Compaoré, Sankara's former colleague, who took over as Burkina Faso's president. He held the position until October 2014.[^cite_note-54] After the coup and although Sankara was known to be dead, some CDRs mounted an armed resistance to the [army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Army_of_Burkina_Faso) for several days.[^cite_note-55][^cite_note-56][^cite_note-57] A majority of Burkinabè citizens hold that [France's foreign ministry](250002.md), the [Quai d'Orsay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quai_d'Orsay), was behind Compaoré in organizing the coup. There is some evidence for France's support of the coup.[^cite_note-58]

Compaoré gave the deterioration in relations with neighbouring countries as one of the reasons for the coup.[^cite_note-59] He argued that Sankara had jeopardised foreign relations with the former colonial power ([France](5843419.md)) and with neighbouring [Ivory Coast](57744.md).[^cite_note-ReutersChe22-60] Following the coup, Compaoré immediately reversed the nationalizations, overturned nearly all of Sankara's policies, returned the country back into the IMF fold, and ultimately spurned most of Sankara's legacy. Following an alleged [coup-attempt in 1989](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1989_Burkina_Faso_coup_attempt), Compaoré introduced limited democratic reforms in 1990. Under the new (1991) [constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Burkina_Faso), Compaoré was [re-elected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1991_Burkinab%C3%A9_presidential_election) without opposition in December 1991. In 1998 Compaoré won [election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1998_Burkinab%C3%A9_presidential_election) in a landslide. In 2004, 13 people were tried for plotting a coup against President Compaoré and the coup's alleged mastermind was sentenced to life imprisonment.[^cite_note-61]

In 2000, the constitution was amended to reduce the presidential term to five years and set term limits to two, preventing successive re-election. The amendment took effect during the 2005 elections. If passed beforehand, it would have prevented Compaoré from being reelected. Other presidential candidates challenged the election results. But in October 2005, the constitutional council ruled that the amendment would not apply until the end of his second term in office. This cleared the way for his candidacy in [the 2005 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_Burkinab%C3%A9_presidential_election). On 13 November 2005, he was reelected in a landslide.

In the [2010 presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Burkinab%C3%A9_presidential_election), Compaoré was re-elected. Only 1.6 million Burkinabè voted, out of a total population 10 times that size. In February 2011, the death of a schoolboy provoked the [2011 Burkinabè protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Burkinab%C3%A8_protests), a series of popular protests, coupled with a military mutiny and a magistrates' strike, that called for Compaoré's resignation, democratic reforms, higher wages for troops and public servants and economic freedom.[^cite_note-62][^cite_note-63][^cite_note-64] As a result, governors were replaced and wages for public servants were raised.[^cite_note-65][^cite_note-66] In April 2011, there was an [army mutiny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Burkina_Faso_mutiny); the president named new chiefs of staff, and a curfew was imposed in [Ouagadougou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouagadougou).[^cite_note-67]

Compaoré's government played the role of negotiator in several West-African disputes, including the [2010–2011 Ivorian crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010%E2%80%932011_Ivorian_crisis), the Inter-Togolese Dialogue (2007), and the [2012 Malian Crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Malian_coup_d'%C3%A9tat). As of 2014, Burkina Faso remained one of the [least-developed countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_developed_country) in the world.[^cite_note-68]

#### Kafando presidency

Main article: [2014 Burkinabè uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Burkinab%C3%A8_uprising)



Thousands of protesters march through Ouagadougou, 2014

Starting on 28 October 2014 protesters began to march and demonstrate in Ouagadougou against President Compaoré, who appeared ready to amend the constitution and extend his rule. On 30 October protesters set fire to the parliament building[^cite_note-69] and took over the national TV headquarters.[^cite_note-70] [Ouagadougou International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouagadougou_Airport) closed and MPs suspended the vote on changing the constitution (the change would have allowed Compaoré to stand for re-election in 2015). Later in the day, the military dissolved all government institutions and imposed a [curfew](297681.md).[^cite_note-71]

On 31 October 2014, Compaoré resigned.[^cite_note-72] [Lt. Col. Isaac Zida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yacouba_Isaac_Zida) said that he would lead the country during its transitional period before the planned [2015 presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_Burkinab%C3%A8_general_election), but there were concerns over his close ties to the former president.[^cite_note-73] In November 2014 opposition parties, [civil-society](209443.md) groups and religious leaders adopted a plan for a transitional authority to guide Burkina Faso to elections.[^cite_note-74] Under the plan [Michel Kafando](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Kafando) became the transitional president and Lt. Col. Zida became the acting Prime Minister and Defense Minister.

On 16 September 2015, the [Regiment of Presidential Security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regiment_of_Presidential_Security) (RSP) carried out a [coup d'état](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_Burkina_Faso_coup_d'%C3%A9tat), seizing the president and prime minister and then declaring the [National Council for Democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Council_for_Democracy) the new national government.[^cite_note-75] However, on 22 September 2015, the coup leader, [Gilbert Diendéré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert_Diend%C3%A9r%C3%A9), apologized and promised to restore civilian government.[^cite_note-76] On 23 September 2015 the prime minister and interim president were restored to power.[^cite_note-77]

#### Kaboré presidency and Jihadist insurgency (2015–2023)

Main articles: [Jihadist insurgency in Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jihadist_insurgency_in_Burkina_Faso) and [Terrorism in Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrorism_in_Burkina_Faso)

[General elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_Burkinab%C3%A8_general_election) took place on 29 November 2015. [Roch Marc Christian Kaboré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roch_Marc_Christian_Kabor%C3%A9) won the election in the first round with 53.5% of the vote, defeating businessman [Zéphirin Diabré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z%C3%A9phirin_Diabr%C3%A9), who took 29.7%.[^cite_note-Kabore_wins-78] Kaboré was sworn in as president on 29 December 2015.[^cite_note-swears_in_new_president-79] Kaboré was re-elected in the [general election of 22 November 2020](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Burkinab%C3%A8_general_election), but his party Mouvement du Peuple pour le Progrès (MPP), failed to reach absolute parliamentary majority. It secured 56 seats out of a total of 127. The Congress for Democracy and Progress (CDP), the party of former President Blaise Compaoré, was distant second with 20 seats.[^cite_note-80]



Smoke rising from French Embassy in Ouagadougou, 2 March 2018, during the [2018 Ouagadougou attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Ouagadougou_attacks)

A [Jihadist insurgency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jihadist_insurgency_in_Burkina_Faso) began in August 2015, part of the [Islamist insurgency in the Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamist_insurgency_in_the_Sahel). Between August 2015 and October 2016, seven different posts were attacked across the country.[^cite_note-Croix191016-81][^cite_note-82] On 15 January 2016, terrorists [attacked the capital city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Ouagadougou_attacks) of [Ouagadougou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouagadougou), killing 30 people. [Al-Qaeda in the Islamic Maghreb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Qaeda_in_the_Islamic_Maghreb) and [Al-Mourabitoune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mourabitoun_\(militant_group\)), which until then had mostly operated in neighbouring [Mali](19127.md), claimed responsibility for the attack.[^cite_note-83][^cite_note-Fig160116-84]

In 2016, attacks increased after a new group [Ansarul Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ansar_ul_Islam_\(Western_Africa\)), led by imam [Ibrahim Malam Dicko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibrahim_Malam_Dicko), was founded.[^cite_note-RFI100217-85][^cite_note-86] Its attacks focused particularly on [Soum province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soum_Province)[^cite_note-RFI100217-85][^cite_note-87] and it killed dozens of people in the [attack on Nassoumbou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nassoumbou_attack) on 16 December.[^cite_note-JA090117-88]

Between 27 March and 10 April 2017, the governments of [Mali](19127.md), France, and Burkina Faso launched a joint operation named "Operation Panga", which involved 1,300 soldiers from the three countries, in the Fhero Forest, near the [Burkina Faso-Mali border](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burkina_Faso-Mali_border), considered a sanctuary for Ansarul Islam.[^cite_note-89][^cite_note-90] The head of Ansarul Islam, Ibrahim Malam Dicko, was killed in June 2017 and Jafar Dicko became leader.[^cite_note-91]

On 2 March 2018, [Jama'at Nasr al-Islam wal Muslimin](53401088.md) [attacked the French embassy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Ouagadougou_attacks) in [Ouagadougou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouagadougou) as well as the general staff of the Burkinabè army. Eight soldiers and eight attackers were killed, and a further 61 soldiers and 24 civilians were injured.[^cite_note-Monde070318-92] The insurgency expanded to the east of the country[^cite_note-93][^cite_note-94][^cite_note-95] and, in early October, the Armed Forces of Burkina Faso launched a major military operation in the country's East, supported by French forces.[^cite_note-96][^cite_note-97][^cite_note-98] Between mid-2018 to February 2019, at least 42 people were murdered by jihadists and a minimum of 116 mostly Fulani civilians were killed by military forces without trial.[^cite_note-\:3-99] The attacks [increased significantly in 2019](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_attacks_in_Burkina_Faso). According to the [ACLED](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armed_Conflict_Location_and_Event_Data_Project), armed violence in Burkina Faso jumped by 174% in 2019, with nearly 1,300 civilians dead and 860,000 displaced.[^cite_note-100] Jihadist groups also began to specifically target [Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_Burkina_Faso).[^cite_note-101][^cite_note-\:2-102][^cite_note-103][^cite_note-104]



Displaced Fulani civilians in the aftermath of the [Yirgou massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yirgou_massacre)

On 8 July 2020, the United States raised concerns after a [Human Rights Watch](46530.md) report revealed mass graves with at least 180 bodies, which were found in northern Burkina Faso where soldiers were fighting jihadists.[^cite_note-105] On 4 June 2021, the Associated Press reported that according to the government of Burkina Faso, gunmen killed at least 100 people in Solhan village in northern Burkina Faso near the Niger border. A local market and several homes were also burned down. A government spokesman blamed jihadists. Heni Nsaibia, senior researcher at the [Armed Conflict Location and Event Data Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armed_Conflict_Location_and_Event_Data_Project) said it was the deadliest attack recorded in Burkina Faso since the beginning of the jihadist insurgency.[^cite_note-106]

From 4–5 June 2021, unknown militants [massacred more than 170 people in the villages of Solhan and Tadaryat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solhan_and_Tadaryat_massacres). Jihadists killed 80 people in [Gorgadji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gorgadji_Department) on 20 August.[^cite_note-107] On 14 November, the [Jama'at Nasr al-Islam wal Muslimin](53401088.md) attacked a gendarmerie in [Inata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inata,_Burkina_Faso), killing 53 soldiers, the heaviest loss of life by the Burkinabe military during the insurgency, and a major [morale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morale) loss in the country.[^cite_note-108] In December Islamists killed 41 people in an ambush, including the popular vigilante leader Ladji Yoro. Yoro was a central figure in the [Volunteers for the Defense of the Homeland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volunteers_for_the_Defense_of_the_Homeland) (VDP) a pro-government militia that had taken a leading role in the struggle against Islamists.[^cite_note-109]

In 2023, shortly after the murder of a Catholic priest by insurgents, the bishop of Dori, Laurent Dabiré, claimed in an interview with Catholic charity [Aid to the Church in Need](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aid_to_the_Church_in_Need) that around 50% of the country was in the hands of Islamists.[^cite_note-110]

#### 2022 coups d'état

Main articles: [January 2022 Burkina Faso coup d'état](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/January_2022_Burkina_Faso_coup_d'%C3%A9tat) and [September 2022 Burkina Faso coup d'état](71882074.md)



The [Alliance of Sahel States](74840743.md) between Burkina Faso, Mali and Niger



A map of the [Islamist insurgency in Burkina Faso](69566376.md), November 2024. The government controlled areas of Burkina Faso are in blue.

In a successful coup on 24 January 2022, mutinying soldiers arrested and deposed President [Roch Marc Christian Kaboré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roch_Marc_Christian_Kabor%C3%A9) following gunfire.[^cite_note-111] The [Patriotic Movement for Safeguard and Restoration](69877187.md) (MPSR) supported by the military declared itself to be in power,[^cite_note-112][^cite_note-\:12-113] led by Lieutenant Colonel [Paul-Henri Sandaogo Damiba](69868896.md).[^cite_note-114] On 31 January, the military junta restored the constitution and appointed Damiba interim president. In the aftermath of the coup, [ECOWAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_Community_of_West_African_States) and the [African Union](10634933.md) suspended Burkina Faso's membership.[^cite_note-115][^cite_note-116]

On 10 February, the Constitutional Council declared Damiba president.[^cite_note-117] He was sworn in as president on 16 February.[^cite_note-118] On 1 March 2022, the junta approved a charter allowing a military-led transition of 3 years.[^cite_note-119] The charter provides for the transition process to be followed by the holding of elections.[^cite_note-120] [President Kaboré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roch_Marc_Christian_Kabor%C3%A9), who had been detained since the military junta took power, was released on 6 April 2022.[^cite_note-121]

The insurgency continued following the coup, with about 60% of the country under government control.[^cite_note-122] The [Siege of Djibo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Djibo) began in February 2022[^cite_note-Douce-123][^cite_note-Sankara-124] and continued as of June 2023.[^cite_note-125][^cite_note-126] Between 100 and 165 people [were killed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Seytenga_massacre) in [Seytenga Department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seytenga_Department), [Séno Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%A9no_Province) on 12–13 June and around 16,000 people fled their homes.[^cite_note-127][^cite_note-128] In June 2022, the Government announced the creation of "military zones", which civilians were required to vacate so that the country's Armed and Security Forces could fight insurgents without any "hindrances".[^cite_note-129][^cite_note-130]

On 30 September 2022, Damiba was ousted in a military coup led by Capt. [Ibrahim Traoré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibrahim_Traor%C3%A9).[^cite_note-131][^cite_note-132] This came eight months after Damiba had seized power. The rationale given by Traoré for the coup d'état was the purported inability of Paul-Henri Sandaogo Damiba to deal with an Islamist insurgency.[^cite_note-133] Damiba resigned and left the country.[^cite_note-134] On 6 October 2022, Captain Ibrahim Traoré was officially appointed as president.[^cite_note-135] [Apollinaire Joachim Kyélem de Tambèla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollinaire_Joachim_Ky%C3%A9lem_de_Tamb%C3%A8la) was appointed interim Prime Minister on 21 October 2022.[^cite_note-136]

On 13 April 2023, authorities in Burkina Faso declared a mobilisation in order to give the nation all means necessary to combat terrorism and create a "legal framework for all the actions to be taken" against the insurgents in recapturing 40% of the national territory from Islamist insurgents.[^cite_note-137] On 20 April, the Rapid Intervention Brigade committed the [Karma massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karma_massacre), rounding up and executing civilians *en masse*. Between 60 and 156 civilians were killed.[^cite_note-138][^cite_note-\:8-139][^cite_note-HRW-140][^cite_note-141]

On 25 August 2024, [JNIM](53401088.md) again launched [a major attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Barsalogho_attack) in the region of [Barsalogho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barsalogho_Department), killing at least 400 people.[^cite_note-142]

In 2025, the armed forces foiled an attempted coup, which they said had been planned by plotters based in the [Ivory Coast](57744.md). Thousands rallied in Ouagadougou in support of the military government after the foiled coup plot.[^cite_note-143] In May, reports of a digital [cult](1008588.md) around Traoré began to surface.[^cite_note-144][^cite_note-145][^cite_note-146]

## Geography

Main article: [Geography of Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Burkina_Faso)



A satellite image of Burkina Faso



A map of Burkina Faso

Burkina Faso lies mostly between latitudes [9°](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/9th_parallel_north) and [15° N](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/15th_parallel_north) (a small area is north of 15°), and longitudes [6° W](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/6th_meridian_west) and [3° E](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3rd_meridian_east).

There are two major types of countryside. The larger part of the country is covered by a [peneplain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peneplain), which forms a gently undulating landscape with, in some areas, a few isolated hills, the last vestiges of a [Precambrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precambrian) [massif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massif). The southwest of the country is a [sandstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandstone) massif, where the highest peak, [Ténakourou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C3%A9nakourou), is found at an elevation of 749 meters (2,457 ft). The massif is bordered by sheer cliffs up to 150 m (492 ft) high. The average altitude of Burkina Faso is 400 m (1,312 ft) and the difference between the highest and lowest terrain is no greater than 600 m (1,969 ft).

The country owes its former name of Upper Volta to three rivers which cross it: the [Black Volta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Volta) (or *Mouhoun*), the [White Volta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Volta) (*Nakambé*) and the [Red Volta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Volta) (*Nazinon*). The Black Volta is one of the country's only two rivers which flow year-round, the other being the [Komoé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Komo%C3%A9_River), which flows to the southwest. The basin of the [Niger River](21437.md) also [drains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage_basin) 27% of the country's surface.

The Niger's [tributaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tributary) – the Béli, Gorouol, Goudébo, and Dargol – are [seasonal streams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torrent_\(stream\)) and flow for only four to six months a year. They still can flood and overflow, however. The country also contains numerous lakes – the principal ones are Tingrela, [Bam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Bam), and Dem. The country contains large ponds, such as Oursi, Béli, Yomboli, and Markoye. [Water shortages](37335.md) are often a problem, especially in the north of the country.



[Savannah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savanna) near the [Gbomblora Department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gbomblora_Department), on the road from [Gaoua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaoua) to [Batié](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bati%C3%A9,_Burkina_Faso)

Burkina Faso lies within two terrestrial ecoregions: [Sahelian Acacia savanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahelian_Acacia_savanna) and [West Sudanian savanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Sudanian_savanna).[^cite_note-DinersteinOlson2017-147]

In Burkina Faso [forest cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_cover) is around 23% of the total land area, equivalent to 6,216,400 hectares (ha) of forest in 2020, down from 7,716,600 hectares (ha) in 1990. In 2020, naturally regenerating forest covered 6,039,300 hectares (ha) and planted forest covered 177,100 hectares (ha). Of the naturally regenerating forest 0% was reported to be [primary forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_forest) (consisting of native tree species with no clearly visible indications of human activity) and around 16% of the forest area was found within protected areas. For the year 2015, 100% of the forest area was reported to be under [public ownership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_ownership).[^cite_note-148][^cite_note-149]

### Climate



A map of Burkina Faso's [climate zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification)

Burkina Faso has a primarily tropical climate with two very distinct seasons. In the rainy season, the country receives between 600 and 900 mm (24 and 35 in) of rainfall. In the dry season, the [harmattan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmattan) – a hot dry wind from the Sahara – blows. The rainy season lasts around four months, May/June to September, but is shorter in the north of the country. There are three climatic zones: the [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel), the Sudan-Sahel, and the Sudan-Guinea. The Sahel in the north typically receives less than 600 mm (24 in)[^cite_note-relationship-150] of rainfall per year and has high temperatures, 5–47 [°C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celsius) (41–117 [°F](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fahrenheit)).

A relatively dry [tropical savanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_savanna), the Sahel extends beyond the borders of Burkina Faso, from the [Horn of Africa](62142.md) to the Atlantic Ocean, and borders the [Sahara](325363.md) to its north and the fertile region of the [Sudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudan_\(region\)) to the south. Situated between 11° 3′ and 13° 5′ north [latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latitude), the Sudan-Sahel region is a transitional zone with regard to rainfall and temperature. Further to the south, the Sudan-Guinea zone receives more than 900 mm (35 in)[^cite_note-relationship-150] of rain each year and has cooler average temperatures.



Damage caused by the Dourtenga floods in 2007

Geography and environment contribute to Burkina Faso's food insecurity.[^cite_note-151] As the country is situated in the [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel) region, it has some of the most radical climatic variation in the world, ranging from severe flooding to extreme drought.[^cite_note-152] The unpredictable climatic shocks can make it very difficult for Burkina Faso citizens to rely on and prosper from agriculture.[^cite_note-153]

Burkina Faso's climate renders its crops vulnerable to insect attacks, including attacks from [locusts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locust) and [crickets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cricket_\(insect\)), which destroy crops and further inhibit food production.[^cite_note-154] Most of the population of Burkina Faso is dependent upon agriculture as a source of income, and rely upon the agricultural sector to directly feed the household.[^cite_note-Coping-155] Due to the vulnerability of agriculture, more families are having to look for other sources of non-farm income,[^cite_note-156] and often have to travel outside of their regional zone to find work.[^cite_note-Coping-155]

### Natural resources

[Burkina Faso's natural resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_resources_of_Burkina_Faso) include [gold](12240.md), [manganese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manganese), [limestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limestone), [marble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marble), [phosphates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphate), [pumice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pumice), and [salt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_mining).

### Wildlife

Further information: [Wildlife of Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildlife_of_Burkina_Faso)

Burkina Faso has a larger number of [elephants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elephant) than many countries in West Africa. Lions, leopards and buffalo can also be found here, including the [dwarf or red buffalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_forest_buffalo). Other large predators live in Burkina Faso, such as the cheetah, the caracal or African lynx, the spotted hyena and the African painted dog, one of the continent's most endangered species.[^cite_note-157]

Burkina Faso's [fauna and flora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildlife_in_Burkina_Faso) are protected in four national parks:

- The [W National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W_National_Park) in the east, other sections of which are located in [Benin](3459.md) and [Niger](21373.md)

- The Arly Wildlife Reserve ([Arly National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arly_National_Park) in the east)

- The Léraba-Comoé Classified Forest and Partial Reserve of Wildlife in the west

- The [Mare aux Hippopotames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mare_aux_Hippopotames) in the west

and several reserves: see [List of national parks in Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_national_parks_in_Africa) and [List of protected areas of Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_protected_areas_of_Burkina_Faso).

## Government and politics

Main article: [Politics of Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics_of_Burkina_Faso)



The [National Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Assembly_of_Burkina_Faso) building in downtown [Ouagadougou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouagadougou)

The June 1991 [constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Burkina_Faso) established a [semi-presidential government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-presidential_system): its parliament could be dissolved by the [President of the Republic](378610.md), who was to be [elected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elections_in_Burkina_Faso) for a term of seven years. In 2000, the constitution was amended to reduce the presidential term to five years and set term limits to two, preventing successive re-election. The amendment took effect during the 2005 elections.

The parliament consisted of [one chamber](204304.md) known as the [National Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Assembly_\(Burkina_Faso\)), which had 111 seats with members elected to serve five-year terms. There was a constitutional chamber, with ten members, and an economic and social council whose roles were purely consultative. The 1991 constitution created a [bicameral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicameralism) parliament, but the upper house (Chamber of Representatives) was abolished in 2002.

The Compaoré administration worked to [decentralize power](49139.md#Government_decentralization) by devolving some of its powers to regions and municipal authorities. The widespread distrust of politicians and lack of political involvement by many residents complicated this process. Critics described this as a hybrid decentralisation.[^cite_note-158]

[Political freedoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_freedom) are severely restricted in Burkina Faso. Human rights organizations had criticised the Compaoré administration for numerous acts of state-sponsored violence against journalists and other politically active members of society.[^cite_note-159][^cite_note-160]

The prime minister is head of government and is appointed by the president with the approval of the National Assembly. He is responsible for recommending a cabinet for appointment by the president.[^cite_note-161]

### Constitution

Main article: [Constitution of Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Burkina_Faso)

In 2015, Kaboré promised to revise the 1991 constitution. The revision was completed in 2018. One condition prevents any individual from serving as president for more than ten years either consecutively or intermittently and provides a method for impeaching a president. A referendum on the constitution for the Fifth Republic was scheduled for 24 March 2019.[^cite_note-162]

Certain rights are also enshrined in the revised wording: access to drinking water, access to decent housing and a recognition of the right to civil disobedience, for example. The referendum was required because the opposition parties in Parliament refused to sanction the proposed text.[^cite_note-163]

Following the [January 2022 coup d'état](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/January_2022_Burkina_Faso_coup_d'%C3%A9tat), the military dissolved the parliament, government and constitution.[^cite_note-\:12-113] On 31 January, the military junta restored the constitution,[^cite_note-164] but it was suspended again following the [September 2022 coup d'état](71882074.md).[^cite_note-165]

The [Progressive and Popular Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_and_Popular_Revolution) has led to increased scepticism that the constitution will ever be restored in its old form.

### Administrative divisions

Main articles: [Regions of Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regions_of_Burkina_Faso), [Provinces of Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provinces_of_Burkina_Faso), and [Departments of Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Departments_of_Burkina_Faso)

The country is divided into [13 administrative regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regions_of_Burkina_Faso). These regions encompass [45 provinces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provinces_of_Burkina_Faso) and [301 departments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Departments_of_Burkina_Faso). Each region is administered by a governor.

### Foreign relations

Main article: [Foreign relations of Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_Burkina_Faso)

Burkina Faso is a member of the [Community of Sahel–Saharan States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community_of_Sahel%E2%80%93Saharan_States), the [Organisation of Islamic Cooperation](47725.md), and United Nations. It formally withdrew from [ECOWAS](36848.md) and [La Francophonie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Francophonie) in 2025, following its suspension by both organisations in 2022 over the military coup.[^cite_note-166][^cite_note-167] In addition, Burkina Faso remains suspended from the [African Union](10634933.md).[^cite_note-168] Until December 2023, Burkina Faso was a member of [G5 Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G5_Sahel), a small group formed in 2014 to co-operate with development and security matters. However, in December 2023 the country withdrew from the organisation due to concerns of "serving foreign interests to the detriments of our people".

### Military

Main article: [Military of Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_of_Burkina_Faso)

The [army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Army_of_Burkina_Faso) has 6,000 men in voluntary service, augmented by a part-time national People's Militia composed of civilians between 25 and 35 years of age who are trained in military and civil duties. According to *Jane's Sentinel Country Risk Assessment* in 2022, Burkina Faso's Army is undermanned for its force structure and poorly equipped, but has wheeled light-armour vehicles, and may have developed useful combat expertise through interventions in Liberia and elsewhere in Africa.[^cite_note-169]

In terms of training and equipment, the regular Army is believed to be neglected in relation to the elite [Regiment of Presidential Security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidential_Security_Regiment) (French: *Régiment de la Sécurité Présidentielle* – RSP). Reports have emerged in recent years of disputes over pay and conditions.[^cite_note-170] There is an air force with some 19 operational aircraft, but no navy, as the country is landlocked. Military expenses constitute approximately 1.2% of the nation's GDP.

### Law enforcement

Main article: [Law enforcement in Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_enforcement_in_Burkina_Faso)

Burkina Faso employs numerous police and security forces, generally modeled after organizations used by [French police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_police). France continues to provide significant support and training to police forces. The *Gendarmerie Nationale* is organized along military lines, with most police services delivered at the brigade level. The [Gendarmerie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gendarmerie) operates under the authority of the Minister of Defence, and its members are employed chiefly in the rural areas and along borders.[^cite_note-Das,_pp._139–141-171]

There is a municipal police force controlled by the [Ministry of Territorial Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Territorial_Administration), a national police force controlled by the Ministry of Security, and an autonomous [Regiment of Presidential Security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regiment_of_Presidential_Security) (*Régiment de la Sécurité Présidentielle*, or RSP), a 'palace guard' devoted to the protection of the President of the Republic. Both the gendarmerie and the national police are subdivided into both administrative and judicial police functions; the former are detailed to protect public order and provide security, the latter are charged with criminal investigations.[^cite_note-Das,_pp._139–141-171]

All foreigners and citizens are required to carry photo ID passports, or other forms of identification or risk a fine, and police spot identity checks are commonplace for persons traveling by auto, [bush-taxi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Share_taxi), or bus.[^cite_note-172][^cite_note-173]

## Economy

Main article: [Economy of Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Burkina_Faso)



GDP per capita in Burkina Faso, 1950 to 2018



Processing facilities at the Essakane Mine in Burkina Faso



The *Grand marché* in [Koudougou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koudougou), Burkina Faso



The railway station in [Bobo Dioulasso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobo_Dioulasso) was built during the colonial era and remains in operation.

Burkina Faso remains one of the least developed countries in the world, with an economy based primarily on subsistence farming and livestock raising. The country has an average income purchasing-power-parity per capita of \$2,980 and nominal per capita of \$1,110 in 2025. More than 80% of the population relies on subsistence agriculture, with only a small fraction directly involved in industry and services. A large part of the economic activity of the country is funded by international aid, despite having gold ores in abundance.

### Economic overview

Burkina Faso's economy showed signs of recovery in recent years. According to the World Bank, the economy grew by 4.9% in 2024 compared to 3.0% in 2023. Real per capita GDP growth also increased from 0.7% to 2.5% over the same period. This acceleration was attributed mainly to the performance of services and agriculture, supported by an improved security situation, favorable weather conditions, and increased government support to the agriculture sector.

Inflation increased in 2024 to 4.2% from 0.7% in 2023, driven by spikes in food prices caused by market speculation linked to a late start to the rainy season. Despite this, the strong growth in the agriculture and services sectors in 2024 reduced the extreme poverty rate by 3 points to 23.2%, with a sharper decline in rural areas. Nevertheless, the absolute number of people living in poverty remains high, exceeding 5.5 million.

The country faces significant economic challenges, including highly variable rainfall patterns, poor soils, lack of adequate communications and other infrastructure, low literacy rate, high population density with few natural resources, and fragile soil conditions.

### Agriculture

Main article: [Agriculture in Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture_in_Burkina_Faso)

Agriculture represents approximately 32% of Burkina Faso's gross domestic product and occupies 80% of the working population. It consists mostly of rearing livestock, especially in the south and southwest, where people grow crops of [sorghum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorghum), [pearl millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_millet), maize (corn), peanuts, rice and cotton, with surpluses to be sold. Cotton is the main cash crop.

### Mining

Main article: [Mining in Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining_in_Burkina_Faso)

There is mining of [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper), [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron), [manganese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manganese), [gold](12240.md), [cassiterite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassiterite) (tin ore), and phosphates.[^cite_note-176] These operations provide employment and generate international aid. Gold production increased 32% in 2011 at six gold mine sites, making Burkina Faso the fourth-largest gold producer in Africa, after South Africa, Mali and Ghana.[^cite_note-177]

Gold accounts for roughly 70% of Burkina Faso's export earnings. With production of 66.9 tons valued at \$7.18 billion, gold accounted for an estimated 16% of Burkina Faso's gross domestic product in 2023. Since then, gold production has declined dramatically owing to growing insecurity, which has led to the closure of 7 out of the country's 17 industrial mines (Inata, Taparko, Karma, Boungou, Youga, Nétiana, Yaramoko). The junta has also nationalized five mines. Industrial gold production has subsequently dropped by 20% to 53.4 tons.

Zabsone et al. (2020) show find that gold extraction increases household expenditures and reduces poverty rates in producing municipalities. Nevertheless, it also increased inequality and child labor.[^cite_note-178]

### External trade

The value of Burkina Faso's exports fell from \$2.77 billion in 2011 to \$754 million in 2012.[^cite_note-179]

The top five export commodities in 2017 were, in order of importance: gems and precious metals, US\$1.9 billion (78.5% of total exports), cotton, \$198.7 million (8.3%), ores, slag, ash, \$137.6 million (5.8%), fruits, nuts: \$76.6 million (3.2%) and oil seeds: \$59.5 million (2.5%).[^cite_note-180]

In 2023, exports totaled \$5.87 billion while imports were \$7.31 billion, resulting in a trade deficit. The current account deficit improved from 8.0% of GDP in 2023 to 6.4% in 2024, due to the rise in gold prices which boosted the value of exports.

### Economic challenges and outlook

The economic outlook for Burkina Faso remains subject to multiple significant risks. The fragile security situation continues to threaten economic gains, with ongoing instability potentially disrupting key sectors. Climate shocks, particularly irregular rainfall patterns, persistently affect agricultural production and food security. Debt refinancing presents substantial challenges, requiring careful management of the country's financial obligations. The financial sector remains vulnerable due to banks' overexposure to the cotton sector, creating systemic risks. Foreign investment has declined precipitously since the military coup, with FDI inflows dropping from \$670 million in 2022 to \$83 million in 2024, severely limiting capital for development projects.[^cite_note-WorldBank2025-175]

The World Bank recommends strengthening the mobilization and efficiency of public resources through several key measures. These include continuous modernization of the tax administration, broadening of the tax base, optimization of public spending, improving debt management strategies, and mobilizing more concessional financing to support sustainable development.[^cite_note-181]

### Infrastructure

#### Water management

The National Office for Water and Sanitation (ONEA), a [state-owned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State-owned) [utility company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utility_company) operated along commercial lines. A 2011 report by Overseas Development instittute found that ONEA's operational and financial performance had improved substantially and described it as "closing the gap" with the best performing utilities in Africa.[^cite_note-ODI1-182] The report cited increased autonomy, improved staff productivity, reduced water losses, improved financial reporting, and increased revenue among the factors accompanying its improved performance.[^cite_note-ODI1-182]

Since 2000, nearly 2 million more people have access to water in the four principal urban centres in the country; the company has kept the quality of [infrastructure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_infrastructure) high (less than 18% of the water is lost through leaks – one of the lowest in [sub-Saharan Africa](27067.md)), improved financial reporting, and increased its annual revenue by an average of 12% (well above inflation).[^cite_note-ODI1-182] Challenges remain, including difficulties among some customers in paying for services, with the need to rely on international aid to expand its infrastructure.[^cite_note-ODI1-182] The state-owned, commercially run venture has helped the nation reach its [Millennium Development Goal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennium_Development_Goal) (MDG) targets in water-related areas, and has grown as a viable company.[^cite_note-ODI1-182]

However, access to drinking water has improved over the last 28 years. According to UNICEF, access to drinking water has increased from 39 to 76% in rural areas between 1990 and 2015. In this same time span, access to drinking water increased from 75 to 97% in urban areas.[^cite_note-183]

#### Energy

Main article: [Energy in Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_in_Burkina_Faso)

Access to electricity remains limited in Burkina Faso, with a rate well below the regional average. This situation constitutes a major obstacle to inclusive growth and reduces economic opportunities for a large part of the population, particularly in rural areas.

A 33-megawatt solar power plant in Zagtouli, near Ouagadougou, came online in late November 2017. At the time of its construction, it was the largest solar power facility in West Africa.[^cite_note-184]

#### Transport

Main article: [Transport in Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_in_Burkina_Faso)

Transport in Burkina Faso is limited by relatively underdeveloped infrastructure.

As of June 2014 the main international airport, [Ouagadougou Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouagadougou_Airport), had regularly scheduled flights to many destinations in West Africa as well as Paris, [Brussels](3708.md) and [Istanbul](3391396.md). The other international airport, [Bobo Dioulasso Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobo_Dioulasso_Airport), has flights to Ouagadougou and [Abidjan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Bouet_Airport).

[Rail transport in Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_transport_in_Burkina_Faso) consists of a single line which runs from [Kaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaya,_Burkina_Faso) to [Abidjan](21371767.md) in Ivory Coast via [Ouagadougou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouagadougou), [Koudougou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koudougou), Bobo Dioulasso and [Banfora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banfora). *Sitarail* operates a passenger train three times a week along the route.[^cite_note-185]

There are 15,000 kilometres of roads in Burkina Faso, of which 2,500 kilometres are paved.[^cite_note-186]

### Science and technology

Main article: [Science and technology in Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_and_technology_in_Burkina_Faso)

In 2009, Burkina Faso spent 0.20% of GDP on research and development (R\&D), one of the lowest ratios in West Africa. There were 48 researchers (in full-time equivalents) per million inhabitants in 2010, which is more than twice the average for sub-Saharan Africa (20 per million population in 2013) and higher than the ratio for [Ghana](12067.md) and [Nigeria](21383.md) (39). It is, however, much lower than the ratio for [Senegal](27278.md) (361 per million inhabitants). In Burkina Faso in 2010, 46% of researchers were working in the health sector, 16% in engineering, 13% in natural sciences, 9% in agricultural sciences, 7% in the humanities and 4% in social sciences.[^cite_note-UNESCO_Science_Report-187]

In January 2011, the government created the Ministry of Scientific Research and Innovation. Up until then, management of science, technology and innovation had fallen under the Department of Secondary and Higher Education and Scientific Research. Within this ministry, the Directorate General for Research and Sector Statistics is responsible for planning. A separate body, the Directorate General of Scientific Research, Technology and Innovation, coordinates research. This is a departure from the pattern in many other West African countries where a single body fulfills both functions. The move signals the government's intention to make science and technology a development priority.[^cite_note-UNESCO_Science_Report-187] Burkina Faso was ranked 126th in the [Global Innovation Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Innovation_Index) in 2025.[^cite_note-188][^cite_note-189]

### International relations

Main article: [Foreign relations of Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_Burkina_Faso)

Burkina Faso is part of the [West African Monetary and Economic Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_African_Monetary_and_Economic_Union) (UMEOA) and has adopted the [CFA franc](3791898.md). This is issued by the [Central Bank of the West African States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Bank_of_the_West_African_States) (BCEAO), situated in [Dakar](44251.md), Senegal. The BCEAO manages the monetary and reserve policy of the member states, and provides regulation and oversight of financial sector and banking activity. A legal framework regarding licensing, bank activities, organizational and capital requirements, inspections and sanctions (all applicable to all countries of the Union) is in place, having been reformed significantly in 1999. [Microfinance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microfinance) institutions are governed by a separate law, which regulates microfinance activities in all WAEMU countries. The insurance sector is regulated through the Inter-African Conference on Insurance Markets (CIMA).[^cite_note-190]

Burkina Faso is a member of the [Organization for the Harmonization of Business Law in Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organization_for_the_Harmonization_of_Business_Law_in_Africa) (OHADA).[^cite_note-191] The country also belongs to the United Nations, International Monetary Fund, World Bank, and World Trade Organization.[^cite_note-bn-192]

In January 2024, Burkina Faso announced its exit from ECOWAS and the African Union after helping form the Alliance of Sahel States (AES).

Burkina Faso is an ethnically integrated, secular state where most people are concentrated in the south and centre, where their density sometimes exceeds 48 inhabitants per square kilometre (120/sq mi). Hundreds of thousands of Burkinabè migrate regularly to Ivory Coast and Ghana, mainly for seasonal agricultural work. ### Ethnic groups

Main article: [Ethnic groups in Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_groups_in_Burkina_Faso)

Burkina Faso's 23 million people belong to two major West African ethnic cultural groups: the [Voltaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gur_languages) and the [Mandé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mand%C3%A9_peoples) (whose common language is [Dioula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dioula_language)). The Voltaic [Mossi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mossi_people) make up about one-half of the population. The Mossi claim descent from warriors who migrated to present-day Burkina Faso from [northern Ghana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Region_\(Ghana\)) around 1100 AD. They established an empire that lasted more than 800 years. Predominantly farmers, the Mossi kingdom is led by the *[Mogho Naba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mogho_Naba),* whose court is in Ouagadougou.[^cite_note-bn-192] There are approximately 5,000 Europeans.[^cite_note-199]

The [working languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Working_language) are [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language), which was introduced during the colonial period, and [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language).[^cite_note-\:4-20] In December 2023, due to deteriorating relations between Burkina Faso and the French government, the Burkina Faso government announced it was elevating national languages to the status of official languages. French was dropped as an official language, becoming a working language with English instead.[^cite_note-constitution-200][^cite_note-201][^cite_note-202][^cite_note-203] The 2024 transitional charter adopted under President Traoré mentions "all national languages of Burkina Faso" would serve as the country's official languages.[^cite_note-\:5-17][^cite_note-\:6-204] The charter does not name any of those official or national languages.[^cite_note-\:5-17][^cite_note-\:6-204]

An estimated 69 languages are spoken in the country,[^cite_note-205] of which about 60 languages are [indigenous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_language). The Mooré language is the most spoken language in Burkina Faso, spoken by about half the population, mainly in the central region around the capital, Ouagadougou.

In the west, [Mandé languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mande_languages) are widely spoken, the most predominant being [Dyula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyula_language) (also known as Jula or Dioula), others including [Bobo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobo_language), [Samo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samo_language_\(Burkina\)), and [Marka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marka_language). ### Religion

Main article: [Religion in Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_Burkina_Faso)



The [Grand Mosque of Bobo-Dioulasso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Mosque_of_Bobo-Dioulasso)

The [government of Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Burkina_Faso)'s 2019 census reported that 63.8% of the population practiced Islam, and that the majority of this group belong to the [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni) branch,[^cite_note-\:1-16][^cite_note-report2010-207] while a small minority adheres to [Shia Islam](26961.md).[^cite_note-208] A significant number of Sunni Muslims identify with the [Tijaniyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tijaniyah) [Sufi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufi) order.

The 2019 census also found that 26.3% of the population were Christians (20.1% being Roman Catholics and 6.2% members of Protestant denominations) and 9.0% followed [traditional indigenous beliefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_traditional_religion) such as the [Dogon religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dogon_religion), 0.2% followed other religions, and 0.7% were non-religious.[^cite_note-\:1-16][^cite_note-report2010-207]

[Animists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animism) are the largest religious group in the country's [Sud-Ouest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sud-Ouest_Region_\(Burkina_Faso\)) region, forming 48.1% of its total population.[^cite_note-\:1-16]

### Health

Main article: [Health in Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_in_Burkina_Faso)

In 2016, the average life expectancy was estimated at 60 for males and 61 for females. In 2018, the under-five mortality rate and the infant mortality rate was 76 per 1000 live births.[^cite_note-WHO-209] In 2024, the median age of its inhabitants was 18 and the estimated population growth rate was 2.4%.[^cite_note-worldfactbook-4]

In 2011, health expenditures was 6.5% of GDP; the maternal mortality ratio was estimated at 300 deaths per 100000 live births and the physician density at 0.05 per 1000 population in 2010. In 2012, it was estimated that the adult HIV [prevalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prevalence) rate (ages 15–49) was 1.0%.[^cite_note-210] In 2011, HIV prevalence was declining among pregnant women who attend antenatal clinics.[^cite_note-211] In 2005, an estimated 72.5% of Burkina Faso's girls and women had undergone [female genital mutilation](11408.md), administered according to traditional rituals.[^cite_note-212]

Central government spending on health was 3% in 2001.[^cite_note-213] As of 2009, studies estimated there were as few as 10 physicians per 100,000 people.[^cite_note-autogenerated1-214] There were 41 nurses and 13 midwives per 100,000 people.[^cite_note-autogenerated1-214] [Demographic and Health Surveys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographic_and_Health_Surveys) has completed three surveys in Burkina Faso since 1993, and had another in 2009.[^cite_note-Burkina_Surveys-215]

A [Dengue fever](39669.md) outbreak in 2016 killed 20 patients.[^cite_note-216]

In the 2024 Global Hunger Index (GHI), Burkina Faso ranks 98th out of 127 countries and has a serious level of hunger.[^cite_note-217]

### Education

Main article: [Education in Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_in_Burkina_Faso)



The [Gando](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gando,_Burkina_Faso) primary school, designed by [Diébédo Francis Kéré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Di%C3%A9b%C3%A9do_Francis_K%C3%A9r%C3%A9). The innovative clay/cement hybrid structure provides thermal protection and natural ventilation, addressing characteristic problems of educational buildings in the region. Kéré received the [Aga Khan Award for Architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aga_Khan_Award_for_Architecture) in 2004 for this design.

Education in Burkina Faso is divided into primary, secondary and higher education. The Education Act makes schooling compulsory from age 6 to 16, with French as the official language of instruction.

By law, education is free, but the government lacks adequate resources to provide universal free primary education. Children must pay for school supplies, and communities are often responsible for constructing primary school buildings and teachers' housing. Children from poor families can receive tuition-free education through junior high and high school if their grades qualify.

High school costs approximately CFA 25,000 (US\$50) per year, which is far above the means of most Burkinabè families. Boys receive preference in schooling; as such, girls' education and literacy rates are far lower than their male counterparts’. An increase in girls' schooling has been observed because of the government's policy of making school cheaper for girls and granting them more scholarships.

To proceed from primary to middle school, middle to high school or high school to college, national exams must be passed. Institutions of higher education include the [University of Ouagadougou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Ouagadougou), [The Polytechnic University of Bobo-Dioulasso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Polytechnic_University_of_Bobo-Dioulasso), and the [University of Koudougou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Koudougou), which is also a teacher training institution. There are some small private colleges in the capital city of [Ouagadougou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouagadougou) but these are affordable to only a small portion of the population.

Higher education provision is highly centralized in Ouagadougou. The University of Ouagadougou had around 40,000 students (83% of the national population of university students) in 2010/2011, the University of Koudougou had 5,600 students, and the Polytechnic University of Bobo-Dioulasso had 2,600. The private universities each had less than 1,000 students. Supervision rates vary significantly between institutions - at the University of Ouagadougou there is one lecturer for every 24 students, while at The Polytechnic University of Bobo-Dioulasso there is one lecturer for every three students.

As of 2023, the gross enrollment ratio for tertiary education among males was reported at 12.1%. The gross enrollment ratio represents the ratio of total enrollment, regardless of age, to the population of the age group that officially corresponds to the level of education shown.

There is also the [International School of Ouagadougou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_School_of_Ouagadougou) (ISO), an American-based private school located in Ouagadougou.

The 2008 UN Development Program Report ranked Burkina Faso as the country with the lowest level of literacy in the world, despite a concerted effort to double its literacy rate from 12.8% in 1990 to 25.3% in 2008.

#### Influencing factors

Several factors continue to influence educational outcomes in Burkina Faso, such as the limited number of actual schools, particularly in rural areas, and a shortage of qualified instructors, especially for higher education. Families must pay for school supplies and school fees despite nominal free education, and there is widespread poverty with very low family incomes. There are the opportunity costs of sending children to school rather than having them contribute to family income. Another factor is language barriers, as education is conducted primarily in French while only approximately 15% of Burkinabè speak French as a first language. \[citation needed]

## Food insecurity

According to the [Global Hunger Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Hunger_Index), a multidimensional tool used to measure and track a country's hunger levels,[^cite_note-218] Burkina Faso ranked 65 out of 78 countries in 2013.[^cite_note-Know_About_Hunger-219] It is estimated that there are currently over 1.5 million children who are at risk of food insecurity in Burkina Faso, with around 350,000 children who are in need of emergency medical assistance.[^cite_note-Know_About_Hunger-219] However, only about a third of these children will actually receive adequate medical attention.[^cite_note-Cost_of_Hunger-220] Only 11.4 percent of children under the age of two receive the daily recommended number of meals.[^cite_note-Know_About_Hunger-219] Stunted growth as a result of food insecurity is a severe problem in Burkina Faso, affecting at least a third of the population from 2008 to 2012.[^cite_note-221] Additionally, stunted children, on average, tend to complete less school than children with normal growth development,[^cite_note-Cost_of_Hunger-220] further contributing to the low levels of education of the Burkina Faso population.[^cite_note-222]

The [European Commission](9974.md) expects that approximately 500,000 children under age 5 in Burkina Faso will suffer from acute malnutrition in 2015, including around 149,000 who will suffer from its most life-threatening form.[^cite_note-ECHO_Factsheet-223] Rates of micronutrient deficiencies are also high.[^cite_note-DHS_Program-224] According to the [Demographic and Health Survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographic_and_Health_Surveys) (DHS 2010), 49 percent of women and 88 percent of children under the age of five suffer from anemia.[^cite_note-DHS_Program-224] Forty percent of infant deaths can be attributed to [malnutrition](258979.md), and in turn, these infant mortality rates have decreased Burkina Faso's total work force by 13.6 percent, demonstrating how food security affects more aspects of life beyond health.[^cite_note-Know_About_Hunger-219]

These high rates of food insecurity and the accompanying effects are even more prevalent in rural populations compared to urban ones, as access to health services in rural areas is much more limited and awareness and education of children's nutritional needs is lower.[^cite_note-225]

An October 2018 report by [USAid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USAid) stated that droughts and floods remained problematic, and that "violence and insecurity are disrupting markets, trade and livelihoods activities in some parts of Burkina Faso's northern and eastern areas". The report estimated that over 954,300 people needed food security support, and that, according to [UNICEF](19698110.md), an "estimated 187,200 children under 5 years of age will experience severe acute malnutrition". Agencies providing assistance at the time included USAID's Office of Food for Peace (FFP) working with the UN [World Food Programme](33582.md), the NGO [Oxfam Intermón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxfam_Interm%C3%B3n) and [ACDI/VOCA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACDI/VOCA).[^cite_note-226]

### Approaches to improving food security

#### World Food Programme

The United Nations' [World Food Programme](33582.md) has worked on programs that are geared towards increasing food security in Burkina Faso. The Protracted Relief and Recovery Operation 200509 (PRRO) was formed to respond to the high levels of malnutrition in Burkina Faso, following the food and nutrition crisis in 2012.[^cite_note-World_Food_Programme-227] The efforts of this project are mostly geared towards the treatment and prevention of malnutrition and include take home rations for the caretakers of those children who are being treated for malnutrition.[^cite_note-World_Food_Programme-227] Additionally, the activities of this operation contribute to families' abilities to withstand future food crises. Better nutrition among the two most vulnerable groups, young children and pregnant women, prepares them to be able to respond better in times when food security is compromised, such as in droughts.[^cite_note-World_Food_Programme-227]

The Country Programme (CP) has two parts: food and nutritional assistance to people with HIV/AIDS, and a school feeding program for all primary schools in the Sahel region.[^cite_note-Brief-228] The HIV/AIDS nutrition program aims to better the nutritional recovery of those who are living with HIV/AIDS and to protect at-risk children and orphans from malnutrition and food security.[^cite_note-Brief-228] As part of the school feeding component, the Country Programme's goals are to increase enrollment and attendance in schools in the Sahel region, where enrollment rates are below the national average.[^cite_note-World_Food_Programme-227] Furthermore, the program aims at improving gender parity rates in these schools, by providing girls with high attendance in the last two years of primary school with take-home rations of cereals as an incentive to households, encouraging them to send their girls to school.[^cite_note-World_Food_Programme-227]

The WFP concluded the formation of a subsequently approved plan in August 2018 "to support the Government's vision of 'a democratic, unified and united nation, transforming the structure of its economy and achieving a strong and inclusive growth through patterns of sustainable consumption and production.' It will take important steps in WFP's new strategic direction for strengthened national and local capacities to enable the Government and communities to own, manage, and implement food and nutrition security programmes by 2030".[^cite_note-229]

In April 2026, India sent one thousand metric tonnes of rice to Burkina Faso as humanitarian assistance, to support food security for vulnerable communities and internally displaced persons.

#### World Bank

One of the main projects the [World Bank](36752.md) is working on to reduce food insecurity in Burkina Faso is the Agricultural Productivity and Food Security Project.[^cite_note-AgProdFSP-230] According to the World Bank, the objective of this project is to "improve the capacity of poor producers to increase food production and to ensure improved availability of food products in rural markets."[^cite_note-AgProdFSP-230] The Agricultural Productivity and Food Security Project has three main parts. Its first component is to work towards the improvement of food production, including financing grants and providing 'voucher for work' programs for households that cannot pay their contribution in cash.[^cite_note-AgProdFSP-230]

The project's next component involves improving the availability of food products, particularly in rural areas.[^cite_note-AgProdFSP-230] This includes supporting the marketing of food products and aims to strengthen the capabilities of stakeholders to control the variability of food products and supplies at local and national levels.[^cite_note-AgProdFSP-230] Lastly, the third component of this project focuses on institutional development and capacity building. Its goal is to reinforce the capacities of service providers and institutions that are specifically involved in project implementation.[^cite_note-AgProdFSP-230] The project's activities aim to build capacities of service providers, strengthen the capacity of food producer organizations, strengthen agricultural input supply delivery methods, and manage and evaluate project activities.[^cite_note-AgProdFSP-230]

The December 2018 report by the World Bank indicated that the poverty rate fell slightly between 2009 and 2014, from 46% to a still high 40.1%. The report provided this updated summary of the country's development challenges: "Burkina Faso remains vulnerable to climatic shocks related to changes in rainfall patterns and to fluctuations in the prices of its export commodities on world markets. Its economic and social development will, to some extent, be contingent on political stability in the country and the sub-regions, its openness to international trade, and export diversification".[^cite_note-231]

## Culture

Main article: [Culture of Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Burkina_Faso)



A masked Winiama dancer, c. 1970

[Literature in Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literature_in_Burkina_Faso) is based on the [oral tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_tradition), which remains important. In 1934, during French occupation, Dim-Dolobsom Ouedraogo published his *Maximes, pensées et devinettes mossi* (*Maxims, Thoughts and Riddles of the Mossi*), a record of the oral history of the [Mossi people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mossi_people).[^cite_note-KS-232]

The oral tradition continued to have an influence on Burkinabè writers in the post-independence Burkina Faso of the 1960s, such as [Nazi Boni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Boni) and Roger Nikiema.[^cite_note-TJA-233] The 1960s saw a growth in the number of playwrights being published.[^cite_note-KS-232] Since the 1970s, literature has developed in Burkina Faso with many more writers being published.[^cite_note-JM-234]

The [theatre of Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_Burkina_Faso) combines traditional Burkinabè performance with the colonial influences and post-colonial efforts to educate rural people to produce a distinctive national theatre. Traditional ritual ceremonies of the many ethnic groups in Burkina Faso have long involved dancing with [masks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mask). Western-style theatre became common during colonial times, heavily influenced by [French theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_theatre). With independence came a new style of theatre inspired by [forum theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_the_Oppressed) aimed at educating and entertaining Burkina Faso's rural people.[^cite_note-235]



Malika Outtara, poet

[Slam poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetry_slam) is increasing in popularity in the country, in part due to the efforts of slam poet [Malika Outtara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malika_Ouattara).[^cite_note-236] She uses her skills to raise awareness around issues such as blood donation, albinism and the impact of COVID-19.[^cite_note-237][^cite_note-\:42-238]

### Arts and crafts

Main article: [Art of Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_of_Burkina_Faso)



Artisan garland of decorative painted [gourds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gourd) in Ouagadougou

In addition to several rich traditional artistic heritages among the peoples, there is a large artist community in Burkina Faso, especially in Ouagadougou. Much of the crafts produced are for the country's growing tourist industry.

Burkina Faso also hosts the International Art and Craft Fair, Ouagadougou. It is better known by its French name as [SIAO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SIAO), *Le Salon International de l' Artisanat de Ouagadougou*, and is one of the most important African handicraft fairs.

### Music

Main article: [Music of Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Burkina_Faso)

The music of Burkina Faso includes the [folk music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_music) of 60 different [ethnic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_group). The [Mossi people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mossi_people), centrally located around Ouagadougou, account for 40% of the population while, to the south, [Gurunsi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gurunsi_people), [Gurma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gurma_people), [Dagaaba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dagaaba_people) and [Lobi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lobi_people) populations, speaking [Gur languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gur_languages) closely related to the [Mossi language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mossi_language), extend into the coastal states. In the north and east the [Fulani](102383.md) of the [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel) preponderate, while in the south and west the [Mande languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mande_languages) are common; [Samo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samo_language_\(Burkina\)), [Bissa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bissa_people), [Bobo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobo_people), [Senufo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senufo_people) and [Marka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marka_people). Burkinabé traditional music has continued to thrive and musical output remains quite diverse. Popular music is mostly in French. Burkina Faso has yet to produce a major pan-African success.

### Media

Main articles: [Media of Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_of_Burkina_Faso) and [Communications in Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communications_in_Burkina_Faso)



A cameraman in Ouagadougou in 2010

The nation's principal media outlet is its state-sponsored combined television and radio service, *[Radio Télévision du Burkina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_T%C3%A9l%C3%A9vision_du_Burkina)* (RTB).[^cite_note-239] RTB broadcasts on two [medium-wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medium-wave) ([AM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplitude_modulation)) and several [FM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_modulation) frequencies. Besides RTB, there are privately owned sports, cultural, music, and religious FM radio stations. RTB maintains a worldwide short-wave news broadcast (*Radio Nationale Burkina*) in the French language from Ouagadougou using a 100 kW transmitter on 4.815 and 5.030 MHz.[^cite_note-240]

Attempts to develop an independent press and media in Burkina Faso have been intermittent. In 1998, investigative journalist [Norbert Zongo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norbert_Zongo), his brother Ernest, his driver, and another man were assassinated by unknown assailants, and the bodies burned. The crime was never solved.[^cite_note-autogenerated2-241] However, an independent Commission of Inquiry later concluded that Norbert Zongo was killed for political reasons because of his investigative work into the death of David Ouedraogo, a chauffeur who worked for François Compaoré, President Blaise Compaoré's brother.[^cite_note-en.rsf.org-242][^cite_note-Outrageous_Denial_of_Justice-243]

In January 1999, François Compaoré was charged with the murder of David Ouedraogo, who had died as a result of torture in January 1998. The charges were later dropped by a military tribunal after an appeal. In August 2000, five members of the President's personal security guard detail (*Régiment de la Sécurité Présidentielle*, or RSP) were charged with the murder of Ouedraogo. RSP members Marcel Kafando, Edmond Koama, and Ousseini Yaro, investigated as suspects in the Norbert Zongo assassination, were convicted in the Ouedraogo case and sentenced to lengthy prison terms.[^cite_note-en.rsf.org-242][^cite_note-Outrageous_Denial_of_Justice-243] Because of his role in the Zongo affair, François Compaoré was arrested on 29 October 2017 by French police on his return from Abidjan.[^cite_note-244] His extradition to Burkina Faso was annulled by the ECHR, following an appeal lodged by his lawyer [François-Henri Briard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois-Henri_Briard).[^cite_note-245]

Since the death of Norbert Zongo, several protests regarding the Zongo investigation and treatment of journalists have been prevented or dispersed by government police and security forces. In April 2007, popular radio reggae host [Karim Sama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sams'K_Le_Jah), whose programs feature reggae songs interspersed with critical commentary on alleged government injustice and corruption, received several death threats.[^cite_note-246]

Sama's personal car was later burned outside the private radio station Ouaga FM by unknown vandals.[^cite_note-247] In response, the [Committee to Protect Journalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_to_Protect_Journalists) (CPJ) wrote to President Compaoré to request his government investigate the sending of e-mailed death threats to journalists and radio commentators in Burkina Faso who were critical of the government.[^cite_note-autogenerated2-241] In December 2008, police in Ouagadougou questioned leaders of a protest march that called for a renewed investigation into the unsolved Zongo assassination. Among the marchers was Jean-Claude Meda, the president of the Association of Journalists of Burkina Faso.[^cite_note-248]

### Cinema

Main article: [Cinema of Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinema_of_Burkina_Faso)

The [cinema of Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinema_of_Burkina_Faso) is an important part of the West African film industry and African film as a whole.[^cite_note-249] Burkina's contribution to [African cinema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_cinema) started with the establishment of the film festival [FESPACO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FESPACO) (Festival Panafricain du Cinéma et de la Télévision de Ouagadougou), which was launched as a film week in 1969. Many of the nation's filmmakers are known internationally and have won international prizes.

For many years the headquarters of the [Pan African Federation of Filmmakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_African_Federation_of_Filmmakers) (FEPACI) was in Ouagadougou, rescued in 1983 from a period of moribund inactivity by the enthusiastic support and funding of [President Sankara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Sankara). (In 2006 the Secretariat of FEPACI moved to South Africa, but the headquarters of the organization is still in Ouagadougou.) Among the best known directors from Burkina Faso are [Gaston Kaboré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaston_Kabor%C3%A9), [Idrissa Ouedraogo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idrissa_Ouedraogo), and [Dani Kouyate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dani_Kouyate).[^cite_note-250] Burkina produces popular television series such as *Les Bobodiouf*. Internationally known filmmakers such as Ouedraogo, Kabore, Yameogo, and Kouyate make popular television series.

### Cuisine

Main article: [Cuisine of Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuisine_of_Burkina_Faso)



A plate of [fufu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fufu) (right) accompanied with [peanut soup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peanut_soup)

Typical of West African cuisine, Burkina Faso's cuisine is based on [staple foods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staple_food) of [sorghum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorghum), [millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millet), rice, maize, peanuts, potatoes, [beans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bean), [yams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yam_\(vegetable\)) and [okra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okra).[^cite_note-ox-251] The most common sources of animal protein are chicken, chicken eggs and freshwater fish. A typical Burkinabè beverage is *banji* or palm wine, which is fermented [palm sap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palm_wine); and zoom-kom, or "grain water", is purportedly the national drink of Burkina Faso. Zoom-kom is milky-looking and whitish, having a water and cereal base, and often drunk with ice cubes. In the more rural regions, in the outskirts of Burkina, *dolo*, a drink made from fermented millet, may be found.[^cite_note-252] In times of crisis, *zamnè*, a legume native to Burkina, can be served as a main dish or in a sauce.[^cite_note-253]

### Cultural festivals and events

Every two years, Ouagadougou hosts the [Panafrican Film and Television Festival of Ouagadougou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panafrican_Film_and_Television_Festival_of_Ouagadougou) (FESPACO), the largest African cinema festival on the continent (February, odd years).

Held every two years since 1988, the [International Art and Craft Fair, Ouagadougou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Art_and_Craft_Fair,_Ouagadougou) (SIAO) is one of Africa's most important trade shows for art and handicrafts (late October-early November, even years).

Also, every two years, the [Symposium de sculpture sur granit de Laongo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symposium_de_sculpture_sur_granit_de_Laongo) takes place on a site located about 35 kilometres (22 miles) from Ouagadougou in the province of [Oubritenga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oubritenga).

The [National Culture Week of Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Culture_Week_of_Burkina_Faso), better known by its French name *La semaine nationale de la culture* (SNC), is one of the most important cultural activities of Burkina Faso. It is a biennial event which takes place every two years in Bobo Dioulasso, the second-largest city in the country.

The [Festival International des Masques et des Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Festival_International_des_Masques_et_des_Arts) (FESTIMA), celebrating [traditional masks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_African_masks), is held every two years in [Dédougou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%A9dougou).

### Sports

Main article: [Sport in Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sport_in_Burkina_Faso)



The [Burkina Faso national football team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burkina_Faso_national_football_team) in white during a match

Sport in Burkina Faso is widespread and includes football, basketball, cycling, rugby union, [handball](13730.md), tennis, boxing and martial arts. Football is the most popular sport in Burkina Faso, played both professionally, and informally in towns and villages across the country. The national team is nicknamed "Les Etalons" ("the Stallions") in reference to the legendary horse of Princess [Yennenga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yennenga).[^cite_note-254]

In 1998, Burkina Faso hosted the [Africa Cup of Nations](250680.md) for which the Omnisport Stadium in Bobo-Dioulasso was built. Burkina Faso qualified for the [2013 African Cup of Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2013_Africa_Cup_of_Nations) in South Africa and reached the final, but then lost to Nigeria 0–1. The country has never qualified for a [FIFA World Cup](11370.md).[^cite_note-255]

Basketball is another sport which enjoys much popularity for both men and women.[^cite_note-256] The country's [men's national team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burkina_Faso_national_basketball_team) had its most successful year in 2013 when it qualified for the [AfroBasket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AfroBasket), the continent's prime basketball event.

At the [2020 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2020_Summer_Olympics), the athlete [Hugues Fabrice Zango](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugues_Fabrice_Zango) won Burkina Faso's first Olympic medal, winning bronze in the [men's triple jump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athletics_at_the_2020_Summer_Olympics_%E2%80%93_Men's_triple_jump).[^cite_note-257] Zango also won the gold medal in the triple jump at the 2023 world championships. Cricket is also picking up in Burkina Faso with Cricket Burkina Faso running a ten-club league.[^cite_note-258]
