# Agriculture in New Zealand



A bullock wagon team taking wool from a [farm station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Station_\(New_Zealand_agriculture\)). The number of sheep in New Zealand peaked in the 1980s and has reduced due to lower profits from wool, and larger breeds of sheep for meat.

In [New Zealand](4913064.md), **agriculture** is the largest sector of the [tradable economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tradable_sector). The country exported [NZ\$](161585.md)46.4 billion worth of agricultural products (raw and manufactured) in the 12 months to June 2019, 79.6% of the country's total exported goods.[^cite_note-1] The agriculture, forestry and fisheries sector directly contributed \$12.653 billion (or 5.1%) of the national GDP in the 12 months to September 2020,[^cite_note-2] and employed 143,000 people, 5.9% of New Zealand's workforce, as of the [2018 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_New_Zealand_census).[^cite_note-Results_spreadsheet-3]

New Zealand is unique in being the only developed country to be totally exposed to the international markets since [subsidies](146719.md), [tax concessions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_credit) and [price supports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price_support) for the [agricultural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture) sector were removed in the 1980s.[^cite_note-4] However, as of 2017, the New Zealand Government still provides state investment in infrastructure which supports agriculture.[^cite_note-5]

[Pastoral farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastoral_farming) is the major land use, but a significant amount of land is also devoted to [horticulture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horticulture).

New Zealand is a member of the [Cairns Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairns_Group), which is seeking to have [free trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_trade) in agricultural goods.[^cite_note-6]

## History

| --- |
| image icon ["Digging with the Kō"](https://www.lindaueronline.co.nz/maori-portraits/digging-with-the-ko) (1910) oil on canvas painting by [Gottfried Lindauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Lindauer) depicting activity of breaking soil for kūmara with special tools called [*kō*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digging_stick), [basket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kete_\(basket\)) of [water gourds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calabash) seen in bottom right corner. |

Following their settlement of New Zealand in the 13th century, the [Māori people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ori_people) developed economic systems involving hunting, foraging, and agriculture.[^cite_note-7] The Māori people valued land and especially horticulture, with many and various traditional Māori proverbs and legends emphasise the importance of gardening.[^cite_note-8] While the tropical Polynesian islands of their ancestors' origin were prime cultivation sites of various important [canoe plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canoe_plants) like [breadfruit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breadfruit) and [coconut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coconut), the colder climates of New Zealand hindered this continuation drastically reducing their inventory to 6, three of them being [root crops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_crop) ([kūmara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweet_potato_cultivation_in_Polynesia#New_Zealand), [uwhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dioscorea_alata) and [taro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taro)) and were only optimally their most productive in the [North Island](62046.md).[^cite_note-9]

European and American explorers, missionaries and settlers introduced new animals and plants from 1769, and mass European settlement and land transfer led in the second half of the 19th century to an agricultural system featuring large Australian-style pastoral [runs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheep_station) raising sheep. Immigrant land-hunger, innovations in refrigeration in the 1880s and the rise of dairying fostered the land reforms of [John McKenzie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_McKenzie_\(New_Zealand_politician\)) in the 1890s, permitting an agricultural landscape of smaller [family-based farms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_farm) which became New Zealand's 20th-century agricultural norm (the oft-repeated cliché trumpets that agriculture/farming/farmers constitute "the backbone of the \[New Zealand] economy") - challenged only in recent years by the growth in large-scale commercial [industrial agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_agriculture)[^cite_note-10] and in [lifestyle blocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hobby_farm).[^cite_note-11]

A industry based on [the gathering and production of native *Phormium* fibres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flax_in_New_Zealand) (esp. [*P. tenax*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phormium_tenax), known in English as "flax") existed from about 1860 until a decline up to 1985.



Early label from a tin of [mutton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutton) from New Zealand

The [Department of Agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Agriculture_and_Forestry_\(New_Zealand\)) controlled all meat-exporting [slaughterhouses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slaughterhouse). By 1921 there were 32 abattoir inspectors and 86 inspectors of meat works. New Zealand [mutton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutton) was marked as government inspected and pure.[^cite_note-12]

The government offered a number of subsidies during the 1970s to assist farmers after the [United Kingdom joined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_enlargement_of_the_European_Communities) the [European Economic Community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Economic_Community)[^cite_note-13] and by the early 1980s government support provided some farmers with 40 percent of their income.[^cite_note-14] In 1984 the Labour government ended all farm subsidies under [Rogernomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogernomics),[^cite_note-15] and by 1990 the agricultural industry became the most deregulated sector in New Zealand.[^cite_note-16] To stay competitive in the heavily subsidised European and US markets New Zealand farmers had to increase the efficiency of their operations.[^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18]

## Pastoral farming



Beef and dairy cattle are important components of New Zealand agriculture.

[Pastoral farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_husbandry) in New Zealand is significant to the countries [economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_New_Zealand) and occupies 38% of the land area, with dairy, sheep and beef being of the most significance.[^cite_note-19]

In [Northland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northland_Region), [Auckland](676368.md), [Gisborne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gisborne_District) & [Marlborough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marlborough_District) the major form of pastoral farming is beef cattle. In the [Waikato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waikato), [Bay of Plenty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bay_of_Plenty), [Taranaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taranaki_Region), [Manawatū-Whanganui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manawat%C5%AB-Whanganui) and [West Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Coast,_New_Zealand) regions dairy cattle predominate and sheep farming predominate in [Hawke's Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawke's_Bay), [Wellington](33804.md), [Nelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nelson,_New_Zealand)-[Tasman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tasman_District), [Otago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otago) & [Southland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southland_Region). Sheep farming overall has been declining in recent years with dairy rising to compensate.[^cite_note-20]

### Dairy farming

See also: [Dairy farming in New Zealand](24443459.md)

There were 6.26 million dairy cattle in New Zealand as of June 2019.[^cite_note-\:4-21] By 2025 the number had fallen to 5.7 million.[^cite_note-\:11-22]For the 2019–20 season, 4.92 million cows were milked in 11,179 herds, producing 21.1 billion litres (4.6×10<sup>9</sup> imp gal; 5.6×10<sup>9</sup> US gal) of raw milk containing 1.9 million tonnes of milk solids (protein and milkfat).[^cite_note-\:3-23][^cite_note-\:5-24] Dairy farms covered an effective area of 17,304 km<sup>2</sup> (6,681 sq mi), around 6.46% of New Zealand's total land area.[^cite_note-\:5-24]

&#x20;The dairy cattle farming industry employed 39,264 people as of the 2018 census, 1.6% of New Zealand's workforce, making it the country's tenth-largest employment industry.[^cite_note-Results_spreadsheet-3] Around 56% of dairy farms in New Zealand are owner-operated as of 2015, while 29% are operated by [sharemilkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharemilking) and 14% are operated by contract milkers.[^cite_note-\:5-24] Herd-owning *s*haremilkers (formerly 50\:50 sharemilkers) own their own herd, and are responsible for employing workers and the day-to-day operations of the farm, in return for receiving a percentage (typically 50%) of the milk income. Variable order sharemilkers do not own their own herd, and receive a lower percentage (typically 20-30%) of the milk income, while contract milkers are paid a fixed price per unit of milk.[^cite_note-25]



Hay bales in [Otago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otago)

Dairy farming in New Zealand is primarily [pasture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasture)-based. Dairy cattle primarily feed on grass, supplemented by [silage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silage), hay and other crops during winter&#x20;

and other times of slow pasture growth.[^cite_note-\:6-26] The dairy farming year in New Zealand typically runs from 1 June to 31 May. The first day of the new year, known as "Moving Day" or "Gypsy Day", sees a large-scale migration as sharemilkers and contract milkers take up new contracts and move herds and equipment between farms.[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28] Calving typically takes place in late winter (July and August), and cows are milked for nine months before being dried off in late autumn (April and May).[^cite_note-\:7-29][^cite_note-\:6-26] Some farms employ winter milking, either wholly or partly, with calving in late summer and early autumn (February and March).[^cite_note-\:6-26]

Dairy farmers sell their milk to processors and are paid per kilogram of milk solids (kgMS). In the 2019–20 season, processors paid an average of \$7.20 per kgMS (excluding [GST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goods_and_Services_Tax_\(New_Zealand\))), with the payout varying between \$6.25 and \$9.96 per kgMS depending on the processor.[^cite_note-\:5-24][^cite_note-30] [Fonterra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fonterra) is the main processor of milk in New Zealand, processing 82 percent of all milk solids as of 2018. Other large dairy companies are Open Country Dairy (7.4%), [Synlait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synlait) and [Westland Milk Products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westland_Milk_Products) (3.4% each), Miraka (1.4%), Oceania Dairy (1.1%), and [Tatua Co-operative Dairy Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tatua_Co-operative_Dairy_Company) (0.7%).[^cite_note-31]

Only 3% of dairy production is consumed domestically, with the rest exported.[^cite_note-\:6-26] New Zealand is the world's largest exporter of whole milk powder and butter, and the third-largest exporter (behind the European Union and the United States) of skim milk powder and cheese.[^cite_note-32]

### Sheep farming

See also: [Sheep farming in New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheep_farming_in_New_Zealand)



Rural landscape with sheep

There were 26.82 million sheep in New Zealand as of June 2019.[^cite_note-\:4-21] By 2025 it had decreased to 23.6 million sheep. [^cite_note-\:10-33] This works out to 4.5 sheep for each person in New Zealand. [^cite_note-\:10-33] This is a very large decrease from 1982, when there were 22 sheep per capita. [^cite_note-\:10-33]The sheep population peaked at 70.3 million sheep in 1982 and has steadily declined ever since.[^cite_note-34]

In the 12 months to December 2020, 19.11 million lambs and 3.77 million adult sheep were processed, producing 362,250 tonnes of lamb and 97,300 tonnes of hogget and mutton.[^cite_note-\:9-35] 164,000 tonnes of clean wool was produced in 2006–7. Around 95% of sheep meat and 90% of wool production is exported, with the rest consumed domestically.[^cite_note-\:6-26] In 2019, domestic consumption of lamb and mutton was 3.6 kg (7.9 lb) per capita.[^cite_note-\:2-36]

### Beef farming

There were 3.89 million beef cattle in New Zealand as of June 2019.[^cite_note-\:4-21]

In the 12 months to December 2020, 1.59 million adult beef cattle and 1.15 million adult dairy cattle were processed, producing 698,380 tonnes of beef. In addition, 1.86 million calves and vealers were processed, producing 30,150 tonnes of veal.[^cite_note-\:9-35] Around 80% of beef and veal is exported, with the remaining 20% consumed domestically.[^cite_note-\:6-26] In 2019, domestic consumption of beef and veal was 11.6 kg (26 lb) per capita.[^cite_note-\:2-36] There are 7 million hectares of grassland available. [^cite_note-\:11-22]

### Pig farming

In the first half of the 20th century, pigs were often farmed alongside dairy cattle. Most dairy processors collected cream only, so dairy farmers separated the whole milk into cream and skim milk and fed the pigs the skim milk. In the 1950s and 60s, improved technology saw dairy processors switch to collecting whole milk. Pig farming subsequently became specialised and the majority of farms moved to grain-producing areas such as Canterbury.[^cite_note-37][^cite_note-\:7-29]

There were 255,900 pigs in New Zealand in June 2019.The amount had risen to approximately 620,000 by 2022. [^cite_note-\:12-38]Canterbury is by far the largest pig-farming region with 161,600 pigs, 63.1% of the national population.[^cite_note-\:4-21]

Pigs are usually kept indoors, either in [gestation crates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gestation_crate), [farrowing crates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farrowing_crate), fattening pens, or group housing.[^cite_note-39][^cite_note-\:12-38] 55% of the commercial herd is raised indoors.[^cite_note-\:12-38] 42% of the commercial herd are raised free farm.[^cite_note-\:12-38] 3% of the commercial herd is raised free range. [^cite_note-\:12-38]

In the 12 months to December 2020, 636,700 pigs were processed, producing 44,950 tonnes of meat.[^cite_note-\:9-35] In 2019, domestic consumption of pork, ham and bacon was 18.9 kg (42 lb) per capita.[^cite_note-\:2-36] Domestic production only meets around 45% of demand, with imported pork, ham and bacon, mainly from the European Union, North America and Australia, supplementing domestic supply. A small amount of meat is exported to supply nearby Pacific Island nations.[^cite_note-\:6-26]

### Poultry farming

There were 3.87 million laying hens in New Zealand in June 2022, producing 1,100 million eggs annually.[^cite_note-40]

Before the 1960s, chicken meat was largely a by-product of the egg industry; chickens for sale were generally cockerels or spent hens. The introduction of [broiler chickens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broiler) in the 1960s saw the [meat industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meat_industry) grow from 8,000 tonnes per year in 1962 to over 40,000 tonnes in the mid-1980s.[^cite_note-\:0-41] In the late 1990s, chicken overtook beef as the most-consumed meat in New Zealand.[^cite_note-\:6-26] In the 12 months to December 2020, 118.7 million chickens were raised for meat, producing 217,200 tonnes of chicken meat.[^cite_note-42]

Chickens account for over 98% of the country's poultry production, with [turkeys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey_as_food) and [ducks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duck_as_food) accounting for the majority of the rest. Around 500,000 turkeys and 200,000 ducks are sold per year, with 90% of turkeys sold in the weeks preceding Christmas.[^cite_note-\:6-26]

In 2019, domestic consumption of chicken and other poultry was 41.1 kg (91 lb) per capita.[^cite_note-\:2-36] Most of the poultry meat produced in New Zealand is consumed domestically. Due to biosecurity restrictions, importing poultry meat and eggs into New Zealand is prohibited.[^cite_note-\:6-26]

### Other pastoral farming

[Deer farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deer_farm) has increased dramatically from a herd of 150,000 in 1982 to 1.59 million in 2006, with 1,617 deer farms occupying 218,000 hectares of land in 2005.[^cite_note-Yearbook2008_p358-43] \$252 million of venison was exported in the year ending 30 September 2007. New Zealand is the largest exporter of farmed venison in the world.[^cite_note-Yearbook2008_p360-44] In the 1970s and 80s there was a huge industry carrying out live deer recovery from forested areas of New Zealand. The deer are a pest animal that has a negative impact on the [biodiversity of New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity_of_New_Zealand). The deer-farm stock was bred from the recovered wild animals.

Goats are also farmed for meat, milk, and mohair, and to control weeds.[^cite_note-Yearbook2008_p360-44]

## Horticulture



Vineyard in [Marlborough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marlborough_Region)

New Zealand has around 125,200 hectares (309,000 acres) of horticultural land. Total horticultural exports in 2019 were valued at \$6,200 million, of which \$4,938 million (79.6%) come from three products: [kiwifruit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiwifruit), [wine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wine), and [apples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple).[^cite_note-\:1-45]

### Fruit

See also: [Kiwifruit industry in New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiwifruit_industry_in_New_Zealand) and [New Zealand wine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_wine)

Fruit growing occupies around 68,300 ha (169,000 acres) of land as of 2017. The largest crops by planted area are [wine grapes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grape) (33,980 ha), [kiwifruit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiwifruit) (11,700 ha), [apples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple) (8,620 ha), [avocadoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avocado) (3,980 ha), [berries](52948.md) (2,320 ha), and [stone fruit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drupe) (2,140 ha).[^cite_note-\:1-45]

Wine grapes occupied 39,935 ha (98,680 acres) of land as of 2020, with the largest regions being [Marlborough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marlborough_wine_region) (27,808 ha), [Hawke's Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawke's_Bay_wine_region) (5,034 ha), and [Central Otago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Otago_wine_region) (1,930 ha). The largest varieties are [sauvignon blanc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sauvignon_blanc) (25,160 ha), [pinot noir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinot_noir) (5,642 ha), [chardonnay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chardonnay) (3,222 ha), [pinot gris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinot_gris) (2,593 ha) and [merlot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merlot) (1,087 ha).[^cite_note-46] Wine exports totalled \$1,807 million in 2019.[^cite_note-\:1-45]

Kiwifruit is primarily grown in the Bay of Plenty, especially around [Te Puke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Te_Puke), but is also grown in small quantities in the Northland, Auckland, Gisborne and Tasman regions.[^cite_note-47] The fruit is picked in the autumn (March to May) and kept in coolstore until sold or exported. The New Zealand kiwifruit season runs from April to December; during the off-season, kiwifruit is imported to fulfil domestic demand.[^cite_note-48] There are around 2,750 kiwifruit growers, producing 157.7 million trays (567,720 tonnes) in the year to June 2019. Around 545,800 tonnes of kiwifruit was exported in the same period worth \$2,302 million, making kiwifruit New Zealand's largest horticultural export by value.[^cite_note-\:1-45]

Apples are primarily grown in the Hawke's Bay and Tasman regions.[^cite_note-\:8-49] The two largest apple cultivars are [Royal Gala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gala_\(apple\)) and [Braeburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braeburn), followed by [Fuji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuji_\(apple\)), [Scifresh (Jazz)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz_\(apple\)), [Cripps Pink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cripps_Pink), Scired (Pacific Queen), and [Scilate (Envy)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Envy_\(apple\)). All except Fuji and Cripps Pink were developed in New Zealand from cross-breeding or, in the case of Braeburn, a chance seedling.[^cite_note-\:1-45][^cite_note-50] Around 12% of apples are consumed domestically, 28% are processed domestically (mainly into juice), and 60% are exported.[^cite_note-\:8-49] Around 395,000 tonnes of apples, worth \$829 million, were exported in the year to December 2019.[^cite_note-\:1-45]

Avocados are primarily grown in the subtropical areas of Northland and Bay of Plenty. Around 60% of the crop is exported, with \$104.3 million worth of avocadoes being exported in the year to December 2019.[^cite_note-51][^cite_note-\:1-45]

Stone fruit, including [peaches and nectarines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peach), [apricots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apricot), [plums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plum), and [cherries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherry), is primarily grown in Central Otago and Hawke's Bay. While apricots and cherries are exported, most stone fruit is consumed domestically.[^cite_note-52]

In 2019, fresh fruit exports totalled \$3,392 million while processed fruit exports (excluding wine) totalled \$138 million.[^cite_note-\:1-45]

### Vegetables

Outdoor vegetable growing occupies around 45,200 ha (112,000 acres) of land as of 2017, with Indoor vegetable growing occupying another 264 ha (650 acres). The largest crops by planted area are [potatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potato) (9,450 ha), [onions](51258.md) (6,010 ha), [squash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cucurbita) (5,790 ha), [peas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pea) and [beans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bean) (4,700 ha), [sweet corn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweet_corn) (3,870 ha), and [brassicas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brassica) (3,630 ha). The largest indoor crops are [tomatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomato) (84 ha) and [capsicums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_pepper) (61 ha).[^cite_note-\:1-45]

Auckland (namely [Pukekohe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pukekohe)), Manawatū-Whanganui (namely [Ohakune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohakune) and the [Horowhenua district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horowhenua_District)), and Canterbury are the major growing regions for potatoes, onions, brassicas (e.g. [cabbage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabbage), [broccoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broccoli) and [cauliflower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauliflower)), leafy vegetables (e.g. [lettuce](57079.md), [silverbeet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chard) and [spinach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinach)), and carrots and parsnips. Southland also grows a significant proportion of potatoes and carrots, and the [Matamata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matamata) area in Waikato and Hawke's Bay also grow a significant proportion of onions. Squash is mainly grown in Gisborne and Hawke's Bay. Sweet corn is mainly grown in Gisborne, Hawke's Bay, Marlborough and Canterbury. [Kūmara (sweet potato)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweet_potato) is almost exclusively grown in Northland.[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-54]

Due to their short shelf-life, most fresh vegetables are grown for domestic consumption and processing, with those exported mainly supplying nearby Pacific Island nations.[^cite_note-55] The largest vegetable exports are longer-life fresh vegetables such as onions and squash, along with processed vegetables such as [french fries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_fries) and [potato chips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potato_chip), and frozen and canned peas, beans and sweet corn. In 2019, fresh vegetable exports totalled \$304 million while processed vegetable exports totalled \$396 million.[^cite_note-\:1-45]

### Seeds and flowers

Seeds and flowers are primarily grown in Canterbury, Auckland, Otago and Southland. In 2019, New Zealand exported \$90 million of seeds, \$43 million of bulbs and live plants, and \$20 million of [cut flowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cut_flowers).[^cite_note-\:1-45]

## Arable crops

Almost all hay and silage is consumed on the same farm as it is produced. Most supplementary feed crops are grown in the South Island, where the colder climate forces additional feeding of stock during winter.

### Cereals



A field of maize in [Waipu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waipu,_New_Zealand)

Cereal crops occupies around 124,000 hectares (310,000 acres) of land as of June 2019. The largest crops by planted area are [barley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barley) (55,500 ha), [wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheat) (45,000 ha), [maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize) (16,700 ha) and [oats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oat) (2,100 ha).[^cite_note-56]

The majority of wheat, barley and oats is grown in the South Island, namely the Canterbury, Southland and Otago regions. Canterbury alone grows approximately 80-90% of the country's wheat, 68% of its barley and 60% of its oats. In contrast, almost all of the country's maize is grown in the North Island.[^cite_note-57]

Wheat, barley and oats are grown both for human consumption, [malting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malting), and for stock feed. Maize is grown as animal feed or for silage.[^cite_note-58]

## Forestry



Logging tracks in [Marlborough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marlborough_Region)

Main article: [Forestry in New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forestry_in_New_Zealand)

Milling of New Zealand's extensive native forests was one of the earliest industries in the settlement of the country. The long, straight hardwood from the [kauri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agathis) was ideal for ship masts and spars. As the new colony was established, timber was the most common building material, and vast areas of native forest were cleared. [Rimu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dacrydium_cupressinum), [tōtara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Podocarpus_totara), [matai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prumnopitys_taxifolia), and [miro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prumnopitys_ferruginea) were the favoured timbers. The Monterrey Pine, *[Pinus radiata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinus_radiata)* was introduced to New Zealand in the 1850s.[^cite_note-yearbook90-59] It thrived in the conditions, reaching maturity in 28 years, much faster than in its native California. It was found to grow well in the infertile acidic soil of the volcanic plateau, where attempts at agriculture had failed. The Government initiated planting of exotic forests in 1899 at [Whakarewarewa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whakarewarewa), near Rotorua. This was to address growing timber shortages as slow-growing native forests were exhausted.[^cite_note-60] In the 1930s, vast areas of land were planted in *Pinus radiata* by relief workers. The largest tract was the 188,000-hectare [Kāingaroa forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaingaroa_Forest), the largest plantation forest in the world. As the major forests matured, processing industries such as the [Kinleith Mill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinleith_Mill) at [Tokoroa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokoroa) and the [Tasman Mill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tasman_Mill) at [Kawerau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kawerau) were established.

Plantation forests of various sizes can now be found in all regions of New Zealand except Central Otago and Fiordland. In 2006 their total area was 1.8 million hectares, with 89% in *Pinus radiata* and 5% in Douglas fir (*[Pseudotsuga menziesii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudotsuga_menziesii)*)[^cite_note-Maf-61] Log harvesting in 2006 was 18.8 million m<sup>3</sup>, down from 22.5 million m<sup>3</sup> in 2003. This is projected to rise as high as 30 million m<sup>3</sup> as newer forests mature. The value of all forestry exports (logs, chips, sawn timber, panels and paper products) for the year ended 31 March 2006 was \$NZ 3.62 billion. This is projected to rise to \$4.65 billion by 2011. Australia accounts for just over 25% of export value, mostly paper products, followed by Japan, South Korea, China and the United States.[^cite_note-Maf-61] Within the New Zealand economy, forestry accounts for approximately 4% of national GDP. On the global stage, the New Zealand forestry industry is a relatively small contributor in terms of production, accounting for 1% of global wood supply for industrial purposes.[^cite_note-MAF-overview-62]

## Aquaculture



[Mussel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mussel) long line in [Pelorus Sound / Te Hoiere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelorus_Sound_/_Te_Hoiere)

Main article: [Aquaculture in New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquaculture_in_New_Zealand)

[Aquaculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquaculture) started in New Zealand in the late 1960s and is dominated by mussels, oysters and salmon. In 2007, aquaculture generated about NZ\$360 million in sales on an area of 7,700 hectares with a total of \$240 million earned in exports. In 2006, the aquaculture industry in New Zealand developed a strategy aimed at achieving a sustainable annual billion NZ dollar business by 2025. In 2007, the government reacted by offering more support to the growing industry.

## Beekeeping

Main article: [Beekeeping in New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beekeeping_in_New_Zealand)

New Zealand had 2,602 beekeepers at the end of 2007, who owned 313,399 hives. Total honey production was 9700 tonnes. Pollen, beeswax, and [propolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propolis) are also produced. Beekeepers provide pollination services to horticulturalists, which generates more income than the products of bee culture. Approximately 20–25,000 queen bees, and 20 tonnes of packaged bees (which include worker bees and a queen) are exported live each year.[^cite_note-63]

## Environmental issues

See also: [Environmental issues with agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_issues_with_agriculture), [Environmental impact of meat production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_of_meat_production), and [Water pollution in New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_pollution_in_New_Zealand)

Both the original [Māori people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ori_people) and the European colonists made huge changes to New Zealand over a relatively short time. Māori burned forest to flush out [game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_\(food\)) and to encourage the growth of [bracken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pteridium_esculentum) fern, which was used as a food source, and practised agriculture using plants they brought from tropical Polynesia. The Europeans logged and burned off a third of the forest cover to convert land to pastoral farming.

In 1993, the [National Institute of Water and Atmospheric Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_of_Water_and_Atmospheric_Research) summarised available data on the quality of water in rivers. They concluded that "lowland river reaches in agriculturally developed catchments are in poor condition" reflecting "agriculturally derived diffuse and point source waste inputs in isolation or in addition to urban or industrial waste inputs". The key contaminants identified in lowland rivers were dissolved inorganic nitrogen, dissolved reactive phosphorus and faecal contamination. Small streams in dairy farming areas were identified as being in very poor condition.[^cite_note-smith-64] New Zealand's rivers and lakes are becoming increasingly [nutrient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutrient) enriched and degraded by [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen), animal faecal matter, and eroded [sediment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sediment).[^cite_note-Oram,_R._2007-65] Many waterways are now unsafe for swimming. [Fish and Game New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish_and_Game_New_Zealand) launched a "[dirty dairying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirty_dairying)" campaign to highlight the effect of intensive agriculture on waterways. [Fonterra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fonterra), the largest dairy company in New Zealand, in conjunction with government agencies responded with the [Dairying and Clean Streams Accord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dairying_and_Clean_Streams_Accord). In 2009, the [Crafar Farms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crafar_Farms) group of dairy farms in the North Island became known as the 'poster boys for dirty dairying' after a string of prosecutions in the [Environment Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environment_Court) for unlawful discharges of dairy effluent.[^cite_note-66][^cite_note-67]

In 2004 the [Parliamentary Commissioner for the Environment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_Commissioner_for_the_Environment) released a report on the environmental effects of farming in New Zealand.[^cite_note-68] It noted that the trend was towards an increasing pressure on New Zealand's [natural capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_capital). Between 1994 and 2002 the number of [dairy cows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dairy_cows) increased by 34% and the land area used grew by just 12% resulting in a more intensive land use. In the same period [synthetic fertiliser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_fertiliser) use across all sectors grew by 21% and [urea](31734.md) use grew by 160%.

Almost half of the [greenhouse gas emissions](9528025.md) in New Zealand are due to the agricultural sector. A portion of this is due to [methane](18582230.md) from belching [ruminants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruminant). An [agricultural emissions research levy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_emissions_research_levy) was proposed, quickly becoming known as the "Fart Tax".[^cite_note-scoop-69] The proposed levy encountered opposition from the farming sector and the [National Party](333149.md), resulting in plans for the levy being abandoned. The [Pastoral Greenhouse Gas Research Consortium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastoral_Greenhouse_Gas_Research_Consortium) was formed as an alternative to imposing the levy on farmers.

### Organic farming

Main article: [Organic farming in New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_farming_in_New_Zealand)

[Organic farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_farming) practices began on a commercial scale in the 1980s and is now an increasing segment of the market with some of the larger companies such as [Wattie's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wattie's) becoming involved.

## Agricultural pests

See also: [Invasive species in New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasive_species_in_New_Zealand)

A number of plant and animal introductions into New Zealand has reduced the income from farming. Tight border controls to improve [biosecurity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biosecurity) have been put into place to ensure any new and unwanted pests and diseases do not enter the country. Monitoring is done around sea and airports to check for any incursions.

### Animal pests

The [common brushtail possum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_brushtail_possum_in_New_Zealand) was introduced from Australia to establish a fur trade. It soon became one of New Zealand's most problematic [invasive species](67554.md) because of the huge effect on the [biodiversity of New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity_of_New_Zealand), as well affecting agricultural production, as it is a vector for [bovine tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bovine_tuberculosis). The disease is now endemic in possums across approximately 38 per cent of New Zealand (known as 'vector risk areas'). In these areas, nearly 70 per cent of new herd infections can be traced back to possums or ferrets. The [Biosecurity Act 1993](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biosecurity_Act_1993), which established a National Pest Management Strategy, is the legislation behind control of the disease in New Zealand. The [Animal Health Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_Health_Board_\(New_Zealand\)) (AHB) operates a nationwide programme of cattle testing and possum control with the goal of eradicating *M. bovis* from wild vector species across 2.5 million hectares – or one quarter – of New Zealand's at-risk areas by 2026 and, eventually, eradicating the disease entirely.[^cite_note-70]

Possums are controlled through a combination of [trapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trapping), ground-baiting and, where other methods are impractical, aerial treatment with [1080 poison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_fluoroacetate).[^cite_note-71]

From 1979 to 1984, possum control was stopped due to lack of funding. In spite of regular and frequent TB testing of cattle herds, the number of infected herds snowballed and continued to increase until 1994.[^cite_note-72] The area of New Zealand where there were TB wild animals expanded from about 10 to 40 per cent.

That possums are such effective transmitters of TB appears to be facilitated by their behaviour once they succumb to the disease. Terminally ill TB possums will show increasingly erratic behaviour, such as venturing out during the daytime to get enough food to eat, and seeking out buildings in which to keep warm. As a consequence they may wander onto paddocks, where they naturally attract the attention of inquisitive cattle and deer. This behaviour has been captured on video.[^cite_note-73]

The introduced [Canada goose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canada_goose_in_New_Zealand) became prolific and began to adversely affect pastures and crops. In 2011 restrictions on hunting them were dropped to allow them to be culled.

### Plant pests

[Gorse was introduced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gorse_in_New_Zealand) as a hedgerow plant but has become the most expensive agricultural plant pest costing millions of dollars in efforts to control its spread over farmland.

Other serious pasture and crop land plant pests are nodding thistle (*[Carduus nutans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carduus_nutans)*), Californian thistle (*[Cirsium arvense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cirsium_arvense)*), ragwort (*[Senecio jacobaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senecio_jacobaea)*), broom (*[Cytisus scoparius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cytisus_scoparius)*), giant buttercup (*[Ranunculus acris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranunculus_acris)*), fat-hen (*[Chenopodium album](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chenopodium_album)*), willow weed (*[Polygonum persicaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygonum_persicaria)*), and hawkweed (*[Hieracium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hieracium)* species).[^cite_note-74]

## Biosecurity

Main article: [Biosecurity in New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biosecurity_in_New_Zealand)

Because of its geographical isolation New Zealand is free of some pest and diseases that are problematic for agricultural production in other countries. With a high level of international trade and large numbers of inbound tourists [biosecurity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biosecurity) is of great importance since any new pest or diseases brought into the country could potentially have a huge effect on the [economy of New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_New_Zealand).

There have been no outbreaks of [foot-and-mouth disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foot-and-mouth_disease) in New Zealand. If an outbreak did occur there is potential for severe economic losses given that agricultural exports are a large segment of exports.[^cite_note-75] New Zealand has strict biosecurity[^cite_note-76] measures in place to prevent the introduction of unwanted pests and diseases.

In 2017, some cattle near [Oamaru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oamaru) in the South Island were found to be [Mycoplasma bovis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycoplasma_bovis) positive, see [2017 Mycoplasma bovis outbreak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_Mycoplasma_bovis_outbreak).

## Tenure review

Main article: [Tenure review (South Island)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenure_review_\(South_Island\))

Many areas of the high country of the South Island were set up as large sheep and cattle stations in the late 19th century. Much of this land was leased from [The Crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Crown) but after the passing of the [Crown Pastoral Land Act 1998](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_Pastoral_Land_Act_1998) the leases were reviewed. Environmentalists and academics raised concerns about the process saying that farmers were gaining an advantage and that conservation issues were not being resolved. Farmers were concerned that environmentalists and academics used the tenure review process to lock land up for conservation purposes without regard to the property rights of farmers or planning for how to manage that land in the future, and much land has been degraded by pests and weeds since it was retired from farming.

## Policy, promotion and politics

The [Ministry for Primary Industries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_for_Primary_Industries_\(New_Zealand\)) (MPI), the government agency responsible for the agricultural sector, has both policy and operational arms.

[Federated Farmers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federated_Farmers), a lobby group, represents farmers' interests. It has a voluntary membership which in April 2021 stood at 13,000 members.[^cite_note-77]

The [Soil & Health Association of New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_&_Health_Association_of_New_Zealand), established in 1941, promotes organic food and farming.

The [New Zealand Young Farmers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Young_Farmers), a national organisation formed in 1927 with regional clubs throughout the country, runs the annual Young Farmer Contest.

Irrigation New Zealand, a national body representing farmers who use [irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrigation) as well as the irrigation industry, opposes [water conservation orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_conservation_order).[^cite_note-78]

## Foreign ownership

Almost 180,000 hectares of farming land was purchased or leased by foreign interests between 2010 and 2021. The United States is the biggest nation owning land in New Zealand, China is second.[^cite_note-79][^cite_note-80]

There is opposition to foreign ownership in New Zealand, The populist [New Zealand First](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_First) party is the largest party opposed to foreign ownership. In a 2011 Poll found that 82% believed foreign ownership of farms and agriculture land was a "bad thing". Only 10% believed it a "good thing" and 8% were unsure.[^cite_note-81]

There are two main views on the immediate future of New Zealand agriculture. One is that, due to fast-rising consumer demand in India and China, the world is entering a golden age for commodities, and New Zealand is well placed to take advantage of this. The other view is that New Zealand will only gain limited rewards from this boom because of increasing production competition from developing countries. For New Zealand to remain competitive, farmers will either have to intensify production to remain commodity producers (increasing stock and fertiliser per hectare) or, instead, become producers of higher value, more customised products.[^cite_note-Oram,_R._2007-65]

[AgResearch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AgResearch) Ltd (New Zealand's largest [Crown Research Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_Research_Institute)) believes that new technologies will allow New Zealand farmers to double their output by 2020, while simultaneously reducing greenhouse-gas emissions and other detrimental environmental impacts associated with farming practices.[^cite_note-Oram,_R._2007-65]

Joint research by [Healthier Lives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Healthier_Lives) and [Our Land and Water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Our_Land_and_Water) examined agricultural scenarios aimed at optimising a healthy diet while minimising nitrate and phosphate water pollution. They suggested that New Zealand could meet its environmental goals by changing land use in parts of the country that would otherwise be unable to meet water-quality targets, for example as a result of agricultural contamination. This research was included in a Ministerial briefing to support a New Zealand National Food Strategy.[^cite_note-82]

Joint research by [Our Land and Water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Our_Land_and_Water) and [Deep South](https://deepsouthchallenge.co.nz/) identified vulnerabilities for the main farming systems in New Zealand under projected climate scenarios. Results showed a general pattern of climate suitability for crops moving south, and animal health issues intensifying and also moving south.[^cite_note-83] This information is available at the [Data Supermarket](https://landuseopportunities.nz/).

## Impact on New Zealand culture

Rural New Zealand has affected the culture of New Zealand.

*[Country Calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Country_Calendar)* is a factual television programme about farming methods and country life, and is watched by both rural and urban New Zealanders. The show first premièred on 6 March 1966, and is the country's longest-running locally made television series.[^cite_note-84]

The [gumboot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gumboot), a waterproof boot commonly used by farmers and others, is a cultural icon with Taihape hosting an annual [Gumboot Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gumboot_Day). [Fred Dagg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Dagg), a comedy character created by [John Clarke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Clarke_\(satirist\)), was a stereotypical farmer wearing a black singlet, shorts and gumboots.

[Number 8 wire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_8_wire) is used for fencing and has become part of the cultural lexicon. It is used for all manner of tasks and it describes the [do it yourself](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Do_it_yourself) mentality of New Zealanders.

### Agricultural and Pastoral shows

A fixture in many rural towns, the annual Agricultural and Pastoral (A\&P) show[^cite_note-85] organises competitions for the best livestock and farm produce. Carnivals, sideshows, equestrian events and craft competitions also take place in association with A\&P shows.
