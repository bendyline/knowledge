The **Indian Ocean** is the third-largest of the world's five [oceanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean) divisions, covering 70,560,000 km<sup>2</sup> (27,240,000 sq mi) or approximately 20% of the water area of [Earth's surface](9228.md#Surface).[^cite_note-NOAA-volume-4] It is bounded by Asia to the north, Africa to the west and [Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australia_\(continent\)) to the east. To the south it is bounded by the [Southern Ocean](20611428.md) or [Antarctica](18959138.md), depending on the definition in use.[^cite_note-5] The Indian Ocean has large [marginal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marginal_sea) or regional seas, including the [Andaman Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andaman_Sea), the [Arabian Sea](2747.md), the [Bay of Bengal](47892.md), and the [Laccadive Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laccadive_Sea).

Geologically, the Indian Ocean is the youngest of the oceans (being directly preceded by the [Tethys Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tethys_Ocean), which fragmented into the [Mediterranean Sea](19006.md) and the Indian Ocean only 20 million years ago),[^cite_note-\:12-6] and it has distinct features such as narrow [continental shelves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_shelf). Its average depth is 3,741 m.[^cite_note-7] It is the warmest ocean, with a significant impact on [global climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_climate) due to its interaction with the atmosphere. Its waters are affected by the Indian Ocean [Walker circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walker_circulation), resulting in unique oceanic currents and upwelling patterns. The Indian Ocean is ecologically diverse, with important ecosystems such as coral reefs, mangroves, and seagrass beds.[^cite_note-\:3-8] It hosts a significant portion of the world's tuna catch and is home to endangered marine species. The climate around the Indian Ocean is characterized by [monsoons](57630.md).[^cite_note-\:3-8]

The Indian Ocean has been a hub of cultural and commercial exchange since ancient times. It played a key role in early human migrations and the spread of civilizations. In modern times, it remains crucial for global trade, especially in oil and hydrocarbons.[^cite_note-\:4-9] Environmental and geopolitical concerns in the region include [climate change](5042951.md), [overfishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overfishing), [pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollution), [piracy](50715.md), and disputes over island territories.[^cite_note-\:4-9]

## Etymology

The Indian Ocean has been known by its present name since at least 1515, when the Latin form *Oceanus Orientalis Indicus* (lit. 'Indian Eastern Ocean') is attested, named after [India](14533.md), which [projects into it](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_peninsula). It was earlier known as the *Eastern Ocean*, a term that was still in use during the mid-18th century, as opposed to the *Western Ocean* ([Atlantic](698.md)) before the [Pacific](23070.md) was [surmised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surmise).[^cite_note-HarperIndianOcean-10] The modern [Hindi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindi) name for the Ocean is हिंद महासागर (*Hind Mahāsāgar*; lit. transl. Ocean of [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindustan)).

Conversely, [Chinese explorers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ming_treasure_voyages) (e.g., [Zheng He](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zheng_He) during the [Ming dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ming_dynasty)) who traveled to the Indian Ocean during the 15th century called it the *Western Oceans*.[^cite_note-11]

In [Ancient Greek geography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Graeco-Roman_geographers), the Indian Ocean region known to the Greeks was called the [Erythraean Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erythraean_Sea).[^cite_note-Periplus-12]

In traditional Indian geography, the ocean is historically called the *Mahodadhi* ([Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_language): [महोदधि](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%B9%E0%A5%8B%E0%A4%A6%E0%A4%A7%E0%A4%BF#Sanskrit), [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Sanskrit): *Mahōdadhi*, [lit.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literal_translation) 'ocean, big sea'), divided into the two seas *Purvapayodhi* ([Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_language): [पूर्वपयोधि](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E0%A4%AA%E0%A5%82%E0%A4%B0%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%B5%E0%A4%AA%E0%A4%AF%E0%A5%8B%E0%A4%A7%E0%A4%BF#Sanskrit), [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Sanskrit): *Pūrvapayōdhi*, [lit.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literal_translation) 'sunrise', referring to the [Bay of Bengal](47892.md)) and *Apara* ([Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_language): [अपर](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E0%A4%85%E0%A4%AA%E0%A4%B0#Sanskrit), [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Sanskrit): *Apara*, [lit.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literal_translation) 'sunset', referring to the [Arabian Sea](2747.md)).[^cite_note-13] Another name used for the Indian Ocean is *Ratnakara* ([Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_language): [रत्नाकर](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E0%A4%B0%E0%A4%A4%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%A8%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%95%E0%A4%B0#Sanskrit), [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Sanskrit): *Ratnākara*, [lit.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literal_translation) 'one having jewels, mine of jewels, repository of jewels').[^cite_note-14]

### Extent and data

The [borders of the Indian Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borders_of_the_oceans#Indian_Ocean), as delineated by the [International Hydrographic Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Hydrographic_Organization) in 1953, included the [Southern Ocean](20611428.md) but not the marginal seas along the northern rim.[^cite_note-15] In 2002 the IHO delimited the Southern Ocean separately, which removed waters south of 60°S from the Indian Ocean but included the northern marginal seas.[^cite_note-IHO-2002-16]

[Meridionally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridional), the Indian Ocean is delimited from the Atlantic Ocean by the [20° east meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/20th_meridian_east), running south from [Cape Agulhas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Agulhas), South Africa, and from the Pacific Ocean by the meridian of 146°49'E, running south from [South East Cape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_East_Cape) on the island of [Tasmania](29944.md) in Australia. The northernmost extent of the Indian Ocean (including marginal seas) is approximately [30°N](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/30th_parallel_north) in the [Persian Gulf](24761.md).[^cite_note-IHO-2002-16]

The Indian Ocean covers 70,560,000 km<sup>2</sup> (27,240,000 sq mi), including the [Red Sea](26191.md) and the Persian Gulf but excluding the Southern Ocean, or 19.5% of the world's oceans. Its volume is 264,000,000 km<sup>3</sup> (63,000,000 cu mi) or 19.8% of the world's oceans' volume; it has an average depth of 3,741 m (12,274 ft) and a maximum depth of 7,290 m (23,920 ft).[^cite_note-NOAA-volume-4]

All of the Indian Ocean is in the [Eastern Hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Hemisphere). The centre of the Eastern Hemisphere, the [90th meridian east](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/90th_meridian_east), passes through the [Ninety East Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ninety_East_Ridge).

Within these waters are a number of islands. These include those controlled by surrounding countries, and independent island states and territories. Of the non-coastal islands, there are two broad clusters: one around Madagascar, and one south of India. A few other oceanic islands are scattered elsewhere.[^cite_note-Bergin2019-17]<sup>: 29–30 </sup>

### Coasts and shelves

In contrast to the Atlantic and Pacific, the Indian Ocean is enclosed by major landmasses and an archipelago on three sides and does not stretch from pole to pole, and can be likened to an embayed ocean. It is centered on the Indian Peninsula. Although this subcontinent has played a significant role in its history, the Indian Ocean has foremostly been a cosmopolitan stage, interlinking diverse regions by innovations, trade, and religion since early in human history.[^cite_note-Prange-2008-p1382-18]

The [active margins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_margin) of the Indian Ocean have an average width (horizontal distance from land to shelf break[^cite_note-19]) of 19 ± 0.61 km (11.81 ± 0.38 mi) with a maximum width of 175 km (109 mi). The [passive margins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passive_margin) have an average width of 47.6 ± 0.8 km (29.58 ± 0.50 mi).[^cite_note-20] The average width of the [slopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_slope) (horizontal distance from shelf break to foot of slope) of the continental shelves are 50.4–52.4 km (31.3–32.6 mi) for active and passive margins respectively, with a maximum width of 205.3–255.2 km (127.6–158.6 mi).[^cite_note-21]

In correspondence of the [Shelf break](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shelf_break), also known as Hinge zone, the [Bouguer gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bouguer_gravity) ranges from 0 to 30 [mGals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gal_\(unit\)) that is unusual for a continental region of around 16 km thick sediments. It has been hypothesized that the "Hinge zone may represent the relict of continental and proto-oceanic crustal boundary formed during the rifting of India from [Antarctica](18959138.md#Geography)."[^cite_note-1365-246X_208_3-22]

Australia, Indonesia, and India are the three countries with the longest shorelines and [exclusive economic zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exclusive_economic_zone). The continental shelf makes up 15% of the Indian Ocean. More than two billion people live in countries bordering the Indian Ocean, compared to 1.7 billion for the Atlantic and 2.7 billion for the Pacific (some countries border more than one ocean).[^cite_note-KeeIrv-2005-2]

#### Rivers

The Indian Ocean [drainage basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage_basin) covers 21,100,000 km<sup>2</sup> (8,100,000 sq mi), similar to that of the Pacific Ocean and half that of the Atlantic basin, or 30% of its ocean surface (compared to 15% for the Pacific). The Indian Ocean drainage basin is divided into roughly 800 individual basins, half that of the Pacific, of which 50% are located in Asia, 30% in Africa, and 20% in Australasia. The rivers of the Indian Ocean are shorter on average (740 km (460 mi)) than those of the other major oceans. The largest rivers are ([order 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strahler_number)) the [Zambezi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zambezi), [Ganges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ganges_River)-[Brahmaputra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmaputra), [Indus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_River), [Jubba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jubba_River), and [Murray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murray_River) rivers and (order 4) the [Shatt al-Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shatt_al-Arab), [Wadi Ad Dawasir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wadi_Ad_Dawasir) (a dried-out river system on the Arabian Peninsula) and [Limpopo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limpopo_River) rivers.[^cite_note-23] After the breakup of East [Gondwana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gondwana) and the formation of the Himalayas, the Ganges-Brahmaputra rivers flow into the world's largest delta known as the [Bengal delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengal_delta) or [Sunderbans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunderbans).[^cite_note-1365-246X_208_3-22]

### Marginal seas

[Marginal seas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marginal_seas), gulfs, bays and straits of the Indian Ocean include:[^cite_note-IHO-2002-16]

Along the east coast of Africa, the [Mozambique Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mozambique_Channel) separates [Madagascar](18964.md) from mainland Africa, while the [Sea of Zanj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_of_Zanj) is located north of Madagascar.

On the northern coast of the [Arabian Sea](2747.md), [Gulf of Aden](58716.md) is connected to the [Red Sea](26191.md) by the strait of [Bab-el-Mandeb](62138.md). In the Gulf of Aden, the [Gulf of Tadjoura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Tadjoura) is located in Djibouti and the [Guardafui Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guardafui_Channel) separates Socotra island from the Horn of Africa. The northern end of the Red Sea terminates in the [Gulf of Aqaba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Aqaba) and [Gulf of Suez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Suez). The Indian Ocean is artificially connected to the [Mediterranean Sea](19006.md) without ship lock through the [Suez Canal](29323.md), which is accessible via the Red Sea. The Arabian Sea is connected to the [Persian Gulf](24761.md) by the [Gulf of Oman](12896.md) and the [Strait of Hormuz](29005.md). In the Persian Gulf, the [Gulf of Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Bahrain) separates Qatar from the [Arabian Peninsula](47858.md).

Along the west coast of India, the [Gulf of Kutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Kutch) and [Gulf of Khambat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Khambat) are located in Gujarat in the northern end while the [Laccadive Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laccadive_Sea) separates the Maldives from the southern tip of India. The [Bay of Bengal](47892.md) is off the east coast of India. The [Gulf of Mannar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Mannar) and the [Palk Strait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palk_Strait) separate Sri Lanka from India, while [Adam's Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam's_Bridge) separates the two. The [Andaman Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andaman_Islands) are between the Bay of Bengal and the [Andaman Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andaman_Sea).

In Indonesia, the so-called [Indonesian Seaway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology_of_Indonesia#Indonesian_Seaway) is composed of the [Java sea](467143.md), [Malacca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malacca_Strait), [Sunda](177179.md) and [Torres Straits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torres_Strait). The [Gulf of Carpentaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Carpentaria) is located on the Australian north coast while the [Great Australian Bight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Australian_Bight) constitutes a large part of its southern coast.[^cite_note-24][^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26]

## Climate



During summer, warm continental masses draw moist air from the Indian Ocean, producing heavy rainfall. The process is reversed during winter, resulting in dry conditions.

Several features make the Indian Ocean unique. It constitutes the core of the large-scale [Tropical Warm Pool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_Warm_Pool) which, when interacting with the atmosphere, affects the climate both regionally and globally. Asia blocks heat export and prevents the ventilation of the Indian Ocean [thermocline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermocline). Asia also drives the Indian Ocean [monsoon](57630.md), the strongest on Earth, which causes large-scale seasonal variations in ocean currents, including the reversal of the [Somali Current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_Current) and [Indian Monsoon Current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Monsoon_Current). Because of the Indian Ocean [Walker circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walker_circulation), there are no continuous equatorial easterlies. [Upwelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upwelling) occurs near the [Horn of Africa](62142.md) and the [Arabian Peninsula](47858.md) in the [Northern Hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Hemisphere) and north of the trade winds in the Southern Hemisphere. The [Indonesian Throughflow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesian_Throughflow) is a unique Equatorial connection to the Pacific.[^cite_note-27]

The climate north of the [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator) is affected by a [monsoon](57630.md) climate. Strong north-east winds blow from October until April; from May until October, south and west winds prevail. In the Arabian Sea, the violent Monsoon brings rain to the Indian subcontinent. In the southern hemisphere, the winds are generally milder, but summer storms near Mauritius can be severe. When the monsoon winds change, cyclones sometimes strike the shores of the [Arabian Sea](2747.md) and the [Bay of Bengal](47892.md).[^cite_note-oceanographer-2001-28]

Some 80% of the total annual rainfall in India occurs during summer and the region is so dependent on this rainfall that many civilisations perished when the Monsoon failed in the past. The huge variability in the Indian Summer Monsoon has also occurred pre-historically, with a strong, wet phase 33,500–32,500 BP; a weak, dry phase 26,000–23,500 BC; and a very weak phase 17,000–15,000 BP, corresponding to a series of dramatic global events: [Bølling–Allerød warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%B8lling%E2%80%93Aller%C3%B8d_warming), [Heinrich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_event), and [Younger Dryas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Younger_Dryas).[^cite_note-29]



Air pollution in the [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent) spread over the Bay of Bengal and beyond.

The Indian Ocean is the warmest ocean in the world.[^cite_note-30] Long-term records from 1901 to 2012 show rapid, continuous warming in the Indian Ocean, with the Indo-Pacific warm pool region warming by approximately 1.2 °C (2.2 °F).[^cite_note-\:0-31] Research indicates that human induced [greenhouse warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_warming), and changes in the frequency and magnitude of [El Niño](317311.md) (or the [Indian Ocean Dipole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean_Dipole)), events are a trigger to this strong warming in the Indian Ocean.[^cite_note-\:0-31] While the Indian Ocean warmed at a rate of 1.2 °C per century between 1950–2020, climate models predict accelerated warming, at a rate of 1.7 °C (3.1 °F)–3.8 °C (6.8 °F) per century during 2020–2100.[^cite_note-\:1-32][^cite_note-\:2-33]

Though the warming is basin-wide, maximum warming is in the northwestern Indian Ocean including the Arabian Sea, and reduced warming off the Sumatra and Java coasts in the southeast Indian Ocean. [Global warming](5042951.md) is projected to push the tropical Indian Ocean into a basin-wide near-permanent heatwave state by the end of the 21st century, where marine heatwaves are projected to increase from 20 days per year (during 1970–2000) to 220–250 days per year.[^cite_note-\:1-32][^cite_note-\:2-33]

South of the Equator (20–5°S), the Indian Ocean is gaining heat from June to October, during the austral winter, while it is losing heat from November to March, during the austral summer.[^cite_note-34]

In 1999, the [Indian Ocean Experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean_Experiment) showed that fossil fuel and biomass burning in South and Southeast Asia caused air pollution (also known as the [Asian brown cloud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_brown_cloud)) that reached as far as the [Intertropical Convergence Zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intertropical_Convergence_Zone). This pollution has implications on both a local and global scale.[^cite_note-35]

## Oceanography

Forty percent of the sediment of the Indian Ocean is found in the Indus and Ganges fans. The oceanic basins adjacent to the continental slopes mostly contain terrigenous sediments. The ocean south of the [polar front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_front) (roughly [50° south latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/50th_parallel_south)) is high in biologic productivity and dominated by non-stratified sediment composed mostly of [siliceous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siliceous) [oozes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelagic_sediment#Oozes). Near the three major mid-ocean ridges the ocean floor is relatively young and therefore bare of sediment, except for the [Southwest Indian Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwest_Indian_Ridge) due to its ultra-slow spreading rate.[^cite_note-36]

The ocean's [currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_current) are mainly controlled by the monsoon. Two large [gyres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyre), one in the northern hemisphere flowing clockwise and one south of the equator moving anticlockwise (including the [Agulhas Current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agulhas_Current) and [Agulhas Return Current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agulhas_Return_Current)), constitute the dominant flow pattern. During the winter monsoon (November–February), however, circulation is reversed north of 30°S and winds are weakened during winter and the transitional periods between the monsoons.[^cite_note-37]

The Indian Ocean contains the largest [submarine fans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_fan) of the world, the [Bengal Fan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengal_Fan) and [Indus Fan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Fan), and the largest areas of [slope terraces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_terrace) and [rift valleys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rift_valley).[^cite_note-38]

The inflow of deep water into the Indian Ocean is 11 [Sv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sverdrup), most of which comes from the [Circumpolar Deep Water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumpolar_Deep_Water) (CDW). The CDW enters the Indian Ocean through the [Crozet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crozet_Islands) and [Madagascar](18964.md) basins and crosses the [Southwest Indian Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwest_Indian_Ridge) at 30°S. In the [Mascarene Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mascarene_Basin) the CDW becomes a deep [western boundary current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_boundary_current) before it is met by a re-circulated branch of itself, the North Indian Deep Water. This mixed water partly flows north into the [Somali Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_Basin?action=edit\&redlink=1) whilst most of it flows clockwise in the Mascarene Basin where an oscillating flow is produced by [Rossby waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rossby_wave#Oceanic_waves).[^cite_note-39]

Water circulation in the Indian Ocean is dominated by the Subtropical Anticyclonic Gyre, the eastern extension of which is blocked by the Southeast Indian Ridge and the 90°E Ridge. Madagascar and the Southwest Indian Ridge separate three cells south of Madagascar and off South Africa. [North Atlantic Deep Water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Atlantic_Deep_Water) reaches into the Indian Ocean south of Africa at a depth of 2,000–3,000 m (6,600–9,800 ft) and flows north along the eastern continental slope of Africa. Deeper than NADW, [Antarctic Bottom Water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctic_Bottom_Water) flows from [Enderby Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enderby_Plain) to [Agulhas Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agulhas_Basin) across deep channels (<4,000 m (13,000 ft)) in the Southwest Indian Ridge, from where it continues into the [Mozambique Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mozambique_Channel) and [Prince Edward fracture zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Edward_fracture_zone).[^cite_note-Rogers-p5-40]

North of [20° south latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/20th_parallel_south) the minimum surface temperature is 22 °C (72 °F), exceeding 28 °C (82 °F) to the east. Southward of [40° south latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/40th_parallel_south), temperatures drop quickly.[^cite_note-oceanographer-2001-28]

The [Bay of Bengal](47892.md) contributes more than half (2,950 km<sup>3</sup> or 710 cu mi) of the [runoff water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_runoff) to the Indian Ocean. Mainly in summer, this runoff flows into the Arabian Sea but also south across the Equator where it mixes with fresher seawater from the [Indonesian Throughflow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesian_Throughflow). This mixed freshwater joins the [South Equatorial Current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Equatorial_Current) in the southern tropical Indian Ocean.[^cite_note-41]

[Sea surface salinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salinity#Seawater) is highest (more than 36 [PSU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salinity#PSU)) in the Arabian Sea because evaporation exceeds precipitation there. In the Southeast Arabian Sea salinity drops to less than 34 PSU. It is the lowest (c. 33 PSU) in the Bay of Bengal because of river runoff and precipitation. The Indonesian Throughflow and precipitation results in lower salinity (34 PSU) along the Sumatran west coast. Monsoonal variation results in eastward transportation of saltier water from the Arabian Sea to the Bay of Bengal from June to September and in westerly transport by the East India Coastal Current to the Arabian Sea from January to April.[^cite_note-42]

An [Indian Ocean garbage patch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean_garbage_patch) was discovered in 2010 covering at least 5 million square kilometres (1.9 million square miles). Riding the southern [Indian Ocean Gyre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean_Gyre), this vortex of [plastic garbage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_pollution) constantly circulates the ocean from Australia to Africa, down the [Mozambique Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mozambique_Channel), and back to Australia in a period of six years, except for debris that gets indefinitely stuck in the centre of the gyre.[^cite_note-43] The garbage patch in the Indian Ocean will, according to a 2012 study, decrease in size after several decades to vanish completely over centuries. Over several millennia, however, the global system of garbage patches will accumulate in the North Pacific.[^cite_note-44]

There are two [amphidromes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphidromic_point) of opposite rotation in the Indian Ocean, probably caused by [Rossby wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rossby_wave) propagation.[^cite_note-45]

[Icebergs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iceberg) drift as far north as [55° south latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/55th_parallel_south), similar to the Pacific but less than in the Atlantic where icebergs reach up to 45°S. The volume of iceberg loss in the Indian Ocean between 2004 and 2012 was 24 [Gt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gigatonne).[^cite_note-46]

Since the 1960s, [anthropogenic warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropogenic_climate_change) of the global ocean combined with contributions of freshwater from retreating land ice causes a global rise in sea level. Sea level also increases in the Indian Ocean, except in the south tropical Indian Ocean where it decreases, a pattern most likely caused by rising levels of [greenhouse gases](21350772.md).[^cite_note-47]

## Marine life







A dolphin off Western Australia and a swarm of [surgeonfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acanthurus_leucosternon) near Maldives Islands represents the well-known, exotic fauna of the warmer parts of the Indian Ocean. King Penguins on a beach in the [Crozet Archipelago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crozet_Islands) near Antarctica attract fewer tourists.

Among the tropical oceans, the western Indian Ocean hosts one of the largest concentrations of [phytoplankton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phytoplankton) blooms in summer, due to the strong [monsoon](57630.md) winds. The monsoonal wind forcing leads to a strong coastal and open ocean [upwelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upwelling), which introduces nutrients into the upper zones where sufficient light is available for [photosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis) and phytoplankton production.[^cite_note-48]

These phytoplankton blooms support the marine ecosystem, as the base of the marine food web, and eventually the larger fish species. The Indian Ocean accounts for the second-largest share of the most economically valuable [tuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuna) catch.[^cite_note-49] Its fish are of great and growing importance to the bordering countries for domestic consumption and export. Fishing fleets from Russia, Japan, [South Korea](27019.md), and [Taiwan](25734.md) also exploit the Indian Ocean, mainly for [shrimp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shrimp) and tuna.[^cite_note-CIAWFB-2018-3]

Research indicates that increasing ocean temperatures are taking a toll on the marine ecosystem. A study on the phytoplankton changes in the Indian Ocean indicates a decline of up to 20% in the marine plankton in the Indian Ocean, during the past six decades. The tuna catch rates have also declined 50–90% during the past half-century, mostly due to increased industrial fisheries, with the [ocean warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_warming) adding further stress to the fish species.[^cite_note-50]

80% of the Indian Ocean is open ocean and includes nine [large marine ecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_marine_ecosystem): the [Agulhas Current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agulhas_Current), [Somali Coastal Current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_Coastal_Current?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Red Sea](26191.md), [Arabian Sea](2747.md), [Bay of Bengal](47892.md), [Gulf of Thailand](198107.md), [West Central Australian Shelf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Central_Australian_Shelf?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Northwest Australian Shelf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Australian_Shelf?action=edit\&redlink=1) and [Southwest Australian Shelf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwest_Australian_Shelf?action=edit\&redlink=1). Coral reefs cover c. 200,000 km<sup>2</sup> (77,000 sq mi). The coasts of the Indian Ocean includes beaches and intertidal zones covering 3,000 km<sup>2</sup> (1,200 sq mi) and 246 larger [estuaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estuary). [Upwelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upwelling) areas are small but important. The hypersaline [salterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saltern) in India covers between 5,000–10,000 km<sup>2</sup> (1,900–3,900 sq mi) and species adapted for this environment, such as *[Artemia salina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemia_salina)* and *[Dunaliella salina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunaliella_salina)*, are important to bird life.[^cite_note-52]

Coral reefs, sea grass beds, and mangrove forests are the most productive ecosystems of the Indian Ocean — coastal areas produce 20 tonnes of fish per square kilometre. These areas are also being urbanised, with populations often exceeding several thousand people per square kilometre and fishing techniques become more effective and often destructive beyond sustainable levels while the increase in [sea surface temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_surface_temperature) spreads coral bleaching.[^cite_note-53]

[Mangroves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mangrove) covers 80,984 km<sup>2</sup> (31,268 sq mi) in the Indian Ocean region, or almost half of the world's mangrove habitat, of which 42,500 km<sup>2</sup> (16,400 sq mi) is located in Indonesia, or 50% of mangroves in the Indian Ocean. Mangroves originated in the Indian Ocean region and have adapted to a wide range of its habitats but it is also where it suffers its biggest loss of habitat.[^cite_note-54]

In 2016, six new animal species were identified at [hydrothermal vents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrothermal_vents) in the Southwest Indian Ridge: a "Hoff" crab, a "giant peltospirid" snail, a whelk-like snail, a limpet, a scaleworm and a polychaete worm.[^cite_note-55]

The [West Indian Ocean coelacanth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Indian_Ocean_coelacanth) was discovered in the Indian Ocean off South Africa in the 1930s and in the late 1990s another species, the [Indonesian coelacanth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesian_coelacanth), was discovered off [Sulawesi Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulawesi_Island), Indonesia. Most extant coelacanths have been found in the Comoros. Although both species represent an order of [lobe-finned fishes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarcopterygii) known from the Early Devonian (410 mya) and thought extinct 66 mya, they are morphologically distinct from their Devonian ancestors. Over millions of years, coelacanths evolved to inhabit different environments — lungs adapted for shallow, brackish waters evolved into gills adapted for deep marine waters.[^cite_note-56]

## Biodiversity

Of Earth's 36 [biodiversity hotspots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity_hotspot) nine (or 25%) are located on the margins of the Indian Ocean.



Madagascar's [elephant bird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elephant_bird), Mauritius's [dodo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dodo) bird and ostrich (from left to right)

- Madagascar and the islands of the western Indian Ocean ([Comoros](5403.md), [Réunion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%A9union), [Mauritius](19201.md), Rodrigues, [the Seychelles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Seychelles), and Socotra), includes 13,000 (11,600 endemic) species of plants; 313 (183) birds; reptiles 381 (367); 164 (97) freshwater fishes; 250 (249) amphibians; and 200 (192) mammals.

  [^cite_note-Mitter-2011-p12-57]

The origin of this diversity is debated. The break-up of Gondwana can explain vicariance older than 100 mya, but the diversity on the younger, smaller islands must have required a Cenozoic dispersal from the rims of the Indian Ocean to the islands. A "reverse colonisation", from islands to continents, apparently occurred more recently; the [chameleons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chameleon), for example, first diversified on Madagascar and then colonised Africa. Several species on the islands of the Indian Ocean are textbook cases of evolutionary processes; the [dung beetles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dung_beetle),[^cite_note-58][^cite_note-59] [day geckos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Day_gecko),[^cite_note-60][^cite_note-61] and [lemurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lemur) are all examples of [adaptive radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaptive_radiation).[^cite_note-62]

Many bones (250 bones per square metre) of recently extinct vertebrates have been found in the [Mare aux Songes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mare_aux_Songes) swamp in Mauritius, including bones of the [Dodo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dodo) bird (*Raphus cucullatus*) and *[Cylindraspis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylindraspis)* giant tortoise. An analysis of these remains suggests a process of aridification began in the southwest Indian Ocean began around 4,000 years ago.[^cite_note-63]

- [Maputaland-Pondoland-Albany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maputaland-Pondoland-Albany_Hotspot) (MPA); 8,100 (1,900 endemic) species of plants; 541 (0) birds; 205 (36) reptiles; 73 (20) freshwater fishes; 73 (11) amphibians; and 197 (3) mammals.

  [^cite_note-Mitter-2011-p12-57]

Mammalian megafauna once widespread in the MPA was driven to near extinction in the early 20th century. Some species have been successfully recovered since then — the population of [white rhinoceros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_rhinoceros) (*Ceratotherium simum simum*) increased from less than 20 individuals in 1895 to more than 17,000 as of 2013. Other species still depend on fenced areas and management programs, including [black rhinoceros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_rhinoceros) (*Diceros bicornis minor*), [African wild dog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_wild_dog) (*Lycaon pictus*), cheetah (*Acynonix jubatus*), elephant (*Loxodonta africana*), and lion (*Panthera leo*).[^cite_note-64]

- [Coastal forests of eastern Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coastal_forests_of_eastern_Africa); 4,000 (1,750 endemic) species of plants; 636 (12) birds; 250 (54) reptiles; 219 (32) freshwater fishes; 95 (10) amphibians; and 236 (7) mammals.

  [^cite_note-Mitter-2011-p12-57]

This biodiversity hotspot (and namesake ecoregion and "Endemic Bird Area") is a patchwork of small forested areas, often with a unique assemblage of species within each, located within 200 km (120 mi) from the coast and covering a total area of c. 6,200 km<sup>2</sup> (2,400 sq mi). It also encompasses coastal islands, including Zanzibar and Pemba, and Mafia.[^cite_note-65]

- [Horn of Africa](62142.md); 5,000 (2,750 endemic) species of plants; 704 (25) birds; 284 (93) reptiles; 100 (10) freshwater fishes; 30 (6) amphibians; and 189 (18) mammals.

  [^cite_note-Mitter-2011-p12-57]



[Coral reefs](87410.md) of the Maldives

This area, one of the only two hotspots that are entirely arid, includes the [Ethiopian Highlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_Highlands), the [East African Rift valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_African_Rift), the [Socotra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socotra) islands, as well as some small islands in the Red Sea and areas on the southern Arabic Peninsula. It also contains many reptiles.[^cite_note-66]

In Somalia, the centre of the 1,500,000 km<sup>2</sup> (580,000 sq mi) hotspot, the landscape is dominated by [Acacia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acacia)-[Commiphora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commiphora) deciduous bushland, but also includes the [Yeheb nut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeheb_nut) (*Cordeauxia edulus*) and species discovered more recently such as the Somali [cyclamen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclamen) (*Cyclamen somalense*), the only cyclamen outside the Mediterranean. From June to September it is an explosion of colours and the Wildflower Festival in Perth in September attracts more than half a million visitors.[^cite_note-72]

## Geology





Left: The oldest ocean floor of the Indian Ocean formed c. 150 Ma when the Indian Subcontinent and Madagascar broke-up from Africa. Right: The India–Asia collision c. 40 Ma completed the closure of the [Tethys Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tethys_Ocean) (grey areas north of India). Geologically, the Indian Ocean is the ocean floor that opened up south of India.

Main category: [Landforms of the Indian Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category\:Landforms_of_the_Indian_Ocean)

As the youngest of the major oceans,[^cite_note-73] the Indian Ocean has active spreading ridges that are part of the worldwide system of [mid-ocean ridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid-ocean_ridge). In the Indian Ocean these spreading ridges meet at the [Rodrigues Triple Point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodrigues_Triple_Point) with the [Central Indian Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Indian_Ridge), including the [Carlsberg Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlsberg_Ridge), separating the [African plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_plate) from the [Indian plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_plate); the [Southwest Indian Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwest_Indian_Ridge) separating the African plate from the [Antarctic plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctic_plate); and the [Southeast Indian Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Indian_Ridge) separating the [Australian plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_plate) from the Antarctic plate. The Central Indian Ridge is intercepted by the [Owen fracture zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Owen_fracture_zone).[^cite_note-Chatt-etal-2013-74]

Since the late 1990s, it has become clear that this traditional definition of the [Indo-Australian plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Australian_plate) cannot be correct; it consists of three plates — the [Indian plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_plate), the [Capricorn plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capricorn_plate), and [Australian plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_plate) — separated by diffuse boundary zones.[^cite_note-75] Since 20 Ma the [African plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_plate) is being divided by the [East African Rift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_African_Rift) System into the [Nubian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nubian_plate) and [Somalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_plate) plates.[^cite_note-76]

There are only two trenches in the Indian Ocean: the 6,000 km (3,700 mi)-long [Java Trench](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java_Trench) between Java and the Sunda Trench and the 900 km (560 mi)-long [Makran Trench](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makran_Trench) south of Iran and Pakistan.[^cite_note-Chatt-etal-2013-74]

A series of ridges and [seamount chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seamount_chain) produced by [hotspots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotspot_\(geology\)) pass over the Indian Ocean. The [Réunion hotspot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%A9union_hotspot) (active 70–40 million years ago) connects [Réunion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%A9union) and the [Mascarene Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mascarene_Plateau) to the [Chagos-Laccadive Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chagos-Laccadive_Ridge) and the [Deccan Traps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deccan_Traps) in north-western India. The [Kerguelen hotspot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerguelen_hotspot) (100–35 million years ago) connects the [Kerguelen Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerguelen_Islands) and [Kerguelen Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerguelen_Plateau) to the [Ninety East Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ninety_East_Ridge) and the [Rajmahal Traps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rajmahal_Traps) in north-eastern India. The Marion hotspot (100–70 million years ago) possibly connects [Prince Edward Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Edward_Islands) to the [Eighty Five East Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eighty_Five_East_Ridge).[^cite_note-77] These hotspot tracks have been broken by the still active spreading ridges mentioned above.[^cite_note-Chatt-etal-2013-74]

There are fewer seamounts in the Indian Ocean than in the Atlantic and Pacific. These are typically deeper than 3,000 m (9,800 ft) and located north of 55°S and west of 80°E. Most originated at spreading ridges but some are now located in basins far away from these ridges. The ridges of the Indian Ocean form ranges of seamounts, sometimes very long, including the [Carlsberg Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlsberg_Ridge), [Madagascar Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madagascar_Ridge?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Central Indian Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Indian_Ridge), [Southwest Indian Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southwest_Indian_Ridge), [Chagos-Laccadive Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chagos-Laccadive_Ridge), [85°E Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eighty_Five_East_Ridge), [90°E Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ninety_East_Ridge), [Southeast Indian Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Indian_Ridge), [Broken Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broken_Ridge), and [East Indiaman Ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Indiaman_Ridge?action=edit\&redlink=1). The [Agulhas Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agulhas_Plateau) and [Mascarene Plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mascarene_Plateau) are the two major shallow areas.[^cite_note-Rogers-p5-40]

The opening of the Indian Ocean began c. 156 Ma when Africa separated from East [Gondwana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gondwana). The Indian Subcontinent began to separate from Australia-Antarctica 135–125 Ma and as the [Tethys Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tethys_Ocean) north of India began to close 118–84 Ma the Indian Ocean opened behind it.[^cite_note-Chatt-etal-2013-74]

## History

The Indian Ocean, [together with the Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Mediterranean), has connected people since ancient times, whereas the Atlantic and Pacific have had the roles of barriers or *[mare incognitum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terra_incognita)*. The written history of the Indian Ocean has been [Eurocentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurocentrism) and largely dependent on the availability of written sources from the [European colonial era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_imperialism_in_Asia). This history is often divided into an ancient period followed by an Islamic period; the subsequent colonial-era periods are often subdivided into [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_Empire), [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_colonial_empire), [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_colonial_empire), and [British](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire) periods.[^cite_note-78] Milo Kearney argues that the [postwar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-war) time period can also be split into a period of competition for oil during the [Cold War](325329.md) followed by American dominance.[^cite_note-79]

A concept of an "Indian Ocean World" (IOW), similar to that of the "[Atlantic World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_World)", exists but emerged much more recently and is not well established. The IOW is, nevertheless, sometimes referred to as the "first global economy" and was based on the monsoon which linked Asia, China, India, and [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia). It developed independently from the European global trade in the Mediterranean and Atlantic and remained largely independent from them until European 19th-century colonial dominance.[^cite_note-80]

The diverse history of the Indian Ocean is a unique mix of cultures, ethnic groups, natural resources, and shipping routes. It grew in importance beginning in the 1960s and 1970s and, after the Cold War, it has undergone periods of political instability, most recently with the emergence of India and China as regional powers.[^cite_note-81]

### First settlements



According to the *Coastal hypothesis*, modern humans spread from Africa along the northern rim of the Indian Ocean.

Pleistocene fossils of *[Homo erectus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homo_erectus)* and other pre–*H. sapiens* hominid fossils, similar to *[H. heidelbergensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homo_heidelbergensis)* in Europe, have been found in India. According to the [Toba catastrophe theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toba_catastrophe_theory), a supereruption c. 74,000 years ago at [Lake Toba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Toba), Sumatra, covered India with volcanic ashes and wiped out one or more lineages of such archaic humans in India and Southeast Asia.[^cite_note-82]

The [*Out of Africa*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recent_African_origin_of_modern_humans) theory states that *Homo sapiens* spread from Africa into mainland Eurasia. The more recent *[Southern Dispersal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Dispersal)* or *Coastal hypothesis* instead advocates that modern humans spread along the coasts of the Arabic Peninsula and southern Asia. This hypothesis is supported by [mtDNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitochondrial_DNA) research which reveals a rapid dispersal event during the [Late Pleistocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Pleistocene) (11,000 years ago). This coastal dispersal began in East Africa 75,000 years ago and occurred intermittently from estuary to estuary along the northern perimeter of the Indian Ocean at a rate of 0.7–4.0 km (0.43–2.49 mi) per year. It eventually resulted in modern humans migrating from [Sunda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sundaland) over [Wallacea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallacea) to [Sahul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australia_\(continent\)) (Southeast Asia to Australia).[^cite_note-83]

Since then, waves of migration have resettled people and, clearly, the Indian Ocean littoral had been inhabited long before the first civilisations emerged. 5000–6000 years ago six distinct cultural centres had evolved around the Indian Ocean: East Africa, the Middle East (West Asia), the [Indian Subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent) (South Asia), South East Asia, the Malay World and Australia; each interlinked to its neighbours.[^cite_note-84]

Food globalisation began on the Indian Ocean littoral c. 4,000 years ago. Five African crops — [sorghum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorghum_bicolor), [pearl millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennisetum_glaucum), [finger millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleusine_coracana), [cowpea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vigna_unguiculata) and [hyacinth bean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lablab_purpureus) — somehow found their way to [Gujarat](53707.md) in India during the [Late Harappan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Harappan) (2000–1700 BCE). [Gujarati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gujarati_people) merchants evolved into the first explorers of the Indian Ocean as they traded African goods such as ivory, tortoise shells, and slaves. [Broomcorn millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panicum_miliaceum) found its way from Central Asia to Africa, together with chicken and [zebu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zebu) cattle, although the exact timing is disputed. Around 2000 BCE [black pepper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piper_nigrum) and [sesame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sesamum_indicum), both native to Asia, appear in [Egypt](8087628.md), albeit in small quantities. Around the same time the [black rat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rattus_rattus) and the [house mouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mus_musculus) emigrate from Asia to Egypt. Banana reached Africa around 3000 years ago.[^cite_note-85]



The [Andaman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andamanese_peoples) [Negritos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negrito) are thought to be the first inhabitants of the [Andaman Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andaman_Islands), having emigrated from the mainland tens of thousands of years ago.

At least eleven prehistoric tsunamis have struck the Indian Ocean coast of Indonesia between 7400 and 2900 years ago. Analysing sand beds in caves in the Aceh region, scientists concluded that the intervals between these tsunamis have varied from series of minor tsunamis over a century to dormant periods of more than 2000 years preceding megathrusts in the Sunda Trench. Although the risk for future tsunamis is high, a major megathrust such as the one in 2004 is likely to be followed by a long dormant period.[^cite_note-86]

A group of scientists have argued that two large-scale impact events have occurred in the Indian Ocean: the [Burckle Crater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burckle_Crater) in the southern Indian Ocean in 2800 BCE and the Kanmare and Tabban craters in the [Gulf of Carpentaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Carpentaria) in northern Australia in 536 CE. Evidences for these impacts, the team argue, are micro-ejecta and [Chevron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chevron_\(land_form\)) [dunes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dune) in southern Madagascar and in the Australian gulf. Geological evidences suggest the tsunamis caused by these impacts reached 205 m (673 ft) above sea level and 45 km (28 mi) inland. The impact events must have disrupted human settlements and perhaps even contributed to [major climate changes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_weather_events_of_535%E2%80%93536).[^cite_note-87]

### Antiquity

The history of the Indian Ocean is marked by maritime trade; cultural and commercial exchange probably date back at least seven thousand years.[^cite_note-Alpers-pp1-2-88] Human culture spread early on the shores of the Indian Ocean and was always linked to the cultures of the Mediterranean and the Persian Gulf. Before c. 2000 BCE, cultures on its shores were only loosely tied to each other. Bronze, for example, was developed in Mesopotamia c. 3000 BCE but remained uncommon in Egypt before 1800 BCE.[^cite_note-89]

During this period, independent, short-distance oversea communications along its [littoral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Littoral) margins evolved into an all-embracing network. The début of this network was not the achievement of a centralised or advanced civilisation but of local and regional exchange in the Persian Gulf, the Red Sea, and the Arabian Sea. Sherds of [Ubaid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ubaid_period) (2500–500 BCE) pottery have been found in the western Gulf at [Dilmun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dilmun), present-day [Bahrain](18933277.md); traces of exchange between this trading centre and [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia). The [Sumerians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumer) traded grain, pottery, and [bitumen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitumen) (used for [reed boats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reed_boat)) for copper, stone, timber, tin, dates, onions, and pearls.[^cite_note-Alpers-pp19-22-90] Coast-bound vessels transported goods between the [Indus Valley civilisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Valley_civilisation) (2600–1900 [BCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BCE)) in the Indian subcontinent (modern-day Northwest India and Pakistan) and the Persian Gulf and Egypt.[^cite_note-Alpers-pp1-2-88]



The [Austronesian maritime trade network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean_trade#Austronesian_maritime_trade_network) was the first trade routes in the Indian Ocean.

The Red Sea, one of the main trade routes in Antiquity, was explored by [Egyptians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Dynasty_of_Egypt) and [Phoenicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenicia) during the last two millennia BCE. In the 6th century, BCE Greek explorer [Scylax of Caryanda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scylax_of_Caryanda) made a journey to India, working for the Persian king [Darius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darius_the_Great), and his now-lost account put the Indian Ocean on the maps of Greek geographers. The Greeks began to explore the Indian Ocean following the conquests of [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great), who ordered a circumnavigation of the Arabian Peninsula in 323 BCE. During the two centuries that followed the reports of the explorers of [Ptolemaic Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_Kingdom) resulted in the best maps of the region until the Portuguese era many centuries later. The main interest in the region for the Ptolemies was not commercial but military; they explored Africa to hunt for [war elephants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_elephant).[^cite_note-91]

The [Rub' al Khali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rub'_al_Khali) desert isolates the southern parts of the Arabian Peninsula and the Indian Ocean from the [Arab world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_world). This encouraged the development of maritime trade in the region linking the Red Sea and the Persian Gulf to East Africa and India. The [monsoon](57630.md), from *mawsim*, the Arabic word for season, was used by sailors long before being "discovered" by Hippalus in the 1st century. Indian wood has been found in Sumerian cities; there is evidence of Akkad coastal trade in the region, and contacts between India and the Red Sea date back to 2300 B.C.[^cite_note-92]

The archipelagoes of the central Indian Ocean, the [Lakshadweep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakshadweep) and Maldives islands, were probably populated during the 2nd century B.C. from the [Indian mainland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainland_India). They appear in written history in the account of merchant [Sulaiman al-Tajir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulaiman_al-Tajir) in the 9th century, but the treacherous reefs of the islands were most likely cursed by the sailors of Aden long before the islands were even settled.[^cite_note-93]



[Greco-Roman trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Roman_trade_relations) with ancient India according to the *[Periplus of the Erythraean Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periplus_of_the_Erythraean_Sea)* 1st century CE

*[Periplus of the Erythraean Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periplus_of_the_Erythraean_Sea)*, an [Alexandrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria) guide to the world beyond the Red Sea — including Africa and India — from the first century CE, not only gives insights into trade in the region but also shows that Roman and Greek sailors had already gained knowledge about the [monsoon](57630.md) winds.[^cite_note-Alpers-pp1-2-88] The contemporaneous settlement of [Madagascar](18964.md) by [Austronesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austronesian_people) sailors shows that the littoral margins of the Indian Ocean were being both well-populated and regularly traversed at least by this time. Albeit the monsoon must have been common knowledge in the Indian Ocean for centuries.[^cite_note-Alpers-pp1-2-88]

The Indian Ocean's relatively calmer waters opened the areas bordering it to trade earlier than the Atlantic or Pacific oceans. The powerful monsoons also meant ships could easily sail west early in the season, then wait a few months and return eastwards. This allowed ancient Indonesian peoples to cross the Indian Ocean to settle in [Madagascar](18964.md) around 1 CE.[^cite_note-94]

In the 2nd or 1st century BCE, [Eudoxus of Cyzicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eudoxus_of_Cyzicus) was the first Greek to cross the Indian Ocean. The probably fictitious sailor [Hippalus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippalus) is said to have learnt the direct route from [Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabia) to India around this time.[^cite_note-95] During the 1st and 2nd centuries CE intensive [trade relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_commerce) developed between [Roman Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Egypt) and the [Tamil kingdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamil_people) of the [Cheras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chera_Dynasty), [Cholas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chola_Dynasty) and [Pandyas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pandyas) in [Southern India](678583.md). Like the Indonesian people above, the western sailors used the monsoon to cross the ocean. The unknown author of the *[Periplus of the Erythraean Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periplus_of_the_Erythraean_Sea)* describes this route, as well as the commodities that were traded along various commercial ports on the coasts of the Horn of Africa and India circa 1 CE. Among these trading settlements were [Mosylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosylon) and [Opone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opone) on the Red Sea littoral.[^cite_note-Periplus-12]

### Age of Discovery



The economically important [Silk Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silk_Road) was blocked from Europe by the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md) in c. 1453 with the fall of the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire). This spurred exploration, and a new sea route around Africa was found, triggering the [Age of Discovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Discovery).





Preferred sailing routes across the Indian Ocean

Unlike the Pacific Ocean, where the civilization of the [Polynesians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesians) reached most of the far-flung islands and atolls and populated them, almost all the islands, archipelagos and [atolls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atoll) of the Indian Ocean were uninhabited until colonial times. Although there were numerous ancient civilizations in the coastal states of Asia and parts of Africa, the [Maldives](19117.md) were the only island group in the Central Indian Ocean region where an ancient civilization flourished.[^cite_note-96] [Maldivians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhivehi_people), on their annual trade trip, took their oceangoing trade ships to [Sri Lanka](26750.md) rather than mainland India, which is much closer, because their ships were dependent of the [Indian Monsoon Current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Monsoon_Current).[^cite_note-97]

Arabic missionaries and merchants began to [spread Islam along the western shores of the Indian Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spread_of_Islam#East_Africa) from the 8th century, if not earlier. A [Swahili](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swahili_culture) stone mosque dating to the 8th–15th centuries has been found in [Shanga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanga,_Pate_Island), Kenya. Trade across the Indian Ocean gradually introduced Arabic script and rice as a [staple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staple_food) in Eastern Africa.[^cite_note-98] [Muslim](19541.md) merchants traded an estimated 1,000 African slaves annually between 800 and 1700, a number that grew to approximately 4,000 during the 18th century, and 3,700 during the period 1800–1870. Slave trade also occurred in the eastern Indian Ocean before the Dutch settled there around 1600, but the volume of this trade is unknown.[^cite_note-Allen-2017-overview-99]

From 1405 to 1433, Admiral [Zheng He](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zheng_He) is said to have led large fleets of the [Ming dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ming_dynasty) on several [treasure voyages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasure_voyages) through the Indian Ocean, ultimately reaching the coastal countries of East Africa.[^cite_note-100]



For most of the 16th century, the Portuguese dominated the [Indian Ocean trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean_trade).

The [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_people) navigator [Vasco da Gama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vasco_da_Gama) rounded the [Cape of Good Hope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_of_Good_Hope) during his first voyage in 1497 and became the first European to sail to India. The [Swahili people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swahili_people) he encountered along the African east coast lived in a series of cities and had established trade routes to India and to China. Among them, the Portuguese kidnapped most of their pilots in coastal raids and on board ships. A few of the pilots were given by local [Swahili](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swahili_people) rulers, including the sailor from [Gujarat](53707.md), a gift by a [Malindi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malindi) ruler in [Kenya](188171.md), who helped the Portuguese to reach India.[^cite_note-101]

In expeditions after 1500, the Portuguese attacked and colonised cities along the African coast.[^cite_note-102] European slave trade in the Indian Ocean began when Portugal established [Estado da Índia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_India) in the early 16th century. From then until the 1830s, c. 200 slaves were exported from [Mozambique](19301.md) annually, and similar figures have been estimated for slaves brought from Asia to the [Philippines](23440.md) during the [Iberian Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_Union) (1580–1640).[^cite_note-Allen-2017-overview-99]

The [Ottoman Empire](22278.md) began its expansion into the Indian Ocean in 1517 with the conquest of Egypt under Sultan [Selim I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selim_I). Although the Ottomans shared the same religion as the trading communities in the Indian Ocean, the region was unexplored by them. Maps that included the Indian Ocean had been produced by [Muslim geographers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_geographers) centuries before the Ottoman conquests. Muslim scholars, such as [Ibn Battuta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Battuta) in the 14th century, had visited most parts of the known world. Contemporarily with Vasco da Gama, Arab navigator [Ahmad ibn Mājid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmad_ibn_M%C4%81jid) had compiled a guide to navigation in the Indian Ocean. The Ottomans, nevertheless, began their own parallel era of discovery which rivalled the European expansion.[^cite_note-103]



The British [invasion of Isle de France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasion_of_Isle_de_France) on 29 November 1810 during the [Napoleonic Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars)

The establishment of the [Dutch East India Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_East_India_Company) in the early 17th century led to a quick increase in the volume of the slave trade in the region. There were perhaps up to 500,000 slaves in various [Dutch colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_colonial_empire) during the 17th and 18th centuries in the Indian Ocean. For example, some 4,000 African slaves were used to build the [Colombo fortress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_\(Colombo\)) in [Dutch Ceylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Ceylon). Bali and neighbouring islands supplied regional networks with c. 100,000–150,000 slaves 1620–1830. Indian and Chinese slave traders supplied [Dutch Indonesia](23476997.md) with perhaps 250,000 slaves during the 17th and 18th centuries.[^cite_note-Allen-2017-overview-99]

The [English East India Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_India_Company) (EIC) was established during the same period, and in 1622 one of its ships carried slaves from the [Coromandel Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coromandel_Coast) to the [Dutch East Indies](23476997.md). The EIC mostly traded in African slaves but also some Asian slaves purchased from Indian, Indonesian and Chinese slave traders. The French established colonies on the islands of [Réunion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%A9union) and [Mauritius](19201.md) in 1721; by 1735 some 7,200 slaves populated the [Mascarene Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mascarene_Islands), a number which had reached 133,000 in 1807. The [British](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_of_Great_Britain_and_Ireland) captured the islands in 1810. Because the British had [prohibited the slave trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slave_Trade_Act_1807) in 1807, a system of clandestine slave trade developed to bring slaves to French planters on the islands; in all, 336,000–388,000 slaves were exported to the [Mascarene Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mascarene_Islands) from 1670 until 1848.[^cite_note-Allen-2017-overview-99]

In all, European traders exported 567,900–733,200 slaves within the Indian Ocean between 1500 and 1850, and almost that same number were exported from the Indian Ocean to the Americas during the same period. Slave trade in the Indian Ocean was, nevertheless, very limited compared to c. 12,000,000 slaves exported across the Atlantic.[^cite_note-Allen-2017-overview-99] The island of [Zanzibar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zanzibar) was the center of the [Indian Ocean slave trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean_slave_trade) in the 19th century. In the mid-19th century, as many as 50,000 slaves passed annually through the port.[^cite_note-104]

### Late modern era



[Malé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mal%C3%A9)'s population has increased from 20,000 people in 1987 to more than 220,000 people in 2020.

Scientifically, the Indian Ocean remained poorly explored before the [International Indian Ocean Expedition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Indian_Ocean_Expedition) in the early 1960s. However, the [*Challenger* expedition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Challenger_expedition) 1872–1876 only reported from south of the polar front. The [*Valdivia* expedition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valdivia_Expedition) 1898–1899 made deep samples in the Indian Ocean. In the 1930s, the John Murray Expedition mainly studied shallow-water habitats. The [Swedish Deep Sea Expedition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albatross_expedition) 1947–1948 also sampled the Indian Ocean on its global tour, and the Danish [*Galathea*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galathea_expeditions) sampled deep-water fauna from Sri Lanka to South Africa on its second expedition 1950–1952. The Soviet research vessel [*Vityaz*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RV_Vityaz_\(1939\)) also did research in the Indian Ocean.[^cite_note-Demo-etal-intro-1]

The [Suez Canal](29323.md) opened in 1869 when the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution) dramatically changed global shipping – the sailing ship declined in importance, as did the importance of European trade in favour of trade in East Asia and Australia.[^cite_note-105] The construction of the canal introduced many non-indigenous species into the Mediterranean. For example, the goldband goatfish (*[Upeneus moluccensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upeneus_moluccensis)*) has replaced the red mullet (*[Mullus barbatus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mullus_barbatus)*); since the 1980s, huge swarms of [scyphozoan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scyphozoa) jellyfish (*[Rhopilema nomadica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhopilema_nomadica)*) have affected tourism and fisheries along the Levantine coast and clogged power and desalination plants. Plans announced in 2014 to [build a new, much larger Suez Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_Canal_Area_Development_Project) parallel to the 19th-century canal will most likely boost the economy in the region but also cause ecological damage in a much wider area.[^cite_note-106]



A [Chagossian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chagossians) on [Diego Garcia](8630.md) in 1971, before the British [expelled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expulsion_of_the_Chagossians) the islanders. He spoke a [French-based creole language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French-based_creole_languages) and his ancestors were likely brought as slaves in the 19th century.

Throughout the colonial era, islands such as [Mauritius](19201.md) were important shipping nodes for the Dutch, French, and British. Mauritius, an inhabited island, became populated by slaves from Africa and [indenture labour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_indenture_system) from India. The end of [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean_in_World_War_II) marked the end of the colonial era. The British left Mauritius after granting independence to the island on 12 March 1968, and with 70% of the population of Indian descent, [Mauritius became a close ally of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India%E2%80%93Mauritius_relations). In the 1980s, during the Cold War, the [South African apartheid regime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_South_Africa_\(1948%E2%80%931994\)) acted to destabilise several island nations in the Indian Ocean, including the [Seychelles](27288.md), [Comoros](5403.md), and [Madagascar](18964.md). India intervened in Mauritius to prevent a coup d'état, backed up by the United States, which feared the Soviet Union could gain access to [Port Louis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Louis) and threaten the U.S. base on [Diego Garcia](8630.md) (the fact that [India and the Soviet Union were close allies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India%E2%80%93Soviet_Union_relations), especially [since the 1970s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Soviet_Treaty_of_Friendship_and_Cooperation)).[^cite_note-107] [Iranrud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranrud) was an unrealised plan by Iran and the Soviet Union to build a canal between the Caspian Sea and the Persian Gulf (thwarted by the U.S.- and U.K.-led regime change in [Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pahlavi_Iran) in 1953, known as the [Operation Ajax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1953_Iranian_coup_d'%C3%A9tat)).

Testimonies from the colonial era are stories of African slaves, Indian indentured labourers and white settlers. But while there was a clear racial line between free men and slaves in the Atlantic World, this delineation is less distinct in the Indian Ocean — there were Indian slaves and settlers as well as black indentured labourers. There were also a string of prison camps across the Indian Ocean, such as [Cellular Jail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_Jail) in the [Andaman Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andaman_Islands), in which prisoners, exiles, POWs, forced labourers, merchants and people of different faiths were forcefully united. On the islands of the Indian Ocean, therefore, a trend of [creolization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creolization) emerged.[^cite_note-108]

On 26 December 2004, fourteen countries around the Indian Ocean were hit by a wave of [tsunamis](31161.md) caused by the [2004 Indian Ocean earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Indian_Ocean_earthquake). The waves radiated across the ocean at speeds exceeding 500 km/h (310 mph), reached up to 20 m (66 ft) in height, and resulted in an estimated 236,000 deaths.[^cite_note-109]

In the late 2000s, the ocean evolved into a hub of [pirate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piracy_in_Somalia) activity. By 2013, attacks off the [Horn region](62142.md)'s coast had steadily declined due to active private security and international navy patrols, especially by the [Indian Navy](604522.md).[^cite_note-110]

[Malaysia Airlines Flight 370](42142305.md), a [Boeing 777-2H6ER](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_777) with 239 occupants on board, disappeared on 8 March 2014 and is alleged to have crashed into the southern Indian Ocean about 2,500 km (1,600 mi) from the coast of southwest [Western Australia](33613.md). Despite an extensive search, the whereabouts of the remains of the aircraft are unknown.[^cite_note-111]

The [Sentinelese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentinelese) people of [North Sentinel Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Sentinel_Island), which lies near [South Andaman Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Andaman_Island) in the Bay of Bengal, have been called by experts the most [isolated people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncontacted_peoples) in the world.[^cite_note-112]

The sovereignty of the [Chagos Archipelago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chagos_Archipelago) in the Indian Ocean is [disputed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chagos_Archipelago_sovereignty_dispute) between the [United Kingdom](31717.md) and [Mauritius](19201.md).[^cite_note-113] In February 2019, the [International Court of Justice](14918.md) in [The Hague](30269.md) issued an advisory opinion [stating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_Consequences_of_the_Separation_of_the_Chagos_Archipelago_from_Mauritius_in_1965) that the UK must transfer the Chagos Archipelago to Mauritius.[^cite_note-114]

## Geopolitics

The Indian Ocean is crucial for global trade, especially in oil and hydrocarbons.[^cite_note-TheDiplomatDeSilva2011-115] Environmental and geopolitical concerns in the region include the effects of [climate change](5042951.md), the [illegal drug trade](218449.md), [illegal, unreported and unregulated fishing](6346579.md), maritime safety and security, and increasing major power competition.[^cite_note-Bergin2019-17]<sup>: 31–35 </sup>

### Trade

Main article: [Indian Ocean trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Ocean_trade)



Major [ocean trade routes](28769.md) in the world include the northern Indian Ocean.

The sea lanes in the Indian Ocean are considered among the most strategically important in the world with more than 80 percent of the world's seaborne trade in oil transits through the Indian Ocean and its vital chokepoints, with 40 percent passing through the Strait of Hormuz, 35 percent through the Strait of Malacca and 8 percent through the Bab el-Mandab Strait.[^cite_note-TheDiplomatDeSilva2011-115]



A [dhow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhow) off the coast of [Kenya](188171.md)

The Indian Ocean provides major sea routes connecting Africa, as well as West, South, Southeast, and East Asia with Europe and the Americas. It carries a particularly heavy traffic of petroleum and petroleum products from the oil fields of the [Persian Gulf](24761.md) and [Indonesia](14579.md). Large reserves of hydrocarbons are being tapped in the offshore areas of [Saudi Arabia](349303.md), [Iran](14653.md), [India](14533.md), and [Western Australia](33613.md). An estimated 40% of the world's offshore oil production comes from the Indian Ocean.[^cite_note-CIAWFB-2018-3] Beach sands rich in heavy minerals and offshore placer deposits are actively exploited by bordering countries, particularly [India](14533.md), [Pakistan](23235.md), [South Africa](17416221.md), [Indonesia](14579.md), [Sri Lanka](26750.md), and [Thailand](30128.md).



[Mombasa](200152.md) Port on Kenya's Indian Ocean coast

In particular, the maritime part of the [Silk Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silk_Road) leads through the Indian Ocean, on which a large part of the global container trade is carried out. The Silk Road runs with its connections from the Chinese coast and its large container ports to the south via [Hanoi](56667.md) to [Jakarta](16275.md), [Singapore](27318.md) and [Kuala Lumpur](16854.md) through the [Strait of Malacca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strait_of_Malacca) via the Sri Lankan [Colombo](56636.md) opposite the southern tip of India via [Malé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mal%C3%A9), the capital of the Maldives, to the East African [Mombasa](200152.md), from there to [Djibouti](17207794.md), then through the Red Sea over the [Suez Canal](29323.md) into the Mediterranean, there via [Haifa](55606.md), [Istanbul](3391396.md) and [Athens](1216.md) to the Upper Adriatic to the northern Italian junction of [Trieste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trieste) with its international free port and its rail connections to [Central](5188.md) and Eastern Europe.[^cite_note-116][^cite_note-117][^cite_note-118][^cite_note-119]

The Silk Road has become internationally important again on the one hand through European integration, the end of the Cold War and free world trade and on the other hand through Chinese initiatives. Chinese companies have made investments in several Indian Ocean ports, including [Gwadar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwadar), [Hambantota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hambantota), [Colombo](56636.md) and [Sonadia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonadia). This has sparked a debate about the strategic implications of these investments.[^cite_note-120] There are also Chinese investments and related efforts to intensify trade in East Africa and in European ports such as [Piraeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piraeus) and [Trieste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trieste).[^cite_note-121][^cite_note-122][^cite_note-123]
