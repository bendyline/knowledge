# Boxcar

For other uses, see [Boxcar (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boxcar_\(disambiguation\)).

Not to be confused with [Bockscar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bockscar).

This article is about the American freight car. For UIC, Australian and New Zealand railway practice, see [Covered goods wagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covered_goods_wagon). For the Wild West wagon, see [Covered wagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covered_wagon).

A **boxcar** is the [North American](21139.md) ([AAR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_of_American_Railroads)) and [South Australian Railways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Australian_Railways) term for a [railroad car](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railroad_car#Freight_cars) that is enclosed and generally used to carry [freight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freight). The boxcar, while not the simplest [freight car](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railroad_car#Freight_cars) design, is considered one of the most versatile since it can carry most loads. Boxcars have side [sliding doors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sliding_door) of varying size and operation, and some include end doors and adjustable [bulkheads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulkhead_\(partition\)) to load very large items.

Similar covered freight cars outside North America are [covered goods wagons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covered_goods_wagon) and, depending on the region, are called *goods van* ([UK](31717.md) and [Australia](4689264.md)), *covered wagon* ([UIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Union_of_Railways) and UK) or simply *van* (UIC, UK and Australia).[^cite_note-2]

## Use



A hicube boxcar



An 86 foot long boxcar used for the transport of automobile parts



Illustration of a boxcar being unloaded by means of a [wheelbarrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheelbarrow)

Boxcars can carry most kinds of freight. Originally they were hand-loaded, but in more recent years mechanical assistance such as [forklifts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forklift) have been used to load and empty them faster. Their generalized design is still slower to load and unload than specialized designs of car, and this partially explains the decline in boxcar numbers since [World War II](32927.md). The other cause for this decline is the dramatic shift of waterborne [cargo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cargo) transport to [container](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermodal_container) shipping. Effectively a boxcar without the wheels and [chassis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chassis), a container is designed to be amenable to [intermodal freight transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermodal_freight_transport), whether by [container ships](262084.md), [trucks](31456.md) or [flatcars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flatcar), and can be delivered door-to-door.

Boxcars were used for bulk commodities such as [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal), particularly in the [Midwestern United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midwestern_United_States) in the early 20th century. This use was sufficiently widespread that several companies developed competing box-car loaders to automate coal loading. By 1905, 350 to 400 such machines were in use, mostly at Midwestern coal mines.[^cite_note-3]

### Passenger use

In the [Philippines](23440.md), boxcars were used as additional third-class accommodations by the [Manila Railway Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manila_Railroad_Company) during the early 1900s as there was a shortage of true [passenger railroad cars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passenger_railroad_car).[^cite_note-4] These problems were considered solved by the 1910s as British manufacturer [Metropolitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metro-Cammell) and American builders such as [Harlan and Hollingsworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harlan_and_Hollingsworth) constructed more passenger cars for the railroad.[^cite_note-5]

In the present day, [hobos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hobo) and [migrant workers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Migrant_worker) have often used boxcars in their journeys (see [freighthopping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freighthopping)), since they are enclosed and cannot be seen by [railroad police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railroad_police), as well as being insulated to some degree from cold weather.[^cite_note-6] [Hobo code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hobo_code), a form of hieroglyphs used by hobos, developed as a code to give information to Hobos freighthopping.[^cite_note-7]

## Hicube boxcar

In the 21st century, high cubic capacity (hicube) boxcars have become more common in the US. These are taller than regular boxcars and as such can only run on routes with increased clearance (see [loading gauge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loading_gauge#North_America) and [structure gauge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structure_gauge)). The excess height section of the car end is often painted with a white band to be easily visible if wrongly assigned to a low-clearance line.[^cite_note-8]

The internal height of the 86-foot (26.21 m) hicube boxcars originally used in automotive parts service was generally 12 feet 9 inches (3.89 m).[^cite_note-9]
