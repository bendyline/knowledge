**Propane** ([/ˈproʊpeɪn/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English)) is a three-[carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon) chain [alkane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkane) with the molecular formula C<sub>3</sub>H<sub>8</sub>. It is a gas at [standard temperature and pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_temperature_and_pressure), but becomes liquid when compressed for transportation and storage. A [by-product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/By-product) of [natural gas](22131.md) processing and [petroleum](23195.md) refining, it is often a constituent of [liquefied petroleum gas](361384.md) (LPG), which is commonly used as a [fuel](21188370.md) in domestic and industrial applications and in low-emissions public transportation; other constituents of LPG may include [propylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propene), [butane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butane), [butylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butene), [butadiene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butadiene), and [isobutylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isobutylene). Discovered in 1857 by the French chemist [Marcellin Berthelot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcellin_Berthelot), it became commercially available in the US by 1911. Propane has lower volumetric energy density than gasoline or coal, but has higher gravimetric energy density than them and burns more cleanly.[^cite_note-6]

Propane gas has become a popular choice for barbecues and portable stoves because its low −42 °C [boiling point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boiling_point) makes it vaporise inside pressurised liquid containers (it exists in two phases, vapor above liquid). It retains its ability to vaporise even in cold weather, making it better-suited for outdoor use in cold climates than alternatives with higher boiling points like [butane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butane).[^cite_note-7] LPG powers buses, forklifts, automobiles, outboard boat motors, and [ice resurfacing machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_resurfacer), and is used for heat and cooking in [recreational vehicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recreational_vehicle) and [campers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campervan). Propane is also becoming popular as a replacement [refrigerant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refrigerant) (R290) for [heat pumps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_pump) as it offers greater efficiency than the current refrigerants: [R410A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R410A) / [R32](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difluoromethane), higher temperature heat output and less damage to the atmosphere for escaped gases—at the expense of high gas flammability.[^cite_note-8]

## History

Propane was first synthesized by the French chemist [Marcellin Berthelot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcellin_Berthelot) in 1857 during his researches on [hydrogenation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogenation). Berthelot made propane by heating [propylene dibromide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propylene_dibromide) (C<sub>3</sub>H<sub>6</sub>Br<sub>2</sub>) with [potassium iodide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium_iodide) and water.[^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10]<sup>: p. 9, §1.1 </sup>[^cite_note-11] Propane was found dissolved in [Pennsylvanian](23332.md) light [crude oil](23195.md) by [Edmund Ronalds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Ronalds) in 1864.[^cite_note-12][^cite_note-13] [Walter O. Snelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_O._Snelling) of the [U.S. Bureau of Mines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Bureau_of_Mines) highlighted it as a volatile component in [gasoline](23639.md) in 1910, which marked the "birth of the propane industry" in the United States.[^cite_note-history-14] The volatility of these lighter [hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbons) caused them to be known as "wild" because of the high vapor pressures of unrefined gasoline. On March 31, 1912, *[The New York Times](30680.md)* reported on Snelling's work with liquefied gas, saying "a [steel bottle](1179236.md) will carry enough [gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas) to light an ordinary home for three weeks".[^cite_note-15]

It was during this time that Snellin&#x67;*—*&#x69;n cooperation with Frank P. Peterson, Chester Kerr, and Arthur Ker&#x72;*—*&#x64;eveloped ways to liquefy the LP gases during the refining of gasoline.[^cite_note-history-14] Together, they established American Gasol Co., the first commercial marketer of propane. Snelling had produced relatively pure propane by 1911, and on March 25, 1913, his method of processing and producing LP gases was issued patent #1,056,845.[^cite_note-history-14] A separate method of producing LP gas through compression was developed by Frank Peterson and its patent was granted on July 2, 1912.[^cite_note-lpga-16]

The 1920s saw increased production of LP gases, with the first year of recorded production totaling 223,000 US gallons (840 m<sup>3</sup>) in 1922. In 1927, annual marketed LP gas production reached 1 million US gallons (3,800 m<sup>3</sup>), and by 1935, the annual sales of LP gas had reached 56 million US gallons (210,000 m<sup>3</sup>). Major industry developments in the 1930s included the introduction of railroad tank car transport, gas odorization, and the construction of local bottle-filling plants. The year 1945 marked the first year that annual LP gas sales reached a billion gallons. By 1947, 62% of all U.S. homes had been equipped with either natural gas or propane for cooking.[^cite_note-history-14]

In 1950, 1,000 propane-fueled buses were ordered by the [Chicago Transit Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Transit_Authority), and by 1958, sales in the U.S. had reached 7 billion US gallons (26,000,000 m<sup>3</sup>) annually. In 2004, it was reported to be a growing \$8-billion to \$10-billion industry with over 15 billion US gallons (57,000,000 m<sup>3</sup>) of propane being used annually in the U.S.[^cite_note-17]

During the [COVID-19 pandemic](62750956.md), propane [shortages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortages_related_to_the_COVID-19_pandemic) were reported in the United States due to increased demand.[^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19][^cite_note-20]

## Etymology

The *[prop-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prop-)* root found in *propane* and names of other compounds with three-carbon chains was derived from *[propionic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propionic_acid)*,[^cite_note-21] which in turn was derived from the Ancient Greek words πρῶτος, *protos*, "first" and πίων, *pion*, "fat", as it was the "first" member of the series of [fatty acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatty_acid).[^cite_note-22]

Propane is a colorless, odorless gas. [Ethyl mercaptan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethyl_mercaptan) is added as a safety precaution as an [odorizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odorizer),[^cite_note-23] and is commonly called a "rotten egg" smell.[^cite_note-24] At normal pressure it liquifies below its [boiling point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boiling_point) at −42 °C and solidifies below its [melting point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melting_point) at −187.7 °C. Propane crystallizes in the [space group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_group) P2<sub>1</sub>/n.[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26] The low space-filling of 58.5% (at 90 K), due to the bad stacking properties of the [molecule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule), is the reason for the particularly low melting point.

Propane undergoes [combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion) reactions in a similar fashion to other [alkanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkane). Propane combustion is much cleaner than that of coal or unleaded gasoline. Propane's per-BTU production of CO<sub>2</sub> is almost as low as that of natural gas.[^cite_note-eiagov-28] Propane burns hotter than home heating oil or diesel fuel because of the very high hydrogen content. The presence of [C–C bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon%E2%80%93carbon_bond), plus the multiple bonds of [propylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propylene) and [butylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butene), produce organic exhausts besides carbon dioxide and water vapor during typical combustion. These bonds also cause propane to burn with a visible flame.

### Energy content

The [enthalpy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enthalpy) of combustion of propane gas where all products return to standard state, for example where water returns to its liquid state at standard temperature (known as [higher heating value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_of_combustion#Higher_heating_value)), is (2,219.2 ± 0.5) kJ/mol, or (50.33 ± 0.01) MJ/kg.[^cite_note-nist-27]

The enthalpy of combustion of propane gas where products do not return to standard state, for example where the hot gases including water vapor exit a chimney, (known as [lower heating value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_of_combustion#Lower_heating_value)) is 2,043.455 kJ/mol.[^cite_note-29] The lower heat value is the amount of heat available from burning the substance where the combustion products are vented to the atmosphere; for example, the heat from a fireplace when the flue is open.

### Density

The density of propane gas at 25 °C (77 °F) is 1.808 kg/m<sup>3</sup>, about 1.5× the density of air at the same temperature. The density of liquid propane at 25 °C (77 °F) is 0.493 g/cm<sup>3</sup>, which is equivalent to 4.11 pounds per U.S. liquid gallon or 493 g/L. Propane expands at 1.5% per 10 °F. Thus, liquid propane has a density of approximately 4.2 pounds per gallon (504 g/L) at 60 °F (15.6 °C).[^cite_note-30]

As the density of propane changes with temperature, this fact must be considered every time when the application is connected with safety or custody transfer operations.[^cite_note-31] If enough headspace is not left in a tank when filling, the tank could rupture when it heats up.

### Portable stoves

Propane is a popular choice for barbecues and portable stoves because the low [boiling point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boiling_point) of −42 °C (−44 °F) makes it vaporize as soon as it is released from its pressurized container. Therefore, no [carburetor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carburetor) or other vaporizing device is required; a simple metering nozzle suffices.

### Refrigerant

Blends of pure, dry "isopropane" \[isobutane/propane mixtures of propane (R-290) and [isobutane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isobutane) (R-600a)] can be used as the circulating [refrigerant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refrigerant) in suitably constructed compressor-based refrigeration.[^cite_note-32] Compared to fluorocarbons, propane has a negligible [ozone depletion potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone_depletion_potential) and very low [global warming potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming_potential), or GWP (having a GWP 20 value of 0.072,[^cite_note-33] 1⁄14 that of carbon dioxide, and a GWP 500 of 0.006, 1⁄170) and can serve as a functional replacement for [R-12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dichlorodifluoromethane), [R-22](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorodifluoromethane), [R-134a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1,1,1,2-Tetrafluoroethane), and other [chlorofluorocarbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorofluorocarbon) or [hydrofluorocarbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrofluorocarbon) refrigerants in conventional stationary refrigeration and air conditioning systems.[^cite_note-34] Because its global warming effect is far less than current refrigerants, propane was chosen as one of five replacement refrigerants approved by the EPA in 2015, for use in systems specially designed to handle its flammability.[^cite_note-35]

Such substitution is widely prohibited or discouraged in motor vehicle air conditioning systems, on the grounds that using [flammable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flammability) hydrocarbons in systems originally designed to carry non-flammable refrigerant presents a significant risk of fire or explosion.[^cite_note-36]

Vendors and advocates of hydrocarbon refrigerants argue against such bans on the grounds that there have been very few such incidents relative to the number of vehicle air conditioning systems filled with hydrocarbons.[^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38]

Propane is also instrumental in providing [off-the-grid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Off-the-grid) refrigeration, as the energy source for a [gas absorption refrigerator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_absorption_refrigerator), and is commonly used for camping and recreational vehicles.

It has also been proposed to use propane as a refrigerant in [heat pumps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_pump).[^cite_note-39]

### Domestic and industrial fuel



Domestic spherical steel pressure vessel for propane storage

Since it can be transported easily, it is a popular fuel for home heat and backup electrical generation in sparsely populated areas that do not have natural gas pipelines. In June 2023, Stanford University researchers found propane combustion emitted detectable and repeatable levels of benzene that in some homes raised indoor benzene concentrations above well-established health benchmarks. The research also shows that gas and propane fuels appear to be the dominant source of benzene produced by cooking.[^cite_note-40]

In rural areas of North America, as well as northern Australia, propane is used to heat livestock facilities, in grain dryers, and other heat-producing appliances. When used for heating or [grain drying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grain_drying) it is usually stored in a large, permanently-placed cylinder which is refilled by a propane-delivery truck. As of 2014, 6.2 million American households use propane as their primary heating fuel.[^cite_note-Sloan-2016-41]

In North America, local delivery trucks with an average cylinder size of 3,000 US gallons (11 m<sup>3</sup>), fill up large cylinders that are permanently installed on the property, or other service trucks exchange empty cylinders of propane with filled cylinders. Large tractor-trailer trucks, with an average cylinder size of 10,000 US gallons (38 m<sup>3</sup>), transport propane from the pipeline or refinery to the local bulk plant. The bobtail [tank truck](2863776.md) is not unique to the North American market, though the practice is not as common elsewhere, and the vehicles are generally called *tankers*. In many countries, propane is delivered to end-users via small or medium-sized individual cylinders, while empty cylinders are removed for refilling at a central location.

There are also community propane systems, with a central cylinder feeding individual homes.[^cite_note-42]

### Motor fuel

Main article: [Autogas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autogas)



Fixed propane tank protected by steel [bollards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic_bollard) to prevent vehicle impact

In the U.S., over 190,000 on-road vehicles use propane, and over 450,000 forklifts use it for power. It is the third most popular vehicle fuel in the world,[^cite_note-propane-as-fuel-43] behind [gasoline](23639.md) and [diesel fuel](81761.md). In other parts of the world, propane used in vehicles is known as *autogas*. In 2007, approximately 13 million vehicles worldwide use autogas.[^cite_note-propane-as-fuel-43]

The advantage of propane in cars is its liquid state at a moderate pressure. This allows fast refill times, affordable fuel cylinder construction, and price ranges typically just over half that of gasoline. Meanwhile, it is noticeably cleaner (both in handling, and in combustion), results in less engine wear (due to carbon deposits) without diluting engine oil (often extending oil-change intervals), and until recently was relatively low-cost in North America. The [octane rating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octane_rating) of propane is relatively high at 110. In the United States the propane fueling infrastructure is the most developed of all alternative vehicle fuels. Many converted vehicles have provisions for topping off from "barbecue bottles". Purpose-built vehicles are often in commercially owned fleets with private fueling facilities. A further saving for propane fuel vehicle operators, especially in fleets, is that theft is much more difficult than with gasoline or diesel fuels.

Propane is also used as fuel for [small engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_engine), especially those used indoors or in areas with insufficient fresh air and ventilation to carry away the more toxic exhaust of an engine running on gasoline or diesel fuel. More recently, there have been lawn-care products like [string trimmers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_trimmer), [lawn mowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawn_mowers) and [leaf blowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf_blowers) intended for outdoor use, but fueled by propane in order to reduce [air pollution](10934212.md).[^cite_note-44]

Many heavy-duty highway trucks use propane as a boost, where it is added through the turbocharger, to mix with diesel fuel droplets. The very high hydrogen content of propane helps the diesel fuel to burn hotter and therefore more completely. This provides more torque, more horsepower, and a cleaner exhaust for the trucks. It is normal for a 7-liter medium-duty diesel truck engine to increase fuel economy by 20 to 33 percent when a propane boost system is used. It is cheaper because propane is much cheaper than diesel fuel. The longer distance a cross-country trucker can travel on a full load of combined diesel and propane fuel means they can maintain federal hours of work rules with two fewer fuel stops in a cross-country trip. Truckers, tractor pulling competitions, and farmers have been using a propane boost system for over forty years in North America.

### Other uses

## Purity

The North American standard grade of automotive-use propane is rated HD-5 (Heavy Duty 5%). HD-5 grade has a maximum of 5 percent butane, but propane sold in Europe has a maximum allowable amount of butane of 30 percent, meaning it is not the same fuel as HD-5. The LPG used as auto fuel and cooking gas in Asia and Australia also has very high butane content.

[Propylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propylene) (also called propene) can be a contaminant of commercial propane. Propane containing too much propene is not suited for most vehicle fuels. HD-5 is a specification that establishes a maximum concentration of 5% propene in propane. Propane and other LP gas specifications are established in ASTM D-1835.[^cite_note-47] All propane fuels include an [odorant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aroma_compound), almost always [ethanethiol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethanethiol), so that the gas can be smelled easily in case of a leak. Propane as HD-5 was originally intended for use as vehicle fuel. HD-5 is currently being used in all propane applications.

Typically in the United States and Canada, [LPG](361384.md) is primarily propane (at least 90%), while the rest is mostly [ethane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethane), [propylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propylene), [butane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butane), and odorants including [ethyl mercaptan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethyl_mercaptan).[^cite_note-48][^cite_note-49] This is the HD-5 standard, (maximum allowable propylene content, and no more than 5% butanes and ethane) defined by the American Society for Testing and Materials by its Standard 1835 for internal combustion engines. Not all products labeled "LPG" conform to this standard, however. In Mexico, for example, gas labeled "LPG" may consist of 60% propane and 40% butane. "The exact proportion of this combination varies by country, depending on international prices, on the availability of components and, especially, on the climatic conditions that favor LPG with higher butane content in warmer regions and propane in cold areas".[^cite_note-50]

## Comparison with natural gas

Propane is bought and stored in a liquid form, LPG. It can easily be stored in a relatively small space.

By comparison, [compressed natural gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compressed_natural_gas) (CNG) cannot be liquefied by compression at normal temperatures, as these are well above its [critical temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_temperature). As a gas, very high pressure is required to store useful quantities. This poses the hazard that, in an accident, just as with any compressed gas cylinder (such as a CO<sub>2</sub> cylinder used for a soda concession) a CNG cylinder may burst with great force, or leak rapidly enough to become a self-propelled missile. Therefore, CNG is much less efficient to store than propane, due to the large cylinder volume required. An alternative means of storing natural gas is as a [cryogenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryogenics) liquid in an insulated container as [liquefied natural gas](832128.md) (LNG). This form of storage is at low pressure and is around 3.5 times as efficient as storing it as CNG.

Unlike propane, if a spill occurs, CNG will evaporate and dissipate because it is lighter than air.

Propane is much more commonly used to fuel vehicles than is natural gas, because that equipment costs less. Propane requires just 1,220 kilopascals (177 psi) of pressure to keep it liquid at 37.8 °C (100 °F).[^cite_note-Vapor_Pressure-51]

## Hazards

Propane is a simple [asphyxiant](98534.md).[^cite_note-cdc-52] Unlike [natural gas](22131.md), it is denser than air. It may accumulate in low spaces and near the floor. When abused as an [inhalant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intoxicative_inhalant), it may cause [hypoxia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypoxia_\(medical\)) (lack of oxygen), [pneumonia](52135.md), [cardiac failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiac_failure) or [cardiac arrest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiac_arrest).[^cite_note-gdcada.org-53][^cite_note-54] Propane has low [toxicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toxicity) since it is not readily absorbed and is not [biologically active](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_activity). Commonly stored under pressure at room temperature, propane and its mixtures will [flash evaporate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_evaporation) at atmospheric pressure and cool well below the freezing point of water. The cold gas, which appears white due to moisture condensing from the air, may cause frostbite.

Propane is denser than air. If a leak in a propane fuel system occurs, the vaporized gas will have a tendency to sink into any enclosed area and thus poses a risk of explosion and fire. The typical scenario is a leaking cylinder stored in a basement; the propane leak drifts across the floor to the pilot light on the furnace or water heater, and results in an explosion or fire. This property makes propane generally unsuitable as a fuel for boats. In 2007, a heavily investigated vapor-related [explosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghent,_West_Virginia#2007_Propane_Disaster) occurred in Ghent, West Virginia, U.S., killing four people and completely destroying the Little General [convenience store](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convenience_store) on [Flat Top Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Route_19), causing several injuries.[^cite_note-55][^cite_note-56]

Another hazard associated with propane storage and transport is known as a BLEVE or [boiling liquid expanding vapor explosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boiling_liquid_expanding_vapor_explosion). The [Kingman Explosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingman_Explosion) involved a railroad tank car in Kingman, Arizona, U.S., in 1973 during a propane transfer. The fire and subsequent explosions resulted in twelve fatalities and numerous injuries.[^cite_note-57]

## Production

Propane is produced as a [by-product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/By-product) of two other processes, [natural gas processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas_processing) and [petroleum refining](195137.md). The processing of natural gas involves removal of [butane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butane), propane, and large amounts of [ethane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethane) from the raw gas, to prevent condensation of these volatiles in natural gas pipelines. Additionally, oil refineries produce some propane as a by-product of [cracking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cracking_\(chemistry\)) petroleum into gasoline or heating oil.

The supply of propane cannot easily be adjusted to meet increased demand, because of the by-product nature of propane production. About 90% of U.S. propane is domestically produced.[^cite_note-Sloan-2016-41] The United States imports about 10% of the propane consumed each year, with about 70% of that coming from Canada via pipeline and rail. The remaining 30% of imported propane comes to the United States from other sources via ocean transport.

After it is separated from the crude oil, North American propane is stored in huge [salt caverns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas_storage#Salt_formation). Examples of these are [Fort Saskatchewan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Saskatchewan), Alberta; [Mont Belvieu, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mont_Belvieu,_Texas); and [Conway, Kansas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conway,_Kansas). These salt caverns[^cite_note-58] can store 80,000,000 barrels (13,000,000 m<sup>3</sup>) of propane.

#### United States

As of October 2013, the retail cost of propane was approximately \$2.37 per gallon, or roughly \$25.95 per 1 million BTUs.[^cite_note-59] This means that filling a 500-gallon propane tank, which is what households that use propane as their main source of energy usually require, cost \$948 (80% of 500 gallons or 400 gallons), a 7.5% increase on the 2012–2013 winter season average US price.[^cite_note-60] However, propane costs per gallon change significantly from one state to another: the Energy Information Administration (EIA) quotes a \$2.995 per gallon average on the East Coast for October 2013,[^cite_note-61] while the figure for the Midwest was \$1.860 for the same period.[^cite_note-62]

As of December 2015, the propane retail cost was approximately \$1.97 per gallon,[^cite_note-EIAdecember2015-63] which meant filling a 500-gallon propane tank to 80% capacity cost \$788, a 16.9% decrease or \$160 less from November 2013. Similar regional differences in prices are present with the December 2015 EIA figure for the East Coast at \$2.67 per gallon and the Midwest at \$1.43 per gallon.[^cite_note-EIAdecember2015-63]

As of August 2018, the average US propane retail cost was approximately \$2.48 per gallon. The wholesale price of propane in the U.S. always drops in the summer as most homes do not require it for home heating. The wholesale price of propane in the summer of 2018 was between 86 cents to 96 cents per U.S. gallon, based on a truckload or railway car load. The price for home heating was exactly double that price; at 95 cents per gallon wholesale, a home-delivered price was \$1.90 per gallon if ordered 500 gallons at a time. Prices for home delivery always go up near the end of August or the first few days of September when people start ordering their home tanks to be filled.[^cite_note-EIAdecember2015-63]

## On other planets

Propane was first discovered in space in 1981 in the atmosphere of Saturn's moon [Titan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titan_\(moon\)) based on spectroscopic observations from the [Voyager 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyager_1) [IRIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared_interferometer_spectrometer_and_radiometer) instrument.[^cite_note-64] This was subsequently confirmed by ground-based observations using the TEXES instrument on the [IRTF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA_Infrared_Telescope_Facility) telescope[^cite_note-65] and mapped by the CIRS instrument on [Cassini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassini%E2%80%93Huygens).[^cite_note-66]

Propane was subsequently been detected in the atmosphere of [Saturn](44474.md) using TEXES,[^cite_note-67] and predicted but unconfirmed on Jupiter, Neptune and Uranus.
