***The Wall Street Journal*** (***WSJ***), commonly known as **the *Journal***, is an American newspaper based in [Midtown Manhattan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midtown_Manhattan), New York City. It provides extensive coverage of news, especially business and finance, and operates on a subscription model that requires readers to pay for access to most articles and other content. The *Journal* is published six days a week by [Dow Jones & Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dow_Jones_&_Company), a division of [News Corp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_Corp).

As of 2025, *The* *Wall Street Journal* is the [largest newspaper in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_newspapers_in_the_United_States) by [print circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Print_circulation), with about 412,000 print subscribers. It has more than four million digital subscribers, the second-most in the nation after *[The New York Times](30680.md)*.[^cite_note-circ-2025-1] The newspaper is one of the United States' [newspapers of record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newspaper_of_record).[^cite_note-caulfield1-2][^cite_note-FrostWeingarten20172-3] The first issue of the newspaper was published on July 8, 1889.[^cite_note-4] The [editorial page of the *Journal*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Editorial_board_at_The_Wall_Street_Journal) is typically [center-right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center-right) in its positions, while its news reporting is more centrist.[^cite_note-Benkler-5][^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7][^cite_note-Vernon_2017-8][^cite_note-9] The newspaper has won 39 [Pulitzer Prizes](24230.md).[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12]

## History

### Founding and 19th century



Front page of the first issue of *The Wall Street Journal* on July 8, 1889

A predecessor to *The Wall Street Journal* was the Kiernan News Agency founded by [John J. Kiernan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_J._Kiernan) in 1869.[^cite_note-Wells-13]<sup>: 321, 324 </sup> In 1880, Kiernan hired [Charles H. Dow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_H._Dow) and [Edward D. Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Jones_\(statistician\)) as reporters. On a recommendation of [Collis Potter Huntington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collis_Potter_Huntington), Dow and Jones co-founded their own news service, [Dow Jones & Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dow_Jones_&_Company), with fellow Kiernan reporter [Charles Bergstresser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Bergstresser). Dow Jones was headquartered in the basement of 15 Wall Street, the same building as Kiernan's company next to the [New York Stock Exchange Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Stock_Exchange_Building).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage2mode2upq22johnjkiernan22_2-3\]-14]

The first products of Dow Jones & Company, the publisher of the *Journal*, were brief news bulletins, nicknamed *flimsies*, hand-delivered throughout the day to traders at the [stock exchange](28168.md).[^cite_note-late_1800s-15] In 1883, they were aggregated in a printed daily summary called the *Customers' Afternoon Letter*, sold for \$1.50 per month compared to the \$15 a month Dow Jones bulletin service.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage4mode2upq22afternoonletter22_4\]-16] Dow Jones opened its own printing press at [71 Broadway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_Building_\(Manhattan\)) in 1885.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage4mode2upq22afternoonletter22_4\]-16]

Beginning July 8, 1889, the *Afternoon Letter* was renamed *The Wall Street Journal*. The debut issue of the *Journal* was four pages long, with dimensions of 20 3/4 × 15 1/2 inches and cost of \$0.02 per copy. In its early days, the *Journal* had "a tedious, blow-by-blow account of the day's business without benefit of editing," wrote Edward E. Scharff in 1986.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage4mode2upq22afternoonletter22_4\]-16]

For nearly 40 years, the front page had a four-column format, with the middle two devoted to news briefs and the farther two filled with advertisements for brokerage services. The *Journal* focused on stories from news wires and listings of stocks and bonds, while occasionally covering sports or politics.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage4mode2upq22afternoonletter22_4\]-16] One front-page story on the debut edition of *The Wall Street Journal* was a raw wire report about the boxing match between [John L. Sullivan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_L._Sullivan) and [Jake Kilrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jake_Kilrain), with varying accounts of the fight citing *[The Boston Globe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Boston_Globe)*, the *[Baltimore American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltimore_American)*, and anonymous sources.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage4mode2upq22sullivanandkilrain22_5\]-17] Seldom did *The Wall Street Journal* publish analysis or opinion articles in its early decades.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage6mode2upq22analysiswasrare22_6\]-18] In addition to a private wire to [Boston](24437894.md), the *Journal* had reporters communicate via telegraph from Washington, Philadelphia, Chicago, Pittsburgh, [Albany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albany,_New_York), and London.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage8mode2upqalbany_8\]-19]

In 1896, the *Journal* began publishing two separate Dow Jones stock indicies, the [Dow Jones Industrial Average](47361.md) and [Dow Jones Railroad Average](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dow_Jones_Transportation_Average).[^cite_note-late_1800s-15][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage6mode2upq22twoseparateindexes22_7\]-\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage8mode2upq22oneforindustrials22_8\]-20][^cite_note-21] The first morning edition of the *Journal* was published on November 14, 1898. By the late 1890s, daily circulation reached 7,000.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage6mode2upq22analysiswasrare22_6\]-18]

Charles Dow wrote the first "Review and Outlook" column on April 21, 1899, a front-page editorial column explaining stock prices in terms of human nature; Dow's thinking would later be known as the [Dow theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dow_theory). Scharff regarded Dow's essays from 1899 to 1902 as "stock market classics".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage6mode2upq22Humannature22_6-_7\]-22]

### 20th century

In the months before his death in 1902, Dow arranged to sell Dow Jones and the *Journal* to [Clarence W. Barron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarence_W._Barron), the [Boston](24437894.md) correspondent for the *Journal* since 1889, for \$130,000 (equivalent to \$4,837,500 in 2025).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage8mode2upq22in1902at22_9\]-\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage10mode2upq22clarencewalkerbarron22_10\]-23] Because Barron had financial difficulties, his wife Jessie Waldron Barron made the \$2,500 down payment to buy Dow Jones in 1902; Clarence would first own a Dow Jones share about ten years later.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage14mode2upq22jessiewaldron22_14\]-24]

Under Barron's ownership, [Thomas F. Woodlock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_F._Woodlock) was editor of the *Journal* from 1902 to 1905.[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26] By the end of Woodlock's tenure, daily circulation for the *Journal* rose from 7,000 to 11,000.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage14mode2upq22anestimated112C00022_15\]-27] [William Peter Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Peter_Hamilton) became lead editorial writer in 1908, a time when the *Journal* began reflecting the views of Barron. Hamilton wrote what Scharff considered "daily sermons in support of free-market capitalism".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage16mode2upq22dailysermons22_16\]-28]

Barron and his predecessors were credited with creating an atmosphere of fearless, independent financial reporting—a novelty in the early days of [business journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_journalism). In 1921, *[Barron's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barron's_\(newspaper\))*, the United States's premier financial weekly, was founded.[^cite_note-Crossen-29] Scharff described the newspaper in the Barron era as "Wall Street's public defender" against regulatory efforts by the U.S. Congress.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage26mode2upq22Wallstreet27spublicdefender22_27\]-30] Circulation continued to rise, reaching 18,750 to 1920 and 52,000 briefly in 1928,[^cite_note-31] but did not exceed that of its main competitor, *[The Magazine of Wall Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Magazine_of_Wall_Street_\(The_Ticker\))*, which was the leading financial publication of the era.[^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33][^cite_note-34][^cite_note-35] Barron died in 1928, a year before [Black Tuesday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wall_Street_Crash_of_1929), the stock market crash that greatly affected the [Great Depression in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression_in_the_United_States). Barron's descendants, the [Bancroft family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bancroft_family), would continue to control the company until 2007.[^cite_note-Crossen-29]

The [Wall Street Crash of 1929](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wall_Street_Crash_of_1929) would be yet another challenge to the *Journal* on top of Barron's death. William Peter Hamilton died of pneumonia on December 9, 1929, aged 63.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage34mode2upq22pacificcoastedition22_34\]-36] Circulation of the *Journal*, having surpassed 50,000 in 1928, dropped below 28,000 in the 1930s, and the newspaper downsized from 28 to 16 pages in the 1930s as well.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage36mode2upq22numberofpageswouldalsofall22_36\]-37] Dow Jones president [Hugh Bancroft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Bancroft_\(attorney\)) retired in 1932; following his death in 1933, his widow Jane Waldron Bancroft appointed [Kenneth Craven "Casey" Hogate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_C._Hogate) as new company president.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage42mode2upq22metwithcaseyhogate22_43\]-38][^cite_note-39] Hogate envisioned expanding the scope of the *Journal* to a "more general business paper" beyond stock and bond numbers.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage50mode2upq22moregeneralbusinesspaper22_50\]-40]

Expanding westward, *The Wall Street Journal* debuted a West Coast edition on October 21, 1929, *The Wall Street Journal Pacific Coast Edition*. The *Pacific Coast Edition* focused on California businesses and replicated some items from the regular *Wall Street Journal*; however, its circulation never exceeded 3,000, and the Great Depression led numerous subscription cancellations.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage34mode2upq22pacificcoastedition22_34\],_\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage50mode2upq22pacificcoastedition22_50\]-41]

By 1931, [Bernard Kilgore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Kilgore) became news editor for *The Wall Street Journal*, having joined the *Journal* copy desk in 1929. He began writing a column for the *Pacific Coast Edition* called "Dear George", a feature explaining obscure financial topics in simpler, plain rhetoric. "Dear George" sharply contrasted with other *Wall Street Journal* articles that relied on jargon that was incomprehensible even to its own reporters. The Eastern edition of the *Journal* began carrying "Dear George", and beginning in 1932, Kilgore wrote "Dear George" three times a week from New York for the *Journal*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage50mode2upq22bombaystraddle22_51\]-\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage52mode2upq22Deargeorge22_52\]-42] Then in 1934, Kilgore began writing a daily news digest "What's News" for the *Journal* front page.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage52mode2upq22themajorstoriesoftheday22_53\]-43] Kilgore's writings attracted the attention of the White House; President [Franklin D. Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt) publicly recommended Kilgore's work about pension payments for World War I veterans and a Supreme Court decision on the [National Industrial Recovery Act of 1933](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Industrial_Recovery_Act_of_1933).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage52mode2upq22readkilgore22_53\]-44] However, the *Journal* continued to struggle financially, with circulation stagnant at 32,000 in 1940. Most editions were only 12 to 14 pages long, and Dow Jones made only \$69,000 of profits on \$2 million of revenue, mostly due to its news ticker. Scharff observed a lack of coverage about a possible U.S. role in [World War II](32927.md).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage60mode2upq22attentionwasrivetedoneurope22_60\]-45]

In the 1940s, Dow Jones took steps to restructure the *Journal*. Kilgore was named managing editor of the *Journal* in 1941 and Dow Jones CEO in 1945.[^cite_note-Crossen-29] Scharff described Kilgore's approach to journalism: "A *Journal* story had to satisfy its sophisticated readership, but it also had to be clear enough not to discourage neophytes."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage62mode2upq22satisfyitssophisticated22_63\]-\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage64mode2upq22neverdisplacethehometown22_64\]-46] Additionally, Kilgore aimed to have the newspaper appeal to a national audience, by making the East Coast and West Coast editions of the *Journal* more homogeneous.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage62mode2upq22satisfyitssophisticated22_63\]-\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage64mode2upq22neverdisplacethehometown22_64\]-46] Until printing presses and reporting bureaus could be opened on location, photographs could not be included in the *Journal*.[^cite_note-Crossen-29]

In [1947](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1947_Pulitzer_Prize), the paper won its first [Pulitzer Prize](24230.md) for William Henry Grimes's [editorials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize_for_Editorial_Writing).[^cite_note-Crossen-29] Also in May 1947, the *Journal* launched a Southwest edition based in [Dallas](53838.md).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage102mode2upqdallas_103\]-\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage102mode2upqdallas_105\]-47] The newspaper also added a new slogan in 1949: "Everywhere, Men Who Get Ahead in Business Read *The Wall Street Journal*."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage102mode2upqdallas_103\]-\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage102mode2upqdallas_105\]-47] Circulation grew by nearly four-fold, from 32,000 in 1940 to 140,000 in 1949; however, Kilgore's editorial reforms of the *Journal* had not yet entered popular understanding.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage60mode2upq22attentionwasrivetedoneurope22_60\],_\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage104mode2upq22over1402C00022_104\]-48]

The first major journalism award for the *Journal* was a [Sigma Delta Chi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_of_Professional_Journalists) public service award, for stories in late 1952 exposing links between Empire Mail Order and organized crime. These stories followed news that [Howard Hughes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Hughes) sold [RKO Pictures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RKO_Pictures) to Empire Mail Order.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage120mode2upq22howardhughes22_121\]-\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage122mode2upqrko_122\]-49] Circulation of the *Journal* continued increasing throughout the 1950s, to 205,000 in 1951, 400,000 in 1955, and over 500,000 by 1957.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage154mode2upq22halfmillionmark22_154\]-50]

[Warren H. Phillips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warren_H._Phillips) became managing editor of *The Wall Street Journal* in 1957 after being promoted from Chicago bureau manager. Phillips was Jewish, in contrast to the largely midwestern, [WASP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WASP) management of the *Journal* at the time. Phillips, a former socialist, shifted his political views in the 1950s to reflect social liberalism and fiscal conservatism.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage166mode2upq22warrenphillipsbecame22_167\]-\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage168mode2upq22tragicomicspectacle22_169\]-51] Under Phillips, the *Journal* provided in-depth coverage of the [civil rights movement](49001.md), on the grounds that it "was something that the average businessman needed to know about".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage166mode2upq22warrenphillipsbecame22_167\]-\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage168mode2upq22tragicomicspectacle22_169\]-51] For instance, *Journal* coverage of the [Little Rock Central High School integration crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Rock_Nine) went beyond the largely visual, emotional elements on newspapers and television, in depicting local citizens as supportive of integration in contrast to Governor [Orval Faubus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orval_Faubus) and other local politicians.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage170mode2upq22governorfaubushimself22_170-171\]-52]

During the [1962–1963 New York City newspaper strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1962%E2%80%931963_New_York_City_newspaper_strike), the *Journal* was the only daily newspaper to continue printing in New York City; however, after the strike, Kilgore ordered the printing presses moved from New York to [Chicopee, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicopee,_Massachusetts) effective July 1, 1963. Although Kilgore did not publicly explain his rationale then, Scharff wrote in 1986 that the move resulted from feuds between Dow Jones and the printer's union.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage118mode2upqchicopee_118-119\]-53]

By Kilgore's death in 1967, *Wall Street Journal* circulation exceeded one million.[^cite_note-Crossen-29] In 1967, Dow Jones Newswires began a major expansion outside of the United States ultimately placing its journalists in every major financial center in [Europe](9239.md), [Asia](689.md), [Latin America](18524.md), [Australia](4689264.md), and [Africa](5334607.md). In 1970, Dow Jones bought the Ottaway newspaper chain, which at the time comprised nine dailies and three Sunday newspapers. Later, the name was changed to [Dow Jones Local Media Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_Media_Group).[^cite_note-54] The first strike affecting a *Journal* printing plant was a three-day strike at [Silver Spring, Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_Spring,_Maryland), in 1967; that would be followed by a weeklong strike by truck drivers at the [South Brunswick, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Brunswick,_New_Jersey), in 1970.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage118mode2upqchicopee_118-119\]-53]

Video of U.S. President [Ronald Reagan](25433.md) interviewed by *WSJ* in the [Oval Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oval_Office) in February 1985

The period from 1971 to 1997 brought about a series of launches, acquisitions, and joint ventures, including "[Factiva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factiva)", *[The Wall Street Journal Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wall_Street_Journal_Asia)*, *[The Wall Street Journal Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wall_Street_Journal_Europe)*, the WSJ.com website, Dow Jones Indexes, [MarketWatch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MarketWatch), and "WSJ Weekend Edition". In 2007, [News Corp.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_Corporation) acquired Dow Jones.[^cite_note-55] *[WSJ.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WSJ.)*, a luxury lifestyle magazine, was launched in 2008.[^cite_note-56]

A complement to the print newspaper, *The Wall Street Journal Online*, was launched in 1996 and has allowed access only by subscription from the beginning.[^cite_note-57] A weekly (later daily) crossword edited by [Mike Shenk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mike_Shenk) was introduced in 1998.[^cite_note-58]

### 21st century



[Vladimir Putin](32817.md) with *Wall Street Journal* correspondent [Karen Elliott House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karen_Elliott_House) in 2002

In 2003, Dow Jones began to integrate reporting of the *Journal*'s print and online subscribers together in [Audit Bureau of Circulations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audit_Bureau_of_Circulations_\(North_America\)) statements.[^cite_note-59] In 2007, it was commonly believed to be the largest paid-subscription news site on the Web, with 980,000 paid subscribers.[^cite_note-Crossen-29] Since then, digital subscription has risen to 1.3 million as of September 2018, falling to number two behind *[The New York Times](30680.md)* with 3 million digital subscriptions.[^cite_note-60] In May 2008, an annual subscription to the [digital edition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_edition) of *The Wall Street Journal* cost \$119 for those who do not have subscriptions to the print edition. By June 2013, the monthly cost for a subscription to the online edition was \$22.99, or \$275.88 annually, excluding introductory offers.[^cite_note-61] Digital subscription rates increased dramatically to \$443.88 per year as its popularity increased over print, with first-time subscribers paying \$187.20 per year.[^cite_note-62]

On November 30, 2004, Oasys Mobile and *The Wall Street Journal* released an app that would allow users to access content from *The Wall Street Journal Online* via their mobile phones.[^cite_note-63]

In September 2005, the *Journal* launched a weekend edition, delivered to all subscribers, which marked a return to Saturday publication after a lapse of some 50 years. The move was designed in part to attract more consumer advertising.[^cite_note-Crossen-29]

In 2005, the *Journal* reported a readership profile of about 60 percent top management, an average income of \$191,000, an average household net worth of \$2.1 million, and an average age of 55.[^cite_note-64]

In 2007, the *Journal* launched a worldwide expansion of its website to include major foreign-language editions. The paper had also shown an interest in buying the rival *[Financial Times](136566.md)*.[^cite_note-65]

### Design changes

The nameplate is unique in having a period at the end.[^cite_note-66]

Front-page advertising in the *Journal* was re-introduced on September 5, 2006. This followed similar introductions in the European and Asian editions in late 2005.[^cite_note-67]

After presenting nearly identical front-page layouts for half a century –  always six columns, with the day's top stories in the first and sixth columns, "What's News" digest in the second and third, the "A-hed" feature story in the fourth (with 'hed' being jargon for [headline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Headline)) and themed weekly reports in the fifth column[^cite_note-68] –  the paper in 2007 decreased its [broadsheet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadsheet) width from 15 to 12 inches while keeping the length at 223⁄4 inches, to save [newsprint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newsprint) costs. News design consultant [Mario García](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mario_Garc%C3%ADa_\(designer\)) collaborated on the changes. Dow Jones said it would save \$18 million a year in newsprint costs across all *The Wall Street Journal* papers.[^cite_note-69] This move eliminated one column of print, pushing the "A-hed" out of its traditional location (though the paper now usually includes a quirky feature story on the right side of the front page, sandwiched among the lead stories).

The paper uses ink dot drawings called [hedcuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedcut), introduced in 1979 and originally created by [Kevin Sprouls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevin_Sprouls),[^cite_note-70] in addition to photographs, a method of illustration considered a consistent visual signature of the paper. the *Journal* still heavily employs the use of [caricatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caricatures), including those by illustrator [Ken Fallin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Fallin), such as when [Peggy Noonan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peggy_Noonan) memorialized then-recently deceased newsman [Tim Russert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tim_Russert).[^cite_note-71][^cite_note-72] The use of color photographs and graphics has become increasingly common in recent years with the addition of more "lifestyle" sections.

The daily was awarded by the [Society for News Design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_for_News_Design) World's Best Designed Newspaper award for 1994 and 1997.[^cite_note-73]

### News Corporation and News Corp

On May 2, 2007, [News Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_Corporation) made an unsolicited takeover bid for [Dow Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dow_Jones_&_Company), offering \$60 per share for stock that had been selling for \$36.33 per share.[^cite_note-74] The [Bancroft family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bancroft_family), which controlled more than 60% of the voting stock, at first rejected the offer, but later reconsidered its position.[^cite_note-75]

Three months later, on August 1, 2007, News Corporation and Dow Jones entered into a definitive merger agreement.[^cite_note-76] The \$5 billion sale added *The Wall Street Journal* to [Rupert Murdoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rupert_Murdoch)'s news empire, which already included [Fox News Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fox_News_Channel), [Fox Business Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fox_Business_Network), London's *[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times)*, the *[New York Post](102227.md)*, and the [Fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fox_Broadcasting_Company) [flagship station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flagship_station) [WNYW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WNYW) (Channel 5) and [MyNetworkTV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MyNetworkTV) flagship [WWOR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WWOR-TV) (Channel 9).[^cite_note-77]

On December 13, 2007, shareholders representing more than 60 percent of Dow Jones's voting stock approved the company's acquisition by News Corporation.[^cite_note-78]

In an editorial page column, publisher [L. Gordon Crovitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L._Gordon_Crovitz) said the Bancrofts and News Corporation had agreed that the *Journal*'s news and opinion sections would preserve their editorial independence from their new corporate parent.[^cite_note-Crovitz-79]

A special committee was established to oversee the paper's editorial integrity. When the managing editor [Marcus Brauchli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Brauchli) resigned on April 22, 2008, the committee said that News Corporation had violated its agreement by not notifying the committee earlier. However, Brauchli said he believed that new owners should appoint their own editor.[^cite_note-80]

A 2007 *Journal* article quoted charges that Murdoch had made and broken similar promises in the past. One large shareholder commented that Murdoch has long "expressed his personal, political and [business](39206.md) biases through his newspapers and television stations". Former *Times* assistant editor [Fred Emery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Emery_\(journalist\)) remembers an incident when "Mr. Murdoch called him into his office in March 1982 and said he was considering firing *Times* editor [Harold Evans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Evans). Mr. Emery says he reminded Mr. Murdoch of his promise that editors couldn't be fired without the independent directors' approval. 'God, you don't take all that seriously, do you?' Mr. Murdoch answered, according to Mr. Emery." Murdoch eventually forced out Evans.[^cite_note-81]

In 2011, *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)* found evidence that the *Journal* had artificially inflated its European sales numbers, by paying Executive Learning Partnership for purchasing 16% of European sales. These inflated sales numbers then enabled the *Journal* to charge similarly inflated advertising rates, as the advertisers would think that they reached more readers than they actually did. In addition, the *Journal* agreed to run "articles" featuring Executive Learning Partnership, presented as news, but effectively advertising.[^cite_note-82] The case came to light after a Belgian *Wall Street Journal* employee, [Gert Van Mol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gert_Van_Mol), informed Dow Jones CEO [Les Hinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_Hinton) about the questionable practice.[^cite_note-83] As a result, the then *Wall Street Journal Europe* CEO and Publisher Andrew Langhoff was fired after it was found out he personally pressured journalists into covering one of the newspaper's business partners involved in the issue.[^cite_note-84][^cite_note-85] Since September 2011, all the online articles that resulted from the ethical wrongdoing carry a *Wall Street Journal* disclaimer informing the readers about the circumstances in which they were created.

The *Journal*, along with its parent Dow Jones & Company, was among the businesses News Corporation spun off in 2013 as the new [News Corp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_Corp).[^cite_note-86]

In November 2016, in an effort to cut costs, the *Journal*'s editor-in-chief, [Gerard Baker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerard_Baker), announced layoffs of staff and consolidation of its print sections. The new "Business & Finance" section combined the former "Business & Tech" and "Money & Investing" sections. The new "Life & Arts" section took the place of "Personal Journal" and "Arena". In addition, the *Journal*'s "Greater New York" coverage was reduced and moved to the main section of paper.[^cite_note-87] The section was shuttered on July 9, 2021.[^cite_note-nyt-greater-ny-88]

A 2018 survey conducted by [Gallup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallup_\(company\)) and the [Knight Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight_Foundation) found that *The Wall Street Journal* was considered the third most-accurate and fourth most-unbiased news organization among the general public, tenth among [Democrats](5043544.md), and second among [Republicans](32070.md).[^cite_note-89] In an October 2018 [Simmons Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simmons_Research) survey of 38 news organizations, *The Wall Street Journal* was ranked the most trusted news organization by Americans. [Joshua Benton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joshua_Benton) of the [Nieman Journalism Lab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nieman_Journalism_Lab) at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University) wrote that the paper's "combination of respected news pages and conservative editorial pages seem to be a magic formula for generating trust across the ideological spectrum."[^cite_note-90]

From 2019 through 2022, the *Journal* partnered with [Facebook](7529378.md) to provide content for the social-media site's "News Tab". Facebook paid the *Journal* in excess of \$10 million during that period, terminating the relationship as part of a broader shift away from news content.[^cite_note-91]

On June 13, 2022, the *Journal* launched a [product review website](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Review_site) called *Buy Side*.[^cite_note-axios220622-92] The website remains free and has a distinct team from the *Journal* newsroom.[^cite_note-axios220622-92]

In February 2024, the *Journal* laid off about 20 employees, primarily economics reporters based in [Washington, D.C.](108956.md) Moving forward, those beats will be covered by the newspaper's New York-based business team.[^cite_note-93] The next month the paper laid off another five people from its standards and ethics team.[^cite_note-94] In April, the paper laid off at least 11 people from its video and social media desks.[^cite_note-95] In May, the *Journal* cut six editorial staff positions from its [Hong Kong](13404.md) bureau and another two reporter jobs in [Singapore](27318.md). Moving forward the paper will shift its focus in the region from Hong Kong to Singapore with new the creation of several new jobs at that bureau.[^cite_note-96] More staff were laid off a few weeks later amid further restructuring, including at least eight reporters.[^cite_note-97]

## Features and operations

Since 1980, the *Journal* has been published in multiple sections. The *Journal* increased its maximum issue length to 96 pages, including up to 24 color pages, in 2002.[^cite_note-110]

As of 2012, *The Wall Street Journal* had a global news staff of around 2,000 journalists in 85 news bureaus across 51 countries.[^cite_note-djfactsheet-111][^cite_note-112] As of 2012, it had 26 printing plants.[^cite_note-djfactsheet-111] Its Asia headquarters is in Hong Kong, but will move to Singapore after it stated it would do so in 2024.[^cite_note-113]

Regularly scheduled sections are:

- Section One: Every day; corporate news, as well as political and economic reporting and the opinion pages

- Marketplace: Monday through Friday; coverage of health, technology, [media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_media), and [marketing](59252.md) industries (the second section was launched June 23, 1980)

- Money and Investing: Every day; covers and analyzes international financial markets (the third section was launched October 3, 1988)

- Personal Journal: Published Tuesday through Thursday; covers personal investments, [careers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Career) and cultural pursuits (the section was introduced April 9, 2002)

- Off Duty: Published Saturdays in WSJ Weekend; focuses on fashion, food, design, travel and gear/tech. The section was launched September 25, 2010.

- Review: Published Saturdays in WSJ Weekend; focuses on essays, commentary, reviews and ideas. The section was launched September 25, 2010.

- Mansion: Published Fridays, focuses on high-end real estate. The section was launched October 5, 2012.

- WSJ Magazine: Launched in 2008 as a quarterly, this luxury magazine supplement distributed within the U.S., European and Asian editions of *The Wall Street Journal* grew to 12 issues per year in 2014.

In addition, several columnists contribute regular features to the *Journal* opinion page:

- Mondays: Americas by [Mary O'Grady](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_O'Grady)

- Wednesdays: Business World by [Holman W. Jenkins Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holman_W._Jenkins_Jr.)

- Thursdays: Wonder Land by [Daniel Henninger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Henninger)

- Fridays: Potomac Watch by [Kimberley Strassel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kimberley_Strassel)

- Weekend Edition: Rule of Law, The Weekend Interview (variety of authors), Declarations by [Peggy Noonan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peggy_Noonan)

In addition to editorials and columns from the printed newspaper, wsj.com carries two daily web-only opinion columns:

- *Best of the Web Today* by [James Taranto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Taranto), the editor of the former OpinionJournal.com website (no subscription required).

  [^cite_note-114]

- *Political Diary* edited by [Holman W. Jenkins Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holman_W._Jenkins_Jr.) and featuring [John Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Fund) (separate subscription required).

In addition to these regular opinion pieces, on Fridays the *Journal* publishes a religion-themed op-ed, titled "Houses of Worship", written by a different author each week. Authors range from the [Dalai Lama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalai_Lama) to cardinals.

### *Style & Substance*

***Style & Substance*** is a monthly bulletin on [English language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language) usage. Each issue discusses specific language issues from the perspective of the WSJ's copyeditors according to the newspaper's internal [stylebook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Style_guide). The first issue of *Style & Substance* was published in 1987 under the direction of front page editor Paul R. Martin.[^cite_note-115] Front page editor Bill Power and online editor Jennifer Hicks succeeded him in 2013.[^cite_note-116] The *Journal* announces major stylistic changes through the bulletin, such as the newspaper's abandonment of [courtesy titles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courtesy_title) in 2023.[^cite_note-117]

### *WSJ.*

Main article: [*WSJ Magazine*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WSJ_Magazine)

## Editorial board

Main article: [Editorial board at *The Wall Street Journal*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Editorial_board_at_The_Wall_Street_Journal)

*The Wall Street Journal* editorial board members oversee the *Journal*'s editorial page, dictating the tone and direction of the newspaper's opinion section. *The Wall Street Journal* does not provide details on the exact duties of board members.

Every Saturday and Sunday, three editorial page writers and host [Paul Gigot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Gigot), editor of the editorial page, appear on [Fox News Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fox_News_Channel)'s *[Journal Editorial Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_Editorial_Report)*, where they discuss current issues with a variety of guests. As editors of the editorial page, [Vermont C. Royster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vermont_C._Royster) (1958–1971) and [Robert Bartley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Bartley) (1972–2000) provided a [conservative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservatism_in_the_United_States) interpretation of the news on a daily basis.[^cite_note-vetter-122]

Contrasts have been noted between the *Journal*'s news reporting and its editorial pages.[^cite_note-Smith_10/25/2020-123] "While *Journal* reporters keep busy informing readers," wrote one reporter in 1982, "*Journal* editorial writers put forth views that often contradict the paper's best reporting and news analysis."[^cite_note-124] Two summaries published in 1995 by the progressive blog [Fairness and Accuracy in Reporting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairness_and_Accuracy_in_Reporting), and in 1996 by the *[Columbia Journalism Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_Journalism_Review)*[^cite_note-125] criticized the *Journal*'s editorial page for inaccuracy during the 1980s and 1990s. One reference work in 2011 described the editorial pages as "rigidly neoconservative" while noting that the news coverage "has enjoyed a sterling reputation among readers of all political stripes".[^cite_note-126]

In July 2020, more than 280 *Journal* journalists and Dow Jones staff members wrote a letter to new publisher [Almar Latour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almar_Latour) to criticize the opinion pages' "lack of [fact-checking](595273.md) and transparency, and its apparent disregard for evidence", adding that "opinion articles often make assertions that are contradicted by *WSJ* reporting."[^cite_note-wsj-opinion-news-127][^cite_note-128] The editorial board responded that its opinion pages "won't wilt under cancel-culture pressure" and that the objective of the editorial content is to be independent of the *Journal*'s news content and offer alternative views to "the uniform progressive views that dominate nearly all of today's media."[^cite_note-129] The board's response did not address issues regarding fact-checking that had been raised in the letter.[^cite_note-130]

### Editorial positions

#### Economic

In the 1900s, the *Journal* supported the [antitrust](92025.md) efforts of President [Theodore Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Roosevelt). However, after the ownership change from Dow to Barron, the *Journal* became far more unequivocally supportive of free-market economics in the 1920s.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage16mode2upq22dailysermons22_16\]-28] One editorial in March 1928 criticized Congressional efforts to regulate the securities industry: "People who know nothing about credit, surplus bank funds, collateral, call loans or anything else germane to the question profess to be terrified when the Stock Exchange loans attain the figure of \$4 billion or more."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage28mode2upq22Peoplewhoknownothing22_28-29\]-131]

On April 14, 1932, the *Journal* published a commentary by former editor [Thomas F. Woodlock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_F._Woodlock) criticizing the [Glass–Steagall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glass%E2%80%93Steagall_legislation) banking regulation bill: "There are those who cannot endure the sight of autonomous securities markets beyond the control of legislatures, bureaucrats, and, in fact, of courts."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage36mode2upq22numberofpageswouldalsofall22_36\]-37]

In the 1980s, the newspaper's editorial page was particularly influential as the leading voice for [supply-side economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supply-side_economics). Under the editorship of [Robert L. Bartley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_L._Bartley), it expounded at length on economic concepts such as the [Laffer curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laffer_curve), and how a decrease in certain marginal tax rates and the [capital gains tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_gains_tax_in_the_United_States) could allegedly increase overall tax revenue by generating more economic activity.[^cite_note-bartley-obit-132]

In the economic argument of [exchange rate regimes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange_rate_regime) (one of the most divisive issues among economists), the *Journal* has a tendency to support [fixed exchange rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_exchange_rate) over [floating exchange rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating_exchange_rate).[^cite_note-133]

#### Political



[Mark Rutte](4321056.md) (on right), prime minister of the [Netherlands](21148.md), being interviewed by The *Journal* in 2011

The *Journal*'s editorial pages and columns, run separately from the news pages, have a [conservative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservatism_in_the_United_States) bent and are highly influential in establishment conservative circles.[^cite_note-Vernon_2017-8] Despite this, the *Journal* refrains from endorsing candidates and has not endorsed a candidate since 1928.[^cite_note-134]

The editorial board has long argued for a pro-business [immigration policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration_policy_of_the_United_States).[^cite_note-135]

The *Journal*'s editorial page was seen as critical of many aspects of [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama)'s presidency. In particular, it has been a prominent critic of the [Affordable Care Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patient_Protection_and_Affordable_Care_Act) legislation passed in 2010, and has featured many opinion columns attacking various aspects of the bill.[^cite_note-136] The *Journal*'s editorial page has also criticized the [Obama administration's energy policies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_policy_of_the_Obama_administration) and foreign policy.[^cite_note-137][^cite_note-138][^cite_note-139]

On October 25, 2017, the editorial board called for Special Counsel [Robert Mueller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Mueller) to resign from the investigation into [Russian interference in the 2016 United States elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_interference_in_the_2016_United_States_elections) and accused [Hillary Clinton's 2016 presidential campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hillary_Clinton_2016_presidential_campaign) of colluding with Russia.[^cite_note-140][^cite_note-141] In December 2017, the editorial board repeated its calls for Mueller's resignation.[^cite_note-Business_Insider-142][^cite_note-Frej_2017-143] The editorials by the editorial board caused fractures within *The Wall Street Journal*, as reporters say that the editorials undermine the paper's credibility.[^cite_note-Business_Insider-142][^cite_note-Frej_2017-143][^cite_note-144]

In October 2021, the Journal published a letter from former President Donald Trump in the Letters to the Editor section of the editorial pages. Other news sources described the contents of the letter as false and debunked claims about the [2020 presidential election](48410011.md).[^cite_note-145][^cite_note-146][^cite_note-147] The next day, the editorial board published their own critique of Trump's letter.[^cite_note-148]

#### Scientific

The *Journal* editorial pages were described as a "forum for [climate change denial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_denial)" in 2011 due to columns that attacked climate scientists and accused them of engaging in fraud.[^cite_note-149][^cite_note-150] A 2011 study found that the *Journal* was alone among major American print news media in how, mainly in its editorial pages, it adopted a [false balance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_balance) that overplayed the uncertainty in climate science or denied anthropogenic climate change altogether.[^cite_note-Feldman-151] That year, the [Associated Press](18935732.md) described the *Journal*'s editorial pages as "a place friendly to climate change skeptics".[^cite_note-152] In 2013, the editorial board and other opinion writers vocally criticized President [Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama)'s plan to address climate change, mostly without mentioning climate science.[^cite_note-153] A 2015 study found *The Wall Street Journal* was the newspaper that was least likely to present negative [effects of global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effects_of_global_warming) among several newspapers. It was also the most likely to present negative economic framing when discussing [climate change mitigation](2119179.md) policies, tending to take the stance that the cost of such policies generally outweighs their benefit.[^cite_note-Feldman-151]

[Climate Feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_Feedback), a fact-checking website on media coverage of climate science, determined that multiple opinion articles range between "low" and "very low" in terms of scientific credibility.[^cite_note-154][^cite_note-155] The [Partnership for Responsible Growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partnership_for_Responsible_Growth) stated in 2016 that 14% of the guest editorials on climate change presented the results of "mainstream climate science", while the majority did not. The Partnership also determined that none of the 201 editorials concerning climate change that were published in *The Wall Street Journal* since 1997 conceded that the burning of [fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel) is the main [cause of climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cause_of_climate_change).[^cite_note-156]

In the 1980s and 1990s, the *Journal* published numerous opinion columns opposing and misrepresenting the scientific consensus on the harms of [second-hand smoke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passive_smoking),[^cite_note-157][^cite_note-158][^cite_note-159] [acid rain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_rain), and [ozone depletion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone_depletion),[^cite_note-160] in addition to public policy efforts to curb pesticides and asbestos.[^cite_note-handful-161][^cite_note-162][^cite_note-163][^cite_note-164][^cite_note-165] The *Journal* later recognized that efforts to curb acid rain through cap-and-trade had been successful, a decade after the Clean Air Act Amendments.[^cite_note-166]

The *Journal*'s reporting has been described as "[small-c conservatism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small-c_conservatism)".[^cite_note-Smith_10/25/2020-123] Some of the *Journal*'s former editors and reporters, such as [Sarah Ellison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Ellison) (a *Washington Post* reporter as of October 2025),[^cite_note-167] say that the paper has adopted a more conservative tone since [Rupert Murdoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rupert_Murdoch)'s purchase.[^cite_note-168][^cite_note-169][^cite_note-170]

### Bias in news pages

#### Pre-Murdoch ownership

Its editors stress the independence and impartiality of their reporters.[^cite_note-Crovitz-79] According to CNN in 2007, the *Journal*'s "newsroom staff has a reputation for non-partisan reporting."[^cite_note-171] Ben Smith of the *New York Times* described the *Journal*'s news reporting as "small-c \[conservative]", and noted that its readership leans further to the right than that of other major newspapers.[^cite_note-Smith_10/25/2020-123]

Under the ownership of Clarence W. Barron, the *Journal* generally restricted editorializing to its opinion pages, but a 1922 series of news articles described the [organized labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organized_labor) movement as having "one of the most sordid records of humanity".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage16mode2upq22dailysermons22_16-17\]-172]

In a 2004 study, Tim Groseclose and [Jeffrey Milyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeffrey_Milyo) argue the *Journal*'s news pages have a pro-liberal bias because they more often quote liberal think tanks. They calculated the ideological attitude of news reports in 20 media outlets by counting the frequency they cited particular [think tanks](37101.md) and comparing that to the frequency that legislators cited the same think tanks. They found that the news reporting of the *Journal* was the most liberal (more liberal than [NPR](29697232.md) or *[The New York Times](30680.md)*). The study did not factor in editorials.[^cite_note-173] [Mark Liberman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Liberman) criticized the model used to calculate bias in the study and argued that the model unequally affected liberals and conservatives and that "the model starts with a very peculiar assumption about the relationship between political opinion and the choice of authorities to cite." The authors assume that "think tank ideology ... only matters to liberals."[^cite_note-174]

The company's planned and eventual acquisition by [News Corp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_Corp) in 2007 led to significant media criticism and discussion about whether the news pages would exhibit a rightward slant under [Rupert Murdoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rupert_Murdoch).[^cite_note-175] An August 1, 2007, editorial responded to the questions by asserting that Murdoch intended to "maintain the values and integrity of the *Journal*".[^cite_note-176]

#### During Trump presidency

In 2016 and 2017, the *Journal* leadership under Baker came under fire from staff members and media critics, who viewed the paper's coverage of President [Donald Trump](4848272.md) as too timid.[^cite_note-Ember-177] Particularly controversial was the *Journal*'s November 2016 front-page headline that repeated Trump's false claim that "millions of people" had voted illegally in the [election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_elections,_2016), only noting that the statement was "without corroboration".[^cite_note-Ember-177]

Also controversial was a January 2017 note from Baker to *Journal* editors, directing them to avoid using the phrase "seven majority-Muslim countries" when writing about [Trump's executive order on travel and immigration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_13769); Baker later sent a follow-up note "clarifying that there was 'no ban'" on the phrase, "but that the publication should 'always be careful that this term is not offered as the only description of the countries covered under the ban.'"[^cite_note-Ember-177]

At a town-hall-style meeting with *Journal* staff in February 2017, Baker defended the paper's coverage, saying that it was objective and protected the paper from being "dragged into the political process" through a dispute with the Trump administration.[^cite_note-Ember-177]

On February 19, 2020, China announced the revoking of the press credentials of three *Wall Street Journal* reporters based in Beijing. China accused the paper of failing to apologize for publishing articles that criticized China's efforts to fight the COVID-19 pandemic, and failing to investigate and punish those responsible.[^cite_note-178]

In June 2020, following the [murder of George Floyd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murder_of_George_Floyd) and [subsequent protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Floyd_protests), journalists at the *Journal* sent a letter to editor in chief [Matt Murray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matt_Murray_\(journalist\)) demanding changes to the way the paper covers race, policing and finance. The reporters stated that they "frequently meet resistance when trying to reflect the accounts and voices of workers, residents or customers, with some editors voicing heightened skepticism of those sources' credibility compared with executives, government officials or other entities".[^cite_note-179]

## Notable stories

The *Journal* has won 39 [Pulitzer Prizes](24230.md) in its history. Staff journalists who led some of the newspaper's best-known coverage teams have later published books that summarized and extended their reporting.

### 1939: World War I battleships

In 1939, [Vermont C. Royster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vermont_C._Royster) wrote a series of articles showing the length of time required for [World War I battleships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_battleships_of_World_War_I) to be ready for duty. One story summarized the process of manufacturing armor plates. The U.S. Navy accused Royster, then a Naval Reserve officer, of using classified information for his reporting. Royster said that he obtained the information from *[Encyclopedia Britannica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclopedia_Britannica)*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage58mode2upq22worldwaribattleship22_59\]-\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage60mode2upqbritannica_60\]-180]

### 1952: Empire Mail Order

In October 1952, the Chicago bureau of the *Journal* received a press release announcing that [Howard Hughes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Hughes) sold his controlling interest in [RKO Pictures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RKO_Pictures) to the Empire Mail Order Company. Managing editor Henry Gemmill led reporting efforts that resulted in articles in late 1952 exposing links between Empire Mail Order and organized crime. Following these stories, Hughes canceled the RKO sale. These stories also led to the first major industry award for the *Journal*, a [Sigma Delta Chi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_of_Professional_Journalists) public service award for these stories.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage120mode2upq22howardhughes22_121\]-\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage122mode2upqrko_122\]-49]

### 1954: Automobile models

On May 28, 1954, the *Journal* published a front-page story by John Williams revealing designs of 1955 automobiles by [Ford](30433662.md), [Pontiac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontiac_\(automobile\)), and other auto makers. Williams had talked to factory workers and auto executives for several weeks. The story revealed that the cars would have larger engines and wrap-around windshields. The inclusion of drawings was notable for being a rare use of graphics by the *Journal* of the time. Nearly a week later, [General Motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Motors) canceled its nearly \$250,000 in advertising with the *Journal*; GM president [Harlow Curtice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harlow_Curtice) accused the *Journal* of copyright infringement and breach of confidentiality.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScharff1986\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage126mode2upq22Williamsdidnotattempt22_126\]-\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsworldlypowermaki0000schapage126mode2upq22may28195422_127\]-181] [Richard Tofel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Tofel) of [ProPublica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ProPublica) commented on this story in 2015: "The little-remembered incident helped establish the notion that news organizations could and should preserve their independence from advertisers."[^cite_note-182]

### 1973: Spiro Agnew bribery

On August 7, 1973, in an article by [Jerry Landauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerry_Landauer), the *Journal* was the first publication to report that U.S. Vice President [Spiro Agnew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiro_Agnew) was under federal investigation on bribery, extortion, and tax fraud charges. Agnew released a statement confirming the investigation on the night before the *Journal* article was published.[^cite_note-183][^cite_note-184] After pleading no contest to one charge of income tax evasion, Agnew was sentenced to a \$10,000 fine and three-year suspended jail sentence. On the same day of his sentencing, Agnew resigned as vice president on October 10, 1973.[^cite_note-185]

### 1984: CIA mining in Nicaragua

See also: [CIA activities in Nicaragua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CIA_activities_in_Nicaragua)

On April 6, 1984, the *Journal* first revealed, in a story by David Rogers, that the [Central Intelligence Agency](5183633.md) had placed [acoustic mines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acoustic_mine) on harbors in Nicaragua, following initial belief that opponents of the [Sandinista](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandinista) government had been responsible.[^cite_note-Cannon-186][^cite_note-187] As [Lou Cannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lou_Cannon) recounted in his 1991 book *President Reagan: The Role of a Lifetime*, this story led to "an international outcry".[^cite_note-Cannon-186] Nearly week after this *Journal* story, in a speech on the Senate floor and a stern letter to CIA director [William J. Casey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_J._Casey), U.S. Senator [Barry Goldwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Goldwater) (Republican of Arizona) condemned the lack of disclosure by the Reagan administration about the mining actions.[^cite_note-188][^cite_note-189] By an 84–12 margin, the Senate passed a "sense of the Congress" resolution introduced by Senator [Ted Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ted_Kennedy) (Democrat of Massachusetts) "against the mining of Nicaraguan ports and the withdrawal of World Court jurisdiction over Central America."[^cite_note-190]

### 1987: RJR Nabisco buyout

In 1987, a bidding war ensued between several financial firms for tobacco and food giant [RJR Nabisco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RJR_Nabisco). Bryan Burrough and John Helyar documented the events in more than two dozen *Journal* articles. Burrough and Helyar later used these articles as the basis of a bestselling book, *[Barbarians at the Gate: The Fall of RJR Nabisco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbarians_at_the_Gate:_The_Fall_of_RJR_Nabisco)*, which was turned into a [film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbarians_at_the_Gate_\(film\)) for HBO.

### 1988: Insider trading

In the 1980s, then-*Journal* reporter [James B. Stewart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_B._Stewart) brought national attention to the illegal practice of [insider trading](15368.md). He was awarded the [Pulitzer Prize](24230.md) in explanatory journalism in 1988, which he shared with [Daniel Hertzberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Hertzberg),[^cite_note-191] who went on to serve as the paper's senior deputy managing editor before resigning in 2009. Stewart expanded on this theme in his 1991 book, *[Den of Thieves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Den_of_Thieves_\(Stewart_book\))*.[^cite_note-192]

### 1997: AIDS treatment

David Sanford, a Page One features editor who was infected with [HIV](14170.md) in 1982 in a bathhouse, wrote a front-page personal account of how, with the assistance of improved treatments for HIV, he went from planning his death to planning his retirement.[^cite_note-193] He and six other reporters wrote about the new treatments, political and economic issues, and won the 1997 [Pulitzer Prize for National Reporting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize_for_National_Reporting) about [AIDS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS).[^cite_note-194]

### 2000: Enron

[Jonathan Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Weil), a reporter at the Dallas bureau of *The Wall Street Journal*, is credited with first breaking the story of financial abuses at [Enron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enron) in September 2000.[^cite_note-195] [Rebecca Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebecca_Smith_\(journalist\)) and [John R. Emshwiller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_R._Emshwiller) reported on the story regularly,[^cite_note-196] and wrote a book, *24 Days*. In October 2021, the *Journal* released Bad Bets, a podcast that recounted the papers reporting on Enron.[^cite_note-197]

### 2001: 9/11

The *Journal* claims to have sent the first news report, on the Dow Jones wire, of a plane crashing into the [World Trade Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Trade_Center_\(1973%E2%80%932001\)) on [September 11, 2001](5058690.md).[^cite_note-198] Its headquarters, at [One World Financial Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_World_Financial_Center), was severely damaged by the collapse of the World Trade Center just across the street.[^cite_note-The_Eye_of_the_Storm-199] Top editors worried that they might miss publishing the first issue for the first time in the paper's 112-year history. They relocated to a makeshift office at an editor's home, while sending most of the staff to Dow Jones's [South Brunswick Township, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Brunswick_Township,_New_Jersey), corporate campus.[^cite_note-Harris_2011-200] The paper was on the stands the next day, albeit in scaled-down form. The front page included a first-hand account of the Twin Towers' collapse written by then-Foreign Editor John Bussey.[^cite_note-The_Eye_of_the_Storm-199] Holed up in a ninth-floor office next to the towers, he phoned in live reports to [CNBC](223441.md) as the towers burned.[^cite_note-The_Eye_of_the_Storm-199] He narrowly escaped serious injury when the first tower collapsed, shattering all the windows in the *Journal*'s offices and filling them with dust and debris. The *Journal* won a [2002 Pulitzer Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2002_Pulitzer_Prize) in [Breaking News Reporting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize_for_Breaking_News_Reporting) for that day's stories.[^cite_note-201][^cite_note-Harris_2011-200]

The *Journal* subsequently conducted a worldwide investigation of the causes and significance of 9/11, using contacts it had developed while covering business in the Arab world. In [Kabul, Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabul,_Afghanistan), reporters Alan Cullison and Andrew Higgins bought a pair of looted computers that [Al Qaeda](1921.md) leaders had used to plan assassinations, chemical and biological attacks, and mundane daily activities. The encrypted files were decrypted and translated.[^cite_note-202][^cite_note-203] It was during this coverage that terrorists kidnapped and killed *Journal* reporter [Daniel Pearl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Pearl).

### 2007: Stock option scandal

In 2007, the paper won the [Pulitzer Prize for Public Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize_for_Public_Service), for exposing companies that illegally [backdate stock options](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Options_backdating) they awarded executives to increase their value.

### 2015–present: Theranos investigation

In 2015, a report written by the *Journal*'s [John Carreyrou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Carreyrou) alleged that blood testing company [Theranos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theranos)' technology was faulty and founder [Elizabeth Holmes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Holmes) was misleading investors.[^cite_note-204][^cite_note-Bilton-205][^cite_note-206] According to *[Vanity Fair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanity_Fair_\(magazine\))*, "a damning report published in *The Wall Street Journal* had alleged that the company was, in effect, a sham—that its vaunted core technology was actually faulty and that Theranos administered almost all of its blood tests using competitors' equipment."[^cite_note-Bilton-205] The *Journal* has subsequently published several more reports questioning Theranos' and Holmes' credibility.[^cite_note-207][^cite_note-208] In May 2018, Carreyrou released a book about Theranos, *[Bad Blood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Blood:_Secrets_and_Lies_in_a_Silicon_Valley_Startup)*.[^cite_note-209]

[Rupert Murdoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rupert_Murdoch)—at the time a major investor in Theranos and owner of the *Journal*—lost approximately \$100 million in his investments in Theranos.[^cite_note-210]

### 2018–present: Investigation into Stormy Daniels payment

Main article: [Stormy Daniels–Donald Trump scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stormy_Daniels%E2%80%93Donald_Trump_scandal)

On January 12, 2018, [Michael Rothfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Rothfeld) and [Joe Palazzolo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Palazzolo) reported in *The Wall Street Journal* that during the [2016 presidential campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_United_States_presidential_election), then-candidate [Donald Trump](4848272.md)'s personal lawyer, [Michael Cohen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Cohen_\(lawyer\)) coordinated a \$130,000 payment to [Stormy Daniels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stormy_Daniels) for her silence regarding an alleged affair. In subsequent reports, the method of payment and many other details were extensively covered. In April of that year, [FBI](11127.md) agents stormed Cohen's home, seizing records related to the transaction.[^cite_note-211] On August 21, 2018, Cohen pleaded guilty to eight counts including campaign finance violations in connection with the Daniels payment.[^cite_note-212] The coverage earned the *Journal* the 2019 [Pulitzer Prize for National Reporting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize_for_National_Reporting).[^cite_note-213]

### 2021: Facebook company files leak

This section is an excerpt from [2021 Facebook leak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Facebook_leak).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=2021_Facebook_leak\&action=edit)]

In 2021, an internal [document leak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Document_leak) from [Facebook](7529378.md) (now [Meta Platforms](62420226.md), or Meta) showed it was aware of harmful societal effects from its platforms, yet persisted in prioritizing profit over addressing these harms. The leak, released by [whistleblower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whistleblower) [Frances Haugen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frances_Haugen), resulted in reporting from *The Wall Street Journal* in September, as [*The Facebook Files*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Facebook_leak) series from journalist [Jeff Horwitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeff_Horwitz), as well as the *Facebook Papers*, by a consortium of news outlets the next months.

Primarily, the reports revealed that, based on internally commissioned studies, the company was fully aware of negative impacts on teenage users of [Instagram](31591547.md), and the contribution of [Facebook](7529378.md) activity to violence in developing countries. Other takeaways of the leak include the impact of the company's platforms on spreading false information, and Facebook's policy of promoting inflammatory posts. Furthermore, Facebook was fully aware that harmful content was being pushed through Facebook algorithms reaching young users. The types of content included posts promoting [anorexia nervosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anorexia_nervosa) and [self-harm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-harm) photos.

In October 2021, [Whistleblower Aid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whistleblower_Aid) filed eight anonymous whistleblower complaints with the [U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Securities_and_Exchange_Commission) (SEC) on behalf of Haugen alleging [securities fraud](2902209.md) by the company, after Haugen leaked the company documents the previous month.[^cite_note-2021_Facebook_leak_Zubrow_Gavrilovic_Ortiz_10-4-2021-214][^cite_note-215][^cite_note-216] After publicly revealing her identity on *[60 Minutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/60_Minutes)*,[^cite_note-2021_Facebook_leak_Pelley_10-4-2021-217][^cite_note-218] Haugen testified before the [U.S. Senate Commerce Subcommittee on Consumer Protection, Product Safety, and Data Security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Senate_Commerce_Subcommittee_on_Consumer_Protection,_Product_Safety,_and_Data_Security) about the content of the leaked documents and the complaints.[^cite_note-219] After the company renamed itself as Meta Platforms,[^cite_note-220] Whistleblower Aid filed two additional securities fraud complaints with the SEC against the company on behalf of Haugen in February 2022.[^cite_note-221]

In response to the media fallout, Facebook executives went on press tours to express Facebook's position amidst the frenzy.[^cite_note-222] Facebook also did internal damage control with employees through in-person sessions and memos.[^cite_note-223] The leak was dramatized in the upcoming film *[The Social Reckoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Social_Reckoning)* (2026).[^cite_note-224]

## Controversies

### Criminal conviction for anti-union activity in Hong Kong

In June 2024 *Wall Street Journal* editors learned that *WSJ* journalist [Selina Cheng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selina_Cheng) was a candidate for the leadership of the [Hong Kong Journalists Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong_Journalists_Association), a local press union. Cheng's editor demanded that she withdraw from the election and from participation in the union. However, the right to stand for election and participate in a union without employer consent are established under Hong Kong employment law. Cheng did not withdraw and was elected to the leadership role.[^cite_note-225][^cite_note-226][^cite_note-227][^cite_note-228] In July 2024, the paper's chief editor Gordon Fairclough travelled to Hong Kong and fired her from her role at the *WSJ*. In response to press queries, the *WSJ* declined to comment on Cheng's case except to acknowledge there had been "restructuring". Cheng responded that the restructuring was a layoff of one person.[^cite_note-Cheng2024-229]

The paper's action against Cheng attracted criticism from media organisations, press unions and human rights proponents across the globe.[^cite_note-Walden_2024-230][^cite_note-231] Chinese state media, meanwhile, celebrated her sacking, with the *[Global Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Times)*, a tabloid owned by the [Chinese Communist Party](7175.md), calling the press union "a malignant tumour that harms the city's safety and security".[^cite_note-Walden_2024-230][^cite_note-232] Asked by the *[South China Morning Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_China_Morning_Post)* about his views about the reason for the dismissal, Hong Kong's security minister [Chris Tang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chris_Tang) mentioned what he saw as the possibility that the journal might have wanted not to be associated with the press union, the latter being in his view "not a well-recognized association".[^cite_note-233] Cheng said that the *Wall Street Journal* had fired her to avoid being seen as advocating press freedom in the city.[^cite_note-Cheng2024-229] In July 2024, the [NewsGuild-CWA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NewsGuild-CWA) and Independent Association of Publishers' Employees, the union for [Dow Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dow_Jones_&_Company), expressed their support for Cheng.[^cite_note-234] The latter launched a petition calling on *WSJ* to reinstate Cheng, which has garnered 335 employee signatures in support.[^cite_note-235]

In November 2024, former *Wall Street Journal* reporter [Selina Cheng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selina_Cheng) announced she would pursue legal action against Dow Jones after mediation attempts proved unsuccessful.[^cite_note-236] Cheng had filed a complaint with Hong Kong's [Labour Department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_Department), which investigated but declined to prosecute after seeking legal advice from the [Department of Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Justice_\(Hong_Kong\)).[^cite_note-scmp-private-237]

In February 2025, Cheng launched a [private prosecution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_prosecution) against Dow Jones Publishing Co (Asia) Inc. at the [Eastern Magistrates' Courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Magistrates'_Courts?action=edit\&redlink=1).[^cite_note-scmp-private-237] The company faced two charges under Hong Kong's [Employment Ordinance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Employment_Ordinance_\(Hong_Kong\)?action=edit\&redlink=1): one count of doing an act calculated to prevent or deter an employee from exercising union participation rights, and one count of terminating employment over union participation. Dow Jones pleaded not guilty to both charges, each carrying a maximum fine of HK\$100,000.[^cite_note-ap-trial-238]

Cheng was represented by Senior Counsel Nigel Kat and barrister Nicklaus Pannu-Yuon, with solicitor Adam Paul Clermont as instructing solicitor.[^cite_note-ap-trial-238] Dow Jones was represented by Senior Counsel Benson Tsoi. The trial was heard before Principal Magistrate David Cheung over twelve days.[^cite_note-hkfp-verdict-239]

During the trial, Cheng testified that her supervisor had told her that her participation in the [Hong Kong Journalists Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong_Journalists_Association) election was "problematic" and that the matter needed to be discussed with *Wall Street Journal* management in New York and Dow Jones in-house lawyers.[^cite_note-ap-trial-238] The court heard that a Dow Jones human resources director had informed Cheng by email that she "did not seek, and will not receive the company's approval" to pursue the HKJA chair role.[^cite_note-hkfp-verdict-239] The defence argued that the prosecution had not proven the individuals involved acted as the "directing mind and will" of the company, contending that any offence, if proven, would lie against the individuals rather than the corporate entity.[^cite_note-hkfp-verdict-239] Dow Jones also accused Cheng of abusing the criminal process and acting in bad faith, pointing to emails showing she had demanded HK\$3 million as settlement or reinstatement with a formal apology. Cheng denied that financial compensation was her primary motivation, stating she wanted to hold employers accountable for violations of the law.[^cite_note-240]

On 10 September 2026, Principal Magistrate David Cheung convicted Dow Jones of the first charge of deterring Cheng from exercising her union participation rights. The court acquitted the company on the second charge of terminating Cheng's employment over her union role, with the magistrate finding that the defence had raised sufficient reasonable doubt, as the possibility that the company dismissed Cheng as part of a genuine corporate restructuring could not be ruled out beyond a reasonable doubt.[^cite_note-ap-conviction-241][^cite_note-reuters-conviction-242] Speaking to reporters after the hearing, Cheng said her case had brought awareness of union suppression in Hong Kong and that employers had no right to require employees to consult them before joining a union.[^cite_note-reuters-conviction-242] Sentencing is expected at a later date. The Wall Street Journal faces a maximum fine of HK\$100,000.[^cite_note-ap-conviction-241] Dow Jones did not immediately respond to a request for comment following the verdict.[^cite_note-reuters-conviction-242]

[Hong Kong centrist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centrism_in_Hong_Kong) lawmaker [Tik Chi-yuen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tik_Chi-yuen) criticized the *Wall Street Journal* for [self-censorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-censorship) and fear of offending the authorities, which seriously impacts press freedom in Hong Kong.[^cite_note-243] [Ben Lam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Lam_\(politician\)), a former [Mong Kok East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mong_Kok_East_\(constituency\)) politician residing in the UK, described *WSJ*'s actions as supporting authoritarianism and compared their chief editor to [Walter Duranty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Duranty).[^cite_note-244]

In a series of articles about News Corp, Australian online news outlet [Crikey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crikey) described Cheng's firing as part of "turmoil" at the *WSJ* and that "the global purging of newsrooms is as much about creating a politically correct workforce of reliable journalistic cadres as it is about simply saving costs."[^cite_note-245] The [U.S.-China Economic and Security Review Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States-China_Economic_and_Security_Review_Commission) annual report noted the significance of the event as demonstrating "the pressures on foreign media to self-censor in line with the [CCP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CCP)'s requirements". It concluded that the *WSJ*'s action "calls into question claims that foreign businesses have been unaffected by the new atmosphere following the passage of the [NSL](64033197.md) and [Article 23 Ordinance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safeguarding_National_Security_Ordinance)".[^cite_note-246]

### President Trump feud

On July 17, 2025, the newspaper reported that, in 2003, Donald Trump had sent convicted child sex offender [Jeffrey Epstein](6253522.md) a [50th birthday card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeffrey_Epstein's_birthday_book).[^cite_note-247] The next day, Trump (then a sitting president) sued the newspaper, alleging two counts of defamation and seeking at least \$10 billion for each count. It was believed to be the first time a sitting president had ever sued a journalist or media outlet for personal defamation.[^cite_note-248] The case was assigned to Judge [Darrin Gayles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darrin_P._Gayles).[^cite_note-249] On July 21, the *Journal* was also removed from the White House press pool for Trump's trip to Scotland.[^cite_note-250] Two days later, the *Journal* reported that Trump was informed by the Justice Department in May that his name was found in the [Epstein Files](80415168.md).[^cite_note-251]

### Misinformation on the assassination of Charlie Kirk

This section is an excerpt from [Assassination of Charlie Kirk § "Transgender ideology" misinformation](81040139.md#%22Transgender_ideology%22_misinformation).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Assassination_of_Charlie_Kirk\&action=edit)]

Conservative commentators speculated that the shooter was transgender because Kirk was answering a question about transgender mass shooters when he was shot.[^cite_note-Assassination_of_Charlie_Kirk_Woods_2025-252][^cite_note-253] *The New York Times* noted that this was "a grim coincidence that has fed into online conspiracies and speculation"; and Hunter Kozak, who asked Kirk the question, later said: "I couldn't have asked a worse question."[^cite_note-Assassination_of_Charlie_Kirk_Healy_2025a-254]

Early reporting, notably in *The Wall Street Journal*, falsely said that messaging related to "transgender ideology" was inscribed on the bullets, citing an internal bulletin of the ATF.[^cite_note-Assassination_of_Charlie_Kirk_Migdon_2025-255] The Trans Journalists Association cautioned that "transgender ideology" was a term commonly used in right-wing circles to frame transgender identity as a political choice.[^cite_note-256] *The New York Times* reported that a senior law enforcement official with knowledge of the case said that the bulletin had not been verified, and that it did not match other summaries of evidence;[^cite_note-Assassination_of_Charlie_Kirk_Migdon_2025-255][^cite_note-257] however, prominent conservative figures still seized on *The Wall Street Journal*'s report to call for [further action against the trans community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persecution_of_transgender_people_under_the_second_Trump_administration), including banning pride flags and incarcerating all transgender people en masse.[^cite_note-258][^cite_note-259] In a May 2026 counter-terrorism strategy pamphlet, the White House characterized Kirk's assassin as "a radical who espoused extreme transgender Ideologies  \[*[sic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sic)*]" and cited the killing in its directive to target "anti-American, radically pro-transgender, and anarchist" groups.[^cite_note-260]

The actual inscribed messages did not contain any references to transgender identity, and the [Human Rights Campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Rights_Campaign) published an open letter demanding a retraction and a public apology, saying: "This reporting was reckless and irresponsible, and it led to a wave of threats against the trans community from right-wing influencers—and a resulting wave of terror for a community that is already living in fear."[^cite_note-Assassination_of_Charlie_Kirk_Migdon_2025-255][^cite_note-261] *The Wall Street Journal* later amended the story with a note from the editor but did not issue a retraction.[^cite_note-Assassination_of_Charlie_Kirk_Wiggins_2025-262][^cite_note-263]

More transgender conspiracy theories were spread after it was reported that the suspected shooter had a transgender partner, with some speculating that Robinson may have been motivated to kill Kirk because [Kirk had been a proponent](53245776.md#Transgender_community) of the unsubstantiated claim that mass shooters disproportionately tend to be transgender.[^cite_note-264] Jacey Thornton, an executive director of the LGBTQ advocacy group Rainbow Utah, noted, "It sounds like \[they're] really stretching to find a way to tie this in to the trans community", adding that this is "very harmful to this ongoing dialogue that's happening, especially on social media".[^cite_note-Assassination_of_Charlie_Kirk_Healy_2025b-265]
