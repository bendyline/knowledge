A **bridge** is a [structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structure#Load-bearing) designed to span an obstacle, such as a river or railway, allowing vehicles, pedestrians, and other loads to pass across. Most bridges consist of a flat [deck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deck_\(bridge\)), supported by beams, arches, or cables. These structures rest on a [foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundation_\(engineering\)) that is carefully designed to transfer the weight of the bridge to the subsoil without [settling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Settlement_\(structural\)).

Bridges can be constructed in a wide variety of forms, determined by the location, intended purpose, and available construction technologies. Simple bridge structures include [beam bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beam_bridge) made from logs, and [suspension bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suspension_bridge) made of ropes or vines. The [Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire) and [ancient Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_China#Ancient_China) built major [arch bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arch_bridge) of timber, stone, and brick. During the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_architecture), advances in science and engineering led to wider bridge [spans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Span_\(engineering\)) and more elegant designs. [Concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concrete) was perfected in the early 19th century, and arch bridges are now built primarily of concrete or steel.

With the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution) came mass-produced steel, which enabled the creation of more complex forms – including [truss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truss_bridge) and [cantilever bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantilever_bridge) – that permitted bridges to cross wide rivers or deep valleys. The longest spans use suspension or [cable-stayed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cable-stayed_bridge) designs, both of which rely on high-strength steel cables to support the deck. Over time, the maximum achievable span of bridges has steadily increased, reaching [2 kilometers (1.2 miles) in 2022](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_longest_suspension_bridge_spans). Other bridge forms include multi-span [viaducts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viaduct), which can cross wide valleys; [trestles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trestle_bridge), a common design for carrying heavy trains; and [movable bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Movable_bridge) including [drawbridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drawbridge) and [swing bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swing_bridge).

The design of a bridge must satisfy many requirements, namely connecting to a transportation network, providing adequate [clearances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clearance_\(civil_engineering\)#Vertical_clearance), and safely transporting its users. A bridge must be strong enough to support its own weight as well as the weight of the traffic passing over it. It must also tolerate violent, hard-to-predict stresses imposed by the environment, including winds, floods, and earthquakes. To meet all these goals, bridge engineers typically use [limit state design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_state_design) processes and the [finite element method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_element_method).

Many bridges are admired for their beauty, and some spectacular bridges serve as iconic landmarks that provide a sense of pride and identity for the local community. In art and literature, bridges are frequently used as metaphors to represent connection or transition. Bridges can create beneficial impacts on a community, including shorter transport times and increased [gross domestic product](12594.md); and also negative effects such as increased [pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollution) and contributions to [global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming).

## History

Main article: [History of bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_bridges)

### Antiquity

A stone arch bridge passing over a river valley

The [Pont du Gard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pont_du_Gard) aqueduct in France was built by the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire) c. 40–60 AD.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown200522–23-1]

The earliest forms of bridges were simple structures for crossing wetlands and creeks, consisting of wooden [boardwalks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boardwalk) or [logs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Log_bridge).[^cite_note-early-2][^cite_note-4] [Pilings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilings) – which are critical elements of bridge construction – were used in Switzerland around 4,000 BC to support [stilt houses](1879233.md) built over water.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett20002-5] Several [corbel arch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corbel_arch) bridges were built c. 13th century BC by the [Mycenaean Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycenaean_Greece) culture, including the [Arkadiko Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arkadiko_Bridge), which is still in existence.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECruickshank201047-6] In the 7th century BC, [Assyrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Assyrian_Empire) king [Sennacherib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sennacherib) constructed stone [aqueducts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqueduct_\(bridge\)) to carry water near the city of [Nineveh](21699.md); one of the aqueducts crossed a small valley at [Jerwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerwan) with five corbelled arches, and was 280 meters (920 ft) long and 20 meters (66 ft) wide.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown200518-7] In [Babylonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonia) in 626 BC, a bridge across the [Euphrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphrates) was built with an estimated length of 120 to 200 meters (390 to 660 ft).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown200518–19-8] In [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_kingdoms_of_India), the *[Arthashastra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthashastra)* treatise by Kautilya mentions the construction of bridges and dams.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDikshitar1993332-9] Ancient China has an extensive history of bridge construction, including [cantilever bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantilever_bridge), rope bridges, and bridges built across floating boats.[^cite_note-10]

The [ancient Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Roman_engineering) built many durable bridges using advanced engineering techniques.[^cite_note-roman-11] Many [Roman aqueducts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_aqueduct) – some still standing today –  used a semicircular arch style.[^cite_note-roman-11] Examples include the [Alcántara Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alc%C3%A1ntara_Bridge) in Spain and the [Pont du Gard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pont_du_Gard) in France.[^cite_note-12] The Romans used [cement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cement) as a construction material, which could be mixed with small rocks to form [concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concrete), or mixed with sand to form [mortar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mortar_\(masonry\)) to join bricks or stones.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDelatte2001-13] Some Roman cements, particularly those containing [volcanic ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pozzolana), were waterproof.[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15] The enormous timber and stone [Trajan's Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajan's_Bridge) (c. 105 AD) crossed the [Danube river](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danube_river) and was over 900 meters (3,000 ft) long.[^cite_note-16]

### 300 to 1400

A graceful stone bridge spanning a river, with trees in the background

The [Anji Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anji_Bridge), which uses a shallow [segmental arch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Segmental_arch), was built in China c. 600 AD.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown200526-17]

The oldest surviving stone bridge in China is the [Anji Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anji_Bridge), built from 595 to 605 AD during the [Sui dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sui_dynasty). This bridge is also historically significant as it is the world's oldest open-spandrel stone segmental arch bridge.[^cite_note-anji-18] [Rope bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_rope_bridge), a simple type of [suspension bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suspension_bridge), were used by the [Inca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_Empire) civilization in the [Andes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andes) mountains of South America prior to European colonization in the 16th century.[^cite_note-19]

In [Medieval Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), bridge design capabilities declined after the [fall of Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Rome), but revived in the [High Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Middle_Ages) in France, England, and Italy with the construction of bridges like the [Pont d'Avignon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pont_d'Avignon), bridges of the [Durance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durance#Middle_Ages) river, and the [Old London Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_London_Bridge).[^cite_note-MedEv-20] Surviving examples include the [Ponte Vecchio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ponte_Vecchio) in Florence, the [Old Exe Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Exe_Bridge), and the [Monnow Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monnow_Bridge) in Wales.[^cite_note-MedEv-20]

### 1400 to 1750

A white bridge, covered with a roof, passing over a canal with buildings on both sides

The [Rialto Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rialto_Bridge), built in 1591, crosses the [Grand Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canal_\(Venice\)) in [Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venice).

In 15th- and 16th-century Europe, the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_architecture) brought a new emphasis on science and engineering.[^cite_note-21] Figures such as [Galileo Galilei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_Galilei), [Fausto Veranzio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fausto_Veranzio), and [Andrea Palladio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrea_Palladio) (author of *[I quattro libri dell'architettura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_quattro_libri_dell'architettura)*) wrote treatises that applied a rigorous, analytic approach to architecture and building.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECruickshank201032–33-22] Their innovations included [truss bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truss_bridge) and stone segmental arches, resulting in Florence's [Ponte Santa Trinita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ponte_Santa_Trinita), the [Rialto Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rialto_Bridge) in Venice, and Paris's [Pont Neuf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pont_Neuf).[^cite_note-23] Military and commercial bridges were constructed in India by the [Mughal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire) administration.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENath1982183–185-24] The [Asante Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asante_Empire) in Africa built bridges over streams and rivers using tree trunks and beams.[^cite_note-25]

### 1750 to 1900

In the late 18th century, the design of arch bridges was revolutionized in Europe by [Jean-Rodolphe Perronet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Rodolphe_Perronet) and [John Rennie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Rennie_the_Elder). They designed arches that were flatter than semicircular Roman arches, which yielded faster construction times, better water flow under the bridge, and slimmer piers. These designs were used for the [Pont de la Concorde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pont_de_la_Concorde_\(Paris\)) and [New London Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_London_Bridge).[^cite_note-rennie-26]

A large suspension bridge, with large towers made of stone

The mass production of steel enabled the construction of large suspension bridges. The [Brooklyn Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooklyn_Bridge), built in the 1870s, was the first suspension bridge to use steel for its cables.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett200022-27]

With the advent of the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution), iron became an important construction material for bridges.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown200545–51-28] Both [cast iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cast_iron) (which is strong under [compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compression_\(physics\)), but brittle) and [wrought iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrought_iron) (which was more ductile and better under [tension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tension_\(physics\))) were used for building bridges.[^cite_note-29] [The Iron Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Iron_Bridge) in England – made of cast iron and completed in 1781 – was the first major bridge made entirely of metal.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECruickshank201050–51-30] Several long suspension bridges were built in the early 19th century using iron [eyebars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eyebar) (steel wire, vastly superior, became available later in the century).[^cite_note-31]

The [age of railways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_rail_transport#Steam_power_introduced) began in the 1820s, and led to major innovations in bridge design.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown200564-32] Britain is representative of how railways influenced bridge-building in industrialized nations: led by designers [Isambard Kingdom Brunel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isambard_Kingdom_Brunel), [Robert Stephenson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Stephenson), and [Joseph Locke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Locke), [British railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_rail_transport_in_Great_Britain) bridges steadily grew in size as the decades passed.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown200564–65-33] Notable bridges of that era include the [High Level Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Level_Bridge,_River_Tyne) (1849),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown200565-34] [Royal Border Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Border_Bridge) (1850),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown200565-34] [Britannia Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Britannia_Bridge) (1850),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown200565–67-35] [Royal Albert Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Albert_Bridge) (1859),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown200574–75-36] and [Clifton Suspension Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifton_Suspension_Bridge) (1864).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown200570–73-37] The number of railway bridges in Britain increased from 30,000 to 60,000 during the [Railway Mania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railway_Mania) era.[^cite_note-38] Railway bridges primarily used masonry and stone arch designs, because those could withstand the tremendous loads imposed by trains, but iron beam designs (on masonry or stone piers) were also used.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown200564–66-39] The abundance of inexpensive [lumber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lumber) in North America led that continent to favor timber as a bridge material: using truss designs (for long spans) and [trestle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trestle_bridge) designs (for spanning deep ravines).[^cite_note-timber-40]

The mass production of [steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel) in the late 19th century provided a new material for bridges, enabling lighter, stronger truss bridges and cantilever bridges; and steel wires replaced iron bars as the preferred material for suspension bridge cables.[^cite_note-41] Concrete – which was originally used within the Roman Empire – was improved with the invention of [Portland cement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portland_cement) in the early 19th century, and replaced stone and masonry as the primary material for bridge [foundations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundation_\(engineering\)). When iron or steel is embedded in the concrete, as in [reinforced concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforced_concrete) or [prestressed concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prestressed_concrete), it is a strong, inexpensive material that can be used for horizontal elements of [beam bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beam_bridge) and [box girder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Box_girder) bridges.[^cite_note-42]

### 1900 to present

\=A suspension bridge crossing a deep rocky ravine

The [Sidi M'Cid Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidi_M'Cid_Bridge) in Algeria was the [highest bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highest_bridge) in the world when it was built in 1912.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESakowski20141259–1261-43]

Throughout the 20th century, new bridges – by designer [Othmar Ammann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Othmar_Ammann) and others – repeatedly broke records for span distances, enabling transportation networks to cross increasingly wider rivers and valleys.[^cite_note-44] [Cable-stayed bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cable-stayed_bridges) – which use cable-stays as the exclusive means of support – became a popular bridge design [following World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contemporary_history).[^cite_note-45][^cite_note-49] The late 20th century saw several major innovations in bridge design. [Extradosed bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extradosed_bridge) were introduced and found widespread use, predominantly in Japan.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchlaich20191,_3,_6–7-50] In China, [concrete-filled steel tubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concrete_filled_steel_tube#Bridges) were adopted as a new approach to building [arch bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arch_bridge).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEZhengWang2018-51] [Fiber-reinforced polymers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiber-reinforced_polymer) – which do not suffer from the rust problems that plague steel – were used in bridges for many applications, such as beams, deck slabs, prestressing cables, wraps on the exterior of concrete elements, and internal reinforcing within concrete.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESvecova2014371–375,_382,_384–386,_389–392-52][^cite_note-53] In the 21st century, a bridge span exceeded 2 kilometers (1.2 mi) for the first time with the construction of the [1915 Çanakkale Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1915_%C3%87anakkale_Bridge).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGülkan2023-54][^cite_note-56]

## Uses

The purpose of any bridge is to traverse an obstacle. A bridge can provide support and transport for [railways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railway), cars, pedestrians, pipelines, cables, or any combination of these.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETang20141-57] [Aqueducts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqueduct_\(bridge\)) were developed early in human history, and carried water to towns and cities.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown20058,_18,_20–23-58] [Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canal) systems sometimes include [navigable aqueducts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigable_aqueduct) (also called *canal bridges*) to carry boats across a valley or ravine.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown200553-59]

### Transportation

A bridge carrying canal with water, passing over a valley

The [Magdeburg Water Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magdeburg_Water_Bridge) in Germany carries boats across a valley.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDenison201272-60]

Until the early 19th century, most bridges were designed to carry pedestrians, horses, and horse-drawn carriages.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown200564-32] Following the invention of railways, many rail bridges were built; in Britain the number of bridges doubled during the railway-building boom in the mid-19th century.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown200564-32] Railway bridges have unique requirements because of the heavy loads they carry – a single [locomotive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locomotive) can weigh 197 tonnes (217 short tons).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESorgenfrei2014144.-61] Railway bridges are designed to minimize [deflection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deflection_\(engineering\)) (bending under load), to maximize [robustness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_robustness) (localize the damage caused by accidents), and to tolerate [heavy impacts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_\(mechanics\)) (sudden shocks from, for example, rail wheels striking an imperfection in the track).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESorgenfrei2014144-62] These requirements led railways to avoid curved bridges, suspension bridges, and cable-stayed bridges; instead, straight beam or truss bridges are commonly used.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESorgenfrei2014146-63] The explosive growth of motorway networks in the 20th century required bridges to span ever longer distances to reach islands and cross valleys,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett200017-64] along with the urban introduction of [elevated railways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevated_railway) and [monorails](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monorail).

### Grade separation

Further information: [Grade separation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grade_separation)

An important application of bridges is improving [safety and traffic flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic_engineering_\(transportation\)) at [traffic junctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junction_\(traffic\)) where roads or railways cross at ground level. Such intersections require vehicles to stop, and lead to slower traffic, wasted fuel, and higher incidence of collisions. One technique to mitigate these issues is to build a bridge, enabling one of the roads to [pass over](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overpass) the other: this process is known as [grade separation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grade_separation).[^cite_note-65][^cite_note-66] Grade separation can be implemented at [railway-road intersections](193595.md)[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEOgdenCooper2019-67] or [road-road intersections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intersection_\(road\)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEChase2020-68]

### Pedestrians

Some bridges, known as [footbridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Footbridge), are devoted to pedestrian traffic.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"Footbridge"._''Merriam-Webster''-69] They range from simple [boardwalks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boardwalk) enabling passage over marshy land to elevated [skybridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skyway) – including the [Minneapolis Skyway System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minneapolis_Skyway_System) – which shield pedestrians from harsh winter weather.[^cite_note-70] When [used to cross roads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedestrian_separation_structure) in busy urban areas, footbridges are generally safer than crosswalks, but have been criticized by urbanists and disability advocates for inconveniencing pedestrians, hindering accessibility, diminishing the quality of city life, and perpetuating [car dependency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Car_dependency).[^cite_note-71]

### Military

[Military bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_bridge) are an important type of equipment in the field of [military engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_engineering). They perform a variety of wartime roles, namely quickly traversing obstacles in the midst of battle, or facilitating resupply behind front lines.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETytler1985198-72] Military bridges can be categorized as *wet* bridges that rest on pontoon floats, and *dry* bridges that rest on piers, river banks, or anchorages.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETytler1985198-72] A crude mechanism to cross a small ravine is to place a [fascine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fascine) (a large bundle of pipes or logs) into the ravine to enable vehicles to drive across.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETytler1985200-73]

A metal bridge in a forest

&#x20;Invented for wartime use, [Bailey bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bailey_bridge) found civilian use after WWII.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEOdrobiňák2022-74]

[Armoured vehicle-launched bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armoured_vehicle-launched_bridge) are carried on purpose-built vehicles.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETytler1985200-73] These vehicles typically have the same cross-country performance as a tank, and can carry a bridge to an obstacle and deploy ("launch") the bridge.[^cite_note-75] The UK's [Chieftain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chieftain_\(tank\)) vehicle could launch a 23-meter (75 ft) bridge – capable of supporting 54-tonne (60-short-ton) loads –  in 3 minutes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETytler1985198,_200._The_bridge_could_support_a_\[\[Chieftain_Mark_5\]\]_tank.-76] Military bridges have found use in civilian applications. The [Bailey bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bailey_bridge) was originally invented in 1940 for use in World War II, but continues to be used in peacetime. Bailey bridges are used as small, permanent bridges, as well as temporary bridges used while a permanent bridge is being replaced or repaired.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEOdrobiňák2022-74]

During wartime, bridges are often damaged by bombing or by [combat engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combat_engineers#Countermobility). Bridges can be valuable targets because they are immobile, relatively easy to spot from the air, and damage to the bridge can disrupt the enemy's transportation network.[^cite_note-77] Bridges have been targeted by [artillery](2508.md) since antiquity, and the advent of newer technologies – specifically, aircraft and drones – has made bridges easier to attack. Germany used the [Stuka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junkers_Ju_87) aircraft to perform [dive-bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dive-bomber) attacks on bridges during World War II,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPrice1993-78] and Ukraine damaged the [Crimean Bridge](22563317.md) with a [drone attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_Crimean_Bridge_explosion) in 2023.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcGarvey2023-79]

### Other

Some bridges accommodate uses other than transportation. [Pipeline bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipeline_bridge) carry oil pipes or water pipes across valleys or rivers.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDusseau2002-80] Many historical bridges supported buildings, including shrines, factories, shops, restaurants, and houses. Notable examples were the Old London Bridge and [Ponte Vecchio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ponte_Vecchio).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECruickshank2010144–175-81] Some bridges built in Europe in the Middle Ages incorporated [chapels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapel) into their design.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECruickshank201091–92,_96-82] In the modern era, [bridge-restaurants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridge-restaurant) can be found at some highway [rest areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rest_area); these support a restaurant or shops directly above the highway and are accessible to drivers moving in both directions.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreco201689–100-83] Examples include the [Will Rogers Archway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will_Rogers_Archway) over the [Oklahoma Turnpike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_Turnpike)[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"Will_Rogers_Archway"._''Travel_Oklahoma''-84] and the several [Illinois Tollway oasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illinois_Tollway_oasis) locations. The [Nový Most](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nov%C3%BD_Most) bridge in [Bratislava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bratislava) features a restaurant set atop its single tower.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"Bratislava_UFO_Classed_One_of_the_Craziest_Places"._''Travel_to_Slovakia''-85] Conservationists use [wildlife bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildlife_crossing) to reduce [habitat fragmentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habitat_fragmentation) and animal-vehicle collisions.[^cite_note-86] The first wildlife crossings were built in the 1950s, and these types of bridges are now used worldwide.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENewer2012-87]

## Structure and form

See also: [List of bridge types](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_bridge_types)

Bridges are primarily classified by their basic structural design: arch, truss, cantilever, suspension, cable-stayed, or beam.[^cite_note-88][^cite_note-90] Several other terms can be used to designate various aspects of a bridge's form or design, including [viaduct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viaduct), [trestle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trestle_bridge), and [causeway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causeway).

### Basic structures

The choice of bridge structure to use in a particular situation is based on many factors, including aesthetics, environment, cost, and purpose.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarker200796–108-91] Some bridge spans combine two types of basic structures; for instance, the [Brooklyn Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooklyn_Bridge) is primarily a suspension structure, but also uses cable-stays.[^cite_note-92] Some multi-span bridges – called *hybrid* bridges – use different basic structures for different spans.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDenison201235-93]

#### Arch bridge

[Arch bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arch_bridge)

An arched bridge spanning a river, deck resting on top of the arch

Deck arch

An arched bridge spanning a river, deck suspended below the arch by vertical lines

[Tied arch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tied-arch_bridge)

[Arch bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arch_bridge) consist of a curved arch, under compression, which supports the [deck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deck_\(bridge\)) either above or below the arch.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett199970–75-94] The shape of the arch can be a [semicircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semicircle), [ellipse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellipse), [pointed arch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointed_arch), or [segment of a circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_arc).[^cite_note-95] Arches exert a diagonal force at both ends, requiring strong supports or [abutments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abutment) to prevent the arch from spreading or collapsing.[^cite_note-abutDiag-96] Deck arch bridges hold the deck above the arch; [tied-arch bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tied-arch_bridge) suspend the deck below the arch; and [through arch bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Through_arch_bridge) position the deck through the middle of the arch.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett199975–76-97]

#### Truss bridge

[Truss bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truss_bridge)

A bridge spanning a river, consisting of several triangles, with the bridge deck forming the lower edge of the set of triangles

Through truss

A bridge spanning a river, consisting of several triangles, with the bridge deck forming the upper edge of the set of triangles

Deck truss

A [truss bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truss_bridge) is composed of multiple, connected triangular elements.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett199977–79-98] The set of triangles form a rigid whole, which rests on the foundation at both ends, applying a vertical force downward.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett199977–79-98] The deck can be carried on top of the truss ("deck truss") or at the bottom of the truss ("through truss").[^cite_note-99] Through trusses are useful when more clearance under the bridge is required; deck trusses permit [oversize loads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oversize_load) and do not interfere with overhead objects, such as electrical lines.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWright20224,_7–9,_19–20-100] The individual bars can be made of iron or wood, but most modern truss bridges are made of steel.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett200014–17,_21-101] The horizontal bars along the top are usually in [compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compression_\(physics\)), and the horizontal bars along the bottom are usually in [tension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tension_\(physics\)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett199977–79-98] Bars connecting the top and bottom may be in tension or compression, depending on the layout of the triangles.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett199977-102] Trusses typically have a span-to-depth ratio (the width of a structure divided by its height) ranging from 10 to 16, compared to beam bridges which typically have a ratio ranging from 20 to 30.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECollings2000413-103] Trusses tend to be relatively stiff, and are commonly used for rail bridges which are required to carry very heavy loads.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECollings2000413-103]

#### Cantilever bridge

[Cantilever bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantilever_bridge)

A bridge spanning a river, where there is a solid pier in the middle of the river, and the entire bridge is resting on that pier (and not resting on the banks of the river).

Balanced cantilever on a single pier

A bridge spanning a river, where the bridge is in two disjoint parts: the left part is supported entirely from the leftmost edge where it rests on the ground; and the right part is supported entirely from the rightmost edge where it rests on the ground.

Two cantilevers extending from anchorages

[Cantilever bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantilever_bridge) consist of beams or trusses that are rigidly attached to a support (pier or anchorage) and extend horizontally from the support without additional supports.[^cite_note-104] In ancient Asia, cantilever bridges made of large rocks or timber were used to span small obstacles.[^cite_note-105] In the 1880s, some early cantilever bridges were built from wrought iron, but steel became common starting in the late 19th century.[^cite_note-106] A [balanced cantilever bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balanced_cantilever_bridge) consists of two connected cantilevers extending outward in opposite directions from a single central support.[^cite_note-balcant-107] Other cantilever bridges have two cantilevers, anchored at each end of the span, extending toward the center, and meeting in the center.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett199912-108]  *Cantilever construction* is a method of building a bridge [superstructure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superstructure), which can be utilized for arch and cable-stayed bridges, as well as cantilever bridges. In this technique, construction begins at a support (specifically a pier, abutment, or tower) and extends outwards across the obstacle, with no support from below.[^cite_note-109]

#### Suspension bridge

[Suspension bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suspension_bridge)

A bridge spanning a river, with two tall towers in the river, and a curved cable passing from one riverbank to the other, passing over the tops of the towers. The bridge deck (road) is suspended from the curved cable by vertical lines.

With anchorages

A bridge spanning a river, with a single tall tower in the middle of the river, and a curved cable passing from one riverbank to the other, passing over the top of the tower. The bridge deck (road) is suspended from the curved cable by vertical lines.

[Self-anchored](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-anchored_suspension_bridge)

[Suspension bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suspension_bridge) have large, curved cables attached to the tops of tall towers,[^cite_note-OneTower-110] and suspend the bridge deck from the cables.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown2005203-111][^cite_note-113] In the early 19th century, the first modern suspension bridges – such as the [Jacob's Creek Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob's_Creek_Bridge_\(Pennsylvania\)) – were [chain bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain_bridge) that used iron bars rather than bundled wires for the cables.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown200558-114] After steel wire became widely available, longer cables could be built by stringing hundreds of wires between the towers and bundling them,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett199984,_88–90-115] enabling suspension bridges to achieve spans 2 kilometers (1.2 miles) long. When the bridge crosses a river, stringing the wires across the large span is a complex process.[^cite_note-116] The cable of a suspension bridge assumes the shape of a [catenary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catenary) when initially suspended between the bridge towers; however, once the uniform load of the bridge deck is applied, the cable adopts a [parabolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabola) shape.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECruickshank2010232–233-117] Shorter towers require a smaller sag in the cable, which increases the tension in the cable, and thus requires stronger towers and anchorages.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett199988–89-118]

#### Cable-stayed bridge

[Cable-stayed bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cable-stayed_bridge)

A bridge spanning a river, with two tall towers in the river. The bridge deck (road) is suspended from the two towers by numerous straight, diagonal lines.

Harp pattern, two towers

A bridge spanning a river, with a single tall tower in the middle of the river. The bridge deck (road) is suspended from the tower by numerous straight, diagonal lines.

Fan pattern, single tower

[Cable-stayed bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cable-stayed_bridge) are similar to suspension bridges, but the cables that support the deck connect directly to the towers.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett199992–94,_228-119][^cite_note-OneTower-110] The inclined cables may be arranged in a fan pattern or a harp pattern.[^cite_note-122][^cite_note-124] Modern cable-stayed bridges became popular after World War II, when the design was used for many new bridges in Germany.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett200027–29-125] When traversing a wide obstacle, designers have a choice of suspension or cable-stayed structures. Suspension bridges can achieve a longer span, but cable-stayed bridges use less cable for a given span size, do not require anchorages, and the deck can be readily built by cantilevering outward from the towers.[^cite_note-126]

#### Beam bridge

[Beam bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beam_bridge)

A flat, straight bridge spanning a river. There are no towers or piers: the entire bridge is a flat, wide, rectangular shape.

[Beam bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beam_bridges) are simple structures consisting of one or more parallel, horizontal [beams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beam_\(structure\)) or [girders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girder) that span an obstacle.[^cite_note-beam-127] A [box girder bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Box_girder_bridge) is a variant that is generally shallower than an [I-beam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I-beam) equivalent, permitting shorter and lower approach roads to cross an obstacle of a given height.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett200024-128] Beam bridges are commonly used for both railways and roadways.[^cite_note-beam-127] Beam bridges are often used for spans shorter than about 50 meters (160 ft); for longer spans, trusses or similar structures are generally more efficient.[^cite_note-129] The majority of beam bridges have a flat, horizontal bottom; but some have a bottom that arches upward, called *haunching*. Haunching looks more graceful than a flat bottom, and can provide greater clearance below the bridge, but it tends to be more costly because flat bottom beams are easier to build.[^cite_note-130]

### Other forms

In addition to the basic bridge structures, there are many other forms of bridges. The following sections describe some of the more common forms, but are not an exhaustive list.

#### Movable bridge

A tall drawbridge, open, over a river

[Tower Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_Bridge) in London is a [movable bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Movable_bridge).

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBirnstiel2000688–690-131]

[Movable bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Movable_bridge) are designed so that all or part of the bridge deck can be moved, usually to permit tall traffic – such as tall boats or ships – to pass by.[^cite_note-132] Early movable bridges include [drawbridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drawbridge) that pivoted at one end, and required a large amount of work to raise. Adding counterweights on the pivot side of the drawbridge creates a [bascule bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bascule_bridge), and makes moving the bridge easier and safer.[^cite_note-133] [Swing bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swing_bridge) pivot horizontally around an anchor point on the bank of a canal, or sometimes from a pier in the middle of the water.[^cite_note-134] [Lift bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lift_bridge) are raised vertically between two towers by cables passing over pulleys at the top of the towers.[^cite_note-135] Notable movable bridges include [El Ferdan Railway Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Ferdan_Railway_Bridge) in Egypt, [Erasmusbrug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erasmusbrug) bascule in Rotterdam, and [Limehouse Basin footbridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limehouse_Basin#Swing_bridge) in London.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown2005164–165,_184–185-136] In the modern era, designers sometimes create unusual movable bridges with the intention of establishing signature bridges for a town or locality.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown2005164–165,_184–185-136] Examples include the [Puente de la Mujer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puente_de_la_Mujer) swing bridge in Buenos Aires, the [Gateshead Millennium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gateshead_Millennium_Bridge) – a rare example of a [tilt bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tilt_bridge) – over the [River Tyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Tyne), and the [Hörn Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C3%B6rn_Bridge) in Germany.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown2005164-137][^cite_note-138]

#### Long multi-span bridge

A large bridge, consisting of multiple tall sections, passing over a wide valley

The [Millau Viaduct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millau_Viaduct) crosses the [Tarn river](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarn_\(river\)) valley in France.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown2005192–193-139]

There are a variety of terms that describe long, multi-span bridges – including viaduct, trestle, continuous, and causeway. The usage of the terms can overlap, but each has a specific focus.[^cite_note-140] [Viaducts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viaduct) (carrying vehicles) and [aqueducts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqueduct_\(bridge\)) (carrying water) are bridges crossing a valley or [underpass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subway_\(crossing\)), supported by multiple arches or piers.[^cite_note-141] Romans built many aqueducts, some of which are still standing today.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECruickshank201058,_60–63,_70-142] Notable viaducts include [Penponds Viaduct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penponds_Viaduct) in England,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown200569-143] [Garabit Viaduct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garabit_Viaduct) in France,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown200592–93-144] [Tunkhannock Viaduct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunkhannock_Viaduct) in Pennsylvania,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown2005130–131-145] and [Millau Viaduct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millau_Viaduct) in France.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown2005192–193-139]

A [trestle bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trestle_bridge) – commonly used in the 19th century for railway bridges –  consists of multiple short spans supported by closely spaced structural elements.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECruickshank201046–47-146] A trestle is similar to a viaduct, but viaducts typically have taller [pier supports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pier_\(bridge_structure\)) and longer spans.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHonan2018-147] A [continuous truss bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_truss_bridge) is a long, single truss that rests upon multiple supports. A continuous truss bridge may use less material than a series of simple trusses because a continuous truss distributes live loads across all the spans (in contrast to a series of simple trusses, where each truss must be capable of supporting the entire live load). Visually, a continuous truss looks similar to a cantilever bridge, but a continuous truss experiences [hogging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hogging_and_sagging) stresses at the supports and [sagging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hogging_and_sagging) stresses between the supports.[^cite_note-contTruss-148][^cite_note-150] A [causeway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causeway) is a low road, usually crossing a bog, marsh, lake, or other body of water.[^cite_note-causeway-151] Many causeways are earthen embankments, but some are raised bridges, such as the 38.4-kilometer (23.9-mile) [Lake Pontchartrain Causeway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Pontchartrain_Causeway) in Louisiana.[^cite_note-causeway-151]

#### Extradosed

A concrete bridge over a river

The Shinmeisai Bridge (foreground) in Japan is an example of an [extradosed bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extradosed_bridge).

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHu201640-152]

An [extradosed bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extradosed_bridge) combines features of a box girder bridge and a cable-stayed bridge.[^cite_note-153] Visually, extradosed bridges can be distinguished from cable-stayed bridges because the tower height (above the deck) is relatively low: between seven and thirteen percent of the span width.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchlaich20193–4-154] Extradosed bridges are appropriate for spans ranging from 100 meters (330 ft) to 250 meters (820 ft).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchlaich20193–4-154] Unlike suspension bridges or cable-stayed bridges, the towers of an extradosed bridge often rest on the deck (rather than on a footing) and are solidly connected to the deck.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchlaich20195-155] Because of the relatively flat angle of the cables, the cables of an extradosed bridge compress the deck horizontally, performing a function comparable to prestressing wires that are used within concrete girders.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchlaich20193–7,_58-156] Extradosed bridges may be appropriate in applications where the deck must have a shallow depth to maximize clearance under the bridge; or where towers must be relatively short to abide by aviation safety constraints.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchlaich20196-157]

#### Pontoon bridge

A concrete bridge over a large body of water

Floating concrete pontoons support the weight of the [Nordhordland Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nordhordland_Bridge) as it crosses a deep [fjord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fjord) in Norway.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWatanabe2003129–130-158]

A [pontoon bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontoon_bridge), also known as a floating bridge, uses [floats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Float_\(nautical\)) or shallow-[draft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Draft_\(hull\)) boats to support a continuous deck for pedestrian or vehicle travel over water.[^cite_note-159] Pontoon bridges are typically used where waters are too deep to build piers, or as a mechanism to implement a movable [swing bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swing_bridge) in a canal.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBirnstiel2000668,_679-160]  During the [Second Persian invasion of Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Persian_invasion_of_Greece), Persian ruler [Xerxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerxes_I) built a [large pontoon bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerxes'_pontoon_bridges) across the [Hellespont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellespont), consisting of two parallel rows of 360 boats.[^cite_note-161]

Several pontoon bridges are in use in the modern world. Washington state in the US has several, including [Hood Canal Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hood_Canal_Bridge).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWatanabe2003128–129-162] In Norway, [Nordhordland Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nordhordland_Bridge) crosses a deep [fjord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fjord) by resting on floating concrete pontoons.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWatanabe2003129–130-158] Many armies have pontoon bridges that can be rapidly deployed, including the [PMP Floating Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PMP_Floating_Bridge), designed by the USSR.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"Handbook_on_Soviet_Ground_Forces"._''Department_of_the_Army''6.78–6.80-163]

## Design

Main article: [Bridge design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridge_design)

### Design process

A freeway with several cars driving on it, with two concrete bridges passing overhead

Many overpass bridges in the United States [Interstate Highway System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_Highway_System) are concrete [box girder bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Box_girder_bridge), such as these bridges over [Interstate 280](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstate_280_\(California\)) in California.

The process for designing a new bridge typically goes through several stages, progressively refining the design.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETang20142-164] An early step in the design process – sometimes called *conceptual design* – is to consider the multiple requirements that a bridge must satisfy.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETang20142-164] Requirements that are directly related to function include lifespan, safety, climate, soil condition, traffic volume, the size and nature of the obstacle to be traversed, and clearance required for passage underneath.[^cite_note-reqmnt-165] Other constraints may include construction cost, maintenance cost, aesthetics, time available for construction, owner preference, and experience of the builders.[^cite_note-reqmnt-165] Some bridge designs consider factors such as [impact on the environment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_assessment) and wildlife, and the bridge's economic, social, and historic relationship to the local community.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHu201698,_106–108-166] After the requirements of a bridge are established, a bridge designer uses [structural analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_analysis) methods to identify candidate designs.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarker200745–51-167] Several designs may meet the requirements. The [value engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_engineering) methodology can be used to select a final design from multiple alternatives.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHu201666,_69–72,_106–109,_112-168] This methodology evaluates candidate designs based on weighted scores assigned to several different criteria, including cost, service life, durability, availability of resources, ease of construction, construction time, and [maintenance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridge_maintenance) cost.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHu201666,_69–72,_106–109,_112-168]

An important requirement considered during the design process is the [service life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Service_life), which is a specific number of years that the bridge is expected to remain in operation with routine maintenance (and without requiring major repairs).[^cite_note-169][^cite_note-171] For example, wood bridge [superstructures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superstructure#Bridges) typically have a service life of 10 to 50 years.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFridleyDuan2014348–350-172][^cite_note-174] Concrete highway bridges can have service lives of 75 to 150 years.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHopperLanglois20225-170] A bridge design methodology incorporates the service life into the design process.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHopperLanglois20225–20-175]

### Specifications and standards

One of the requirements a new bridge must satisfy is compliance with the local bridge design specifications and [codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Building_code) which – in some countries – may be legally binding requirements.[^cite_note-176] In many countries, these specifications are developed and published by [standards organizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standards_organizations) that define acceptable bridge-building practices and designs. In Europe, the organization is the [European Committee for Standardization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Committee_for_Standardization), and the standards it publishes are the [Eurocodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurocodes).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"Eurocodes:_Building_the_Future"._''European_Commission''-177] In the United States, the [American Association of State Highway and Transportation Officials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Association_of_State_Highway_and_Transportation_Officials) (AASHTO) publishes the AASHTO LRFD Bridge Design Specifications.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"AASHTO_LRFD_Bridge_Design_Specifications"-178][^cite_note-180] Canada's bridge standard is the Canadian Highway Bridge Design Code, developed by the non-profit [CSA Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CSA_Group).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"CSA_S6\:19,_Canadian_Highway_Bridge_Design_Code"._''CSA_Group''-181] Agencies that regulate [aviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviation_safety) or [waterways](174851.md) may also impose standards that dictate some aspects of a bridge design, such as requirements for [aviation warning lights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviation_obstruction_lighting) at the top of bridge towers, or [navigational warning lights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigational_aid) on bridge supports located in [navigable waterways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigable_waterway).[^cite_note-182]

### Aesthetics

A train moving atop a stone bridge in an attractive valley

The [Brusio spiral viaduct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brusio_spiral_viaduct) – a part of the [Bernina railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernina_railway) in Switzerland – is a [World Heritage Site](44940.md).

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"Rhaetian_Railway_in_the_Albula"._''UNESCO_World_Heritage_Convention''-183]

A bridge's appearance is one of the factors considered during its design.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETang201422–24-184] Attractive bridges can have a positive impact on a community, and some bridges can even be considered as works of art.[^cite_note-185] Bridge designers that are known for emphasizing the visual appeal of their bridges include [Thomas Telford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Telford), [Gustave Eiffel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustave_Eiffel), [John Roebling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Roebling), [Robert Maillart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Maillart), and [Santiago Calatrava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santiago_Calatrava).[^cite_note-186] Qualities that influence the perceived attractiveness of a bridge include proportion, color, texture, order, refinement, environmental integration, and functionality.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELeonhardt201443–46-187]

The art historian [Dan Cruickshank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Cruickshank) notes that bridges are regarded as manifestations of human imagination and ambition, and that many bridges transcend their original utilitarian role and become a work of art.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECruickshank20108–9-188] He writes "\[a] great bridge has an emotional impact, it has a sublime quality and a heroic beauty that moves even those who are not accustomed to having their senses inflamed by the visual arts."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECruickshank20108–9-188]

### Material

A construction site with a halfway built concrete structure

This concrete bridge support is being prepared for a concrete pour. After the concrete [cures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concrete#Curing), the green [reinforcing bars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforcing_bar) will be permanently embedded inside.

[^cite_note-rebar-189]

A bridge designer can select from a wide variety of materials, including wood, brick, rope, stone, iron, steel, and concrete.[^cite_note-190][^cite_note-192] A bridge made from two or more distinct materials (for example, steel and concrete) is known as a composite bridge.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECollings2000407–408-193] Some of the largest arch bridges are composite, because they are made from concrete and steel.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEZhengWang2018-51]

Wood is an inexpensive [renewable resource](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renewable_resource) with a high [strength-to-weight ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strength-to-weight_ratio), but it is rarely used for modern roadway bridges because it is prone to degradation from the environment, and is much weaker than steel or concrete.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEZhao201784–85-194] Wood is primarily used in beam or truss bridges including [covered bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covered_bridge), and is also used to build large [trestle bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trestle_bridge) for railways.[^cite_note-195] When wood is used, it is often in the form of [glued laminated timber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glued_laminated_timber).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEZhao201784–85-194] [Masonry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masonry) includes stone and brick, and is suitable only for elements of a bridge that are under [compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compression_\(physics\)) (as opposed to [tension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tension_\(physics\))), therefore, masonry is limited to structures such as arches or foundations.[^cite_note-196] In the 20th century, large masonry bridges  – although superseded by concrete in the West – continued to be built in China.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEOuChen2005-197]

&#x20;An ornate bridge made of iron, passing over a small, lush valley

[The Iron Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Iron_Bridge) in [Shropshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shropshire), England, completed in 1781, is the first major bridge made entirely of [cast iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cast_iron).

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECruickshank201050–51-30]

Iron – including [cast iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cast_iron) and [wrought iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrought_iron) – was used extensively from the late 18th century to late 19th century, primarily for arch and truss structures. Iron is relatively brittle, and has been replaced by steel for all but ornamental uses.[^cite_note-198] Steel is one of the most common materials used in modern bridges because it is strong in both compression and tension.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett200016–21-199] Steel was made in small quantities in antiquity, but became widely available in the late 19th century following invention of new [smelting](28716.md) processes. Truss bridges and beam bridges are often made of steel, and steel wires are an essential component of virtually all suspension bridges and cable-stayed bridges.[^cite_note-200] Steel is a critical component in concrete bridges, because steel [reinforcing bars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforcing_bars) or steel [prestressed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prestressed) cables must be embedded within concrete to make it sufficiently strong.[^cite_note-201] Steel bridges are more expensive than comparable concrete bridges, but they are much lighter (for the same strength), faster to build, and offer more flexibility during construction and repair.[^cite_note-202]

Concrete is commonly used in modern bridges, and many roadway bridges are built primarily with a [reinforced concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforced_concrete) beam structure, often of the [box girder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Box_girder) variety.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett200025–27-203][^cite_note-205] The shape of concrete elements is determined by the [formwork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formwork) ([mold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molding_\(process\))) into which the concrete is poured (cast): the concrete will adopt the shape of the formwork as it [cures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concrete#Curing).[^cite_note-206] Beams can be [precast offsite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precast_concrete) and transported to the bridge site, or [cast in place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cast-in-place_concrete).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett199953-207] Bridges use concrete that contains embedded steel reinforcing bars – placed within the concrete when it is initially poured – which greatly increase the strength.[^cite_note-rebar-189] Concrete is a strong and inexpensive material, but is brittle and can crack when in tension.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett199968–69-208]  If concrete is used in elements that may experience tension, prestressed cables are usually embedded within the concrete and tightened, which compresses the concrete.[^cite_note-prestress-209] When a horizontal beam is placed into the bridge and carries a load, the undesirable tension (produced by the tendency of the beam to sag) is counteracted by the compression from the prestressed cables.[^cite_note-Cruickshank_2010_322–327-210] The prestressed cables can be pre-tensioned (stretched before – and while – the concrete cures); or post-tensioned (placed within tubes in the concrete, and tightened after the concrete cures).[^cite_note-Cruickshank_2010_322–327-210]

### Double-deck bridge

See also: [List of multi-level bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_multi-level_bridges)

A long, straight, flat bridge over a large body of water

The [Padma Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Padma_Bridge) in Bangladesh carries rail traffic on the lower deck and vehicular traffic on the upper deck.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"Main_Bridge_Details"._''Padma_Multipurpose_Bridge_Project''-211]

Designers may choose to use a double-deck design (also known as double-decked or double-decker), that carries two decks on top of each other. This technique can be used to increase the amount of traffic a bridge can carry; or when the location constrains the size of the bridge.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKumar2025-212] Double-deck bridges also permit two different kinds of traffic to be safely carried. For example, motor vehicles can be separated from pedestrians or railways.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKumar2025-212] Some double-deck bridges carry rail on one deck, and vehicles on the other deck. An early example was the [Niagara Falls Suspension Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niagara_Falls_Suspension_Bridge),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown200587-213] and a modern example is the [Dom Luís I Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dom_Lu%C3%ADs_I_Bridge) in Portugal.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"Dom_Luís_I_Bridge_"._''Structurae''-214] Because of their ability to carry large amounts of motor vehicles, double-deck bridges are often found near large cities carrying cars on both decks, for example, the [DuSable Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DuSable_Bridge) in Chicago, [Tsing Ma Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsing_Ma_Bridge) in Hong Kong,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown2005178–179-215] the [Øresund Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%98resund_Bridge) connecting Copenhagen and Malmö,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown2005181-216] and the [Shimotsui-Seto Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Seto_Bridge#Constituent_bridges) near Kurashiki.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown2005170–171-217] The [George Washington Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington_Bridge) in New York carries 14 motor vehicle lanes (eight above, six below), and is the world's busiest bridge, carrying over 100 million vehicles annually.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"2024_Monthly_Traffic_and_Percent_of_E‐ZPass_Usage"._''Port_Authority_of_New_York_and_New_Jersey''-218]

### Load analysis

A very large suspension bridge passing over a large body of water

The [San Francisco–Oakland Bay Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco%E2%80%93Oakland_Bay_Bridge) is designed to withstand severe earthquakes. The [eastern span](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_span_replacement_of_the_San_Francisco%E2%80%93Oakland_Bay_Bridge), shown above, is a [self-anchored suspension bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-anchored_suspension_bridge) which can survive a once-in-1,500-year earthquake.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"SAS_Maintenance_Travelers"._''California_Department_of_Transportation''-219]

A bridge design must accommodate all loads and forces that the bridge might reasonably experience. The totality of the forces that the bridge must tolerate is the [structural load](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_load), which is often divided into three components: dead load, live load, and environmental load. The [dead load](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_load#Dead_load) is the weight of the bridge itself.[^cite_note-load-220][^cite_note-222] The [live load](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_load#Live_load) is all forces and vibrations caused by traffic passing over the bridge, including weight, braking, and acceleration.[^cite_note-load-220] The [environmental load](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_load#Environmental_load) encompasses all forces applied by the bridge's surroundings, including weather, earthquakes, mudslides, water currents, flooding, soil subsidence, frost heaving, temperature fluctuations, and collisions.[^cite_note-load-220]

For sporadic events like floods, earthquakes, collisions, and hurricanes, bridge designers select a maximum severity that the design must accommodate.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarker2007197,_201-223] The severity is based on the [return period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Return_period), which is average time between events of a given magnitude. Return periods range from 10 to 2,500 years, depending on type of event and the country in which the bridge is located.[^cite_note-returnPeriod-224][^cite_note-225] Longer return periods are used for bridges that are a critical part of the transportation infrastructure. For example, if the bridge is a key lifeline in case of emergencies, the designer may utilize relatively long return period, for instance, 2,000 years; in this example, the design must endure the strongest storm that is expected to happen once every 2,000 years.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarker2007161,_197,_201-226]

#### Stress and strain

Further information: [Stress (mechanics)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_\(mechanics\))

The load forces acting on a bridge cause the components of the bridge to become [stressed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_\(mechanics\)). Stress is a measure of the internal force experienced within a material. Strain is a measure of how much a bridge component bends, stretches, or twists in response to stress. Some strain (bending or twisting) may be acceptable in a bridge component if the material is [elastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elasticity_\(physics\)). For example, steel can tolerate some stretching or bending without failing. In contrast, concrete is inelastic, and the change in its shape when stressed is negligible (until the stress becomes excessive and the concrete fails).[^cite_note-stressstrain-227]

A critical phase of the design process is calculating the maximum stress that each bridge component will experience, and selecting an appropriate design and size for the components to ensure they will safely tolerate the loads on the bridge. Stresses are categorized based on the nature of the force that causes the stress, namely: compression, tension, shear, and torsion. Compression forces compact a component by pushing inward (for example, as felt by a bridge foundation when a heavy tower is resting on it). Tension is a stretching force experienced by a component when pulled (for example by the cables of a suspension bridge). [Shear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_force) is a sliding force experienced by a component when two offset external forces are applied in opposite directions (for example, during an earthquake when the upper part of a structure is pulled north, and the lower part is pulled south). [Torsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsion_\(mechanics\)) is a twisting force.[^cite_note-stress-228]

The bridge design process typically employs [structural analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_analysis) methods that divide the bridge into smaller components, and analyze the components individually, subject to certain constraints.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarker2007283–288-229] A proposed bridge design is then usually [modeled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_model) with formulas or computer applications.[^cite_note-computr-230] The models incorporate the loads the bridge will experience, calculate the stresses in the bridge, and provide data to the designer indicating whether the design meets the required design goals.[^cite_note-computr-230][^cite_note-232] To ensure that a proposed bridge design is sufficiently strong to endure foreseeable stresses, many bridge designers use [limit state design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_state_design) methodologies (used in Europe and China) or [Load and Resistance Factor Design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Load_and_Resistance_Factor_Design) (LRFD) methodologies (used in US).[^cite_note-233]

#### Vibration

A collapsed concrete bridge, with a broken support pier

The [1994 Northridge earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1994_Northridge_earthquake) damaged several bridges.

[^cite_note-234]

Many loads imposed on a bridge – wind, earthquakes, and vehicular traffic – can cause a bridge to experience irregular or periodic forces, which may cause bridge components to vibrate or [oscillate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscillate).[^cite_note-235] Some bridge components have inherent [resonant frequencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resonant_frequencies) to which they are particularly susceptible, and vibrations near those frequencies can cause very large stresses.[^cite_note-236]

Winds can produce a variety of vibrational forces on a bridge, including [flutter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeroelasticity#Flutter), [galloping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galloping_\(wind\)?action=edit\&redlink=1), and [vortex shedding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vortex_shedding).[^cite_note-flutter_vortex-237] Considering wind forces during the design process is especially important for long, slender bridges (typically suspension or cable-stayed bridges).[^cite_note-238]  If resonance issues are identified in the design process, they must be mitigated. Common techniques to address vibration include increasing the rigidity of the bridge deck by adding trusses and adding dampers to cables and towers.[^cite_note-239] Neglecting to account for vibrations and oscillations can lead to bridge failure. The [Tacoma Narrows Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacoma_Narrows_Bridge_\(1940\)#Collapse) collapsed in 1940 in winds of 68 km/h (42 mph), even though the bridge was designed to withstand winds up to 206 km/h (128 mph). Investigations revealed that the designer failed to account for wind-induced flutter and resonant vibrations.[^cite_note-narrows-240]

Bridges can suffer severe damage when subjected to [earthquake](10106.md) ground motions.[^cite_note-241] During a seismic event, several phenomena can occur, such as long-period velocity pulses, shear cracks, large ground motions, vertical accelerations, and [soil liquefaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_liquefaction).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEYashinsky201453–56-242] To mitigate risks, [earthquake engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthquake_engineer) study seismic data to classify and quantify the motions experienced by bridges.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEYashinsky201453–54-243] These studies are used by governments to create and revise design standards that specify the types of seismic movements that new bridges must withstand.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEYashinsky201453-244]

## Construction

Further information: [Bridge design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridge_design)

The structural elements of a bridge are generally divided into the [substructure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substructure_\(engineering\)) and the [superstructure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superstructure#Bridges).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEZhao20174–7-245] The substructure consists of the lower portions of the bridge, including the [footings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundation_\(engineering\)),[^cite_note-247] [abutments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abutment), piers, pilings, anchorages, and bearings.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEZhao20176,_7-248] The superstructure rests upon the substructure, and consists of the [deck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deck_\(bridge\)), trusses, arches, towers, cables, beams, and girders.[^cite_note-249]

### Construction process

A schematic diagram identifying the various parts of a hypothetical bridge

Some elements of a fictional bridge. 1 Approach, 2 Arch, 3 Truss, 4 Abutment, 5 Bearing, 6 Deck and beams, 7 Pier Cap, 8 Pier, 9 Piling, 10 Footing, 11 Caisson, 12 Subsoil.

[^cite_note-250]

Construction of a bridge is typically managed by [construction engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Construction_engineer), who are responsible for planning and supervising the construction process. Important aspects of this role include budgeting, scheduling, periodically conducting formal [design reviews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Design_review), and communicating with the bridge designers to interpret and update the [design plans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering_drawing).[^cite_note-251][^cite_note-253] When an existing bridge is being replaced or refurbished, the impact on traffic flow can have a detrimental effect on residents and services. [Accelerated bridge construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accelerated_bridge_construction) processes – that focus on using pre-fabricated components and a rapid timetable – may be used to mitigate the impacts.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETang2014a175–179-254]

The forces experienced by a bridge during construction can be larger or have a different nature than the forces it will experience after completion. The bridge design process typically focuses on the strength of the fully completed bridge, but it should also consider the unusual stresses that individual elements will experience during construction. Special techniques may be required during construction to avoid excessive stresses, such as temporary supports under the bridge, temporary bracing or reinforcement, or permanently strengthening specific elements.[^cite_note-255] For instance, when a cable-stayed bridge with concrete towers is complete, the towers will experience desirable compression forces from the heavy load of the cables; but during construction, without that load, the towers may experience undesirable tension forces caused by lateral winds.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGimsing1997375-256]

### Substructure

Two schematic diagrams showing how force is transmitted in a flat bridge compared to an arched bridge

Abutments are an important element of a substructure. Beam bridges (left) direct force vertically into the abutments; some arch bridges (right) direct forces diagonally. 1 Deck, 2 Abutments, 3 Subsoil, 4 Load on bridge, 5 Force from abutment into subsoil.

[^cite_note-abutDiag-96]

Construction of all bridge types begins by creating the substructure. The first elements built are usually the footings and abutments, which are typically large blocks of reinforced concrete, entirely or partially buried underground. The footings and abutments support the entire weight of the bridge, and transfer the weight to the [subsoil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsoil).[^cite_note-foundation-257] Based on their height-to-width ratio, footings are categorized as: [shallow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shallow_foundations) (height is less than width) or [deep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_foundations) (height is greater than width).[^cite_note-deep-258] If the subsoil cannot support the load placed on the footings, [pilings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piling) must first be driven below the footings: pilings are long structures – made of wood, steel, or concrete – placed vertically below footings.[^cite_note-piles-259] Some pilings reach down and rest on [bedrock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bedrock); others rely on friction to prevent the footing from sinking lower.[^cite_note-piles-259]

Abutments are usually located at the ends of a bridge deck, where it reaches the subsoil.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEZhao20176,_23,_356–362-260] They direct the weight into the subsoil, either vertically or diagonally.[^cite_note-abutDiag-96] Abutments may also act as retaining walls, keeping the subsoil under the approach road from eroding.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEZhao20176,_23,_356–362-260] After footings for the [piers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pier_\(bridge_structure\)) have been created, the piers and pier caps are built to complete the substructure.[^cite_note-261][^cite_note-263] Suspension bridges usually require anchorages, which are large reinforced concrete blocks solidly anchored into the earth – they must be exceptionally heavy and tied into the subsoil because they must withstand the lateral pull of the large cables that hold the entire deck and live load.[^cite_note-anchor-264][^cite_note-266]

#### Constructing supports in water

A large concrete structure in the middle of a river, kept dry by a steel wall surrounding it

This concrete bridge pier is being built within a steel [cofferdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cofferdam).

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"I-205_Abernethy_Bridge"._''Oregon_Department_of_Transportation''-267]

When bridge supports (piers or towers) are built in a river, lake, or ocean, special technologies must be utilized.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGerwick2014137–138-268] [Caissons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caisson_\(engineering\)) can be used to provide a workspace while constructing the submerged portion of the supports. A caisson is a large, watertight, hollow structure, open on the bottom. It is usually sunk to the bottom of the water and workers can work inside, preparing the ground for the footings. When excavation is complete, a caisson is typically filled with concrete to create all or part of the footing.[^cite_note-caisson-269] Air pressure inside a sealed caisson must be kept high to prevent water from seeping in.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECruickshank201053-270] Workers, if they do not properly [decompress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decompression_\(diving\)) when exiting the caisson, can get [decompression sickness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decompression_sickness).[^cite_note-bends-271] Early bridge builders did not understand decompression, and deaths were common: thirteen workers died from decompression sickness when building the [Eads Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eads_Bridge) (completed in 1874).[^cite_note-bends-271]

Another approach for constructing foundations in water is a [box caisson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caisson_\(engineering\)#Box), which is a large steel or concrete box, open on top, which is towed by [tugboats](216434.md) to the bridge site, then sunk to the bottom and filled with concrete.[^cite_note-boxCaisson-272] The [Akashi Kaikyo suspension bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akashi_Kaikyo_Bridge) used box caissons for its two foundations – each 70 meters (230 ft) tall and 80 meters (260 ft) in diameter. The caissons were sunk to the bottom in water 60 meters (200 ft) deep, and each was filled with 355,000 cubic meters of concrete. The foundations rest directly on the ocean bottom, without pilings or footings.[^cite_note-boxCaisson-272] An alternative to a caisson is a [cofferdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cofferdam), which is a temporary dam surrounding the support location, open on top, where workers may work while constructing the footings.[^cite_note-coffer-273]

#### Bearings

Main article: [Bridge bearing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridge_bearing)

Two cylinders of steel, supporting a large steel bridge, and resting on a concrete support

&#x20;[Bearings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridge_bearing) can prevent damage to the superstructure by permitting small movements.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEZhao2017424–434-274]

[Bearings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridge_bearing) are often placed between the superstructure and the substructure at the points of contact. Bearings are mechanical devices that enable small movements – which may result from [thermal expansion and contraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_expansion), [material creep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creep_\(deformation\)), or minor [seismic events](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seismic_event). Without bearings, the bridge structure may be damaged when such movements occur. Bearings can be selected to permit small rotational or slipping movements in a specific direction, without permitting movements in other directions. Types of bearings used on bridges include hinge bearings, roller bearings, rocker bearings, sliding bearings, spring bearings, and [elastomeric bearings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elastomeric_bridge_bearing).[^cite_note-bearing-275]

### Superstructure

After the substructure is complete, the superstructure is built, resting on the substructure. [Beam bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beam_bridge) superstructures may be built in place, or fabricated off-site ([precast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precast)) and transported to the bridge site.[^cite_note-276] Precast beams may be placed atop the supports by a crane or [gantry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gantry_crane).[^cite_note-277] If the span crosses a deep ravine, a technique known as [launching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incremental_launching) may be used: the beams and deck are assembled on the approach road, then pushed horizontally across the obstacle.[^cite_note-launch-278][^cite_note-279]

A bridge being constructed, with two large cranes on top

[Gantries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gantry_crane) are one technique used to gradually assemble a bridge deck.

[^cite_note-280]

[Arch bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arch_bridge) superstructure construction methods depend on the material. Concrete or stone arches use a temporary wood structure known as [falsework](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falsework) or [centering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centering) to support the arch while it is built.[^cite_note-falsework-281] Some steel arch bridges are constructed with falsework, but others use cantilevering to build both halves out from the abutments.[^cite_note-282]

[Cantilever bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantilever_bridge) superstructures are usually built incrementally by proceeding outward from anchorages or piers. Most cantilever superstructures can be built without temporary support piers, as the bridge can support itself as it extends outward. A similar process is used for steel or concrete cantilevers: prefabricated sections may be positioned at ground (or water) level and hoisted into place with a gantry, or may be transported horizontally along the previously completed portion of the cantilever. Concrete cantilevers require steel prestressing cables to be passed through tubes within each section and tightened, which will put the concrete into compression.[^cite_note-283] [Truss bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truss_bridge) are built using a variety of methods, including piece-by-piece, cantilevering, or falsework.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKulicki2014a307–308-284]

[Cable-stayed bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cable-stayed_bridge) superstructures begin with the construction of one or more towers which rest directly on footings that are part of the substructure. The deck is constructed in pieces beginning at the towers[^cite_note-OneTower-110] and moving outward. The pieces can be put into place by hoisting, supporting from below, [launching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incremental_launching), or cantilevering from the portion of the deck that has been assembled.[^cite_note-CabStyConstr-285] As each piece of the deck is added, it is connected to towers with steel cables, and the cables are tightened to take the load of the deck.[^cite_note-CabStyConstr-285]

[Suspension bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suspension_bridge) superstructure construction usually begins with the towers.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGimsing1997434–436-286][^cite_note-OneTower-110] The towers may be steel or concrete, and rest directly on footings. The large cables are created by hauling a large pulley back and forth across the span, stringing multiple wires between the anchorages in each pass, in a process termed *spinning*. After the wires are spun, they are bundled together to form the cables.[^cite_note-288] The cables are securely fastened to the anchorages at both ends.[^cite_note-290] Vertical wires called *hangers* are suspended from the cables, then small sections of the deck are attached to the hangers, and the sections are attached to each other.[^cite_note-291]

#### Towers

A thick steel cable passing over the top of a suspension bridge tower

A [suspension bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suspension_bridge) cable transfers its load to the tower by resting on a curved saddle.

Towers, made of either concrete or steel, are an important component of the superstructure of cable-stayed bridges and suspension bridges.[^cite_note-293] Concrete is generally suitable for towers up to about 250 meters (820 ft) tall, whereas steel towers can be taller.[^cite_note-294][^cite_note-296] Towers support the bridge cables, which hold the weight of the deck and the traffic. Most of the load imposed on a tower is applied vertically downward on the tower, rather than sideways.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGimsing1997345–347-297] Towers experience a compression stress, in contrast to cables, which experience a tension stress.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett199984-112] There are two mechanisms used to attach a cable to a tower: saddles or anchors. Saddles are curved structures which allow a cable to pass through (or over the top of) a tower. An anchor holds the end of a cable. Saddles are often used in suspension bridges, and anchors are often used in cable-stayed bridges.[^cite_note-298]

#### Cables

Two men are standing high in the air on a walkway, and a wheel is above them, suspended by wires.

A spinning wheel pulls two wires at a time to gradually build up a suspension bridge cable.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETalese201465–77-299]

Steel cables are an element of both cable-stayed bridges and suspension bridges. Cables are made of one or more strands, and each strand consists of multiple wires. A wire is a thin, flexible piece of solid steel, of higher tensile strength than normal steel, and with a diameter of 3mm to 7mm.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGimsing199787–88-300][^cite_note-302] Cables are typically constructed at the bridge site by unspooling wires or strands from large [reels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reel).[^cite_note-303] Large suspension bridges may use cables that are over 1 meter (3 ft 3 in) in diameter and weigh over 20,000 tonnes (44,092,450 lb).[^cite_note-304]

Before building the cables of a suspension bridge, temporary [catwalks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Footbridge#Catwalk) must be constructed to support the wires while they are drawn across the span and over the tops of the towers.[^cite_note-305] There are two approaches to pulling the wires across the span: the air spinning method (in which individual wires are carried across by pulleys); and the prefabricated strand method (in which entire strands are pulled across).[^cite_note-306][^cite_note-308]

The air spinning method was used for all suspension bridges until the prefabricated strand method was invented in the 1960s.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGimsing199737–38-307]  After 300 to 500 wires are pulled, aluminum bands are used to bundle them into strands.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGimsing1997424–425-309][^cite_note-311] The wires within a strand may be parallel, or they may wrap around each other in a twisted (spiral) pattern.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJonesHowells2000599–602-312] Air spinning always produces strands that contain parallel wires. The prefabricated strand method can utilize strands with parallel or twisted wires.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJonesHowells2000599–602-312][^cite_note-314]

#### Deck

Main article: [Deck (bridge)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deck_\(bridge\))

A large concrete arch bridge being constructed

The deck of this arch bridge is being [horizontally pushed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incremental_launching) onto the substructure with [jacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_\(device\)).

[^cite_note-launch-278]

The [deck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deck_\(bridge\)) of a bridge is the flat, horizontal surface that extends across the full span of a bridge. Decks generally rest on beams or box girders. When a deck is rigidly attached to its supporting beams or girders, they function together as a single structure.[^cite_note-deckgirder-315] Two common types of decks are concrete decks and [orthotropic steel decks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthotropic_steel_deck).[^cite_note-316][^cite_note-319] Concrete decks are flat [slabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concrete_slab) of reinforced concrete. The slabs may [precast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precast_concrete) off-site, or [cast-in-place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cast-in-place_concrete) by pouring concrete into [forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formwork) on the bridge superstructure.[^cite_note-320][^cite_note-322] Orthotropic steel decks consist of a flat steel plate, coated with a [wearing surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wearing_surface).[^cite_note-323] Numerous small steel [ribs](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/rib) are welded to the underside of the top plate, running in the direction of the bridge roadway.[^cite_note-325] Below the ribs are steel floor beams, placed crosswise to the ribs.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMangus2014590,_592-326][^cite_note-327] Orthotropic steel decks are more expensive than concrete steel decks, but weigh less. They are useful in applications where weight is critical, a thin deck is required, or the environment is subject to earthquakes or extreme cold weather.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMangus2014590,_604-328]

Many decks have a wearing surface on top, which is a layer of material designed to be periodically replaced after it is worn away by vehicular traffic. Wearing surfaces are typically made of [aggregate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Construction_aggregate) (small rocks) mixed with a [binder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binder_\(material\)) such as asphalt, polyurethane, epoxy resins, or polyester.[^cite_note-329][^cite_note-331] Railway bridge decks are categorized as open decks (the [ties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railroad_tie) rest directly on beams or girders, with air gaps between) and [ballast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Track_ballast) decks (the ties rest on ballast rocks, and the ballast rests on a deck slab).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESorgenfrei2014147–148-332]

Constructing the deck (and its supporting beams or girders) can be difficult when the bridge is over water or a deep valley. A variety of techniques are available, and the choice depends on the topography of the site, the deck material (concrete or steel), traffic or obstacles under the bridge, and whether sections can be built off-site and transported to the bridge. Methods of deck construction include building atop [temporary supports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falsework#In_bridge_construction), [jacking up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_\(device\)) from the ground, [incremental launching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incremental_launching) (building the entire deck on the approach road and pushing it horizontally), lifting from below with a [hoist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoist_\(device\)) mounted on the bridge, [cantilevering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantilever#In_bridges,_towers,_and_buildings) (incrementally extending the deck, starting from towers or abutments), and lifting with a [floating crane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crane_vessel).[^cite_note-constrMethod-333]

### Protection

A thick, old wire cable, with paint that is partially worn off

Paint can be used to reduce deterioration of steel components. Steel bridges need to be repainted periodically, as seen in this wire hanger from the [Golden Gate Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Gate_Bridge), which is painted [international orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_orange#Golden_Gate_Bridge).

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"Painting_The_Golden_Gate_Bridge"._''Golden_Gate_Bridge,_Highway_and_Transportation_District''-334]

To achieve the designed service life, a bridge must be protected from deterioration by incorporating certain features into the design. Bridges can deteriorate due to a variety of causes, including rust, corrosion, chemical actions, and mechanical abrasion. Deterioration is sometimes visible as rust on steel components, or cracks and [spalling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spall#Spalling_in_refractory_concrete) in concrete.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMulheron2000805–807-335] Deterioration can be slowed with various measures, primarily aimed at excluding water and oxygen from the bridge elements.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMulheron2000807–809-336] Techniques to prevent water-based damage include drainage systems, waterproofing membranes (such as polymer films), and eliminating [expansion joints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expansion_joints).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMulheron2000810–816-337][^cite_note-339] Concrete bridge elements can be protected with waterproof seals and coatings.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMulheron2000816–822-340][^cite_note-342] Reinforcing steel within concrete can be protected by using high-quality concrete and increasing the thickness of the concrete surrounding the steel.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMulheron2000822–830-343] Steel elements of a bridge can be protected by paints or by [galvanizing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galvanizing) with zinc.[^cite_note-344] Paint can be avoided entirely for steel members by using [stainless steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stainless_steel) or [weathering steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weathering_steel) (a steel alloy that eliminates the need for paint, by forming a protective outer layer of rust).[^cite_note-345]

[Bridge scour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridge_scour) is a potentially serious problem when bridge footings are located in water. Currents in the water can cause the sand and rocks around and below the footings to wash away over time. This effect can be mitigated by placing a cofferdam around the footings, or surrounding the footings with [large, carefully placed rocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rip-rap).[^cite_note-346][^cite_note-348] Suspension bridges and cable-stayed bridges have large cables containing hundreds of steel wires. Several techniques are used to minimize corrosion inside the cables, including wrapping the cables with galvanized wire, injecting the cables with grout or epoxy, using interlocking S-profile wires, and circulating dry air through the interior of the cable.[^cite_note-349] Bridges with supports in navigable waterways are designed to withstand [ship strikes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship_strike) up to a specific, predefined magnitude. In addition to waterway markings and pilot warning systems, bridge supports in water may be surrounded by physical protections such as [fenders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fender_\(boating\)), [pilings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolphin_\(structure\)), or small artificial islands.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnottPrucz2014107–109-350]

## Operation and financing

### Management

Further information: [Bridge management system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridge_management_system)

After a bridge is completed and becomes operational, management processes are employed to ensure that it remains open to traffic, avoids safety incidents, and achieves its intended lifespan. These processes – collectively referred to as *bridge management* –  include technical activities – namely, maintenance, inspection, [monitoring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_health_monitoring), and testing.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEVassie2000849-351] In addition to technical tasks, management encompasses planning, budgeting, and prioritization of maintenance activities.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEVassie2000849-351] Bridge managers use [bridge management systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridge_management_system) and [life-cycle cost analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life-cycle_cost_analysis) methodologies to manage a bridge and estimate the maintenance costs of a bridge throughout its lifetime.[^cite_note-352] Annual maintenance costs increase as the bridge ages and degrades.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHu201667-353]

### Maintenance

A small, movable cage with two persons standing in it, is hanging below a large white suspension bridge

A crew of workers are using a maintenance traveler (the mobile cage structure) to inspect the [Clifton Suspension Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifton_Suspension_Bridge).&#x20;

Maintenance activities seek to prolong the life of the bridge, reduce lifecycle costs, and ensure the safety of the community.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAlampalli2014269–272-354] Maintenance tasks can be categorized as corrective tasks and preventive tasks.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAlampalli2014276,_282-355] Corrective tasks are implemented in response to unexpected issues that arise, for example, repairing structural elements (piers, beams, girders, towers, or cables) and replacing bearings.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAlampalli2014288–292-356]

Preventive tasks include washing, painting, lubricating bearings, sealing the deck, filling cracks, removing snow, filling potholes, and repairing minor issues with structures and electrical fixtures.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAlampalli2014282–288-357] Some preventive tasks are performed on a periodic schedule. An example schedule for periodic bridge maintenance tasks is: washing entire structure (1–2 years); sealing deck surface (4–6 years); lubricating bearings (4 years); painting steel bridge components (12–15 years); replacing the deck's wearing surface (12 years); sealing sidewalks (5 years); filling cracks (4 years); and cleaning drains (2 years).[^cite_note-358]

### Inspection and monitoring

A tall bridge covered in temporary scaffolding

[Scaffolding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scaffolding) is erected under the *[Sitterviadukt](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sitterviadukt%20\(S%C3%BCdostbahn\))* rail bridge in Switzerland while maintenance on the deck truss is performed.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEQuirchmair2022-359]

An important part of maintenance is inspecting a bridge for damage or degradation, and taking steps to mitigate any issues detected. Degradation can come from environmental sources, including expansion/contraction from freeze/thaw cycles, rain, oxidation of steel, and sea spray. Human activities may also cause damage, for example: vehicular traffic, mechanical abrasion, poor bridge design, and improper repair procedures.[^cite_note-360] Some countries mandate periodic inspection schedules, for example, routine inspections every 24 months, or inspecting underwater foundations for scouring every 60 months.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECopelan2014338-361]

&#x20;Relying solely on visual inspection to assess degradation of a bridge can be unreliable, so inspectors use a variety of [nondestructive testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nondestructive_testing) techniques.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWasher2014301–305-362] These techniques include [hammer strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schmidt_hammer) tests, [ultrasonic pulse velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrasonic_testing) tests, [seismic tomography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seismic_tomography), and [ground penetrating radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_penetrating_radar).[^cite_note-363]  Various electrical tests that assess [permeability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permeability_\(electromagnetism\)) and [resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_resistance) can give insight into the condition of surface concrete.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAbdunur2000904–905-364] X-rays can be passed through concrete to obtain data about concrete density and condition.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAbdunur2000907–909-365] Videography using slender probes can be used where access is available.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAbdunur2000910–912-366] Measurements of the state of a bridge may be made automatically and periodically using [structural health monitoring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_health_monitoring) (SHM) technologies.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFrangopol2014247–248-367] Some testing – termed [destructive testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Destructive_testing) – requires removing samples from the bridge and taking them to a laboratory for analysis with microscopes, sonic devices, or X-ray diffraction.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAbdunur2000896–898,_955-368] Destructive testing is performed by removing [cores drilled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Core_sample) from concrete, or a small piece of steel wire cut from a cable.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAbdunur2000896–898,_955-368]

### Financing

Funding for bridge construction and operation comes from a variety of sources, including [fuel taxes](360781.md), annual [vehicle registration fees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vehicle_registration_plate), [tolls](63993672.md), [congestion fees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congestion_pricing), and usage fees based on satellite tracking.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEQueiroz20167–9-369] Some bridges – particularly in [developing countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developing_countries) – are financed by international sources including the [World Bank](36752.md) or China's [Belt and Road Initiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belt_and_Road_Initiative).[^cite_note-370] [Toll systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toll_bridge) are generally an inefficient mechanism for collecting funding, particularly when [tollbooths](65442834.md) are used, because they are expensive to build and manage. Tollbooths can slow down traffic and interfere with the construction of entry or exit points.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEQueiroz20167–8-371]

The cost of building a bridge is typically borne by government agencies, but since 1990 an increasing number of bridges are built and paid for by private companies using a [public–private partnership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public%E2%80%93private_partnership) (PPP) agreement. In a PPP project, the government grants the right to build the bridge to a company, and the company recoups its expenses by collecting tolls for a fixed period of time.[^cite_note-372][^cite_note-374] At the end of the period, the bridge is transferred to government ownership, and the government may choose to continue to charge tolls or not. Notable bridges constructed with a PPP model include the [Queen Elizabeth II Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Elizabeth_II_Bridge) (built in 1991, toll collection period 20 years) and the [Second Severn Crossing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Severn_Crossing) (built in 1996, toll collection period 30 years).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEngel202028–29-373]

### Failures

See also: [List of bridge failures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_bridge_failures)

A broken bridge, which has fallen into the water over which it used to pass

The [Nanfang'ao Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanfang'ao_Bridge) in Taiwan collapsed because of excessive corrosion that went undetected.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"TTSB_Details_Reasons_for_Nanfang'ao_Bridge_Collapse"._''Focus_Taiwan''-375]

Bridge failures are of special importance to [structural engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_engineers), because the [analyses of the failures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Failure_analysis) provide [lessons learned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lessons_learned) that serve to improve design and construction processes.[^cite_note-376] Bridge failures have a variety of causes, which can be categorized as natural factors (flood, scour, earthquake, landslide, and wind) and human factors (improper design and construction method, collision, overloading, fire, corrosion, and lack of inspection and maintenance).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEChoudhuryHasnat201526–28-377] Over time, bridge failures have led to significant improvements in bridge design, construction, and maintenance practices.[^cite_note-378] Before the advent of bridge engineering procedures based on rigorous, scientific principles, bridges frequently failed. Failures were most common in the mid-19th century, when the rapidly expanding railway networks were building hundreds of new bridges every year around the globe.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBarker200722-379] In the United States, 40 bridges per year failed in the 1870s, amounting to 25% of all bridges built in that decade.[^cite_note-380]

In the modern era, in spite of advances in bridge engineering methodologies, bridge failures continue to occur regularly.[^cite_note-381] In Australia, the [King Street Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Street_Bridge_\(Melbourne\)) collapsed in 1962, a year after opening, due to improper welding techniques.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEChoudhuryHasnat201530–31-382] In Palau, the [Koror–Babeldaob Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koror%E2%80%93Babeldaob_Bridge) collapsed in 1996, three months after a repair operation made major changes to the bridge.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBurgoyneScantlebury2008-383] In 1998, the [Turag-Bhakurta Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turag-Bhakurta_Bridge?action=edit\&redlink=1) in Bangladesh collapsed due to river waters scouring away the soil around the bridge supports.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEChoudhuryHasnat201532-384] The [Millennium Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennium_Bridge,_London) in London opened in 2000, but closed two days later due to excessive swaying. It did not open until two years later – after dampers were installed.[^cite_note-millen-385] About half of all bridge failures in the early 21st century in the US were due to flood damage or scouring (water currents undermining the bridge supports).[^cite_note-386]

## Society and culture

### Signature bridges

A large bridge crossing a river, in nighttime, with skyscrapers in the background

The [Dagu Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dagu_Bridge) in China was designed to be a signature bridge.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETang201419–20-387]

Many bridges – known as *signature bridges* – are strongly identified with a particular community.[^cite_note-388][^cite_note-389] Large suspension bridges, in particular, are often regarded as iconic landmarks that symbolize the cities in which they are located. Notable examples include the [Brooklyn Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooklyn_Bridge) in New York; the [Golden Gate Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Gate_Bridge) in San Francisco; the [Clifton Suspension Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifton_Suspension_Bridge) in Bristol; and the [Széchenyi Chain Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sz%C3%A9chenyi_Chain_Bridge) in Budapest.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECruickshank2010233-390][^cite_note-392] Some visually impressive bridges, such as the [Dagu Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dagu_Bridge) in China, are designed with the express goal of creating a landmark for the host city.[^cite_note-393] Dan Cruickshank notes that some bridges have the ability to "transform a place a community and ... can make its mark on the landscape and in men's minds, capture the imagination, engender pride and sense of identity and define a time and place."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECruickshank20108–9-188]

### Economic and environmental impact

Bridges can have significant impacts – both positive and negative –  on a community's environment, society, and economy. During the bridge design process, these effects may be modeled with [life cycle sustainability assessment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life-cycle_assessment) or [building information modeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Building_information_modeling_in_green_building), and the results can be used to adjust the bridge's design to improve its effect on the environment, society, and economy.[^cite_note-sustain-394]

Positive effects of a new bridge can include shorter transport times, employment opportunities, improvements to social equity, improved productivity, and increases to the [gross domestic product](12594.md) (GDP).[^cite_note-sustain-394] Construction of a new bridge can increase wages in the surrounding region, but can also increase income inequity between genders (men see larger wage gains than women) and between education levels (higher-educated persons see more gains than lower-educated persons).[^cite_note-395] In locales where flooding is common, bridges can increase overall income by providing reliable crossings across rivers.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrooks2021-396] In underdeveloped regions with mountainous topography, construction of bridges that cross deep valleys can bring major benefits to the communities they connect. Without bridges, such areas often have a core region that is more prosperous, surrounded by less developed peripheral regions. Building bridges over deep valleys can reduce developmental disparities between areas, as well as generate economic development, and improve accessibility to goods and services.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECaiDeng2024-397]

[Global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming) can be exacerbated by the creation of a new bridge, because the [production of concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cement#CO2_emissions) significantly contributes to the [greenhouse effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_effect).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEZhou2021-398][^cite_note-399] Although bridges can boost the economy of the surrounding region, they also increase environmental [pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollution) proportionally.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEZhou2021-398] [Corruption](3608404.md) endemic in the construction industry (including bridge building) can produce negative societal and economic consequences.[^cite_note-corrupt-400] Bridges that carry highways can result in increased vehicular collisions, which have economic costs (medical care and lost productivity) averaging over €14,000 each.[^cite_note-401]

### Suicide

[Suicides](16831059.md) are sometimes carried out by [jumping off bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suicide_bridge). This method can account for 20% to 70% of suicides in urban areas with access to tall bridges.[^cite_note-403] In some regions, suicide by jumping disproportionately affects young adults, who tend to have lower [inhibitory control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inhibitory_control). Specific bridges can gain notoriety and attract persons experiencing a [suicidal crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suicidal_crisis), which creates a [feedback loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feedback_loop). High-risk bridges often have [suicide prevention barriers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suicide_barrier) installed,[^cite_note-404] which dramatically decrease the suicide rate on the bridge.[^cite_note-405] Installing barriers on a high-risk bridge generally reduces the jumping suicide rate in a region, although in some instances, other bridges become substitutes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMerliCostanza2024-402]

### Profession and regulation

Further information: [Regulation and licensure in engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulation_and_licensure_in_engineering)

The profession of [civil engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_engineering) – which includes the discipline of bridge building – began to be formalized in the 18th century when a school of engineering was created in France within the [Corps des Ponts et Chaussées](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corps_des_Ponts_et_Chauss%C3%A9es) at the [École de Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89cole_de_Paris_\(engineering\)?action=edit\&redlink=1), under the direction of [Jacques Gabriel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Gabriel).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett199924-406] In 1747 the first school dedicated to bridge building was founded: the [École Nationale des Ponts et Chaussées](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89cole_Nationale_des_Ponts_et_Chauss%C3%A9es) led by French engineers [Daniel-Charles Trudaine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel-Charles_Trudaine) and [Jean-Rodolphe Perronet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Rodolphe_Perronet).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBennett199924-406] The first professional organization focused on civil engineering was the [Institution of Civil Engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institution_of_Civil_Engineers) founded in 1818 in the UK, initially led by [Thomas Telford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Telford).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWatson1988viii,_14–16,_53-407]

In the modern era, bridge engineering is regulated by national organizations, such as the [National Council of Examiners for Engineering and Surveying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Council_of_Examiners_for_Engineering_and_Surveying) (US), the [Canadian Council of Professional Engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Council_of_Professional_Engineers) (Canada), and the [Engineering Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering_Council) (UK).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"The_History_of_the_National_Council"._''NCEES''1,_161,_366-408] In many countries, bridge engineers must be licensed or meet minimal educational requirements.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"Engineering:_Issues_Challenges_and_Opportunities"._''UNESCO''27–28,_135–136,_358–365-409] Some countries require engineers to pass qualification examinations, for example, in the US engineers must pass the [Fundamentals of Engineering exam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamentals_of_Engineering_exam) followed by the [Principles and Practice of Engineering exam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principles_and_Practice_of_Engineering_exam).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"Occupational_Outlook_Handbook"._''Department_of_Labor''148-410] In Poland, bridge engineers are required to obtain certification by accumulating several years of experience under a senior engineer, and passing an exam administered by the [Polish Chamber of Civil Engineers](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polska%20Izba%20In%C5%BCynier%C3%B3w%20Budownictwa).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBiliszczuk2014632-411] International cooperation in the field of engineering is facilitated by the [World Federation of Engineering Organizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Federation_of_Engineering_Organizations).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE"The_History_of_the_National_Council"._''NCEES''70–71,_88,_123–125,_366,_377-412]

### Art and culture

Further information: [Bridges in art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridges_in_art)

A man blowing a trumpet, with a rainbow in the background

In [Norse mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norse_mythology), the [Bifröst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bifr%C3%B6st) rainbow bridge connects Earth with [Asgard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asgard).

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWatson19371-413]

> Reaching for the world, as our lives do,\
> &#x20;As all lives do, reaching that we may give\
> &#x20;The best of what we are and hold as true:\
> &#x20;Always it is by bridges that we live.

— [Philip Larkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Larkin) "Bridge for the Living" (1981)

[^cite_note-Cruickshank_2010_13–14-414]

Bridges occur extensively in art, legend, and literature, often employed as metaphors or symbols of human accomplishment, lifespan, or experience.[^cite_note-415] In [Norse mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norse_mythology), the home of the gods – [Asgard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asgard) – is connected to the earth by [Bifröst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bifr%C3%B6st), a rainbow bridge.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWatson19371-413] Many bridges in Europe are named *[Devil's Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devil's_Bridge)*, and in some cases have folkloric stories that explain why the bridge is associated with the devil.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWatson193733,_35–39-416] Christian legend holds that [St. Bénézet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._B%C3%A9n%C3%A9zet) lifted a huge boulder to begin construction of the [Pont Saint-Bénézet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pont_Saint-B%C3%A9n%C3%A9zet) bridge, and went on to found the apocryphal [Bridge-Building Brotherhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridge-Building_Brotherhood).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWatson193743–48-417] Bridges feature prominently in paintings – often in the background – as in the *[Mona Lisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mona_Lisa)*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECruickshank201014–16-418]

In the modern era, bridges continue to feature prominently in culture. Bridges are often the setting for pageants, celebrations, and processions.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWatson1937103–106-419] Authors have used bridges as the centerpiece of novels, notably *[The Bridge on the Drina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bridge_on_the_Drina)* by [Ivo Andrić](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivo_Andri%C4%87) and [Thornton Wilder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thornton_Wilder)'s *[The Bridge of San Luis Rey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bridge_of_San_Luis_Rey)*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECruickshank201017–19-420] British poet [Philip Larkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Larkin), inspired by the construction of the [Humber Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humber_Bridge) near his home, wrote "Bridge for the Living" in 1981.[^cite_note-Cruickshank_2010_13–14-414] Neighboring nations have chosen to designate some shared bridges as *friendship bridges* or *peace bridges*.[^cite_note-421][^cite_note-422] In 1996, the [European Commission](9974.md) held a competition to select art for the [euro banknotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_banknotes). [Robert Kalina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Kalina), an Austrian designer, won with a set of illustrations of bridges, chosen because they symbolize links between states in the union and paths to the future.[^cite_note-euro-423]
