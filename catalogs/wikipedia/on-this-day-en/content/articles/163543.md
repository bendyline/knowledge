**Modern art** includes artistic work produced during the period extending roughly from the 1860s to the 1970s, and denotes the styles and [philosophies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophies) of the [art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art) produced during that era.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAtkins1997\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsartspeakguidetoc00atki_0page118mode2up_118–119\]-1] The term is usually associated with art in which the traditions of the past have been thrown aside in a spirit of experimentation.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGombrich1995\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsstoryofart00gomb_0page557mode1up_557\]-2] Modern artists experimented with new ways of seeing and with fresh ideas about the nature of materials and functions of art. A tendency away from the [narrative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narrative), which was characteristic of the traditional arts, toward [abstraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstraction) is characteristic of much modern art. More recent artistic production is often called [contemporary art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contemporary_art) or [Postmodern art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postmodern_art).

Modern art begins with the post-impressionist painters like [Vincent van Gogh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincent_van_Gogh), [Paul Cézanne](24472.md), [Paul Gauguin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Gauguin), [Georges Seurat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Seurat) and [Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_de_Toulouse-Lautrec). These artists were essential to modern art's development.[^cite_note-3] At the beginning of the 20th century [Henri Matisse](60203.md) and several other young artists including the [pre-cubists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Cubism) [Georges Braque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Braque), [André Derain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andr%C3%A9_Derain), [Raoul Dufy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raoul_Dufy), [Jean Metzinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Metzinger) and [Maurice de Vlaminck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_de_Vlaminck) revolutionized the Paris art world with "wild," multi-colored, expressive landscapes and figure paintings that the critics called [Fauvism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fauvism).[^cite_note-4] Matisse's two versions of *[The Dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dance_\(painting\))* signified a key point in his career and the development of modern painting.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEClement1996114-5] It reflected Matisse's incipient fascination with [primitive art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_art): the intense warm color of the figures against the cool blue-green background and the rhythmical succession of the dancing nudes convey the feelings of emotional liberation and [hedonism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedonism).

At the start of [20th-century Western painting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/20th-century_Western_painting), and initially influenced by Toulouse-Lautrec, Gauguin and other late-19th-century innovators, [Pablo Picasso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pablo_Picasso) made his first [Cubist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubism) paintings.[^cite_note-6] Picasso based these works on Cézanne's idea that all depiction of nature can be reduced to three solids: [cube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cube), [sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphere) and [cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cone_\(geometry\)).[^cite_note-7] Picasso dramatically created a new and radical picture depicting a raw and primitive brothel scene with five prostitutes, violently painted women, reminiscent of [African tribal masks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_tribal_masks) and his new Cubist inventions.[^cite_note-8] Between 1905 and 1911 [German Expressionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Expressionism) emerged in [Dresden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Die_Br%C3%BCcke) and [Munich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Der_Blaue_Reiter) with artists like [Ernst Ludwig Kirchner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Ludwig_Kirchner), [Wassily Kandinsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wassily_Kandinsky), [Franz Marc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Marc), [Paul Klee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Klee) and [August Macke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Macke).[^cite_note-auto-9][^cite_note-auto1-10] [Analytic cubism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_cubism) was jointly developed by Picasso and Georges Braque, exemplified by *Violin and Candlestick, Paris,* from about 1908 through 1912.[^cite_note-11] Analytic cubism, the first clear manifestation of cubism, was followed by [Synthetic cubism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_cubism), practiced by Braque, Picasso, [Fernand Léger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fernand_L%C3%A9ger), [Juan Gris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Gris), [Albert Gleizes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Gleizes), [Marcel Duchamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcel_Duchamp) and several other artists into the 1920s. [Synthetic cubism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_cubism) is characterized by the introduction of different textures, surfaces, [collage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collage) elements, [papier collé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papier_coll%C3%A9) and a large variety of merged subject matter.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEScobie1988\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsgertrudesteinma004194page104mode2upqsynthetic_103–107\]-12][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJohn-Steiner2006\[httpsbooksgooglecombooksidfEFRDAAAQBAJpgPA69_69\]-13]

The notion of modern art is closely related to [Modernism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernism).[^cite_note-15]

### Roots in the 19th century



[Édouard Manet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89douard_Manet), *[Boy Blowing Bubbles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boy_Blowing_Bubbles)*, 1867, [Calouste Gulbenkian Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calouste_Gulbenkian_Museum)

&#x20;Although modern [sculpture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sculpture) and [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture) are reckoned to have emerged at the end of the 19th century, the beginnings of modern [painting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Painting) can be located earlier.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEArnasonPrather1998\[httpsarchiveorgdetailshistoryofmoderna00arna_0page17mode1up_17\]-16] [Francisco Goya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_Goya) is considered by many as the Father of Modern Painting without being a Modernist himself, a fact of art history that later painters associated with Modernism as a style, acknowledge him as an influence.[^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19] The date perhaps most commonly identified as marking the birth of modern art as a movement is 1863,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEArnasonPrather1998\[httpsarchiveorgdetailshistoryofmoderna00arna_0page17mode1up_17\]-16] the year that [Édouard Manet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89douard_Manet) showed his painting *[Le déjeuner sur l'herbe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_d%C3%A9jeuner_sur_l'herbe)* in the [Salon des Refusés](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salon_des_Refus%C3%A9s) in Paris.[^cite_note-20] Earlier dates have also been proposed, among them 1855 (the year [Gustave Courbet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustave_Courbet) exhibited *[The Artist's Studio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Artist's_Studio)*) and 1784 (the year [Jacques-Louis David](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques-Louis_David) completed his painting *[The Oath of the Horatii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Oath_of_the_Horatii)*).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEArnasonPrather1998\[httpsarchiveorgdetailshistoryofmoderna00arna_0page17mode1up_17\]-16] In the words of art historian [H. Harvard Arnason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._Harvard_Arnason): "Each of these dates has significance for the development of modern art, but none categorically marks a completely new beginning .... A gradual metamorphosis took place in the course of a hundred years."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEArnasonPrather1998\[httpsarchiveorgdetailshistoryofmoderna00arna_0page17mode1up_17\]-16]

The strands of thought that eventually led to modern art can be traced back to the [Enlightenment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment).[^cite_note-22] The modern art critic [Clement Greenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clement_Greenberg), for instance, called [Immanuel Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant) "the first real Modernist" but also drew a distinction: "[The Enlightenment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Enlightenment) criticized from the outside ... Modernism criticizes from the inside."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenberg1982\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsmodernartmoderni00fraspage5mode1up_5\]-23] The [French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution) of 1789 uprooted assumptions and institutions that had for centuries been accepted with little question and accustomed the public to vigorous political and social debate.[^cite_note-24] This gave rise to what art historian [Ernst Gombrich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Gombrich) called a "self-consciousness that made people select the style of their building as one selects the pattern of a wallpaper."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGombrich1995\[httpsarchiveorgdetailsstoryofart00gomb_0page477mode1up_477\]-25]

The pioneers of modern art were [Romantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism), [Realists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realism_\(visual_arts\)) and [Impressionists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impressionism).[^cite_note-26][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEArnasonPrather1998\[httpsarchiveorgdetailshistoryofmoderna00arna_0page22mode1up_22\]-27] By the late 19th century, additional movements which were to be influential in modern art had begun to emerge: [Post-Impressionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-Impressionism) and [Symbolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolism_\(arts\)).

Influences upon these movements were varied: from exposure to Eastern decorative arts, particularly [Japanese printmaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japonism), to the coloristic innovations of [Turner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._M._W._Turner) and [Delacroix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eug%C3%A8ne_Delacroix), to a search for more [realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realism_\(arts\)) in the depiction of common life, as found in the work of painters such as [Jean-François Millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Fran%C3%A7ois_Millet). The advocates of realism stood against the [idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idealism) of the tradition-bound [academic art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_art) that enjoyed public and official favor.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECorinthSchusterVitaliButts199625-28] The most successful painters of the day worked either through commissions or through large public exhibitions of their work. There were official, government-sponsored painters' unions, while governments regularly held public exhibitions of new fine and decorative arts.

The Impressionists argued that people do not see objects but only the light that they reflect, and therefore painters should paint in natural light ([en plein air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/En_plein_air)) rather than in studios and should capture the effects of light in their work.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECogniat197561-29] Impressionist artists formed a group, *Société Anonyme Coopérative des Artistes Peintres, Sculpteurs, Graveurs* ("Association of Painters, Sculptors, and Engravers") which, despite internal tensions, mounted a series of independent exhibitions.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECogniat197543–49-30] The style was adopted by artists in different nations, in preference to a "national" style. These factors established the view that it was a ["movement."](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_movement) These traits—establishment of a working method integral to the art, the establishment of a movement or visible active core of support, and international adoption—would be repeated by artistic movements in the Modern period in art.

  [Franz Marc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Marc), *Rehe im Walde* (*Deer in Woods*), 1914, [Kunsthalle Karlsruhe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kunsthalle_Karlsruhe)

Among the movements that flowered in the first decade of the 20th century were [Fauvism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fauvism), [Cubism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubism), [Expressionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expressionism), and [Futurism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Futurism_\(art\)).

In 1905, a group of four German artists, led by [Ernst Ludwig Kirchner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Ludwig_Kirchner), formed [Die Brücke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Die_Br%C3%BCcke) (The Bridge) in the city of [Dresden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dresden).[^cite_note-auto-9] This was arguably the founding organization for the [German Expressionist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Expressionist) movement, though they did not use the word itself. A few years later, in 1911, a like-minded group of young artists formed [Der Blaue Reiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Der_Blaue_Reiter) (The Blue Rider) in Munich.[^cite_note-auto1-10] The name came from [Wassily Kandinsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wassily_Kandinsky)'s *Der Blaue Reiter* painting of 1903. Among their members were [Kandinsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wassily_Kandinsky), [Franz Marc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Marc), [Paul Klee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Klee), and [August Macke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Macke). However, the term "Expressionism" did not firmly establish itself until 1913.[^cite_note-Sheppard-31]<sup>: 274 </sup>

Futurism took off in Italy a couple years before [World War I](4764461.md) with the publication of [Filippo Tommaso Marinetti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filippo_Tommaso_Marinetti)'s *[Futurist Manifesto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifesto_of_Futurism)*.[^cite_note-32] [Benedetta Cappa Marinetti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedetta_Cappa), wife of Filippo Tommaso Marinetti, created the second wave of the artistic movement started by her husband. "Largely thanks to Benedetta, her husband F.T. Marinetti re orchestrated the shifting ideologies of Futurism to embrace feminine elements of intuition, spirituality, and the mystical forces of the earth."[^cite_note-33] She painted up until his death and spent the rest of her days tending to the spread and growth of this period in Italian art, which celebrated technology, speed, and all things new.[^cite_note-34]

During the years between 1910 and the end of [World War I](4764461.md) and after the heyday of [cubism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubism), several movements emerged in Paris. [Giorgio de Chirico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giorgio_de_Chirico) moved to Paris in July 1911, where he joined his brother Andrea (the poet and painter known as [Alberto Savinio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberto_Savinio)).[^cite_note-35] Through his brother, he met Pierre Laprade, a member of the jury at the [Salon d'Automne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salon_d'Automne) where he exhibited three of his dreamlike works: *Enigma of the Oracle*, *Enigma of an Afternoon* and *Self-Portrait*. In 1913, he exhibited his work at the [Salon des Indépendants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salon_des_Ind%C3%A9pendants) and Salon d'Automne, and his work was noticed by [Pablo Picasso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pablo_Picasso), [Guillaume Apollinaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guillaume_Apollinaire), and several others. His compelling and mysterious paintings are considered instrumental to the early beginnings of [Surrealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrealism). *[Song of Love](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Song_of_Love_\(Giorgio_de_Chirico\))* (1914) is one of the most famous works by de Chirico and is an early example of the surrealist style, though it was painted ten years before the movement was "founded" by [André Breton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andr%C3%A9_Breton) in 1924. The [School of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School_of_Paris), centered in [Montparnasse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montparnasse), flourished between the two world wars.

World War I brought an end to this phase but indicated the beginning of many [anti-art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-art) movements, such as the in [Zürich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dada#Z%C3%BCrich) and [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dada#Berlin) emerging [Dada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dada), including the work of [Emmy Hennings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmy_Hennings), [Hannah Höch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hannah_H%C3%B6ch), [Hugo Ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugo_Ball) and [Marcel Duchamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcel_Duchamp), and of Surrealism.[^cite_note-36] Artist groups like [de Stijl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Stijl) and [Bauhaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bauhaus) developed new ideas about the interrelation of the arts, architecture, design, and art education.[^cite_note-37]

Modern art was introduced to the United States with the [Armory Show](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armory_Show) in 1913 and through European artists who moved to the U.S. during [World War I](4764461.md).[^cite_note-38]

### After World War II

It was only after [World War II](32927.md), however, that the U.S. became the focal point of new artistic movements.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESaunders2013-39] The 1950s and 1960s saw the emergence of [Abstract Expressionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_Expressionism), [Color field painting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_field_painting), [Conceptual artists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conceptual_art) of [Art & Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_&_Language), [Pop art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pop_art), [Op art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Op_art), [Hard-edge painting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard-edge_painting), [Minimal art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_art), [Lyrical Abstraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyrical_Abstraction), [Fluxus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluxus), [Happening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Happening), [video art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_art), [Postminimalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postminimalism), [Photorealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photorealism) and various other movements. In the late 1960s and the 1970s, [Land art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_art), [performance art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Performance_art), conceptual art, and other new art forms attracted the attention of curators and critics, at the expense of more traditional media.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMullins200614-40] Larger [installations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Installation_art) and [performances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Performance_art) became widespread.

By the end of the 1970s, when cultural critics began speaking of "the end of painting" (the title of a provocative essay written in 1981 by [Douglas Crimp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Crimp)), [new media art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_media_art) had become a category in itself, with a growing number of artists experimenting with technological means such as [video art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_art).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMullins20069-41] Painting assumed renewed importance in the 1980s and 1990s, as evidenced by the rise of [neo-expressionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-expressionism) and the revival of [figurative painting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Figurative_art).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMullins200614–15-42]

Towards the end of the 20th century, many artists and architects started questioning the idea of "the modern" and created typically [Postmodern works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postmodern_art).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJencks1987\[\[Category\:Wikipedia_articles_needing_page_number_citations_from_April_2021\]\]<sup_class="noprint_Inline-Template_"_style="white-space\:nowrap;">\[<i>\[\[Wikipedia\:Citing_sources|<span_title="This_citation_requires_a_reference_to_the_specific_page_or_range_of_pages_in_which_the_material_appears. (April_2021)">page needed</span>\]\]</i>\]</sup>-43]
