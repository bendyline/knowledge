The **euro area** (**EA**),[^cite_note-8] commonly called the **eurozone** (**EZ**), is a [currency union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monetary_union) of 21 [member states](10890716.md) of the [European Union](9317.md) (EU) that have adopted the [euro](9472.md) ([€](1797747.md)) as their primary [currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency) and sole [legal tender](267793.md), and have thus fully implemented [Economic and Monetary Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_and_Monetary_Union_of_the_European_Union) policies.&#x20;

The 21 eurozone members are: [Austria](26964606.md), [Belgium](3343.md), [Bulgaria](3415.md), [Croatia](5573.md), [Cyprus](5593.md), [Estonia](28222445.md), [Finland](10577.md), [France](5843419.md), [Germany](11867.md), [Greece](12108.md), [Ireland](14560.md), [Italy](14532.md), [Latvia](17514.md), [Lithuania](17675.md), [Luxembourg](17515.md), [Malta](19137.md), the [Netherlands](21148.md), [Portugal](23033.md), [Slovakia](26830.md), [Slovenia](27338.md), and [Spain](26667.md).

The largest economies in the eurozone are [Germany](11867.md), [France](5843419.md), [Italy](14532.md), and [Spain](26667.md). Four non-EU member states, namely [Andorra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andorra%E2%80%93European_Union_relations), [Monaco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monaco%E2%80%93European_Union_relations), [San Marino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Marino%E2%80%93European_Union_relations), and [Vatican City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_See-European_Union_relations), have formal agreements with the EU to use the euro as their official currency and issue their own coins.[^cite_note-agreements2-9][^cite_note-design2-10][^cite_note-amisdeleuro2-11] In addition, [Kosovo](17391.md) and [Montenegro](20760.md) have adopted the euro unilaterally, relying on euros already in circulation rather than printing or minting currencies of their own.[^cite_note-agreements_22-12] These six countries, however, have no representation in any eurozone institution.[^cite_note-13]

The [Eurosystem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurosystem) is the [monetary authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monetary_authority) of the eurozone, the [Eurogroup](21533929.md) is an informal body of [finance ministers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finance_minister) that makes [fiscal policy](132645.md) for the currency union, and the [European System of Central Banks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_System_of_Central_Banks) is responsible for fiscal and monetary cooperation between eurozone and non-eurozone EU members. The [European Central Bank](9474.md) (ECB) makes monetary policy for the eurozone, sets its base [interest rate](163115.md), and issues euro banknotes and coins. Since the [2008 financial crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_financial_crisis), the eurozone has established and used provisions for granting emergency loans to member states in return for enacting economic reforms.[^cite_note-14] The eurozone has also enacted some limited [fiscal integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiscal_union); for example, in peer review of each other's national budgets. The issue is political and in a state of flux in terms of what further provisions will be agreed for eurozone change.

The eurozone comprises about half the countries in geographical [Europe](9239.md).[^cite_note-15] Within the European Union (EU), six member states have not yet adopted the euro and continue to use their own national currencies: the [Czech Republic](5321.md), [Denmark](76972.md), [Hungary](13275.md), [Poland](22936.md), [Romania](25445.md), and [Sweden](5058739.md). Of these, all except Denmark are legally committed to adopting the euro once they meet the required [convergence criteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_convergence_criteria).[^cite_note-16] To date, no country has left the eurozone, and there are no formal provisions for either voluntary withdrawal or expulsion.[^cite_note-17]

## Territory

### Eurozone



Press conference at the Council of the EU for the launching of the Euro in 1998

In 1998, eleven [member states of the European Union](10890716.md) had met the [euro convergence criteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_convergence_criteria), and the eurozone came into existence with the official launch of the euro (alongside national currencies) on 1 January 1999 in those countries: Austria, Belgium, Finland, France, Germany, Ireland, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Portugal, and Spain.[^cite_note-EuroStart01-18] Greece qualified in 2000 and was admitted on 1 January 2001.[^cite_note-EuroStartGreece01-19]

These twelve founding members introduced physical [euro banknotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_banknotes) and [euro coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_coins) on 1 January 2002. After a short transition period, they took out of circulation and rendered invalid their pre-euro national coins and notes.

Between 2007 and 2026, nine more states have acceded: Bulgaria, Croatia, Cyprus, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Malta, Slovakia, and Slovenia.&#x20;

#### With formal agreement

The euro is also used in countries outside the EU. Four [microstates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microstate) (Andorra, Monaco, San Marino, and Vatican City) have signed formal agreements with the EU to use the euro and issue their own coins.[^cite_note-agreements-41][^cite_note-agreements_2-42] Nevertheless, they are not considered part of the eurozone by the ECB and do not have a seat in the ECB or Euro Group.

[Akrotiri and Dhekelia](1025144.md) are located on the island of Cyprus but are [British Overseas Territories](207114.md) which are part of the United Kingdom. There are agreements between the United Kingdom and Cyprus and between United Kingdom and EU about their partial adoption of Cypriot law, including the usage of euro in Akrotiri and Dhekelia.[^cite_note-43]

Several currencies are pegged to the euro, some of them with a fluctuation band and others with an exact rate. The [Bosnia and Herzegovina convertible mark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosnia_and_Herzegovina_convertible_mark) was once pegged to the [Deutsche mark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsche_mark) at par, and continues to be pegged to the euro today at the Deutsche mark's old rate (1.95583 per euro). The [West African](3791898.md) and [Central African CFA francs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_African_CFA_franc) are pegged exactly at 655.957 CFA to 1 EUR. In 1998, in anticipation of [Economic and Monetary Union of the European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_and_Monetary_Union_of_the_European_Union), the [Council of the European Union](5866.md) addressed the monetary agreements France had with the [CFA Zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CFA_franc) and Comoros, and ruled that the ECB had no obligation towards the convertibility of the CFA and [Comorian francs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comorian_franc). The responsibility of the free convertibility remained in the [French Treasury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Treasury).

#### Without formal agreement

[Kosovo](17391.md) and [Montenegro](20760.md) unilaterally adopted the euro as their sole currency without an agreement and, therefore, have no issuing rights.[^cite_note-agreements_2-42] These states are not considered part of the eurozone by the ECB. However, sometimes the term *eurozone* is applied to all territories that have adopted the euro as their sole currency.[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46] Further unilateral adoption of the euro ([euroisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euroisation)), by both non-euro EU and non-EU members, is opposed by the ECB and EU.[^cite_note-47]

### Historical eurozone enlargements and exchange-rate regimes for EU members

Further information: [History of the euro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_euro)

The chart below provides a full summary of all applying [exchange-rate regimes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange-rate_regime) for [EU members](10890716.md), since the birth, on 13 March 1979, of the [European Monetary System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Monetary_System) with its [Exchange Rate Mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange_Rate_Mechanism) and the related new common currency [ECU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Currency_Unit). On 1 January 1999, the euro replaced the ECU 1\:1 at the exchange rate markets. During 1979–1999, the [Deutsche Mark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsche_Mark) functioned as a de facto anchor for the ECU, meaning there was only a minor difference between pegging a currency against the ECU and pegging it against the Deutsche Mark.

The eurozone was established with its first 11 member states on 1 January 1999. The first [enlargement of the eurozone](12641034.md), to Greece, took place on 1 January 2001, one year before the euro physically entered into circulation. The next enlargements were to states which [joined the EU in 2004](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_enlargement_of_the_European_Union), and then joined the eurozone on 1 January of the year noted: Slovenia in 2007, Cyprus and Malta in 2008, Slovakia in 2009, Estonia in 2011, Latvia in 2014, and Lithuania in 2015. Croatia, which [joined the EU in 2013](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2013_enlargement_of_the_European_Union), adopted the euro in 2023; while Bulgaria, which [joined in 2007](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_enlargement_of_the_European_Union), adopted the euro in 2026.

All new EU members joining the bloc after the signing of the [Maastricht Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maastricht_Treaty) in 1992 committed to adopt the euro under the terms of their accession treaties once they comply with the five economic [convergence criteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_convergence_criteria). The last of these is the exchange rate stability criterion, which requires having been an ERM-member for a minimum of two years without the presence of "severe tensions" for the currency exchange rate.

In September 2011, a diplomatic source close to the euro adoption preparation talks with the seven remaining new member states who had yet to adopt the euro at that time (Bulgaria, the Czech Republic, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, and Romania), claimed that the monetary union (eurozone) they had thought they were going to join upon their signing of the accession treaty may very well end up being a very different union, entailing a much closer fiscal, economic, and political convergence than originally anticipated. This changed legal status of the eurozone could potentially cause them to conclude that the conditions for their promise to join were no longer valid, which "could force them to stage new referendums" on euro adoption.[^cite_note-48]

Before joining the eurozone, a state must spend at least two years in the [European Exchange Rate Mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Exchange_Rate_Mechanism) (ERM II). As of January 2026, only the central bank of Denmark participates in ERM II.

Denmark obtained a special [opt-out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opt-outs_in_the_European_Union) in the [Maastricht Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maastricht_Treaty), and thus is legally exempt from joining the eurozone unless its government decides otherwise, either by parliamentary vote or [referendum](51582.md). The United Kingdom likewise had an opt-out prior to [withdrawing from the EU](41688778.md) in 2020.

The remaining five countries have committed to adopt the euro in future, once they meet the convergence criteria. They should join as soon as they do so, which include being part of ERM II for two years. [Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweden_and_the_euro), which joined the EU in 1995 after the Maastricht Treaty was signed, rejected euro adoption in a [2003 referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_Swedish_euro_referendum) and since then the country has intentionally avoided fulfilling the adoption requirements by not joining ERM II, which is voluntary.[^cite_note-49][^cite_note-50]

Interest in joining the eurozone increased in Denmark, and initially in Poland, as a result of the [2008 financial crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_financial_crisis). In Iceland, there was an increase in interest in joining the European Union, a pre-condition for adopting the euro.[^cite_note-51] However, by 2010 the debt crisis in the eurozone caused interest from Poland, as well as the Czech Republic, Denmark and Sweden to cool.[^cite_note-52]

At the time of Bulgaria’s euro adoption in 2026, the Polish government under Donald Tusk has expressed a lack of economic readiness to join, and the Polish president Karol Nawrocki has said that he is explicitly against Poland's future adoption of the euro.[^cite_note-53]

### Expulsion and withdrawal

Main article: [Withdrawal from the eurozone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Withdrawal_from_the_eurozone)

See also: [Greek withdrawal from the eurozone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_withdrawal_from_the_eurozone)

In the opinion of journalist Leigh Phillips and Charles Proctor from the US law firm [Locke Lord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locke_Lord),[^cite_note-noone-54][^cite_note-locke-55] there is no provision in any European Union treaty for an exit from the eurozone. In fact, they argued, the Treaties make it clear that the process of [monetary union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_and_Monetary_Union_of_the_European_Union) was intended to be "irreversible" and "irrevocable".[^cite_note-locke-55] However, in 2009, a [European Central Bank](9474.md) legal study argued that, while voluntary withdrawal is legally not possible, expulsion remains "conceivable".[^cite_note-ecblegal-56] Although an explicit provision for an exit option does not exist, many experts and politicians in Europe have suggested an option to leave the eurozone should be included in the relevant treaties.[^cite_note-activ-57]

On the issue of leaving the eurozone, the [European Commission](9974.md) has stated that "\[t]he irrevocability of membership in the euro area is an integral part of the Treaty framework and the Commission, as a guardian of the EU Treaties, intends to fully respect \[that irrevocability]."[^cite_note-rehn-58] It added that it "does not intend to propose \[any] amendment" to the relevant Treaties, the current status being "the best way going forward to increase the resilience of euro area Member States to potential economic and financial crises".[^cite_note-rehn-58] The [European Central Bank](9474.md), responding to a question by a [Member of the European Parliament](258909.md), has stated that an exit is not allowed under the Treaties.[^cite_note-59]

Likewise there is no provision for a state to be expelled from the euro.[^cite_note-60] Some, however, including the Dutch government, favour the creation of an expulsion provision for the case whereby a heavily indebted state in the eurozone refuses to comply with an EU economic reform policy.[^cite_note-phillips-61]

In a Texas law journal, [University of Texas at Austin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Texas_at_Austin) law professor Jens Dammann has argued that even now EU law contains an implicit right for member states to leave the eurozone if they no longer meet the criteria that they had to meet in order to join it.[^cite_note-Jens_Dammann-62] Furthermore, he has suggested that, under narrow circumstances, the European Union can expel member states from the eurozone.[^cite_note-Jens_Dammann2-63]

## Administration and representation

Further information: [European Central Bank](9474.md), [Eurogroup](21533929.md), and [Euro summit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_summit)



The [European Central Bank](9474.md) ([seat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seat_of_the_European_Central_Bank) in [Frankfurt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankfurt_am_Main) depicted) is the supranational [monetary authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_bank) of the eurozone.

The monetary policy of all countries in the eurozone is managed by the [European Central Bank](9474.md) (ECB) and the [Eurosystem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurosystem) which comprises the ECB and the central banks of the EU states who have joined the eurozone. Countries outside the eurozone are not represented in these institutions. Whereas all EU member states are part of the [European System of Central Banks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_System_of_Central_Banks) (ESCB), non EU member states have no say in all three institutions, even those with monetary agreements such as Monaco. The ECB is entitled to authorise the design and printing of [euro banknotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_banknotes) and the volume of [euro coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_coins) minted, and [its president](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_European_Central_Bank) is currently [Christine Lagarde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christine_Lagarde).

The eurozone is represented politically by its finance ministers, known collectively as the [Eurogroup](21533929.md), and is presided over by a president, currently [Kyriakos Pierrakakis](61389979.md). The finance ministers of the EU member states that use the euro meet a day before a meeting of the [Economic and Financial Affairs Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_and_Financial_Affairs_Council) (Ecofin) of the [Council of the European Union](5866.md). The Group is not an official Council formation but when the full EcoFin council votes on matters only affecting the eurozone, only Euro Group members are permitted to vote on it.[^cite_note-64][^cite_note-FT_EGE_BG-65][^cite_note-66]

Since the [2008 financial crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_financial_crisis), the Euro Group has met irregularly not as finance ministers, but as heads of state and government (like the European Council). It is in this forum, the [Euro summit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_summit), that many eurozone reforms have been decided upon. In 2011, former [French President](24899.md) [Nicolas Sarkozy](557007.md) pushed for these summits to become regular and twice a year in order for it to be a 'true economic government'.[^cite_note-67]

### Reform

In April 2008 in [Brussels](3708.md), future [European Commission](9974.md) [President](24468.md) [Jean-Claude Juncker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Claude_Juncker) suggested that the eurozone should be represented at the [IMF](15251.md) as a bloc, rather than each member state separately: "It is absurd for those 15 countries not to agree to have a single representation at the IMF. It makes us look absolutely ridiculous. We are regarded as buffoons on the international scene".[^cite_note-Common_IMF-68] In 2017 Juncker stated that he aims to have this agreed by the end of his mandate in 2019.[^cite_note-69] However, [Finance Commissioner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Commissioner_for_Economic_&_Financial_Affairs) [Joaquín Almunia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joaqu%C3%ADn_Almunia) stated that before there is common representation, a common political agenda should be agreed upon.[^cite_note-Common_IMF-68]

Leading EU figures including the commission and national governments have proposed a variety of reforms to the eurozone's architecture; notably the creation of a Finance Minister, a larger eurozone budget, and reform of the current bailout mechanisms into either a "European Monetary Fund" or a eurozone [Treasury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasury). While many have similar themes, details vary greatly.[^cite_note-70][^cite_note-71][^cite_note-72][^cite_note-73]

### Fiscal policies

See also: [European Fiscal Compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Fiscal_Compact)



Comparison of government surplus/[deficit](4360909.md) (2001–2012) of eurozone, United States and United Kingdom

The primary means for fiscal coordination within the EU lies in the [Broad Economic Policy Guidelines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broad_Economic_Policy_Guidelines) which are written for every member state, but with particular reference to the 21 current members of the eurozone. These guidelines are not binding, but are intended to represent policy coordination among the EU member states, so as to take into account the linked structures of their economies.

For their mutual assurance and stability of the currency, members of the eurozone have to respect the [Stability and Growth Pact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stability_and_Growth_Pact), which sets agreed limits on [deficits](4360909.md) and [national debt](695460.md), with associated sanctions for deviation. The Pact originally set a limit of 3% of GDP for the yearly deficit of all eurozone member states; with fines for any state which exceeded this amount. In 2005, Portugal, Germany, and France had all exceeded this amount, but the Council of Ministers had not voted to fine those states. Subsequently, reforms were adopted to provide more flexibility and ensure that the deficit criteria took into account the economic conditions of the member states, and additional factors.

The [Fiscal Compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiscal_Compact)[^cite_note-84][^cite_note-EC_ready-85] (formally, the Treaty on Stability, Coordination and Governance in the Economic and Monetary Union),[^cite_note-86] is an [intergovernmental treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enhanced_cooperation#Related_intergovernmental_treaties) introduced as a new stricter version of the [Stability and Growth Pact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stability_and_Growth_Pact), signed on 2 March 2012 by all [member states of the European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Member_states_of_the_European_Union) (EU), except the Czech Republic, the United Kingdom,[^cite_note-signed-87] and Croatia (subsequently [acceding the EU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accession_of_Croatia_to_the_European_Union) in July 2013). The treaty entered into force on 1 January 2013 for the 16 states which completed ratification prior of this date.[^cite_note-Fiscal_Compact_entry_into_force-88] As of 1 April 2014, it had been ratified and entered into force for all 25 signatories.

[Olivier Blanchard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olivier_Blanchard) suggests that a fiscal union in the eurozone can mitigate devastating effects of the single currency on the eurozone peripheral countries. But he adds that the currency bloc will not work perfectly even if a fiscal transfer system is built, because, he argues, the fundamental issue about competitiveness adjustment is not tackled. The problem is, since the eurozone peripheral countries do not have their own currencies, they are forced to adjust their economies by decreasing their wages instead of devaluation.[^cite_note-89]

## Bailout provisions

See also: [History of the euro § Recession era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_euro#Recession_era)

The [2008 financial crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_financial_crisis) prompted several reforms in the eurozone. One was a U-turn on the eurozone's [bailout](1469849.md) policy that led to the creation of a specific fund to assist eurozone states in trouble. The [European Financial Stability Facility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Financial_Stability_Facility) (EFSF) and the [European Financial Stability Mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Financial_Stability_Mechanism) (EFSM) were created in 2010 to provide, alongside the [International Monetary Fund](15251.md) (IMF), a system and fund to bail out members. However, the EFSF and EFSM were temporary, small and lacked a basis in the EU treaties. Therefore, it was agreed in 2011 to establish a [European Stability Mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Stability_Mechanism) (ESM) which would be much larger, funded only by eurozone states (not the EU as a whole as the EFSF/EFSM were) and would have a permanent [treaty basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaties_of_the_European_Union). As a result of that its creation involved agreeing an amendment to TEFU Article 136 allowing for the ESM and a new ESM treaty to detail how the ESM would operate. The ESM became operational in September 2012.

In February 2016, the UK secured further confirmation that countries that do not use the [Euro](9472.md) would not be required to contribute to bailouts for eurozone countries.[^cite_note-90]

## Peer review

See also: [Euro Plus Pact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_Plus_Pact)

In June 2010, a broad agreement was finally reached on a controversial proposal for member states to peer review each other's budgets prior to their presentation to [national parliaments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_parliaments_of_the_European_Union). Although showing the entire budget to each other was opposed by Germany, Sweden and the UK, each government would present to their peers and the Commission their estimates for growth, inflation, revenue and expenditure levels six months before they go to national parliaments. If a country was to run a deficit, they would have to justify it to the rest of the EU while countries with a debt more than 60% of GDP would face greater scrutiny.[^cite_note-peer_1-91]

The plans would apply to all EU members, not just the eurozone, and have to be approved by EU leaders along with proposals for states to face sanctions before they reach the 3% limit in the [Stability and Growth Pact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stability_and_Growth_Pact). Poland has criticised the idea of withholding regional funding for those who break the deficit limits, as that would only impact the poorer states.[^cite_note-peer_1-91] In June 2010 France agreed to back Germany's plan for suspending the voting rights of members who breach the rules.[^cite_note-Merkel_Spain-92] In March 2011 was initiated a new reform of the [Stability and Growth Pact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stability_and_Growth_Pact#Reform) aiming at straightening the rules by adopting an automatic procedure for imposing of penalties in case of breaches of either the deficit or the debt rules.[^cite_note-93][^cite_note-94]

## Criticism

See also: [Phillips curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phillips_curve) and [Nominal rigidity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nominal_rigidity)

In 1997, [Arnulf Baring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnulf_Baring) expressed concern that the European Monetary Union would make Germans the most hated people in Europe. Baring suspected the possibility that the people in Mediterranean countries would regard Germans and the currency bloc as economic policemen.[^cite_note-WST16Jul2015NAGAB-95]

In 2001, [James Tobin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Tobin) thought that the euro project would not succeed without making drastic changes to European institutions, pointing out the difference between the US and the eurozone.[^cite_note-JT2001EMUd-96] Concerning monetary policies, the system of [Federal Reserve banks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_bank) in the US aims at both growth and reducing unemployment, while the [ECB](9474.md) tends to give its first priority to price stability under the [Bundesbank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsche_Bundesbank)'s supervision. As the price level of the currency bloc is kept low, the unemployment level of the region has become higher than that of the US since 1982.[^cite_note-JT2001EMUd-96] Concerning fiscal policies, 12% of the US federal budget is used for transfers to states and local governments. The US government does not impose restrictions on state budget policies, whereas the Maastricht Treaty requires each eurozone member country to keep its budget deficit below 3% of its GDP.[^cite_note-JT2001EMUd-96]

In 2008, a study by [Alberto Alesina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberto_Alesina) and [Vincenzo Galasso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincenzo_Galasso) found that the adoption of [euro](9472.md) promoted [market deregulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deregulation) and [market liberalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_trade).[^cite_note-Alesina-97][^cite_note-Belsie-98] Furthermore, the euro was also linked to wage moderation, as [wage growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wage_growth) slowed down in countries that adopted the new currency.[^cite_note-Alesina-97] [Oliver Hart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Hart_\(economist\)), who received the [Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences](23535306.md) in 2016, criticized the euro, calling it a "mistake" and emphasising his opposition to monetary union since its inception.[^cite_note-rodri-99] He also expressed opposition to [European integration](683704.md), arguing that the European Union should instead focus on [decentralisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decentralisation) as it has "gone too far in centralising power".[^cite_note-rodri-99] In 2018, a study based on [DiD methodology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difference_in_differences) found that the adoption of euro produced no systematic growth effects, as no growth-enhancing effects were found when compared to European economies outside the eurozone.[^cite_note-Ioannatos-100]

The eurozone has also been criticized for deepening inequality in Europe, particularly between the richest and poorest countries.[^cite_note-Kartnitschnig-101] According to a study by [Bertelsmann Stiftung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertelsmann_Stiftung), countries such as Austria and the Netherlands benefited significantly from the common currency, while southern and eastern European members of the eurozone gained very little,[^cite_note-reuters1-102] and some countries are considered to have suffered adverse effects from adopting the euro.[^cite_note-Stiglitz1-103] In an article for [Politico](9172097.md), [Joseph Stiglitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Stiglitz) argues that "\[t]he result for the eurozone has been slower growth, and especially for the weaker countries within it. The euro was supposed to usher in greater prosperity, which in turn would lead to renewed commitment to European integration. It has done just the opposite — increasing divisions within the EU, especially between creditor and debtor countries."[^cite_note-Stiglitz1-103] Matthias Matthijs believes that the euro resulted in a "winner-take-all" economy, as national income differences between eurozone members have widened further.[^cite_note-matthijs-104] He argues that countries such as Austria and Germany have gained from the eurozone at the expense of southern countries like Italy and Spain.[^cite_note-matthijs-104]

By adopting the euro and abandoning their national currencies, eurozone countries gave up their ability to conduct independent [monetary policy](297032.md); as such, monetary policies used to combat recession, such as [monetary stimulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stimulus_\(economics\)) or [currency devaluation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devaluation), are no longer available.[^cite_note-matthijs-104] During the [European debt crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_debt_crisis), several eurozone countries (Greece, Italy, Portugal, Ireland, Spain, and Cyprus) were unable to repay their debt without third-party intervention by the [European Central Bank](9474.md) and the [International Monetary Fund](15251.md).[^cite_note-Copelovitch-105] In order to grant the bailout, the ECB and the IMF forced the affected countries to adopt strict [austerity](684037.md) measures.[^cite_note-matthijs-104] The European bailouts were largely about shifting exposure from banks onto European taxpayers,[^cite_note-LSEHO_23Mar2012-106][^cite_note-107][^cite_note-108] and exacerbated issues such as high unemployment and poverty.[^cite_note-cavero-109][^cite_note-110]

In 2019, a study from the [Centre for European Policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre_for_European_Policy) concluded that while some countries had gained from adopting the euro, several countries were poorer than they would have been had they not adopted it, with France and Italy being particularly affected.[^cite_note-111][^cite_note-112] The publication prompted a large number of reactions, pushing its authors to put out a statement clarifying some points.[^cite_note-113] In 2020, a study from the University of Bonn reached a different conclusion: the adoption of the euro made "some mild losers (France, Germany, Italy, and Portugal) and a clear winner (Ireland)".[^cite_note-114] Both studies used the [synthetic control method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_control_method) to estimate what might have happened if the euro had not been adopted.
