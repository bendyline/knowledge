A **reusable launch vehicle** has parts that can be recovered and reflown, while carrying [payloads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Payload) from the surface to [outer space](177602.md). [Rocket stages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_stage) are the most common [launch vehicle](1306157.md) parts aimed for reuse. Smaller parts such as [fairings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Payload_fairing), [boosters](373603.md) or [rocket engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_engine) can also be reused, though [reusable spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reusable_spacecraft) may be launched on top of an expendable launch vehicle. Reusable launch vehicles do not need to make these parts for each launch, therefore reducing its [launch cost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Launch_cost) significantly. However, these benefits can be diminished by the cost of recovery and refurbishment.

Reusable launch vehicles may contain additional [avionics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avionics) and [propellant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propellant), making them heavier than their expendable counterparts. Reused parts may need to [enter the atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_entry) and navigate through it, so they are often equipped with [heat shields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_shield), [grid fins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grid_fin), and other [flight control surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_control_surfaces). By modifying their shape, [spaceplanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaceplane) can leverage [aviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviation) mechanics to aid in its recovery, such as [gliding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gliding) or [lift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lift_\(force\)). In the atmosphere, [parachutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parachute) or [retrorockets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrorocket) may also be needed to slow it down further. Reusable parts may also need specialized recovery facilities such as [runways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runway) or [autonomous spaceport drone ships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_spaceport_drone_ship). Some concepts rely on ground infrastructures such as [mass drivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_driver) to accelerate the launch vehicle beforehand.

Since at least the early 20th century, [single-stage-to-orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-stage-to-orbit) reusable launch vehicles have existed in [science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction). In the 1970s, the first reusable launch vehicle, the [Space Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle), was developed. However, in the 1990s, due to the program's failure to meet expectations, reusable launch vehicles were reduced to prototype testing. The growth of [private spaceflight](752732.md) companies in the 2010s lead to a resurgence of their development, such as in [SpaceShipOne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceShipOne), [New Shepard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Shepard), [New Glenn](51585988.md), [Electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_Lab_Electron), [Falcon 9](2647515.md), and [Falcon Heavy](31406060.md). At the same time, the US [Boeing X-37](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_X-37) and Chinese [CSSHQ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CSSHQ) military [spaceplanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaceplane) began deploying small satellites. Many launch vehicles are now expected to debut with reusability in the 2020s, such as the US [Starship](59118602.md), [Neutron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_Lab_Neutron), [Terran R](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terran_R), [Nova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoke_Space_Nova), and [Eclipse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eclipse_\(rocket\)), the Chinese [Long March 10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_March_10) and [12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_March_12), [*Tianlong*-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tianlong-3), [*Zhuque*-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhuque-3), and the European [Maia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maia_\(rocket\)) and [Miura 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miura_5).

The impact of reusability in launch vehicles has been momentous for the spaceflight industry. In 2024, the [Cape Canaveral Space Force Station](306778.md) initiated a 50-year forward looking plan for the Cape that involved major infrastructure upgrades (including to [Port Canaveral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Canaveral)) to support a higher anticipated launch cadence and landing sites for the new generation of vehicles.[^cite_note-1]

## Configurations

### Fully reusable launch vehicle



The [SpaceX Starship](59118602.md) is being built as a fully-reusable [super heavy-lift launch vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_heavy-lift_launch_vehicle) with minimal refurbishment required.

Several companies are currently developing fully reusable launch vehicles as of January 2026. Each of them is working on a [two-stage-to-orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-stage-to-orbit) system. [SpaceX](832774.md) is testing Starship, which has been in development since 2016 and has made [an initial test flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_flight_test_1) in April 2023[^cite_note-2] and a total of [12 flights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Starship_launches) as of May 2026. [Stoke Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoke_Space)'s Nova vehicle is also planned to be reusable.[^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4]

As of June 2026, SpaceX's [launch vehicle](1306157.md) Starship has made significant progress towards full reuse of the entire launch vehicle, both first and second stages. The [Super Heavy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Super_Heavy) boosters have demonstrated capability to return to the launch site where they are caught by a "chopstick system" on the [launch tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Service_structure) where they can then be reused. The Starship upper stage has completed six controlled splashdowns in the ocean but has not yet been recovered or reused.[^cite_note-5]

### Partially reusable launch systems



Recovery of a [Space Shuttle Solid Rocket Booster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_Solid_Rocket_Booster) for refurbishment.

Partial reusable launch systems, in the form of multiple stage to orbit systems have been so far the only reusable configurations in use. The historic [Space Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle) reused its [Solid Rocket Boosters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_Solid_Rocket_Booster), its [RS-25](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RS-25) engines and the [Space Shuttle orbiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_orbiter) that acted as an orbital insertion stage, but it did not reuse the [External Tank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/External_Tank) that fed the RS-25 engines. This is an example of a reusable launch system which reuses specific components of rockets. [ULA's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Launch_Alliance) [Vulcan Centaur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulcan_Centaur) was originally designed to reuse the first stage engines, while the tank is expended. The engines would splashdown on an inflatable [aeroshell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeroshell), then be recovered. On 23 February 2024, one of the nine Merlin engines powering a Falcon 9 launched for the 22nd time, making it the most reused liquid fuel engine used in an operational manner, having already surpassed [Space Shuttle Main Engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RS-25) number 2019's record of 19 flights. As of 2026, Falcon 9, Falcon Heavy and New Glenn are the only orbital rockets to reuse their boosters[^cite_note-6], although multiple other systems are in development. All aircraft-launched rockets reuse the aircraft.

Other than that, a range of [non-rocket liftoff systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-rocket_launch) have been proposed and explored over time as reusable systems for liftoff, from balloons[^cite_note-7] to [space elevators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_elevator). Existing examples are systems which employ winged horizontal jet-engine powered liftoff. Such aircraft can [air launch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_launch) expendable rockets and can because of that be considered partially reusable systems if the aircraft is thought of as the first stage of the launch vehicle. An example of this configuration is the [Orbital Sciences Pegasus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_Sciences_Pegasus). For suborbital flight the [SpaceShipTwo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceShipTwo) uses for liftoff a carrier plane, its [mothership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mothership) the [Scaled Composites White Knight Two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scaled_Composites_White_Knight_Two). Rocket Lab is working on Neutron, and the [European Space Agency](10363.md) is working on [Themis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Themis_programme). Both vehicles are planned to recover the first stage.[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9]

So far, most launch systems achieve [orbital insertion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_insertion) with at least partially expended [multistaged rockets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multistaged_rocket), particularly with the second and third stages. Only the [Space Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle) has achieved a reuse of the orbital insertion stage, by using the engines and fuel tank of [its orbiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_orbiter). The [Buran spaceplane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buran_\(spacecraft\)) and [Starship spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starship_\(spacecraft\)) are two other reusable spacecraft that were designed to be able to act as orbital insertion stages and have been produced, however the former only made one uncrewed test flight before the project was cancelled, and the latter is not yet operational, having completed [eleven suborbital test flights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Starship_launches), as of November 2025, which achieved all of its mission objectives at the fourth flight.

### Reusable spacecraft

Main article: [Reusable spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reusable_spacecraft)



The [Space Shuttle orbiters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_orbiter) were once prominent examples of reusable spacecraft.

Launch systems can be combined with reusable spaceplanes or capsules. The [Space Shuttle orbiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_orbiter), [SpaceShipTwo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceShipTwo), Dawn Mk-II Aurora, and the under-development Indian [RLV-TD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RLV-TD) are examples for a reusable space vehicle (a [spaceplane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaceplane)) as well as a part of its launch system. Contemporary reusable orbital vehicles include the [X-37](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_X-37), [Dragon 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Dragon_2), and the upcoming [Dream Chaser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_Chaser), Indian RLV-TD and the upcoming European [Space Rider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Rider) (successor to the [IXV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IXV)).

As with launch vehicles, all pure spacecraft during the early decades of human capacity to achieve spaceflight were designed to be single-use items. This was true both for [satellites](27683.md) and [space probes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_probes) intended to be left in space for a long time, as well as any object designed to return to Earth such as [human-carrying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_spaceflight) [space capsules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_capsule) or the sample return canisters of space matter collection missions like [Stardust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stardust_\(spacecraft\)) (1999–2006)[^cite_note-newscientist20060115-10] or [Hayabusa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hayabusa) (2005–2010).[^cite_note-indyposted201006-11][^cite_note-sdc20100613-12] Exceptions to the general rule for space vehicles were the US [Gemini SC-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemini_SC-2), the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) spacecraft [Vozvraschaemyi Apparat (VA)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VA_spacecraft), the US Space Shuttle orbiter (mid-1970s-2011, with 135 flights between 1981 and 2011) and the Soviet [Buran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buran_\(spacecraft\)) (1980–1988, with just one uncrewed test flight in 1988). Both of these spaceships were also an integral part of the launch system (providing launch acceleration) as well as operating as medium-duration spaceships in [space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_spaceflight). This began to change in the mid-2010s.



Vehicles like the [Orion's Crew Module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orion_\(spacecraft\)) are featured with thermal protection system, allowing theoretical reusability of the capsules.

In the 2010s, the [space transport cargo capsule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Resupply_Services) from one of the suppliers resupplying the [International Space Station](15043.md) was designed for reuse, and after 2017,[^cite_note-Dragon_reused-13] NASA began to allow the reuse of the SpaceX [Dragon cargo spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_1) on these NASA-contracted transport routes. This was the beginning of design and operation of a reusable space vehicle. The [Boeing Starliner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_Starliner) capsules also reduce their fall speed with parachutes and deploy an airbag shortly before touchdown on the ground, in order to retrieve and reuse the vehicle. As of 2021, SpaceX is building and testing the Starship spaceship to be capable of surviving multiple [hypersonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypersonic) [reentries through the atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_reentry) so that they become truly reusable long-duration spaceships; no Starship operational flights have yet occurred.

### Heat shield

See also: [Atmospheric entry § Thermal protection systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_entry#Thermal_protection_systems)

With possible inflatable [heat shields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_shield#Spacecraft), as developed by the US (Low Earth Orbit Flight Test Inflatable Decelerator - LOFTID)[^cite_note-noaa-20190703-14] and China,[^cite_note-15] single-use rockets like the [Space Launch System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Launch_System) are considered to be retrofitted with such heat shields to salvage the expensive engines, possibly reducing the costs of launches significantly.[^cite_note-16] Heat shields allow an orbiting spacecraft to land safely without expending very much fuel. They need not take the form of inflatable heat shields, they may simply take the form of heat-resistant tiles that prevent [heat conduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_conduction). Heat shields are also proposed for use in combination with retrograde thrust to allow for full reusability as seen in Starship.

### Retrograde thrust

Main articles: [Retrorocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrorocket) and [Thrust reversal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrust_reversal)

Reusable launch system stages such as the Falcon 9 and the New Shepard employ retrograde burns for re-entry, and landing.

## Landing systems

Reusable systems can come in [single](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-stage-to-orbit) or multiple ([two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-stage-to-orbit) or [three](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-stage-to-orbit)) stages to orbit configurations. For some or all stages the following landing system types can be employed.

### Parachutes and airbags

Main articles: [Splashdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Splashdown), [Airbag § Spacecraft airbag landing systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbag#Spacecraft_airbag_landing_systems), and [Parachute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parachute)

See also: [Water landing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_landing)

These are landing systems that employ parachutes and bolstered hard landings, like in a [splashdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Splashdown) at sea or a touchdown at land. The latter may require an engine burn just before landing as parachutes alone cannot slow the craft down enough to prevent injury to astronauts. This can be seen in the Soyuz capsule. Though such systems have been in use since the beginning of [astronautics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronautics) to recover space vehicles, only later have the vehicles been reused.

Examples include:

- [Space Shuttle Solid Rocket Boosters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_Solid_Rocket_Boosters)

- [SpaceX Dragon capsule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Dragon)

### Horizontal (winged)

Main article: [Spaceplane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaceplane)



An [An-225](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonov_An-225_Mriya) carrying the winged [Buran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buran_programme) on her back.

Single or main stages, as well as fly-back boosters can employ a horizontal landing system. These vehicles land on earth much like a plane does, but they usually do not use propellant during landing. Vehicles that land horizontally on a runway require wings and undercarriage. These typically consume about 9-12% of the landing vehicle mass, which either reduces the payload or increases the size of the vehicle. Concepts such as [lifting bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lifting_bodies) offer some reduction in wing mass, as does the [delta wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_wing) shape of the Space Shuttle. A variant is an in-air-capture tow back system, advocated by a company called EMBENTION with its FALCon project.[^cite_note-17]

The following spaceplanes have made successful landings from suborbital spaceflights:

### Vertical (retrograde)

Main articles: [VTVL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VTVL), [Retrorocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrorocket), and [Thrust reversal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrust_reversal)

Systems like the [McDonnell Douglas DC-X (Delta Clipper)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McDonnell_Douglas_DC-X) and those by [SpaceX](832774.md) are examples of a retrograde system. The boosters of Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy land using one of their nine engines. The Falcon 9 rocket is the first orbital rocket to vertically land its first stage on the ground. The first stage of Starship is caught by the same arms that raise it to the launch platform after performing most of the typical steps of a retrograde landing.[^cite_note-SSCatch-18] Starship's second stage is also planned to be caught by arms attached to a tower when landing on Earth or to land vertically on the Moon or Mars. [Blue Origin](887418.md)'s New Shepard suborbital rocket also lands vertically back at the launch site. Retrograde landing typically requires about 10% of the total first stage propellant, reducing the payload that can be carried due to the [rocket equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_equation).[^cite_note-19]

As of August 2026, VTVL first-stage recovery of orbital rockets has been achieved by the following:[^cite_note-20][^cite_note-21]

- [SpaceX](832774.md) - [Falcon 9](2647515.md) and [Falcon Heavy](31406060.md)

- [Blue Origin](887418.md) - [New Glenn](51585988.md)

- [China Aerospace Science and Technology Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China_Aerospace_Science_and_Technology_Corporation) - [Long March 10B](82301965.md)

- [LandSpace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LandSpace) - [Zhuque-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhuque-3)

### Landing using aerostatic force

There is also the concept of a launch vehicle with an inflatable, reusable first stage. The shape of this structure will be supported by excess internal pressure (using light gases). It is assumed that the bulk density of the first stage (without propellant) is less than the bulk density of air. Upon returning from flight, such a first stage remains floating in the air (without touching the surface of the Earth). This will ensure that the first stage is retained for reuse. Increasing the size of the first stage increases aerodynamic losses. This results in a slight decrease in payload. This reduction in payload is compensated for by the reuse of the first stage.[^cite_note-22]

## Constraints

### Extra weight

Reusable stages weigh more than equivalent [expendable stages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expendable_launch_vehicle). This is unavoidable due to the supplementary systems, landing gear and/or surplus propellant needed to land a stage. The actual mass penalty depends on the vehicle and the return mode chosen.[^cite_note-IAC2017-23]

### Refurbishment

After the launcher lands, it may need to be refurbished to prepare it for its next flight. This process may be lengthy and expensive. While the [Space Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle) was considered invaluable for its unique capabilities, the [processes between landing and relaunch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle#Post-landing_processing) were extensive. The launcher may not be able to be recertified as human-rated after refurbishment, although SpaceX has flown reused Falcon 9 boosters for human missions.[^cite_note-24] There is eventually a limit on how many times a launcher can be refurbished before it has to be retired, but how often a launcher can be reused differs significantly between the various launch system designs. As of the 9th of September, 2026, Spacex Falcon 9 booster [B1067](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Falcon_9_first-stage_boosters#Reuse_and_recovery_records) has been successfully launched and recovered a record 37 times.

## Return to launch site

After 1980, but before the 2010s, two orbital launch vehicles developed the capability to **return to the launch site** (RTLS). Both the US Space Shuttle—with one of its [abort modes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_abort_modes#Return_to_launch_site_\(RTLS\))[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26]—and the Soviet [Buran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buran_\(spacecraft\))[^cite_note-ng2016041-27] had a designed-in capability to return a part of the launch vehicle to the launch site via the mechanism of [horizontal-landing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTHL) of the [spaceplane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaceplane) portion of the launch vehicle. In both cases, the main vehicle thrust structure and the large propellant tank were expendable, as had been the standard procedure for all orbital launch vehicles flown prior to that time. Both were subsequently demonstrated on actual orbital nominal flights, although both also had an abort mode during launch that could conceivably allow the crew to land the spaceplane following an off-nominal launch.

In the 2000s, both SpaceX and Blue Origin have [privately developed](752732.md) a set of technologies to support [vertical landing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_landing) of the booster stage of a launch vehicle. After 2010, SpaceX undertook a [development program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_reusable_launch_system_development_program) to acquire the ability to bring back and [vertically land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VTVL) a part of the Falcon 9 orbital launch vehicle: the [first stage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_stage_\(rocketry\)). The first successful landing was done in December 2015,[^cite_note-abc2015121-28] since then several additional rocket stages landed either at a [landing pad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landing_Zones_1_and_2) adjacent to the launch site or on an [landing platform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_Spaceport_Drone_Ship) at sea, some distance away from the launch site.[^cite_note-29] The Falcon Heavy is similarly designed to reuse the three cores comprising its first stage. On its [first flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Heavy_test_flight) in February 2018, the two outer cores successfully returned to the launch site landing pads while the center core targeted the landing platform at sea but did not successfully land on it.[^cite_note-30]

Blue Origin developed similar technologies for bringing back and landing their [suborbital](455295.md) *New Shepard*, and successfully demonstrated return in 2015, and successfully reused the same booster on a second suborbital flight in January 2016.[^cite_note-31] By October 2016, Blue had reflown, and landed successfully, that same launch vehicle a total of five times.[^cite_note-sn20161005-32] It must however be noted that the launch trajectories of both vehicles are very different, with New Shepard going straight up and down without achieving orbital flight, whereas Falcon 9 has to cancel substantial horizontal velocity and return from a significant distance downrange, while delivering the payload to orbit with the second stage.

Both Blue Origin and SpaceX also have additional reusable launch vehicles under development. Blue is developing the first stage of the orbital New Glenn LV to be reusable, with first flight planned for no earlier than 2024. SpaceX has a new super-heavy launch vehicle under development for missions to [interplanetary space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interplanetary_spaceflight). The SpaceX Starship is designed to support RTLS, vertical-landing and full reuse of *both* the booster stage and the integrated second-stage/large-spacecraft that are designed for use with Starship.[^cite_note-33] Its [first launch attempt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starship_integrated_flight_test_1) took place in April 2023; however, both stages were lost during ascent. On the [fourth launch attempt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starship_integrated_flight_test_4) however, both the booster and the ship achieved a soft landing in the [Gulf of Mexico](21076367.md) and the [Indian Ocean](14580.md), respectively.

## History



[NEXUS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Dynamics_Nexus) concept

With the development of [rocket propulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_propulsion) in the first half of the twentieth century, [space travel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaceflight) became a technical possibility. Early ideas of a single-stage reusable [spaceplane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaceplane) proved unrealistic and although even the first practical rocket vehicles ([V-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V-2)) could reach the fringes of space, reusable technology was too heavy. In addition, many early rockets were developed to deliver weapons, making reuse impossible by design. The problem of mass efficiency was overcome by using multiple expendable stages in a vertical launch [multistage rocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multistage_rocket). USAF and NACA had been studying orbital reusable spaceplanes since 1958, e.g. [Dyna-Soar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyna-Soar), but the first reusable stages did not fly until the advent of the US Space Shuttle in 1981.

Perhaps the first reusable launch vehicles were the ones conceptualized and studied by [Wernher von Braun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wernher_von_Braun) from 1948 until 1956. The [von Braun ferry rocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Von_Braun_ferry_rocket) underwent two revisions: once in 1952 and again in 1956. They would have landed using parachutes.[^cite_note-34][^cite_note-35]

The [General Dynamics Nexus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Dynamics_Nexus) was proposed in the 1960s as a fully reusable successor to the Saturn V rocket, having the capacity of transporting up to 450–910 t (990,000–2,000,000 lb) to orbit.[^cite_note-36][^cite_note-37] See also [Sea Dragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_Dragon_\(rocket\)), and [Douglas SASSTO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_SASSTO).

The [BAC Mustard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BAC_Mustard) was studied starting in 1964. It would have comprised three identical spaceplanes strapped together and arranged in two stages. During ascent the two outer spaceplanes, which formed the first stage, would detach and glide back individually to earth. It was canceled after the last study of the design in 1967 due to a lack of funds for development.[^cite_note-38]

### The Space Shuttle era

NASA started the [Space Shuttle design process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_design_process) in 1968, with the vision of creating a fully reusable [spaceplane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaceplane) using a crewed [fly-back booster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LFBB_\(NASA\)). This concept proved expensive and complex, therefore the design was scaled back to reusable [solid rocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_rocket) boosters and an expendable [external tank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/External_tank).[^cite_note-nasaStudy1982-39][^cite_note-nasaStudy1980-40] Space Shuttle *[Columbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_Columbia)* launched and landed 27 times and was lost with all crew on the 28th landing attempt; *[Challenger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_Challenger)* launched and landed 9 times and was lost with all crew on the 10th launch attempt; *[Discovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_Discovery)* launched and landed 39 times; *[Atlantis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_Atlantis)* launched and landed 33 times; *[Endeavour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_Endeavour)* launched and landed 25 times. The last mission of Space Shuttle, [STS-135](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STS-135), landed back on Earth on 21 July 2011 after delivering supplies and equipment to the International Space Station.[^cite_note-41]

In 1986 President [Ronald Reagan](25433.md) called for an air-breathing [scramjet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scramjet) [National Aerospace Plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Aerospace_Plane) (NASP)/[X-30](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-30). The project failed due to technical issues and was canceled in 1993.[^cite_note-42]

In the late 1980s a fully reusable version of the Soviet [Energia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energia_\(rocket\)) rocket, the Energia II, was proposed. Its boosters and core would have had the capability of landing separately on a runway.[^cite_note-43] This concept was not developed and even the original expendable Energia flew only twice in the late 1980s. The second flight launched the reusable spacecraft [Buran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buran_\(spacecraft\)) on its first and only, uncrewed mission.[^cite_note-44]

In the 1990s the McDonnell Douglas [Delta Clipper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_Clipper) VTOL SSTO proposal progressed to the testing phase. The [DC-X](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DC-X) prototype demonstrated rapid turnaround time and automatic computer control.[^cite_note-45]

In mid-1990s, British research evolved an earlier [HOTOL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HOTOL) design into the [Skylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skylon_\(spacecraft\)) design, which remained in development at [Reaction Engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_Engines_Limited) until 2024 when the company went bankrupt.[^cite_note-46] In 2025, the [European Space Agency (ESA)](10363.md) announced a plan to use technologies developed for Skylon's [SABRE engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SABRE_\(rocket_engine\)) in its future Flying Engine Testbed initiative INVICTUS.[^cite_note-47]

From the late 1990s to the 2000s, the European Space Agency (ESA) studied the recovery of the [Ariane 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_5) solid rocket boosters.[^cite_note-48] The last recovery attempt took place in 2009.[^cite_note-49]

Two commercial ventures, Kistler Aerospace (later [Rocketplane Kistler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocketplane_Kistler)) and [Rotary Rocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotary_Rocket), attempted to build reusable privately developed rockets in the 1990s before going bankrupt.[^cite_note-50][^cite_note-51][^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53]

NASA proposed reusable concepts to replace the Shuttle technology, to be demonstrated under the [X-33](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-33) and [X-34](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-34) programs, which were both cancelled in the early 2000s due to rising costs and technical issues.[^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55][^cite_note-56]

The [Ansari X Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ansari_X_Prize) contest, created in 1996, was intended to develop private suborbital reusable vehicles. Many private companies competed, with the winner, [Scaled Composites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scaled_Composites), reaching the [Kármán line](738149.md) twice in a two-week period in 2004 with their reusable [SpaceShipOne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceShipOne).[^cite_note-57] The design was later developed into the [space tourism](67896.md) vehicle [SpaceShipTwo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceShipTwo), which flew on multiple suborbital flights, but never reached the [Kármán line](738149.md).[^cite_note-58]

Between 1999 and 2004, the German [DLR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Aerospace_Center) was working on two reusable launch vehicle concepts within the ASTRA (Ausgewählte Systeme und Technologien für Raumtransport) program. The [Liquid Fly-back Booster (LFBB)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_fly-back_booster) was a winged horizontal landing booster for the [Ariane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_\(rocket_family\)) family of rockets.[^cite_note-59][^cite_note-60] The [Hopper spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopper_\(spacecraft\)) was a [rocket sled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_sled)-launched [spaceplane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaceplane). In 2004, DLR performed a series of drop tests with Phoenix RLV, a subscale prototype of Hopper, at the [North European Aerospace Test range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_European_Aerospace_Test_range) in [Kiruna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiruna).[^cite_note-61][^cite_note-62]

In 2001, the Russian [Khrunichev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khrunichev) space centre proposed a reusable fly-back booster [Baikal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baikal_\(rocket_booster\)) for the [Angara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angara_\(rocket_family\)) family of rockets.[^cite_note-63] This vehicle never flew.[^cite_note-64] A similar concept was later proposed by [Roscosmos](406573.md) in 2018 with no subsequent updates.[^cite_note-65]

In 2005, [NASA](18426568.md) initiated the [Commercial Orbital Transportation Services (COTS)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Orbital_Transportation_Services) program supporting private companies in developing [uncrewed cargo vehicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparison_of_space_station_cargo_vehicles) for resupplying the ISS.[^cite_note-66] This program has briefly resurrected the reusable [Kistler K-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kistler_K-1) concept by [Rocketplane Kistler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocketplane_Kistler) before it was cancelled for lack of private funding.[^cite_note-67][^cite_note-68] However, another recipient of [COTS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Orbital_Transportation_Services) funding from NASA, SpaceX, managed to use this support to keep operating and to develop its Falcon 9 rocket, which later became partially reusable.[^cite_note-69][^cite_note-70]

In 2012, SpaceX started a flight test program with [experimental vehicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Grasshopper). These subsequently led to the development of the Falcon 9 reusable rocket launcher.[^cite_note-nsw20130328-71] SpaceX achieved the first vertical soft landing of a reusable orbital rocket stage on December 21, 2015, after delivering 11 [Orbcomm OG-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbcomm_OG-2) commercial satellites into [low Earth orbit](47568.md).[^cite_note-72] The first reuse of a Falcon 9 first stage occurred on 30 March 2017.[^cite_note-73] Since then, SpaceX has been routinely recovering and reusing their first stages, as well as [fairings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_fairing_recovery_program).[^cite_note-74]

In 2015, [Airbus Defence and Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbus_Defence_and_Space) proposed the [Adeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adeline_\(rocket_stage\)) reusable engine pod for the [Ariane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_\(rocket_family\)) family of rockets.[^cite_note-75] In 2018, [CNES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CNES) declared the concept not financially interesting and it hasn't been developed further.[^cite_note-76]

On 23 November 2015 the [New Shepard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Shepard) rocket became the first [Vertical Take-off, Vertical Landing (VTVL)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VTVL) sub-orbital rocket to reach space by passing the Kármán line (100 km or 62 mi), reaching 329,839 ft (100,535 m) before returning for a propulsive landing.[^cite_note-space20151124-77][^cite_note-arstechnica20151125-78]

In November 2016, ESA selected the Spanish Company [PLD Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PLD_Space) to start developing a reusable first stage under the agency's [FLPP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Future_Launchers_Preparatory_Programme) program.[^cite_note-79] This project became known as [Miura 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miura_5) in 2018, when [PLD Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PLD_Space) redesigned the vehicle to increase its payload capacity after a review by ESA.[^cite_note-80] In April 2019, [PLD Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PLD_Space) performed a successful drop and recovery test of a Miura 5 first stage demonstrator.[^cite_note-81][^cite_note-82]

In 2017, the [German Aerospace Center (DLR)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Aerospace_Center) started working on the Reusable Flight Experiment (ReFEx) aiming to demonstrate a winged fly-back rocket booster. As of 2024, its launch was planned for late 2026 atop a Brazilian [VSB-30](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VSB-30) sounding rocket from the [Koonibba Test Range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koonibba_Test_Range) in Australia.[^cite_note-83]

In 2018, China was researching possible reusability for the [Long March 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_March_8) system.[^cite_note-84] This had been later abandoned.[^cite_note-85] However, multiple Chinese private companies developing reusable launch vehicles have been performing [VTVL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VTVL) test flights of varying complexity and success since 2019.[^cite_note-86][^cite_note-87][^cite_note-88][^cite_note-89]

In March 2019, the German Aerospace Center (DLR) started working on the [EU](9317.md)-funded project [RETALT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RETALT) aimed at developing [retropropulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retropropulsion) technologies for reusable rockets.[^cite_note-90]

In 2019 [Rocket Lab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_Lab) announced plans to recover and reuse the first stage of their Electron launch vehicle, intending to use [parachutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parachute) and [mid-air retrieval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid-air_retrieval).[^cite_note-rlab20190806-91] On 20 November 2020, Rocket Lab successfully returned an Electron first stage from an orbital launch, the stage softly splashing down in the Pacific Ocean.[^cite_note-SpaceNews_2020-92] Nine first stage boosters were recovered between November 2020 and January 2024, however after Rocket Lab re-used certain components from the recovered boosters (including [Rutherford rocket engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rutherford_\(rocket_engine\))[^cite_note-93][^cite_note-94]), the company decided not to re-use Electron first stage boosters, citing decreasing marginal financial savings from the booster recovery program, instead focusing on the larger, partially reusable Neutron rocket.[^cite_note-PeterBeckInterview202511-95]

### 2020s

In 2020, the only operational reusable orbital-class launch systems were the Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy, the latter of which is based upon the Falcon 9. SpaceX was also developing the fully reusable Starship launch system.[^cite_note-musk20170929-96] Blue Origin was developing its New Glenn orbital rocket with a reusable first stage.

In October 2020, [Roscosmos](406573.md) signed a development contract for [Amur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amur_\(launch_vehicle\)), a new launcher with a reusable first stage.[^cite_note-roscosmos20201005-97] In 2024, Roscosmos expected the vehicle to fly no earlier than 2030 and announced intention to start developing a prototype first stage in 2025.[^cite_note-98][^cite_note-99] In 2026, Roscosmos expected the first stage demonstrator to perform its first flights in 2028 and Amur to begin flight tests in 2031.[^cite_note-100]

In December 2020, ESA signed contracts to start developing [THEMIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Themis_programme), a prototype reusable first stage.[^cite_note-101] In September 2025, the first THEMIS prototype has been fully assembled at its launch site at [Esrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esrange) in Sweden.[^cite_note-102] Lessons learned through the development and testing of THEMIS, as well as smaller-scale demonstrators [CALLISTO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CALLISTO),[^cite_note-103] FROG-T, and FROG-H[^cite_note-104] will be used in development of future European reusable launchers [Maia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maia_\(rocket\))[^cite_note-\:1-105] and [Ariane Next](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_Next).[^cite_note-106][^cite_note-107][^cite_note-108]

In January 2022, the German Aerospace Center (DLR) initiated the Advanced Technologies for High Energetic Atmospheric Flight of Launcher Stages (ATHEAt) program for demonstrating various technologies related to launch vehicle reusability. The first suborbital test flight of the program successfully launched on 6 October 2025 from [Andøya Space](30864935.md) in Norway and the second, using a different rocket booster, is scheduled for 2026 from [Esrange Space Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esrange) in Sweden.[^cite_note-109][^cite_note-110][^cite_note-111][^cite_note-112][^cite_note-113]

In 2022, China revealed plans to use reusable first stages on the new [Long March 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_March_9) and [10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_March_10) rockets, which are expected to serve the country's [crewed Lunar program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Lunar_Exploration_Program).[^cite_note-114][^cite_note-115] In August and September 2025, China performed first hot fire tests of [Long March 10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_March_10)'s first stage, including a restart sequence likely related to first stage landing maneuvres needed for reusability.[^cite_note-116] [Long March 10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_March_10)'s first stage performed its first soft landing on water on 11 February 2026 during the launch abort test of the [Mengzhou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mengzhou_\(spacecraft\)) spacecraft.[^cite_note-117][^cite_note-118]

In October 2023, the Spanish company [PLD Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PLD_Space), supported by ESA's [FLPP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Future_Launchers_Preparatory_Programme) funding,[^cite_note-119] tested various technologies for its future reusable launch vehicle [Miura 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miura_5) by successfully launching the suborbital rocket [Miura 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miura_1) from the [El Arenosillo Test Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Arenosillo) in [Huelva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huelva), Spain. The company claimed that as much as 70% of the technology needed for Miura 5 could be tested on Miura 1.[^cite_note-120][^cite_note-121]

In September 2024, the [Government of India](553883.md) has approved plans to develop a new partially reusable rocket [NGLV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Next_Generation_Launch_Vehicle). The vehicle, with a VTVL first stage, is expected to be operational around 2033.[^cite_note-122]

In November 2024, China debuted the [Long March 12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_March_12) rocket,[^cite_note-123] whose later version [Long March 12A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_March_12A) is designed to have a reusable first stage.[^cite_note-124] In January 2025, the Longxing-2 [VTVL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VTVL) demonstrator, likely a precursor to Long March 12A's first stage, flew on a high altitude suborbital test flight. The outcome of this test was not made public.[^cite_note-125][^cite_note-126][^cite_note-127] [Long March 12A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_March_12A) had its maiden flight on 23 December 2025. The rocket successfully reached orbit but the first stage was destroyed during its landing attempt.[^cite_note-128]

In 2025, [Arianespace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arianespace) proposed making [Ariane 6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_6) partially reusable by substituting the rocket's solid-fuel expendable boosters with Liquid Reusable Boosters (LRBs) derived from the Maia rocket under development by Arianespace's subsidiary MaiaSpace. The development of the proposal was funded by ESA's Boosters for European Space Transportation (BEST!) initiative.[^cite_note-129][^cite_note-130]

In June 2025, the Japanese company [Honda](13729.md) performed a successful 300 m high VTVL flight of a [liquid-propellant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid-propellant_rocket) demonstrator rocket equipped with [grid fins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grid_fin) and landing legs.[^cite_note-131][^cite_note-132] Another Japanese VTVL demonstrator, [JAXA](716184.md)'s RV-X vehicle which serves as a technology pathfinder for [CALLISTO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CALLISTO), flew for the first time in July 2026.[^cite_note-\:2-133]

In September 2025, ESA awarded a contract to the Italian company [Avio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avio) to start developing a reusable upper stage demonstrator.[^cite_note-134][^cite_note-135][^cite_note-136] Later in 2025, ESA also awarded a related contract to the Italian company Ingegneria Dei Sistemi (IDS) to design a reusable rocket stage recovery vessel.[^cite_note-137] Meanwhile, Avio has been developing the [FD1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FD1_\(rocket\)) and FD2 rocket demonstrators of [methalox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_rocket_propellant) engines for their future Vega Next rocket, with possible reusability-related features like [grid fins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grid_fin).[^cite_note-138][^cite_note-139][^cite_note-140][^cite_note-141][^cite_note-142]

On 20 October 2025, the Chinese company [LandSpace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LandSpace_Technology_Corporation) performed a static-fire test of its new rocket [Zhuque-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhuque-3) intended for partial reusability. The first stage of the rocket was equipped with [grid fins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grid_fin), aerodynamic chines, and landing legs.[^cite_note-143][^cite_note-144][^cite_note-145] The rocket successfully launched and reached orbit on 3 December 2025 but the first stage was destroyed during its landing attempt.[^cite_note-146][^cite_note-147]

On 13 November 2025, Blue Origin's New Glenn rocket launched NASA's twin [ESCAPADE](73006261.md) spacecraft to Mars on its second flight. The rocket's first stage then successfully landed on a barge in the Atlantic Ocean.[^cite_note-148][^cite_note-149] This made Blue Origin the second company after SpaceX to recover an orbital-class booster by a propulsive landing.[^cite_note-150] The same first stage, with a new set of engines, was then successfully reused on 19 April 2026 during the [third flight of New Glenn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_New_Glenn_launches#NG-3), although the flight's primary mission failed due to a second stage issue.[^cite_note-151][^cite_note-152][^cite_note-153][^cite_note-154]

[CZ-10B](82301965.md)'s maiden launch took place at 4\:15 UTC on 10 July 2026 and successfully placed a satellite into its designated orbit. A first stage booster recovery using the LingHangZhe (Navigator) wires-based recovery ship was successful.[^cite_note-STDaily-maidenFlight-155][^cite_note-NSF-24062026-156] This makes CZ-10B the fourth launcher system in the world to achieve first-stage recovery during an orbital or sub-orbital launch attempt, after [Falcon 9](2647515.md) (2015), [Starship](59118602.md) (2024), and [New Glenn](51585988.md) (2025). CZ-10B is the first system in China and the first outside of the United States to accomplish first-stage recovery. It is also the first launcher to succeed at first-stage booster recovery on its very first attempt.[^cite_note-157][^cite_note-158][^cite_note-159]
