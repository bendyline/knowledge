A **chatbot** (originally **chatterbot**)[^cite_note-Mauldin-1] is a [software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software) application or web interface designed to converse through text or speech, functioning as a conversation partner.[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-Caldarini-20223-3][^cite_note-4] Chatbots have existed for decades, but chatbots based on [deep learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_learning) have gained popularity during the [AI boom](73291899.md) of the 2020s, with the releases of [generative AI](73291755.md) (GenAI) chatbots such as [ChatGPT](72417803.md), [Gemini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemini_\(chatbot\)), [Claude](75879512.md), and [Grok](75223933.md). These chatbots typically use [fine-tuned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine-tuning_\(machine_learning\)) [large language models](73248112.md) (LLMs) to generate text. A major area where chatbots have long been used is [customer service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Customer_service) and [support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Customer_support), with various sorts of [virtual assistants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_assistant).[^cite_note-5]

## History

### Turing test

In 1950, [Alan Turing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Turing) published an article entitled "[Computing Machinery and Intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computing_Machinery_and_Intelligence)" in which he proposed what is now called the [Turing test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_test) as a criterion of [intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence). This criterion depends on the ability of a [computer program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_program) to impersonate a human in a [real-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-time_computing) written conversation with a human judge, to the extent that the judge is incapable of reliably distinguishing, on the basis of the conversational content alone, between the program and a real human.[^cite_note-Turing-6]

### Early chatbots

[Joseph Weizenbaum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Weizenbaum)'s program [ELIZA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ELIZA) was first published in 1966. Weizenbaum did not claim that ELIZA was genuinely intelligent, and the introduction to his paper presented it more as a debunking exercise:

> In artificial intelligence, machines are made to behave in wondrous ways, often sufficient to dazzle even the most experienced observer. But once a particular program is unmasked, once its inner workings are explained, its magic crumbles away; it stands revealed as a mere collection of procedures. The observer says to himself "I could have written that". With that thought, he moves the program in question from the shelf marked "intelligent", to that reserved for curios. The object of this paper is to cause just such a re-evaluation of the program about to be "explained". Few programs ever needed it more.[^cite_note-Weizenbaum-7]

&#x20;ELIZA's key method of operation involves the recognition of clue words or phrases in the input, and the output of the corresponding pre-prepared or pre-programmed responses that can move the conversation forward in an apparently meaningful way (e.g. by responding to any input that contains the word 'MOTHER' with 'TELL ME MORE ABOUT YOUR FAMILY').[^cite_note-Weizenbaum-7] Thus an [illusion of understanding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_understanding) is generated, even though the processing involved has been merely superficial. ELIZA showed that such an illusion is surprisingly easy to generate because human judges are ready to give the benefit of the doubt when conversational responses are *capable of being interpreted* as "intelligent".

Following ELIZA, psychiatrist [Kenneth Colby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Colby) developed [PARRY](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PARRY) in 1972.[^cite_note-Güzeldere-8][^cite_note-comphis-9][^cite_note-Sondheim-10][^cite_note-rfc0439-11]

From 1978[^cite_note-12] to some time after 1983,[^cite_note-Kolodner-19832-13] the CYRUS project led by [Janet Kolodner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janet_Kolodner) constructed a chatbot simulating [Cyrus Vance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_Vance) (57th [United States Secretary of State](32293.md)). It used [case-based reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case-based_reasoning), and updated its database daily by parsing wire news from [United Press International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Press_International). The program was unable to process the news items subsequent to the surprise resignation of Cyrus Vance in April 1980, and the team constructed another chatbot simulating his successor, [Edmund Muskie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Muskie).[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-Kolodner-19832-13]

In 1984, an interactive version of the program [Racter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racter) was released which acted as a chatbot.[^cite_note-15]

[A.L.I.C.E.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_Linguistic_Internet_Computer_Entity) was released in 1995. This uses a [markup language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markup_language) called AIML,[^cite_note-Caldarini-20223-3] which is specific to its function as a [conversational agent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialogue_system), and has since been adopted by various other developers of, so-called, Alicebots. A.L.I.C.E. is a [weak AI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_artificial_intelligence) without any reasoning capabilities. It is based on a similar [pattern matching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pattern_matching) technique as ELIZA in 1966. This is not strong AI, which would require [sapience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sapience) and [logical reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_reasoning) abilities.

[Jabberwacky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jabberwacky), released in 1997, learns new responses and context based on [real-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-time_computing) [user interactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human%E2%80%93computer_interaction), rather than being driven from a static [database](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Database).

Chatbot competitions focus on the Turing test or more specific goals. Two such annual contests are the [Loebner Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loebner_Prize) and The Chatterbox Challenge; although the latter has been offline since 2015, its materials remain accessible via web archives.[^cite_note-16]

Pre-dating the current generation of large language models, Gavagai, a Swedish language technology startup, created a Twitter-based bot in 2015[^cite_note-17] and [DBpedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DBpedia) created a chatbot during the 2017 [Google Summer of Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Summer_of_Code)[^cite_note-18] that communicated through [Facebook Messenger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facebook_Messenger).

### Modern chatbots based on large language models



A [Character.ai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Character.ai) conversation with a chatbot simulating the philosopher [Wittgenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wittgenstein)

Modern chatbots like [ChatGPT](72417803.md) are often based on [foundational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundation_model) [large language models](73248112.md) called [generative pre-trained transformers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_pre-trained_transformer) (GPT). They are based on a [deep learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_learning) architecture called the [transformer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformer_\(deep_learning_architecture\)), which contains [artificial neural networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_neural_networks). They generate text after being trained on a large [text corpus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Text_corpus), exhibiting capabilities beyond their explicit training.

Chatbots integrated into apps and websites can call [image-generation models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Text-to-image_model) or search the web.[^cite_note-19][^cite_note-20] Some platforms also enable users to interact with conversational interfaces directly through web-based chat environments, allowing real-time assistance, content generation, and task automation without requiring software installation.

### Messaging apps

Many companies' chatbots run on [messaging apps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messaging_apps) or simply via [SMS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS). They are used for [B2C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B2C) customer service, sales and marketing.[^cite_note-21]

In 2016, Facebook Messenger allowed developers to place chatbots on their platform. There were 30,000 bots created for Messenger in the first six months, rising to 100,000 by September 2017.[^cite_note-22]

Since September 2017, this has also been as part of a pilot program on WhatsApp. Airlines [KLM](16882.md) and [Aeroméxico](84578.md) both announced their participation in the testing;[^cite_note-23][^cite_note-24][^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26] both airlines had previously launched customer services on the Facebook Messenger platform.

The bots usually appear as one of the user's contacts, but can sometimes act as participants in a [group chat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chat_room).

Many banks, insurers, media companies, e-commerce companies, airlines, hotel chains, retailers, health care providers, government entities, and restaurant chains have used chatbots to [answer simple questions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Question_answering), increase [customer engagement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Customer_engagement),[^cite_note-27] for promotion, and to offer additional ways to order from them.[^cite_note-28] Chatbots are also used in [market research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_research) to collect short survey responses.[^cite_note-29]

A 2017 study showed 4% of companies used chatbots.[^cite_note-30] In a 2016 study, 80% of businesses said they intended to have one by 2020.[^cite_note-31]

#### As part of company apps and websites

Previous generations of chatbots were present on company websites, e.g. Ask Jenn from [Alaska Airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alaska_Airlines) which debuted in 2008[^cite_note-nytimes.com-32] or [Expedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expedia)'s virtual customer service agent which launched in 2011.[^cite_note-nytimes.com-32][^cite_note-33] The newer generation of chatbots includes [IBM Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_Watson)-powered "Rocky", introduced in February 2017 by the New York City-based [e-commerce](9611.md) company Rare Carat to provide information to prospective diamond buyers.[^cite_note-34][^cite_note-35]

#### Chatbot sequences

Used by marketers to script sequences of messages, very similar to an [autoresponder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autoresponder) sequence. Such sequences can be triggered by user opt-in or the use of keywords within user interactions. After a trigger occurs a sequence of messages is delivered until the next anticipated user response. Each user response is used in the decision tree to help the chatbot navigate the response sequences to deliver the correct response message.

### Company internal platforms

Companies have used chatbots for customer support, human resources, or in [Internet-of-Things](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_of_Things) (IoT) projects. [Overstock.com](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overstock.com), for one, has reportedly launched a chatbot named Mila to attempt to automate certain processes when customer service employees request sick leave.[^cite_note-36] Other large companies such as Lloyds Banking Group, Royal Bank of Scotland, Renault and Citroën are now using chatbots instead of call centres with humans to provide a first point of contact. In large companies, like in hospitals and aviation organizations, chatbots are also used to share information within organizations, and to assist and replace service desks.[^cite_note-37]

### Customer service

Chatbots have been proposed as a replacement for [customer service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Customer_service) departments.[^cite_note-38] In 2026, *The Financial Times* reported on agentic chatbots that could do shopping for customers once given instructions.[^cite_note-39]

In 2016, Russia-based Tochka Bank launched a chatbot on [Facebook](7529378.md) for a range of financial services, including a possibility of making payments.[^cite_note-40] In July 2016, [Barclays Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABSA_Group_Limited) also launched a Facebook chatbot.[^cite_note-41]

### Healthcare

See also: [Artificial intelligence in healthcare](52588198.md)

Chatbots are also appearing in the healthcare industry.[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43] A study suggested that physicians in the United States believed that chatbots would be most beneficial for scheduling doctor appointments, locating health clinics, or providing medication information.[^cite_note-44] A 2025 review found that participants often rated chatbot responses as more empathic than those from clinicians.[^cite_note-45]

In 2020, [WhatsApp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WhatsApp) worked with the [World Health Organization](33583.md) and the [Government of India](553883.md) to make chatbots to answer users' questions on [COVID-19](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronavirus_disease_2019).[^cite_note-46][^cite_note-47][^cite_note-48][^cite_note-49]

In 2023, US-based [National Eating Disorders Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Eating_Disorders_Association) replaced its human [helpline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helpline) staff with a chatbot but had to take it offline after users reported receiving harmful advice from it.[^cite_note-50][^cite_note-51][^cite_note-52]

### Politics

See also: [Government by algorithm § AI politicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_by_algorithm#AI_politicians)

In New Zealand, the chatbot SAM – short for [Semantic Analysis Machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic_analysis_\(machine_learning\))[^cite_note-53] – has been developed by Nick Gerritsen of Touchtech.[^cite_note-54] It is designed to share its political thoughts, for example on topics such as climate change, healthcare and education, etc. It talks to people through Facebook Messenger.[^cite_note-55][^cite_note-56][^cite_note-57][^cite_note-58]

In 2022, the chatbot "Leader Lars" or "Leder Lars" was nominated for [The Synthetic Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Synthetic_Party) to run in the [Danish](11746442.md) parliamentary election,[^cite_note-59] and was built by the artist collective Computer Lars.[^cite_note-60] Leader Lars differed from earlier virtual politicians by leading a [political party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_party) and by not pretending to be an objective candidate.[^cite_note-61] This chatbot engaged in critical discussions on politics with users from around the world.[^cite_note-62]

In [India](14533.md), the state government has launched a chatbot for its Aaple Sarkar platform,[^cite_note-63] which provides conversational access to information regarding public services managed.[^cite_note-64][^cite_note-65]

### Toys

Chatbots have also been incorporated into devices not primarily meant for computing, such as toys.[^cite_note-virtualagentchat-66]

*Hello [Barbie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbie)* is an Internet-connected version of the doll that uses a chatbot provided by the company ToyTalk,[^cite_note-67] which previously used the chatbot for a range of smartphone-based characters for children.[^cite_note-68] These characters' behaviors are constrained by a set of rules that in effect emulate a particular character and produce a storyline.[^cite_note-69]

The [My Friend Cayla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/My_Friend_Cayla) doll was marketed as a line of 18-inch (46 cm) dolls which uses [speech recognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speech_recognition) technology in conjunction with an [Android](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Android_\(operating_system\)) or [iOS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IOS) mobile app to recognize the child's speech and have a conversation. Like the Hello Barbie doll, it attracted controversy due to vulnerabilities with the doll's [Bluetooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth) stack and its use of data collected from the child's speech.

IBM's [Watson computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watson_\(computer\)) has been used as the basis for chatbot-based educational toys for companies such as *CogniToys,*[^cite_note-virtualagentchat-66] intended to interact with children for educational purposes.[^cite_note-70]

### Malicious use

Malicious chatbots are frequently used to fill [chat rooms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chat_rooms) with spam and advertisements by mimicking human behavior and conversations or to entice people into revealing personal information, such as bank account numbers. They were commonly found on [Yahoo! Messenger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yahoo!_Messenger), [Windows Live Messenger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows_Live_Messenger), [AOL Instant Messenger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AOL_Instant_Messenger) and other [instant messaging](55951.md) protocols. There has also been a published report of a chatbot used in a fake personal ad on a dating service's website.[^cite_note-71]

[Tay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tay_\(bot\)), an AI chatbot designed to learn from previous interactions, caused major controversy after being targeted by internet trolls on Twitter. Soon after its launch, the bot was exploited, and with its "repeat after me" capability, it started releasing racist, sexist, and controversial responses to Twitter users.[^cite_note-72] This suggests that although the bot learned effectively from experience, adequate protection was not put in place to prevent misuse.[^cite_note-73]

If a text-sending [algorithm](775.md) can pass itself off as a human instead of a chatbot, its message would be more credible. Therefore, human-seeming chatbots with well-crafted online identities could start scattering fake news that seems plausible, for instance making false claims during an election. With enough chatbots, it might be even possible to achieve artificial [social proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_proof).[^cite_note-74][^cite_note-75]

### Data security

[Data security](1157832.md) is one of the major concerns of chatbot technologies. Security threats and system vulnerabilities are weaknesses that are often exploited by malicious users. Storage of user data and past communication, that is highly valuable for training and development of chatbots, can also give rise to security threats.[^cite_note-Hasal-2021-76] Chatbots operating on third-party networks may be subject to various security issues if owners of the third-party applications have policies regarding user data that differ from those of the chatbot.[^cite_note-Hasal-2021-76] Security threats can be reduced or prevented by incorporating protective mechanisms. User [authentication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authentication), chat [End-to-end encryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End-to-end_encryption), and self-destructing messages are some effective solutions to resist potential security threats.[^cite_note-Hasal-2021-76]

### Mental health

Chatbots have shown to be an emerging technology used in the field of mental health. Its usage may encourage users to seek advice on matters of mental health as a means to avoid the stigmatization that may come from sharing such matters with other people.[^cite_note-Chin-2023-77] This is because chatbots can give a sense of privacy and anonymity when sharing sensitive information, as well as providing a space that allows for the user to be free of judgment.[^cite_note-Chin-2023-77] An example of this can be seen in a study which found that with social media and AI chatbots both being possible outlets to express mental health online, users were more willing to share their darker and more depressive emotions to the chatbot.[^cite_note-Chin-2023-77] Users may also turn to chatbots because their replies can be perceived as empathic and emotionally supportive.[^cite_note-78]

Findings prove that chatbots have great potential in scenarios in which it is difficult for users to reach out to family or friends for support.[^cite_note-Chin-2023-77] It has been noted that it demonstrates the ability to give young people "various types of social support such as appraisal, informational, emotional, and instrumental support".[^cite_note-Chin-2023-77] Studies have found that chatbots are able to assist users in managing things such as depression and anxiety.[^cite_note-Chin-2023-77] Some examples of chatbots that serve this function are "Woebot, Wysa, Vivibot, and Tess".[^cite_note-Chin-2023-77]

Evidence indicates that when mental health chatbots interact with users, they tend to follow certain conversation flows.[^cite_note-Haque-2023-79] These being guided conversation, semi guided conversation, and open ended conversation.[^cite_note-Haque-2023-79] The most popular, guided conversation, "only allows the users to communicate with the chatbot with predefined responses from the chatbot. It does not allow any form of open input from the users".[^cite_note-Haque-2023-79] It has also been noted in a study looking at the methods employed by various mental health chatbots, that most of them employed a form of cognitive behavior therapy with the user.[^cite_note-Haque-2023-79]

#### Adverse effects

Further information: [Chatbot psychosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chatbot_psychosis)

Research has identified potential barriers to entry that come with the usage of chatbots for mental health.[^cite_note-Coghlan-2023-80] There are ongoing privacy concerns with sharing user's personal data in chat logs with chatbots.[^cite_note-Coghlan-2023-80] There is a lack of willingness from those in lower socioeconomic statuses to adopt interactions with chatbots as a meaningful way to improve upon mental health.[^cite_note-Coghlan-2023-80] Though chatbots may be capable of detecting simple human emotions in interactions with users, they are incapable of replicating the level of empathy that human therapists do.[^cite_note-Coghlan-2023-80]

There is a lack of research about how exactly these interactions help with a user's real life.[^cite_note-Haque-2023-79] There are concerns regarding the safety of users when interacting with such chatbots.[^cite_note-Haque-2023-79] When improvements and advancements are made to such technologies, how that may affect humans is not a priority.[^cite_note-Haque-2023-79] It is possible that this can lead to "unintended negative consequences, such as biases, inadequate and failed responses, and privacy issues".[^cite_note-Haque-2023-79]

A risk in the usage of chatbots to deal with mental health is increased isolation, as well as a lack of support in times of crisis.[^cite_note-Haque-2023-79] A 2025 study by Sentio University evaluated how six major chatbots responded to disclosures of suicide risk and other acute mental health crises, finding that none consistently met clinician determined safety standards.[^cite_note-81] Another notable risk is a general lack of a strong understanding of mental health.[^cite_note-Haque-2023-79] Studies have indicated that mental-health-oriented chatbots have been prone to recommending users medical solutions and to rely upon themselves heavily.[^cite_note-Haque-2023-79]

Obsessive use of chatbots has been linked to [chatbot psychosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chatbot_psychosis)[^cite_note-82] in people already prone to delusional and conspiratorial thinking. This is caused in part by chatbots "hallucinating" information,[^cite_note-RollingStone-83] as they are designed for engagement, and to keep people talking.[^cite_note-84]

## Limitations

Traditional chatbots particularly lacked understanding of user requests, leading to clunky, repetitive conversations. Their pre-programmed responses would often fail to satisfy unexpected user queries, causing frustration. These chatbots were particularly unhelpful for users who lacked a clear understanding of their problem or the service they needed.[^cite_note-85]

Chatbots based on [large language models](73248112.md) are much more versatile, but require a large amount of conversational data to train. These models generate new responses word by word based on user input, and are usually trained on a large dataset of natural-language phrases.[^cite_note-Caldarini-20223-3] They sometimes provide plausible-sounding but incorrect or nonsensical answers, referred to as "[hallucinations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallucination_\(artificial_intelligence\))". They can for example make up names, dates, or historical events.[^cite_note-86] When humans use and apply chatbot content contaminated with hallucinations, this results in "botshit".[^cite_note-87] Given the increasing adoption and use of chatbots for generating content, there are concerns that this technology will significantly reduce the cost it takes humans to generate [misinformation](2203174.md).[^cite_note-88]

### Algorithmic bias

Due to the nature of chatbots being language-learning models trained on numerous datasets, the issue of [algorithmic bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmic_bias) exists.[^cite_note-Coghlan-2023-80] Chatbots with built in biases from their training can have them brought out against individuals of certain backgrounds and may result in incorrect information being conveyed.[^cite_note-Coghlan-2023-80]

## Impact on jobs

Chatbots and technology in general are used to automate repetitive tasks. But advanced chatbots like [ChatGPT](72417803.md) are also targeting high-paying, creative, and knowledge-based jobs, raising concerns about workforce disruption and quality trade-offs in favor of cost-cutting.[^cite_note-89]

Chatbots are increasingly used by [small and medium enterprises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_and_medium_enterprises), to handle customer interactions efficiently, reducing reliance on large [call centers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Call_centre) and lowering operational costs.[^cite_note-90]

[Prompt engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prompt_engineering), the task of designing and refining prompts (inputs) leading to desired AI-generated responses has quickly gained significant demand with the advent of large language models,[^cite_note-91] although the viability of this job is questioned due to new techniques for automating prompt engineering.[^cite_note-92]

## Impact on the environment

See also: [Environmental impact of artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_of_artificial_intelligence) and [Data center § Energy use](579730.md#Energy_use)

Generative AI uses a high amount of [electric power](6168349.md). Due to reliance on [fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel) in its [generation](9540.md), this increases [air pollution](10934212.md), [water pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_pollution), and [greenhouse gas emissions](9528025.md). In 2023, a question to [ChatGPT](72417803.md) consumed on average 10 times as much energy as a Google search.[^cite_note-93] Data centres in general, and those used for AI tasks specifically, use significant amounts of water for cooling.[^cite_note-94][^cite_note-95]
