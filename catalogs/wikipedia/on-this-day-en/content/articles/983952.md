**Elections Alberta** is an independent, non-partisan office of the [Legislative Assembly of Alberta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislative_Assembly_of_Alberta) responsible for administering provincial [elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elections), [by-elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/By-elections), and [referendums](51582.md) within the province. This is in accordance with the Alberta Election Act.[^cite_note-3] Elections Alberta also oversees political parties and candidates in accordance with the Election Finances and Contributions Disclosure Act.[^cite_note-4]

## History

The Office of the Chief Electoral Officer (Elections Alberta), was created in 1977 to act as an independent body to oversee Alberta's Elections Finances and Contribution Disclosure Act and Election Act. Prior to 1977, these acts were overseen by the clerk of the Legislative Assembly.[^cite_note-5]

## Jurisdiction

Elections Alberta oversees the creation of political parties and [riding associations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riding_association), compiles election statistics on [ridings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_district_\(Canada\)), and collects financial statements from [party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_party) candidates and riding associations. It maintains a list of electors, through enumeration that occurs before an election. Elections Alberta is also responsible for enacting legislation relating to elections passed in the Legislative Assembly, including electoral boundary changes and Elections finance laws.[^cite_note-6]

## Services

Elections Alberta is tasked with printing and distributing maps and Elections Alberta–related campaign materials and guides to candidates and party officials. It is mandated to issue an annual report, as well as reports after each election. These reports contain new and deregistered riding associations, party executives and contact information, donations to parties, changes to electors data, election cost by riding and as a whole, the names of returning officers are among the information in annual reports.[^cite_note-7]

## Electoral Boundaries Commission

Electoral boundary changes come from the [Alberta Electoral Boundaries Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberta_Electoral_Boundaries_Commission?action=edit\&redlink=1), made up of five appointed members. The commission collects input from residents and politicians, then draws up the divisions based on enumeration and census data. A bill is then introduced and voted on in the Legislative Assembly. Minor changes may be made and introduced by members. Changes are then implemented by the chief electoral officer.

An expansion from 83 to 87 ridings was recommended by the commission in June 2010, and their report with the new riding boundaries was in effect for the April 2012 general election.[^cite_note-8]

On recommendation from the Chief Electoral Officer, Glen Resler, Bill 7 was introduced on April 12, 2016, which amended the Electoral Boundaries Commission Act to authorize the appointment of a commission on or before October 31, 2016. This is so that there was sufficient time for Elections Alberta to implement the new boundaries in time for the [2019 Alberta general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Alberta_general_election).[^cite_note-9]

In December 2024, the Electoral Boundaries Commission was again mandated to propose new boundaries, this time for 89 ridings, an increase from 87.

In April 2026, the committee completed it's year long work with the committee majority (including the judge), making minor adjustments, adding a seat in Edmonton and a seat in Calgary to account for the surging population growth in both cities.[^cite_note-10] Included in the report was a heavily [gerrymandered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerrymandered) minority map from the UCP members of the committee.[^cite_note-11] For the first time in the committee's history, the UCP led Alberta government did not approve the majority's recommendations, deciding instead to dissolve the independent committee and forming their own partisan led committee, to hastily redraw the electoral boundary maps before the November 2, 2026 deadline.[^cite_note-12] For the [32nd general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/32nd_Alberta_general_election) it is expected that the partisan led committee redrawn boundaries will be used.[^cite_note-13]

## Candidacy fees and refunds

In 1905, the nomination deposit was set at \$100, a figure that was well beyond the means of ordinary citizens at the time. Despite [inflation](38286.md), this fee was not changed until 1992 when it was doubled to \$200, thus remaining a nominal sum to many Albertans. In 2004, the government controversially increased the required [deposit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deposit_account) again, to \$500, although it became easier to get half of the new deposit back. The government contended that the increase was necessary to deter frivolous candidacies, encourage timely filing of financial statements, and help pay for Elections Alberta's expenses. Some smaller parties argue that an excessively high deposit represents an unacceptable "[user fee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/User_fee)" on [democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy).

Prior to the [2004 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Alberta_general_election) a candidate received their deposit back if they were elected or received at least one half the votes of the elected candidate. Candidates who failed to meet these benchmarks forfeited their deposits. Starting with the 2004 election however, the refund policy described above only applies to half of the new deposit (\$250). The other half of the deposit is refunded if the candidate files the required financial statements with Elections Alberta within the prescribed time frame, regardless of how many votes they receive. Therefore, a failed candidate who files timely financial statements would lose \$250. A change in legislation in 2010 led to the deposit being fully refundable to candidates who file their post-election financial report on time.

Prospective senate nominees are required to deposit \$4,000 along with nomination papers signed by 1,500 Albertans. Candidates must be at least 30 years old in accordance with the [Constitution of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Canada) requirement that senators own \$4,000 worth of property.[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15]

## Senate nominees

Alberta began to hold [Alberta Senate nominee elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberta_Senate_nominee_elections) in 1989 and remains the only province to do so. The three candidates who receive the most votes are recommended to the Canadian prime minister as Senate candidates, but there is no legal or constitutional obligation to appoint them. The prime minister has appointed three nominees to the Senate: Reform Party member [Stan Waters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stan_Waters) in 1990 by Prime Minister [Brian Mulroney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Mulroney), and [Bert Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bert_Brown) in 2007 and [Doug Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doug_Black) in 2013, both by Prime Minister [Stephen Harper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Harper). The winners of the April 23, 2012 Senate nominee election were all Progressive Conservative Party candidates, [Doug Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doug_Black), [Scott Tannas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scott_Tannas), and [Mike Shaikh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mike_Shaikh?action=edit\&redlink=1). Doug Black received the most votes and was consequently appointed to the Senate on January 25, 2013.[^cite_note-16]
