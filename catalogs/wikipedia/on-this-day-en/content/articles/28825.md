A **submarine** (often shortened to **sub**) is a [watercraft](301664.md) capable of independent operation underwater. (It differs from a [submersible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submersible), which has more limited underwater capability.)[^cite_note-2] The term "submarine" is also sometimes used historically or informally to refer to [remotely operated vehicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remotely_operated_vehicle) and [robots](1885043.md), or to medium-sized or smaller vessels (such as the [midget submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midget_submarine) and the [wet sub](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wet_sub)). Submarines are referred to as *boats* rather than *ships* regardless of their size.[^cite_note-cutler-3]

Although experimental submarines had been built earlier, submarine design took off during the 19th century, and submarines were adopted by several navies. They were first used widely during [World War I](4764461.md) (1914–1918), and are now used in many [navies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navy), large and small. Their military uses include: attacking enemy surface [ships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship) (merchant and military) or other submarines; [aircraft carrier](2219.md) protection; [blockade running](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blockade_runner); [nuclear deterrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballistic_missile_submarine); stealth operations in denied areas when gathering intelligence and doing [reconnaissance](146911.md); denying or influencing enemy movements; conventional land attacks (for example, launching a [cruise missile](6590.md)); and covert insertion of [frogmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frogman) or [special forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_forces). Their civilian uses include: [marine science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_science); [salvage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_salvage); exploration; and facility inspection and maintenance. Submarines can be modified for specialized functions such as search-and-rescue missions and [undersea cable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undersea_cable) repair. They are also used in the tourism industry and in [undersea archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undersea_archaeology). Modern deep-diving submarines derive from the [bathyscaphe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bathyscaphe), which evolved from the [diving bell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diving_bell).

Most large submarines consist of a cylindrical body with hemispherical (or conical) ends and a vertical structure, usually located amidships, which houses communications and sensing devices as well as [periscopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periscope). In modern submarines, this structure is called the "[sail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sail_\(submarine\))" in American usage and "fin" in European usage. A feature of earlier designs was the "[conning tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conning_tower)": a separate pressure hull above the main body of the [boat](3996.md) that enabled the use of shorter periscopes. There is a propeller (or pump jet) at the rear, and various hydrodynamic control fins. Smaller, deep-diving, and specialty submarines may deviate significantly from this traditional design. Submarines dive and resurface by using [diving planes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diving_plane) and by changing the amount of water and air in [ballast tanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballast_tank) to affect their [buoyancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buoyancy).

Submarines encompass a wide range of types and capabilities. They range from small, autonomous examples, such as one- or two-person subs that operate for a few hours, to vessels that can remain submerged for six months, such as the Russian [Typhoon class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhoon-class_submarine) (the biggest submarines ever built). Submarines can work at depths that are greater than what is practicable (or even survivable) for human [divers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underwater_diving).[^cite_note-4] More countries are building and operating submarines today than ever before in modern history, and China is leading the construction surge.[^cite_note-NN26_China_sub_construction-5]

## History

Main article: [History of submarines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_submarines)

### Etymology

The word *submarine* means 'underwater' or 'under-sea' (as in [submarine canyon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_canyon), [submarine pipeline](40024054.md)) though as a noun it generally refers to a vessel that can travel underwater.[^cite_note-6] The term is a contraction of *submarine boat*[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8] and occurs as such in several languages, e.g. French (*sous-marin*), and Spanish (*submarino*), although others retain the original term, such as Dutch (*Onderzeeboot*), German (*Unterseeboot*), Swedish (*Undervattensbåt*), and Russian (подводная лодка: *podvodnaya lodka*), all of which mean 'submarine boat'. By [naval tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_tradition), submarines are usually referred to as *boats* rather than as *ships*, regardless of their size.[^cite_note-cutler-3] Although referred to informally as *boats*,[^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10] U.S. submarines employ the designation USS ([United States Ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Ship)) at the beginning of their names, such as [USS *Alabama*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Alabama_\(SSBN-731\)). In the Royal Navy, the designation HMS can refer to "His Majesty's Ship" or "His Majesty's Submarine", though the latter is sometimes rendered "HMS/m".[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12]

### Early human-powered submersibles



An early submersible craft, built by [Cornelis Drebbel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornelis_Drebbel), propelled by oars

#### 16th and 17th centuries

According to a report in *Opusculum [Taisnieri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Taisnier)* published in 1562:[^cite_note-13]

> Two Greeks submerged and surfaced in the river [Tagus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tagus) near the City of [Toledo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toledo,_Spain) several times in the presence of [The Holy Roman Emperor Charles V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_V,_Holy_Roman_Emperor), without getting wet and with the flame they carried in their hands still alight.[^cite_note-14]

In 1578, the English mathematician [William Bourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Bourne_\(mathematician\)) recorded in his book *Inventions or Devises* one of the first plans for an underwater navigation vehicle.[^cite_note-15] A few years later the Scottish mathematician and theologian [John Napier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Napier) wrote in his *Secret Inventions* (1596) that "These inventions besides devises of sayling under water with divers other devises and strategems for harming of the enemyes by the Grace of God and worke of expert Craftsmen I hope to perform." It is unclear whether he carried out his idea.[^cite_note-Tiere-16]

[Jerónimo de Ayanz y Beaumont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jer%C3%B3nimo_de_Ayanz_y_Beaumont) (1553–1613) created detailed designs for two types of air-renovated submersible vehicles. They were equipped with oars, autonomous floating snorkels worked by inner pumps, portholes and gloves used for the crew to manipulate underwater objects. Ayanaz planned to use them for warfare, using them to approach enemy ships undetected and set up timed gunpowder charges on their hulls.[^cite_note-17]

The first submersible of whose construction there exists reliable information was designed and built in 1620 by [Cornelis Drebbel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornelis_Drebbel), a Dutchman in the service of [James I of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_VI_and_I). It was propelled by means of oars.[^cite_note-Tiere-16]

#### 18th century

Cutaway replica of Bushnell's "Turtle" at the Submarine Force Library and Museum, Groton, Connecticut

Cutaway replica of Bushnell's "Turtle" at the [Submarine Force Library and Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_Force_Library_and_Museum), Groton, Connecticut

By the mid-18th century, over a dozen patents for submarines/submersible boats had been granted in England. In 1747, Nathaniel Symons patented and built the first known working example of the use of a ballast tank for submersion. His design used leather bags that could fill with water to submerge the craft. A mechanism was used to twist the water out of the bags and cause the boat to resurface. In 1749, the [Gentlemen's Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gentlemen's_Magazine) reported that a similar design had initially been proposed by [Giovanni Borelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Borelli) in 1680. Further design improvement stagnated for over a century, until application of new technologies for propulsion and stability.[^cite_note-vector-18]

The first military submersible was [*Turtle*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turtle_\(submersible\)) (1775), a hand-powered acorn-shaped device designed by the American [David Bushnell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bushnell_\(inventor\)) to accommodate a single person.[^cite_note-19] It was the first verified submarine capable of independent underwater operation and movement, and the first to use [screws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propeller) for propulsion.[^cite_note-20]

#### 19th century

Illustration by Robert Fulton showing a "plunging boat"

1806 illustration by Robert Fulton showing a "plunging boat"

In 1800, France built [*Nautilus*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nautilus_\(1800_submarine\)), a human-powered submarine designed by American [Robert Fulton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Fulton). They gave up on the experiment in 1804, as did the British, when they reconsidered Fulton's submarine design.

In 1850, [Wilhelm Bauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Bauer)'s [*Brandtaucher*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brandtaucher) was built in Germany. It remains the oldest known surviving submarine in the world.[^cite_note-21]

In 1864, late in the [American Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War), the [Confederate navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederate_navy)'s [*H. L. Hunley*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._L._Hunley) became the first military submarine to sink an enemy vessel, the Union [sloop-of-war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sloop-of-war) [USS *Housatonic*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Housatonic_\(1861\)), using a gun-powder-filled keg on a spar as a torpedo charge. The *Hunley* also sank. The explosion's shock waves may have killed its crew instantly, preventing them from pumping the bilge or propelling the submarine.[^cite_note-22]

In 1866, [*Sub Marine Explorer*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub_Marine_Explorer) was the first submarine to successfully dive, cruise underwater, and resurface under the crew's control. The design by German American [Julius H. Kroehl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_H._Kroehl) (in German, *Kröhl*) incorporated elements that are still used in modern submarines.[^cite_note-23]

In 1866, [*Flach*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flach_\(submarine\)) was built at the Chilean government's request by [Karl Flach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Flach), a German engineer and immigrant. It was the fifth submarine built in the world[^cite_note-24] and, along with a second submarine, was intended to defend the port of [Valparaíso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valpara%C3%ADso) against attack by the [Spanish Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Navy) during the [Chincha Islands War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chincha_Islands_War).

### Mechanically powered submarines

Submarines could not be put into widespread or routine service use by navies until suitable engines were developed. The era from 1863 to 1904 marked a pivotal time in submarine development, and several important technologies appeared. A number of nations built and used submarines. [Diesel electric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diesel%E2%80%93electric_transmission) propulsion became the dominant power system and equipment such as the periscope became standardized. Countries conducted many experiments on effective tactics and weapons for submarines, which led to their large impact in [World War I](4764461.md).

#### 1863–1904



The French submarine [*Plongeur*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_submarine_Plongeur)&#x20;

The first submarine not relying on human power for propulsion was the French [*Plongeur*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_submarine_Plongeur) (*Diver*), launched in 1863, which used compressed air at 1,200 [kPa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal_\(unit\)) (180 [psi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound_per_square_inch)). [Narcís Monturiol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narcis_Monturiol_i_Estarriol) designed the first [air-independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air-independent_power) and [combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion)-powered submarine, [*Ictíneo II*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ict%C3%ADneo_II), which was launched in [Barcelona](4443.md), Spain in 1864.

The submarine became feasible as potential weapon with the development of the [Whitehead torpedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitehead_torpedo), designed in 1866 by British engineer [Robert Whitehead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Whitehead_\(engineer\)), the first practical [self-propelled torpedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-propelled_torpedo).[^cite_note-25] The [spar torpedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spar_torpedo) that had been developed earlier by the Confederate States Navy was considered to be impracticable, as it was believed to have sunk both its intended target, and *H. L. Hunley*, the submarine that deployed it.

The Irish inventor [John Philip Holland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Philip_Holland) built a model submarine in 1876 and in 1878 demonstrated the [Holland I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holland_I) prototype. This was followed by a number of unsuccessful designs. In 1896, he designed the Holland Type VI submarine, which used internal combustion engine power on the surface and electric [battery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battery_\(electricity\)) power underwater. Launched on 17 May 1897 at Navy Lt. [Lewis Nixon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Nixon_\(naval_architect\))'s [Crescent Shipyard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crescent_Shipyard) in [Elizabeth, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth,_New_Jersey), *Holland VI* was purchased by the [United States Navy](20518076.md) on 11 April 1900, becoming the Navy's first commissioned submarine, christened [USS *Holland*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Holland_\(SS-1\)).[^cite_note-26]

Discussions between the English clergyman and inventor [George Garrett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Garrett_\(inventor\)) and the Swedish industrialist [Thorsten Nordenfelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorsten_Nordenfelt) led to the first practical steam-powered submarines, armed with torpedoes and ready for military use. The first was *Nordenfelt I*, a 56-tonne, 19.5-metre (64 ft) vessel similar to Garrett's ill-fated [*Resurgam*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resurgam) (1879), with a range of 240 kilometres (130 nmi; 150 mi), armed with a single [torpedo](31424.md), in 1885.



*[Peral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_submarine_Peral)* at [Cartagena](38101530.md), 1888

A reliable means of propulsion for the submerged vessel was only made possible in the 1880s with the advent of the necessary electric battery technology. The first electrically powered boats were built by [Isaac Peral y Caballero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Peral_y_Caballero) in Spain (who built [*Peral*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_submarine_Peral)), [Dupuy de Lôme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Dupuy_de_L%C3%B4me) (who built [*Gymnote*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_submarine_Gymnote_\(Q1\))) and [Gustave Zédé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustave_Z%C3%A9d%C3%A9) (who built [*Sirène*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_submarine_Gustave_Z%C3%A9d%C3%A9_\(1893\))) in France, and James Franklin Waddington (who built *Porpoise*) in England.[^cite_note-27] Peral's design featured torpedoes and other systems that later became standard in submarines.[^cite_note-28][^cite_note-29]



[USS *Plunger*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Plunger_\(SS-2\)), launched in 1902

Commissioned in June 1900, the French steam and electric [*Narval*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_submarine_Narval_\(Q4\)) employed the now typical double-hull design, with a pressure hull inside the outer shell. These 200-ton ships had a range of over 160 km (100 mi) underwater. The French submarine [*Aigrette*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_submarine_Aigrette) in 1904 further improved the concept by using a diesel rather than a gasoline engine for surface power. Large numbers of these submarines were built, with seventy-six completed before 1914.

The Royal Navy commissioned five [Holland-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holland-class_submarine) submarines from [Vickers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vickers), [Barrow-in-Furness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barrow-in-Furness), under licence from the [Holland Torpedo Boat Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holland_Torpedo_Boat_Company) from 1901 to 1903. Construction of the boats took longer than anticipated, with the first only ready for a diving trial at sea on 6 April 1902. Although the design had been purchased entirely from the US company, the actual design used was an untested improvement to the original Holland design using a new 180 horsepower (130 kW) petrol engine.[^cite_note-30]



[*Akula*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_submarine_Akula_\(1909\)) (launched in 1907) was the first Russian submarine able to cruise long distances.

These types of submarines were first used during the [Russo-Japanese War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Japanese_War) of 1904–05. Due to the blockade at [Port Arthur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Arthur,_China), the Russians sent their submarines to [Vladivostok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladivostok), where by 1 January 1905 there were seven boats, enough to create the world's first "operational submarine fleet". The new submarine fleet began patrols on 14 February, usually lasting for about 24 hours each. The first confrontation with Japanese warships occurred on 29 April 1905 when the Russian submarine [*Som*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Som-class_submarine) was fired upon by Japanese torpedo boats, but then withdrew.[^cite_note-31]

#### World War I



The German submarine [SM *U-9*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SM_U-9), which sank three British [cruisers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cruiser) in [less than an hour in September 1914](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_of_22_September_1914)&#x20;

Military submarines first made a significant impact in [World War I](4764461.md). Forces such as the [U-boats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U-boat) of Germany saw action in the [First Battle of the Atlantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Battle_of_the_Atlantic), and were responsible for sinking [RMS *Lusitania*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RMS_Lusitania), which was sunk as a result of [unrestricted submarine warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unrestricted_submarine_warfare) and is often cited among the reasons for the entry of the United States into the war.[^cite_note-32]

At the outbreak of the war, Germany had only twenty submarines available for combat, although these included vessels of the diesel-engined *[U-19](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SM_U-19_\(Germany\))* class, which had a sufficient range of 5,000 miles (8,000 km) and speed of 8 knots (15 km/h) to allow them to operate effectively around the entire British coast.[^cite_note-33] By contrast, the Royal Navy had a total of 74 submarines, though of mixed effectiveness. In August 1914, a flotilla of ten U-boats sailed from their base in [Heligoland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heligoland) to attack Royal Navy warships in the [North Sea](21179.md) in the first submarine war patrol in history.[^cite_note-34]

The U-boats' ability to function as practical war machines relied on new tactics, their numbers, and submarine technologies such as combination diesel–electric power system developed in the preceding years. More submersibles than true submarines, U-boats operated primarily on the surface using regular engines, submerging occasionally to attack under battery power. They were roughly triangular in cross-section, with a distinct [keel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keel) to control rolling while surfaced, and a distinct bow. During World War I more than 5,000 [Allied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_I) ships were sunk by U-boats.[^cite_note-35]

The British responded to the German developments in submarine technology with the creation of the [K-class submarines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_K-class_submarine). However, these submarines were notoriously dangerous to operate due to their various design flaws and poor maneuverability.[^cite_note-36][^cite_note-37]

#### World War II

See also: [List of submarines of World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_submarines_of_World_War_II)



The [Imperial Japanese Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Japanese_Navy)'s [*I-400*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I-400-class_submarine) submarine, the largest submarine type of WWII



A model of [Günther Prien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%BCnther_Prien)'s [*U-47*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_submarine_U-47_\(1938\)), German WWII [Type VII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_VII_submarine) diesel–electric hunter

During [World War II](32927.md), Germany used submarines to devastating effect in the [Battle of the Atlantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Atlantic), where it attempted to cut Britain's supply routes by sinking more [merchant ships](875717.md) than Britain could replace. These merchant ships were vital to supply Britain's population with food, industry with raw material, and armed forces with fuel and armaments. Although the U-boats had been updated in the interwar years, the major innovation was improved communications, encrypted using the [Enigma cipher machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enigma_machine). This allowed for mass-attack [naval tactics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_tactics) (*Rudeltaktik*, commonly known as "[wolfpack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfpack_\(naval_tactic\))"), which ultimately ceased to be effective when [the U-boat's Enigma was cracked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptanalysis_of_the_Enigma). By the end of the war, almost 3,000 [Allied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II) ships (175 warships, 2,825 merchantmen) had been sunk by U-boats.[^cite_note-38] Although successful early in the war, Germany's U-boat fleet suffered heavy casualties, losing 793 U-boats and about 28,000 submariners out of 41,000, a casualty rate of about 70%.[^cite_note-39]

The [Imperial Japanese Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Japanese_Navy) operated the most varied fleet of submarines of any navy, including *[Kaiten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaiten)* crewed torpedoes, midget submarines ([Type A Ko-hyoteki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_A_Ko-hyoteki-class_submarine) and [*Kairyu* classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kairyu-class_submarine)), medium-range submarines, purpose-built supply submarines and long-range [fleet submarines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fleet_submarine). They also had submarines with the highest submerged speeds during World War II ([*I-201*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I-201-class_submarine) submarines) and submarines that could carry multiple aircraft ([*I-400*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I-400-class_submarine) submarines). They were also equipped with one of the most advanced torpedoes of the conflict, the oxygen-propelled [Type 95](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_95_torpedo). Nevertheless, despite their technical prowess, Japan chose to use its submarines for fleet warfare, and consequently were relatively unsuccessful, as warships were fast, maneuverable and well-defended compared to merchant ships.

The submarine force was the most effective anti-ship weapon in the American arsenal. Submarines, though only about 2 percent of the U.S. Navy, destroyed over 30 percent of the Japanese Navy, including 8 aircraft carriers, 1 battleship and 11 cruisers. US submarines also destroyed over 60 percent of the Japanese merchant fleet, crippling Japan's ability to supply its military forces and industrial war effort. [Allied submarines in the Pacific War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allied_submarines_in_the_Pacific_War) destroyed more Japanese shipping than all other weapons combined. This feat was considerably aided by the Imperial Japanese Navy's failure to provide adequate escort forces for the nation's merchant fleet.

During World War II, 314 submarines served in the US Navy, of which nearly 260 were deployed to the Pacific.[^cite_note-O'Kane,_p._333-40] When the Japanese attacked Hawaii in December 1941, 111 boats were in commission; 203 submarines from the [*Gato*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gato-class_submarine), [*Balao*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balao-class_submarine), and [*Tench* classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tench-class_submarine) were commissioned during the war. During the war, 52 US submarines were lost to all causes, with 48 directly due to hostilities.[^cite_note-41] US submarines sank 1,560 enemy vessels,[^cite_note-O'Kane,_p._333-40] a total tonnage of 5.3 million tons (55% of the total sunk).[^cite_note-Blair,_p._878-42]

The [Royal Navy Submarine Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Navy_Submarine_Service) was used primarily in the classic Axis [blockade](293054.md). Its major operating areas were around Norway, in the [Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean) (against the Axis supply routes to North Africa), and in the Far East. In that war, British submarines sank 2 million tons of enemy shipping and 57 major warships, the latter including 35 submarines. Among these is the only documented instance of a submarine sinking another submarine while both were submerged. This occurred when [HMS *Venturer*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Venturer_\(P68\)) [engaged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_of_9_February_1945) [*U-864*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_submarine_U-864); the *Venturer* crew manually computed a successful firing solution against a three-dimensionally maneuvering target using techniques which became the basis of modern torpedo computer targeting systems. Seventy-four British submarines were lost,[^cite_note-43] the majority, forty-two, in the Mediterranean.

#### Cold-War military models



[HMAS *Rankin*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMAS_Rankin), a [*Collins*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collins-class_submarine) submarine at periscope depth



[USS *Charlotte*](1121727.md), a [*Los Angeles*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Angeles-class_submarine) submarine runs with submarines from partner nations during [RIMPAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exercise_RIMPAC) 2014.

The first launch of a [cruise missile](6590.md) ([SSM-N-8 Regulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SSM-N-8_Regulus)) from a submarine occurred in July 1953, from the deck of [USS *Tunny*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Tunny_\(SS-282\)), a World War II fleet boat modified to carry the missile with a [nuclear warhead](21785.md). *Tunny* and its sister boat, [*Barbero*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Barbero), were the United States' first nuclear deterrent patrol submarines. In the 1950s, [nuclear power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_marine_propulsion) partially replaced diesel–electric propulsion. Equipment was also developed to extract [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen) from sea water. These two innovations gave submarines the ability to remain submerged for weeks or months.[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45] Most of the naval submarines built since that time in the US, the Soviet Union (now Russia), the UK, and France have been powered by a [nuclear reactor](22151.md).

In 1959–1960, the first [ballistic missile submarines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballistic_missile_submarine) were put into service by both the United States ([*George Washington* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington-class_submarine)) and the Soviet Union ([Golf class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golf-class_submarine)) as part of the [Cold War](325329.md) [nuclear deterrent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_deterrent) strategy.

During the Cold War, the US and the Soviet Union maintained large submarine fleets that engaged in cat-and-mouse games. The Soviet Union lost at least four submarines during this period: [*K-129*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_submarine_K-129_\(1960\)) was lost in 1968 (a part of which the [CIA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CIA) retrieved from the ocean floor with the [Howard Hughes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Hughes)-designed ship [*Glomar Explorer*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hughes_Glomar_Explorer)), [*K-8*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_submarine_K-8) in 1970, [*K-219*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_submarine_K-219) in 1986, and [*Komsomolets*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_submarine_K-278_Komsomolets) in 1989 (which held a depth record among military submarines—1,000 m (3,300 ft)). Many other Soviet subs, such as [*K-19*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_submarine_K-19) (the first Soviet nuclear submarine, and the first Soviet sub to reach the North Pole) were badly damaged by fire or radiation leaks. The US lost two nuclear submarines during this time: [USS *Thresher*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Thresher_\(SSN-593\)) due to equipment failure during a test dive while at its operational limit, and [USS *Scorpion*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Scorpion_\(SSN-589\)) due to unknown causes.

During the [Indo-Pakistani War of 1971](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Pakistani_War_of_1971), the [Pakistan Navy](1183789.md)'s [*Hangor*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PNS_Hangor_\(S131\)) sank the Indian frigate [INS *Khukri*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/INS_Khukri_\(F149\)). This was the first sinking by a submarine since World War II. During the same war, [*Ghazi*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PNS_Ghazi), a *Tench*-class submarine on loan to Pakistan from the US, was sunk by the [Indian Navy](604522.md). It was the first submarine combat loss since World War II.[^cite_note-BR-46] In 1982 during the [Falklands War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falklands_War), the Argentine cruiser [*General Belgrano*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ARA_General_Belgrano) was sunk by the British submarine [HMS *Conqueror*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Conqueror_\(S48\)), the first sinking by a nuclear-powered submarine in war.[^cite_note-47] Some weeks later, on 16 June, during the [Lebanon War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1982_Lebanon_War), an unnamed [Israeli submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gal-class_submarine) torpedoed and sank the Lebanese coaster *Transit*,[^cite_note-\:0-48] which was carrying 56 Palestinian refugees to [Cyprus](5593.md), in the belief that the vessel was evacuating anti-Israeli militias. The ship was hit by two torpedoes, managed to run aground but eventually sank. There were 25 dead, including her captain. The [Israeli Navy](1370788.md) disclosed the incident in November 2018.[^cite_note-49][^cite_note-\:0-48]

## Usage

### Military

Main articles: [Submarine warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_warfare), [Attack submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attack_submarine), [Ballistic missile submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballistic_missile_submarine), [Cruise missile submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cruise_missile_submarine), and [Nuclear submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_submarine)



German [*UC-1*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Type_UC_I_submarine) World War I submarine. The wires running up from the bow to the conning tower are the [jumping wires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jumping_wire)



[EML *Lembit*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EML_Lembit) in the [Estonian Maritime Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estonian_Maritime_Museum). The *Lembit* is the only minelayer submarine of its series left in the world.

[^cite_note-50]

Before and during [World War II](32927.md), the primary role of the submarine was anti-surface ship warfare. Submarines would attack either on the surface using deck guns, or submerged using [torpedoes](31424.md). They were particularly effective in sinking Allied transatlantic shipping in both World Wars, and in disrupting Japanese supply routes and naval operations in the Pacific in World War II.

[Mine](22102.md)-laying submarines were developed in the early part of the 20th century. The facility was used in both World Wars. Submarines were also used for inserting and removing covert agents and military forces in [special operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_operations), for intelligence gathering, and to rescue aircrew during air attacks on islands, where the airmen would be told of safe places to crash-land so the submarines could rescue them. Submarines could carry cargo through hostile waters or act as supply vessels for other submarines.

Submarines could usually locate and attack other submarines only on the surface, although [HMS *Venturer*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Venturer_\(P68\)) managed to sink [*U-864*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_submarine_U-864) with a four torpedo spread while both were submerged. The British developed a specialized anti-submarine submarine in WWI, the [R class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_R-class_submarine). After WWII, with the development of the homing torpedo, better [sonar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonar) systems, and [nuclear propulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_propulsion), submarines also became able to hunt each other effectively.

The development of [submarine-launched ballistic missile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine-launched_ballistic_missile) and submarine-launched [cruise missiles](6590.md) gave submarines a substantial and long-ranged ability to attack both land and sea targets with a variety of weapons ranging from [cluster bombs](79745.md) to [nuclear weapons](21785.md).

The primary defense of a submarine lies in its ability to remain concealed in the depths of the ocean. Early submarines could be detected by the sound they made. Water is an excellent conductor of sound (much better than air), and submarines can detect and track comparatively noisy surface ships from long distances. Modern submarines are built with an emphasis on [stealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stealth_technology). Advanced [propeller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propeller) designs, extensive sound-reducing insulation, and special machinery help a submarine remain as quiet as ambient ocean noise, making them difficult to detect. It takes specialized technology to find and attack modern submarines.



[Trident II D5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UGM-133_Trident_II) is one of the most advanced [submarine-launched ballistic missiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine-launched_ballistic_missile)

[Active sonar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonar#Active_sonar) uses the reflection of sound emitted from the search equipment to detect submarines. It has been used since WWII by surface ships, submarines and aircraft (via dropped buoys and helicopter "dipping" arrays), but it reveals the emitter's position, and is susceptible to counter-measures.

A concealed military submarine is a real threat, and because of its stealth, can force an enemy navy to waste resources searching large areas of ocean and protecting ships against attack. This advantage was vividly demonstrated in the 1982 [Falklands War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falklands_War) when the British [nuclear-powered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear-powered) submarine [HMS *Conqueror*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Conqueror_\(S48\)) sank the Argentine cruiser [*General Belgrano*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ARA_General_Belgrano). After the sinking the Argentine Navy recognized that they had no effective defense against submarine attack, and the Argentine surface fleet withdrew to port for the remainder of the war. An Argentine submarine remained at sea, however.[^cite_note-51]

### Civilian

Although the majority of the world's submarines are military, there are some civilian submarines, which are used for tourism, exploration, oil and gas platform inspections, and pipeline surveys. Some are also used in illegal activities.

The [Submarine Voyage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_Voyage) ride opened at [Disneyland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disneyland) in 1959, but although it ran under water, it was not a true submarine, as it ran on tracks and was open to the atmosphere.[^cite_note-perry-52] The first tourist submarine was [*Auguste Piccard*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auguste_Piccard_\(PX-8\)), which went into service in 1964 at [Expo64](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expo64).[^cite_note-53] By 1997, there were 45 tourist submarines operating around the world.[^cite_note-54] Submarines with a [crush depth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_depth_ratings#Crush_depth) in the range of 400–500 feet (120–150 m) are operated in several areas worldwide, typically with bottom depths around 100 to 120 feet (30 to 37 m), with a carrying capacity of 50 to 100 passengers.

In a typical operation a surface vessel carries passengers to an offshore operating area and loads them into the submarine. The submarine then visits underwater points of interest such as natural or artificial reef structures. To surface safely without danger of collision the location of the submarine is marked with an air release and movement to the surface is coordinated by an observer in a support craft.

A recent development is the deployment of so-called [narco-submarines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narco-submarine) by South American drug smugglers to evade law enforcement detection.[^cite_note-55] Although they occasionally deploy [true submarines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narco-submarine#True_submarines), most are self-propelled [semi-submersibles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-submersible), where a portion of the craft remains above water at all times. In September 2011, Colombian authorities seized a 16-meter-long submersible that could hold a crew of 5, costing about \$2 million. The vessel belonged to [FARC](26100.md) rebels and had the capacity to carry at least 7 tonnes of drugs.[^cite_note-56]

### Buoyancy and trim



An illustration showing submarine control surfaces and trim tanks



[USS *Seawolf* (SSN-21)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Seawolf_\(SSN-21\)) Ship Control Panel, with yokes for control surfaces (planes and rudder), and Ballast Control Panel (background), to control the water in tanks and ship's trim

All surface ships, as well as surfaced submarines, are in a positively [buoyant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buoyancy) condition, weighing less than the volume of water they would displace if fully submerged. To submerge hydrostatically, a ship must have negative buoyancy, either by increasing its own weight or decreasing its displacement of water. To control their displacement and weight, submarines have [ballast tanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballast_tank), which can hold varying amounts of water and air.[^cite_note-Navpers_16166-64]

For general submersion or surfacing, submarines use the main ballast tanks (MBTs), which are ambient pressure tanks, filled with water to submerge or with air to surface. While submerged, MBTs generally remain flooded, which simplifies their design,[^cite_note-Navpers_16166-64] and on many submarines, these tanks are a section of the space between the light hull and the pressure hull. For more precise control of depth, submarines use smaller depth control tanks (DCTs)—also called hard tanks (due to their ability to withstand higher pressure) or trim tanks. These are [variable buoyancy pressure vessels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variable_buoyancy_pressure_vessel), a type of buoyancy control device. The amount of water in depth control tanks can be adjusted to hydrostatically change depth or to maintain a constant depth as outside conditions (mainly water density) change.[^cite_note-Navpers_16166-64] Depth control tanks may be located either near the submarine's [center of gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_gravity), to minimise the effect on trim, or separated along the length of the hull so they can also be used to adjust static trim by transfer of water between them.

When submerged, the water pressure on a submarine's hull can reach 4 [MPa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal_\(unit\)) (580 [psi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound_per_square_inch)) for steel submarines and up to 10 MPa (1,500 psi) for [titanium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titanium) submarines like [*K-278 Komsomolets*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_submarine_K-278_Komsomolets), while interior pressure remains relatively unchanged. This difference results in hull compression, which decreases displacement. Water density also marginally increases with depth, as the [salinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salinity) and pressure are higher.[^cite_note-nave-65] This change in density incompletely compensates for hull compression, so buoyancy decreases as depth increases. A submerged submarine is in an unstable equilibrium, having a tendency to either sink or float to the surface. Keeping a constant depth requires continual operation of either the depth control tanks or control surfaces.[^cite_note-Physics_Of_Liquids_&_Gases-66][^cite_note-67]

Submarines in a neutral buoyancy condition are not intrinsically trim-stable. To maintain desired longitudinal trim, submarines use forward and aft trim tanks. Pumps move water between the tanks, changing weight distribution and pitching the sub up or down. A similar system may be used to maintain transverse trim.[^cite_note-Navpers_16166-64]

### Control surfaces



[Sail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sail_\(submarine\)) of the French nuclear submarine [*Casabianca*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_submarine_Casabianca_\(S603\)); note the diving planes, [camouflaged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camouflage) masts, periscope, electronic warfare masts, hatch, and [deadlight](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/deadlight).

The hydrostatic effect of variable ballast tanks is not the only way to control the submarine underwater. Hydrodynamic maneuvering is done by several control surfaces, collectively known as [diving planes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diving_plane) or hydroplanes, which can be moved to create hydrodynamic forces when a submarine moves longitudinally at sufficient speed. In the classic cruciform stern configuration, the horizontal stern planes serve the same purpose as the trim tanks, controlling the trim. Most submarines additionally have forward horizontal planes, normally placed on the bow until the 1960s but often on the sail on later designs, where they are closer to the center of gravity and can control depth with less effect on the trim.[^cite_note-68]



Rear view of a model of Swedish submarine [HMS *Sjöormen*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HSwMS_Sj%C3%B6ormen_\(Sor\)), the first production submarine to feature an x-stern

An obvious way to configure the control surfaces at the stern of a submarine is to use vertical planes to control yaw and horizontal planes to control pitch, which gives them the shape of a cross when seen from astern of the vessel. In this configuration, which long remained the dominant one, the horizontal planes are used to control the trim and depth and the vertical planes to control sideways maneuvers, like the rudder of a surface ship.

Alternatively, the rear control surfaces can be combined into what has become known as an X-stern or an X-form rudder.[^cite_note-69] Although less intuitive, such a configuration has turned out to have several advantages over the traditional cruciform arrangement. First, it improves maneuverability, horizontally as well as vertically.[^cite_note-70] Second, the control surfaces are less likely to get damaged when landing on, or departing from, the seabed as well as when mooring and unmooring alongside. Finally, it is safer in that one of the two diagonal lines can counteract the other with respect to vertical as well as horizontal motion if one of them accidentally gets stuck.[^cite_note-71]



[USS *Albacore*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Albacore_\(AGSS-569\)), the first submarine to use an x-rudder in practice, now on display in [Portsmouth, New Hampshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portsmouth,_New_Hampshire)&#x20;

The x-stern was first tried in practice in the early 1960s on the [USS *Albacore*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Albacore_\(AGSS-569\)), an experimental submarine of the US Navy. While the arrangement was found to be advantageous, it was nevertheless not used on US production submarines that followed due to the fact that it requires the use of a computer to manipulate the control surfaces to the desired effect.[^cite_note-72] Instead, the first to use an x-stern in standard operations was the Swedish Navy with its [*Sjöormen* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sj%C3%B6ormen-class_submarine), the lead submarine of which was launched in 1967, before the *Albacore* had even finished her test runs.[^cite_note-73] Since it turned out to work very well in practice, all subsequent classes of Swedish submarines ([*Näcken*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N%C3%A4cken-class_submarine), [*Västergötland*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%A4sterg%C3%B6tland-class_submarine), [*Gotland*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gotland-class_submarine), and [*Blekinge*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blekinge-class_submarine) class) have or will come with an x-rudder.



The x-rudder of [HMS *Neptun*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HSwMS_Neptun_\(Nep\)), a [*Näcken*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N%C3%A4cken-class_submarine) submarine in service with the Swedish Navy 1980–1998, now on display at [Marinmuseum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marinmuseum) in [Karlskrona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karlskrona)&#x20;

The [Kockums shipyard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saab_Kockums) responsible for the design of the x-stern on Swedish submarines eventually exported it to Australia with the [*Collins* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collins-class_submarine) as well as to Japan with the [*Sōryū* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C5%8Dry%C5%AB-class_submarine). With the introduction of the [type 212](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_212_submarine), the German and Italian Navies came to feature it as well. The US Navy with its [*Columbia* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia-class_submarine), the British Navy with its [*Dreadnought* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dreadnought-class_submarine), and the French Navy with its [*Barracuda* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barracuda-class_submarine_\(France\)) are all about to join the x-stern family. Hence, as judged by the situation in the early 2020s, the x-stern is about to become the dominant technology.

When a submarine performs an emergency surfacing, all depth and trim control methods are used simultaneously, together with propelling the boat upwards. Such surfacing is very quick, so the vessel may even partially jump out of the water, potentially damaging submarine systems.

#### Overview



The [US Navy](20518076.md) [*Los Angeles*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Angeles-class_submarine) [USS *Greeneville*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Greeneville) in dry dock, showing cigar-shaped hull

Modern submarines are cigar-shaped. This design, also used in very early submarines, is sometimes called a "[teardrop hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teardrop_hull)". It reduces hydrodynamic [drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drag_\(physics\)) when the sub is submerged, but decreases the sea-keeping capabilities and increases drag while surfaced. Since the limitations of the propulsion systems of early submarines forced them to operate surfaced most of the time, their hull designs were a compromise. Because of the slow submerged speeds of those subs, usually well below 10 [kt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knot_\(unit\)) (18 km/h), the increased drag for underwater travel was acceptable. Late in World War II, when technology allowed faster and longer submerged operation and increased aircraft surveillance forced submarines to stay submerged, hull designs became teardrop shaped again to reduce drag and noise. [USS *Albacore* (AGSS-569)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Albacore_\(AGSS-569\)) was a unique research submarine that pioneered the American version of the teardrop hull form (sometimes referred to as an "Albacore hull") of modern submarines. On modern military submarines the outer hull is covered with a layer of sound-absorbing rubber, or [anechoic plating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anechoic_tile), to reduce detection.

The occupied pressure hulls of deep-diving submarines such as [DSV *Alvin*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DSV_Alvin) are spherical instead of cylindrical. This allows a more even distribution of stress and efficient use of materials to withstand external pressure as it gives the most internal volume for structural weight and is the most efficient shape to avoid buckling instability in compression. A frame is usually affixed to the outside of the pressure hull, providing attachment for ballast and trim systems, scientific instrumentation, battery packs, [syntactic flotation foam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntactic_foam), and lighting.

A raised tower on top of a standard submarine accommodates the [periscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periscope) and electronics masts, which can include radio, [radar](25676.md), [electronic warfare](82272.md), and other systems. It might also include a snorkel mast. In many early classes of submarines (see history), the control room, or "conn", was located inside this tower, which was known as the "[conning tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conning_tower)". Since then, the conn has been located within the hull of the submarine, and the tower is now called the ["sail" or "fin"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sail_\(submarine\)). The conn is distinct from the "bridge", a small open platform in the top of the sail, used for observation during surface operation.

"Bathtubs" are related to conning towers but are used on smaller submarines. The bathtub is a metal cylinder surrounding the hatch that prevents waves from breaking directly into the cabin. It is needed because surfaced submarines have limited [freeboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeboard_\(nautical\)), that is, they lie low in the water. Bathtubs help prevent swamping the vessel.

#### Single and double hulls



[*U-995*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_submarine_U-995), Type VIIC/41 U-boat of World War II, showing the ship-like lines of the outer hull for surface travel, blended into the cylindrical pressure hull structure

Modern submarines and submersibles usually have, as did the earliest models, a single hull. Large submarines generally have an additional hull or hull sections outside. This external hull, which actually forms the shape of submarine, is called the outer hull (*[casing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casing_\(submarine\))* in the Royal Navy) or [light hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_hull), as it does not have to withstand a pressure difference. Inside the outer hull there is a strong hull, or [pressure hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_hull), which withstands sea pressure and has normal atmospheric pressure inside.

As early as World War I, it was realized that the optimal shape for withstanding pressure conflicted with the optimal shape for seakeeping and minimal drag at the surface, and construction difficulties further complicated the problem. This was solved either by a compromise shape, or by using two layered hulls: the internal strength hull for withstanding pressure, and an external fairing for hydrodynamic shape. Until the end of World War II, most submarines had an additional partial casing on the top, bow and stern, built of thinner metal, which was flooded when submerged. Germany went further with the [Type XXI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_XXI), a general predecessor of modern submarines, in which the pressure hull was fully enclosed inside the light hull, but optimized for submerged navigation, unlike earlier designs that were optimized for surface operation.



[Type XXI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_XXI) U-boat, late World War II, with pressure hull almost fully enclosed inside the light hull

After World War II, approaches split. The Soviet Union changed its designs, basing them on German developments. All post-World War II heavy Soviet and Russian submarines are built with a [double hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_hull) structure. American and most other Western submarines switched to a primarily single-hull approach. They still have light hull sections in the bow and stern, which house main ballast tanks and provide a hydrodynamically optimized shape, but the main cylindrical hull section has only a single plating layer. Double hulls are being considered for future submarines in the United States to improve payload capacity, stealth and range.[^cite_note-74]

#### Pressure hull



In 1960, [Jacques Piccard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Piccard) and [Don Walsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Don_Walsh) were the first people to explore the [deepest part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Challenger_Deep) of the world's [ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean), and the deepest location on the surface of the Earth's crust, in the [Bathyscaphe *Trieste*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trieste_\(bathyscaphe\)) designed by [Auguste Piccard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auguste_Piccard).

See also: [Pressure hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_hull)

The pressure hull is generally constructed of thick high-strength steel with a complex structure and high strength reserve, and is separated by watertight [bulkheads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulkhead_\(partition\)) into several [compartments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compartmentalization_\(fire_protection\)). There are also examples of more than two hulls in a submarine, like the [Typhoon class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhoon-class_submarine), which has two main pressure hulls and three smaller ones for control room, torpedoes and steering gear, with the missile launch system between the main hulls, all surrounded and supported by the outer light hydrodynamic hull. When submerged the pressure hull provides most of the buoyancy for the whole vessel.

The [dive depth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_depth_ratings) cannot be increased easily. Simply making the hull thicker increases the structural weight and requires reduction of onboard equipment weight, and increasing the diameter requires a proportional increase in thickness for the same material and architecture, ultimately resulting in a pressure hull that does not have sufficient buoyancy to support its own weight, as in a [bathyscaphe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bathyscaphe). This is acceptable for civilian research submersibles, but not military submarines, which need to carry a large equipment, crew, and weapons load to fulfill their function. Construction materials with greater [specific strength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_strength) and [specific modulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_modulus) are needed.

WWI submarines had hulls of [carbon steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_steel), with a 100-metre (330 ft) maximum depth. During WWII, high-strength [alloyed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alloy) steel was introduced, allowing 200-metre (660 ft) depths. High-strength alloy steel remains the primary material for submarines today, with 250–400-metre (820–1,310 ft) depths, which cannot be exceeded on a military submarine without design compromises. To exceed that limit, a few submarines were built with [titanium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titanium) hulls. Titanium alloys can be stronger than steel, lighter, and most importantly, have higher immersed [specific strength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_strength) and [specific modulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_modulus). Titanium is also not [ferromagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetism), important for stealth. Titanium submarines were built by the Soviet Union, which developed specialized high-strength alloys. It has produced several types of titanium submarines. Titanium alloys allow a major increase in depth, but other systems must be redesigned to cope, so test depth was limited to 1,000 metres (3,300 ft) for the [Soviet submarine *K-278 Komsomolets*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_submarine_K-278_Komsomolets), the deepest-diving combat submarine, though continuous operation at such depths would produce excessive stress on many submarine systems. Titanium does not flex as readily as steel, and may become brittle after many dive cycles. Despite its benefits, the high cost of titanium construction led to the abandonment of titanium submarine construction as the Cold War ended. Deep-diving civilian submarines have used thick [acrylic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poly\(methyl_methacrylate\)) pressure hulls. Although the specific strength and specific modulus of acrylic are not very high, the density is only 1.18g/cm<sup>3</sup>, so it is only very slightly denser than water, and the buoyancy penalty of increased thickness is correspondingly low.

The deepest [deep-submergence vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep-submergence_vehicle) (DSV) to date is [*Trieste*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bathyscaphe_Trieste). On 5 October 1959, *Trieste* departed San Diego for [Guam](11974.md) aboard the freighter *Santa Maria* to participate in *[Project Nekton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Nekton)*, a series of very deep dives in the [Mariana Trench](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariana_Trench). On 23 January 1960, *Trieste* reached the ocean floor in the Challenger Deep (the deepest southern part of the Mariana Trench), carrying [Jacques Piccard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Piccard) (son of Auguste) and Lieutenant [Don Walsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Don_Walsh), USN.[^cite_note-75] This was the first time a vessel, crewed or uncrewed, had reached the deepest point in the Earth's oceans. The onboard systems indicated a depth of 11,521 metres (37,799 ft), although this was later revised to 10,916 metres (35,814 ft) and more accurate measurements made in 1995 have found the Challenger Deep slightly shallower, at 10,911 metres (35,797 ft).

Building a pressure hull is difficult, as it must withstand pressures at its required diving depth. When the hull is perfectly round in cross-section, the pressure is evenly distributed, and causes only hull compression. If the shape is not perfect, the hull deflects more in some places and [buckling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckling) instability is the usual [failure mode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Failure_mode). Inevitable minor deviations are resisted by stiffener rings, but even a one-inch (25 mm) deviation from roundness results in over 30 percent decrease of maximal hydrostatic load and consequently dive depth.[^cite_note-76] The hull must therefore be constructed with high precision. All hull parts must be welded without defects, and all joints are checked multiple times with different methods, contributing to the high cost of modern submarines. (For example, each [*Virginia*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia-class_submarine) attack submarine costs US\$2.6 [billion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1000000000_\(number\)), over US\$200,000 per [ton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_ton) of displacement.)

### Propulsion

Further information: [Marine propulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_propulsion), [Air-independent propulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air-independent_propulsion), [Nuclear marine propulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_marine_propulsion), and [Nuclear submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_submarine)



[HMCS *Windsor*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMCS_Windsor), a [Royal Canadian Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Canadian_Navy) [*Victoria*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria-class_submarine) diesel–electric hunter-killer submarine

The first submarines were propelled by humans. The first mechanically driven submarine was the 1863 French [*Plongeur*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_submarine_Plongeur), which used compressed air for propulsion. Anaerobic propulsion was first employed by the Spanish *[Ictineo II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ictineo_II)* in 1864, which used a solution of [zinc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinc), [manganese dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manganese_dioxide), and [potassium chlorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium_chlorate) to generate sufficient heat to power a steam engine, while also providing [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen) for the crew. A similar system was not employed again until 1940 when the German Navy tested a [hydrogen peroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_peroxide)-based system, the [Walter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellmuth_Walter) [turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbine), on the experimental [V-80 submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V-80_submarine) and later on the naval [*U-791*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_submarine_U-791?action=edit\&redlink=1) and [type XVII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Type_XVII_submarine) submarines;[^cite_note-77] the system was further developed for the British [*Explorer*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explorer-class_submarine), completed in 1958.[^cite_note-78]

Until the advent of [nuclear marine propulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_marine_propulsion), most 20th-century submarines used [electric motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_motor) and batteries for running underwater and [combustion engines](41228673.md) on the surface, and for battery recharging. Early submarines used [gasoline](23639.md) (petrol) engines but this quickly gave way to [kerosene](16992.md) (paraffin) and then [diesel](81761.md) engines because of reduced flammability and, with diesel, improved fuel-efficiency and thus also greater range. A combination of diesel and electric propulsion became the norm.

Initially, the combustion engine and the electric motor were in most cases connected to the same shaft so that both could directly drive the propeller. The combustion engine was placed at the front end of the stern section with the electric motor behind it followed by the propeller shaft. The engine was connected to the motor by a clutch and the motor in turn connected to the propeller shaft by another clutch.

With only the rear clutch engaged, the electric motor could drive the propeller, as required for fully submerged operation. With both clutches engaged, the combustion engine could drive the propeller, as was possible when operating on the surface or, at a later stage, when snorkeling. The electric motor would in this case serve as a generator to charge the batteries or, if no charging was needed, be allowed to rotate freely. With only the front clutch engaged, the combustion engine could drive the electric motor as a generator for charging the batteries without simultaneously forcing the propeller to move.

The motor could have multiple armatures on the shaft, which could be electrically coupled in series for slow speed and in parallel for high speed (these connections were called "group down" and "group up", respectively).

#### Diesel–electric transmission



Recharging battery ([JMSDF](2370744.md))

Further information: [Diesel–electric powertrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diesel%E2%80%93electric_powertrain)

While most early submarines used a direct mechanical connection between the combustion engine and the propeller, an alternative solution was considered as well as implemented at a very early stage.[^cite_note-79] That solution consists in first converting the work of the combustion engine into electric energy via a dedicated generator. This energy is then used to drive the propeller via the electric motor and, to the extent required, for charging the batteries. In this configuration, the electric motor is thus responsible for driving the propeller at all times, regardless of whether air is available so that the combustion engine can also be used or not.

Among the pioneers of this alternative solution was the very first submarine of the [Swedish Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Navy), [HSwMS *Hajen*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HSwMS_Hajen_\(1904\)?action=edit\&redlink=1) (later renamed *Ub no 1*), launched in 1904. While its design was generally inspired by the first submarine commissioned by the US Navy, [USS *Holland*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Holland_\(SS-1\)), it deviated from the latter in at least three significant ways: by adding a periscope, by replacing the gasoline engine by a semidiesel engine (a [hot-bulb engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hot-bulb_engine) primarily meant to be fueled by kerosene, later replaced by a true diesel engine) and by severing the mechanical link between the combustion engine and the propeller by instead letting the former drive a dedicated generator.[^cite_note-80] By so doing, it took three significant steps toward what was eventually to become the dominant technology for conventional (i.e., non-nuclear) submarines.



One of the first submarines with diesel–electric transmission, HMS *Hajen*, on display outside [Marinmuseum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marinmuseum) in [Karlskrona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karlskrona)&#x20;

In the following years, the Swedish Navy added another seven submarines in three different classes (*[Undervattensbåten No 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undervattensb%C3%A5ten_No_2-class_submarine)*, *[Laxen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laxen-class_submarine)*, and [*Abborren* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abborren-class_submarine)) using the same propulsion technology but fitted with true diesel engines rather than semidiesels from the outset.[^cite_note-81] Since by that time, the technology was usually based on the diesel engine rather than some other type of combustion engine, it eventually came to be known as [diesel–electric transmission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diesel%E2%80%93electric_transmission).

Like many other early submarines, those initially designed in Sweden were quite small (less than 200 tonnes) and thus confined to littoral operation. When the Swedish Navy wanted to add larger vessels, capable of operating further from the shore, their designs were purchased from companies abroad that already had the required experience: first Italian ([Fiat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiat_S.p.A.)-[Laurenti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cesare_Laurenti_\(engineer\))) and later German ([A.G. Weser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AG_Weser) and [IvS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NV_Ingenieurskantoor_voor_Scheepsbouw)).[^cite_note-82] As a side-effect, the diesel–electric transmission was temporarily abandoned.

However, diesel–electric transmission was immediately reintroduced when Sweden began designing its own submarines again in the mid-1930s. From that point onwards, it has been consistently used for all new classes of Swedish submarines, albeit supplemented by [air-independent propulsion (AIP)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air-independent_propulsion) as provided by [Stirling engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stirling_engine) beginning with [HMS *Näcken*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HSwMS_N%C3%A4cken_\(N%C3%A4k\)) in 1988.[^cite_note-83]



Two widely different generations of Swedish submarines but both with diesel–electric transmission: [HSwMS *Hajen*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HSwMS_Hajen_\(1904\)?action=edit\&redlink=1), in service 1905–1922, and [HMS *Neptun*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HSwMS_Neptun_\(Nep\)), in service 1980–1998

Another early adopter of diesel–electric transmission was the [US Navy](20518076.md), whose Bureau of Engineering proposed its use in 1928. It was subsequently tried in the [S-class submarines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_S-class_submarine) [*S-3*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_S-3), [*S-6*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_S-6), and [*S-7*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_S-7) before being put into production with the [*Porpoise* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Porpoise-class_submarine) of the 1930s. From that point onwards, it continued to be used on most US conventional submarines.[^cite_note-Book1-84]

Apart from the British [U-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_U-class_submarine) and some submarines of the Imperial Japanese Navy that used separate diesel generators for low speed running, few navies other than those of Sweden and the US made much use of diesel–electric transmission before 1945.[^cite_note-Book1-84] After World War II, by contrast, it gradually became the dominant mode of propulsion for conventional submarines. However, its adoption was not always swift. Notably, the Soviet Navy did not introduce diesel–electric transmission on its conventional submarines until 1980 with its [*Paltus* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilo-class_submarine).[^cite_note-85]

If diesel–electric transmission had only brought advantages and no disadvantages in comparison with a system that mechanically connects the diesel engine to the propeller, it would undoubtedly have become dominant much earlier. The disadvantages include the following:[^cite_note-electrotechnical-officer.com-86][^cite_note-diesel_eletric_drives_guideline-87]

- It entails a loss of fuel-efficiency as well as power by converting the output of the diesel engine into electricity. While both generators and electric motors are known to be very efficient, their efficiency nevertheless falls short of 100 percent.

- It requires an additional component in the form of a dedicated generator. Since the electric motor is always used to drive the propeller it can no longer step in to take on generator service as well.

- It does not allow the diesel engine and the electrical motor to join forces by simultaneously driving the propeller mechanically for maximum speed when the submarine is surfaced or snorkeling. This may, however, be of little practical importance inasmuch as the option it prevents is one that would leave the submarine at a risk of having to dive with its batteries at least partly depleted.

The reason why diesel–electric transmission has become the dominant alternative in spite of these disadvantages is of course that it also comes with many advantages and that, on balance, these have eventually been found to be more important. The advantages include the following:[^cite_note-electrotechnical-officer.com-86][^cite_note-diesel_eletric_drives_guideline-87]

- It reduces external noise by severing the direct and rigid mechanical link between the relatively noisy diesel engine(s) on the one hand and the propeller shaft(s) and hull on the other. With [stealth](1958969.md) being of paramount importance to submarines, this is a very significant advantage.

- It increases the [readiness to dive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crash_dive), which is of course of vital importance for a submarine. The only thing required from a propulsion point of view is to shut down the diesel(s).

- It makes the speed of the diesel engine(s) temporarily independent of the speed of the submarine. This in turn often makes it possible to run the diesel(s) at close to optimal speed from a fuel-efficiency as well as durability point of view. It also makes it possible to reduce the time spent surfaced or snorkeling by running the diesel(s) at maximum speed without affecting the speed of the submarine itself.

- It eliminates the clutches otherwise required to connect the diesel engine, the electric motor, and the propeller shaft. This in turn saves space, increases reliability and reduces maintenance costs.

- It increases flexibility with regard to how the driveline components are configured, positioned, and maintained. For example, the diesel no longer has to be aligned with the electric motor and propeller shaft, two diesels can be used to power a single propeller (or vice versa), and one diesel can be turned off for maintenance as long as a second is available to provide the required amount of electricity.

- It facilitates the integration of additional primary sources of energy, beside the diesel engine(s), such as various kinds of [air-independent power (AIP)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air-independent_propulsion) systems. With one or more electric motors always driving the propeller(s), such systems can easily be introduced as yet another source of electric energy in addition to the diesel engine(s) and the batteries.

#### Snorkel

Main article: [Submarine snorkel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_snorkel)



Head of the snorkel mast from German [type XXI submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_XXI_submarine) [*U-3503*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_submarine_U-3503), scuttled outside [Gothenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothenburg) on 8 May 1945 but raised by the Swedish Navy and carefully studied for the purpose of improving future Swedish submarine designs

During World War II the Germans experimented with the idea of the *schnorchel* (snorkel) from captured Dutch submarines but did not see the need for them until rather late in the war. The *schnorchel* is a retractable pipe that supplies air to the diesel engines while submerged at [periscope depth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periscope_depth), allowing the boat to cruise and recharge its batteries while maintaining a degree of stealth.

Especially as first implemented however, it turned out to be far from a perfect solution. There were problems with the device's valve sticking shut or closing as it dunked in rough weather. Since the system used the entire pressure hull as a buffer, the diesels would instantaneously suck huge volumes of air from the boat's compartments, and the crew often suffered painful ear injuries. Speed was limited to 8 knots (15 km/h), lest the device snap from stress. The *schnorchel* also created noise that made the boat easier to detect with sonar, yet more difficult for the on-board sonar to detect signals from other vessels. Finally, allied radar eventually became sufficiently advanced that the *schnorchel* mast could be detected beyond visual range.[^cite_note-88]

In clear weather, diesel exhausts can be seen on the surface to a distance of about three miles,[^cite_note-89] while "periscope feather" (the wave created by the snorkel or periscope moving through the water) is visible from far off in calm sea conditions. Modern radar is also capable of detecting a snorkel in calm sea conditions.[^cite_note-90]



[USS *U-3008*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_submarine_U-3008) (former German submarine *U-3008*) with her snorkel masts raised at Portsmouth Naval Shipyard, Kittery, Maine

The problem of the diesels causing a vacuum in the submarine when the head valve is submerged still exists in later model diesel submarines but is mitigated by high-vacuum cut-off sensors that shut down the engines when the vacuum in the ship reaches a pre-set point. Modern snorkel induction masts have a fail-safe design using [compressed air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compressed_air), controlled by a simple electrical circuit, to hold the "head valve" open against the pull of a powerful spring. Seawater washing over the mast shorts out exposed electrodes on top, breaking the control, and shutting the "head valve" while it is submerged. US submarines did not adopt the use of snorkels until after WWII.[^cite_note-91]

#### Air-independent propulsion

Main article: [Air-independent propulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air-independent_propulsion)



[German Type XXI submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Type_XXI_submarine)&#x20;



American X-1 Midget Submarine

During World War II, [German Type XXI submarines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Type_XXI_submarine) (also known as "*Elektroboote*") were the first submarines designed to operate submerged for extended periods. Initially they were to carry hydrogen peroxide for long-term, fast air-independent propulsion, but were ultimately built with very large batteries instead. At the end of the War, the [British](31717.md) and Soviets experimented with hydrogen peroxide/kerosene (paraffin) engines that could run surfaced and submerged. The results were not encouraging. Though the Soviet Union deployed a class of submarines with this engine type (codenamed [Quebec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quebec-class_submarine) by NATO), they were considered unsuccessful.

The United States also used hydrogen peroxide in an experimental [midget submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midget_submarine), [X-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_X-1). It was originally powered by a hydrogen peroxide/diesel engine and battery system until an explosion of her hydrogen peroxide supply on 20 May 1957. X-1 was later converted to use diesel–electric drive.[^cite_note-92]

Today several navies use air-independent propulsion. Notably [Sweden](5058739.md) uses [Stirling technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stirling_engine) on the [*Gotland*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gotland-class_submarine) and [*Södermanland*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%B6dermanland-class_submarine) submarines. The Stirling engine is heated by burning diesel fuel with [liquid oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_oxygen) from [cryogenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryogenic) tanks. A newer development in air-independent propulsion is [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) [fuel cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_cell), first used on the [German](11867.md) [Type 212 submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_212_submarine), with nine 34 kW or two 120 kW cells. Fuel cells are also used in the new [Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Navy) [S-80-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-80-class_submarine) submarines although with the fuel stored as ethanol and then converted into hydrogen before use.[^cite_note-93]

One new technology that is being introduced starting with the Japanese Navy's eleventh [*Sōryū*-class submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C5%8Dry%C5%AB-class_submarine) (JS *Ōryū*) is a more modern battery, the [lithium-ion battery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium-ion_battery). These batteries have about double the electric storage of traditional batteries, and by changing out the lead-acid batteries in their normal storage areas plus filling up the large hull space normally devoted to [AIP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air-independent_propulsion) engine and fuel tanks with many tons of lithium-ion batteries, modern submarines can actually return to a "pure" diesel–electric configuration yet have the added underwater range and power normally associated with AIP equipped submarines.

#### Nuclear power

Main articles: [Nuclear submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_submarine) and [Nuclear marine propulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_marine_propulsion)



Battery well containing 126 cells on [USS *Nautilus*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Nautilus_\(SSN-571\)), the first nuclear-powered submarine

Steam power was resurrected in the 1950s with a nuclear-powered steam turbine driving a generator. By eliminating the need for atmospheric oxygen, the time that a submarine could remain submerged was limited only by its food stores, as breathing air was recycled and fresh water [distilled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distillation) from seawater. More importantly, a nuclear submarine has unlimited range at top speed. This allows it to travel from its operating base to the combat zone in a much shorter time and makes it a far more difficult target for most anti-submarine weapons. Nuclear-powered submarines have a relatively small battery and diesel engine/generator powerplant for emergency use if the reactors must be shut down.

Nuclear power is now used in all large submarines, but due to the high cost and large size of nuclear reactors, smaller submarines still use diesel–electric propulsion. The ratio of larger to smaller submarines depends on strategic needs. The US Navy, [French Navy](383256.md), and the British [Royal Navy](26061.md) operate only [nuclear submarines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_submarine),[^cite_note-Submarine_Warfare-94][^cite_note-95] which is explained by the need for distant operations. Other major operators rely on a mix of nuclear submarines for strategic purposes and diesel–electric submarines for defense. Most fleets have no nuclear submarines, due to the limited availability of nuclear power and submarine technology.

Diesel–electric submarines have a stealth advantage over their nuclear counterparts. Nuclear submarines generate noise from coolant pumps and turbo-machinery needed to operate the reactor, even at low power levels.[^cite_note-96][^cite_note-97] Some nuclear submarines such as the American [*Ohio* class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio-class_submarine) can operate with their reactor coolant pumps secured, making them quieter than electric subs. A conventional submarine operating on batteries is almost completely silent, the only noise coming from the shaft bearings, propeller, and flow noise around the hull, all of which stops when the sub hovers in mid-water to listen, leaving only the noise from crew activity. Commercial submarines usually rely only on batteries, since they operate in conjunction with a mother ship.

Several [serious nuclear and radiation accidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_and_radiation_accidents_by_death_toll) have involved nuclear submarine mishaps.[^cite_note-johnston2007-98][^cite_note-timenuke-99] The [Soviet submarine *K-19*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_submarine_K-19) reactor accident in 1961 resulted in 8 deaths and more than 30 other people were over-exposed to radiation.[^cite_note-rad-100] The [Soviet submarine *K-27*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_submarine_K-27) reactor accident in 1968 resulted in 9 fatalities and 83 other injuries.[^cite_note-johnston2007-98] The [Soviet submarine *K-431*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_submarine_K-431) accident in 1985 resulted in 10 fatalities and 49 other radiation injuries.[^cite_note-timenuke-99]

#### Alternative

Oil-fired steam turbines powered the British [K-class submarines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_K-class_submarine), built during [World War I](4764461.md) and later, to give them the surface speed to keep up with the battle fleet. The K-class subs were not very successful, however.

Toward the end of the 20th century, some submarines—such as the British *Vanguard* class—began to be fitted with [pump-jet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pump-jet) propulsors instead of propellers. Though these are heavier, more expensive, and less efficient than a propeller, they are significantly quieter, providing an important tactical advantage.

### Armament



The forward torpedo tubes in HMS *Ocelot*



The torpedo room of *[Vesikko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finnish_submarine_Vesikko)*

The success of the submarine is inextricably linked to the development of the [torpedo](31424.md), invented by [Robert Whitehead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Whitehead_\(engineer\)) in 1866. His invention (essentially the same now as it was 140 years ago), allowed the submarine make the leap from novelty to a weapon of war. Prior to the development and miniaturization of sonar sensitive enough to track a submerged submarine, attacks were exclusively restricted to ships and submarines operating near or at the surface. Targeting of unguided torpedoes was initially done by eye, but by World War II [analog targeting computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torpedo_Data_Computer) began to proliferate, being able to calculate basic firing solutions. Nonetheless, multiple "straight-running" torpedoes could be required to ensure a target was hit. With at most 20 to 25 torpedoes stored on board, the number of attacks a submarine could make was limited. To increase [combat endurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combat_endurance) starting in World War I submarines also functioned as submersible gunboats, using their [deck guns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deck_gun) against unarmed targets, and diving to escape and engage enemy warships. The initial importance of these deck guns encouraged the development of the unsuccessful [Submarine Cruiser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cruiser_submarine) such as the French [*Surcouf*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_submarine_Surcouf) and the [Royal Navy](26061.md)'s [*X1*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_X1) and [M-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_M-class_submarine) submarines. With the arrival of [anti-submarine warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-submarine_warfare) (ASW) aircraft, guns became more for defense than attack. A more practical method of increasing combat endurance was the external torpedo tube, loaded only in port.

The ability of submarines to approach enemy harbours covertly led to their use as [minelayers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minelayer). Minelaying submarines of World War I and World War II were specially built for that purpose. Modern submarine-laid [mines](22102.md), such as the British Mark 5 [Stonefish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stonefish_\(mine\)) and Mark 6 Sea Urchin, can be deployed from a submarine's torpedo tubes.

After World War II, both the US and the USSR experimented with [submarine-launched cruise missiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine-launched_cruise_missile) such as the [SSM-N-8 Regulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SSM-N-8_Regulus) and [P-5 Pyatyorka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-5_Pyatyorka). Such missiles required the submarine to surface to fire its missiles. They were the forerunners of modern submarine-launched cruise missiles, which can be fired from the torpedo tubes of submerged submarines, for example, the US [BGM-109 Tomahawk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomahawk_\(missile_family\)) and Russian [RPK-2 Viyuga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RPK-2_Viyuga) and versions of surface-to-surface [anti-ship missiles](140725.md) such as the [Exocet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exocet) and [Harpoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_Harpoon), encapsulated for submarine launch. Ballistic missiles can also be fired from a submarine's torpedo tubes, for example, missiles such as the anti-submarine [SUBROC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SUBROC). With internal volume as limited as ever and the desire to carry heavier warloads, the idea of the external launch tube was revived, usually for encapsulated missiles, with such tubes being placed between the internal pressure and outer streamlined hulls. Guided torpedoes also proliferated extensively during and after World War II, even further increasing the combat endurance and lethality of submarines and allowing them to engage other submarines at depth (with the latter now being one of the primary missions of the modern [attack submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attack_submarine)).

The strategic mission of the SSM-N-8 and the P-5 was taken up by [submarine-launched ballistic missile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine-launched_ballistic_missile) beginning with the US Navy's [Polaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UGM-27_Polaris) missile, and subsequently the [Poseidon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UGM-73_Poseidon) and [Trident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trident_\(missile\)) missiles.

Germany is working on the torpedo tube-launched short-range [IDAS missile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IDAS_\(missile\)), which can be used against ASW helicopters, as well as surface ships and coastal targets.

### Sensors

Main article: [Sonar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonar)

A submarine can have a variety of sensors, depending on its missions. Modern military submarines rely almost entirely on a suite of passive and active [sonars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonar) to locate targets. Active sonar relies on an audible "ping" to generate echoes to reveal objects around the submarine. Active systems are rarely used, as doing so reveals the sub's presence. Passive sonar is a set of sensitive hydrophones set into the hull or trailed in a towed array, normally trailing several hundred feet behind the sub. The towed array is the mainstay of NATO submarine detection systems, as it reduces the flow noise heard by operators. Hull mounted sonar is employed in addition to the towed array, as the towed array can not work in shallow depth and during maneuvering. In addition, sonar has a blind spot "through" the submarine, so a system on both the front and back works to eliminate that problem. As the towed array trails behind and below the submarine, it also allows the submarine to have a system both above and below the [thermocline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermocline) at the proper depth; sound passing through the thermocline is distorted resulting in a lower detection range. Global [climate change](5042951.md) and warmer oceans may complicate detecting submarines at depth in most places in the world.[^cite_note-101]

Submarines also carry radar equipment to detect surface ships and aircraft. Submarine captains are more likely to use radar detection gear than active radar to detect targets, as radar can be detected far beyond its own return range, revealing the submarine. Periscopes are rarely used, except for position fixes and to verify a contact's identity.

Civilian submarines, such as the [DSV *Alvin*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DSV_Alvin) or the [Russian *Mir* submersibles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIR_\(submersible\)), rely on small active sonar sets and viewing ports to navigate. The human eye cannot detect sunlight below about 300 feet (91 m) underwater, so high intensity lights are used to illuminate the viewing area.

### Navigation

Main article: [Submarine navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_navigation)



The larger search [periscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periscope), and the smaller, less detectable attack periscope on HMS *Ocelot*

Early submarines had few navigation aids, but modern subs have a variety of navigation systems. Modern military submarines use an [inertial guidance system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_guidance_system) for navigation while submerged, but drift error unavoidably builds over time. To counter this, the crew occasionally uses the [Global Positioning System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Positioning_System) to obtain an accurate position. The [periscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periscope)—a retractable tube with a [prism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prism_\(optics\)) system that provides a view of the surface—is only used occasionally in modern submarines, since the visibility range is short. The [*Virginia*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia-class_submarine) and [*Astute*-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astute-class_submarine) submarines use [photonics masts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photonics_mast) rather than hull-penetrating optical periscopes. These masts must still be deployed above the surface, and use electronic sensors for visible light, infrared, laser range-finding, and electromagnetic surveillance. One benefit to hoisting the mast above the surface is that while the mast is above the water the entire sub is still below the water and is much harder to detect visually or by radar.

### Communication

Main article: [Communication with submarines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication_with_submarines)

Military submarines use several systems to communicate with distant command centers or other ships. One is [VLF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VLF) (very low frequency) radio, which can reach a submarine either on the surface or submerged to a fairly shallow depth, usually less than 250 feet (76 m). [ELF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extremely_low_frequency) (extremely low frequency) can reach a submarine at greater depths, but has a very low bandwidth and is generally used to call a submerged sub to a shallower depth where VLF signals can reach. A submarine also has the option of floating a long, buoyant wire antenna to a shallower depth, allowing VLF transmissions by a deeply submerged boat.

By extending a radio mast, a submarine can also use a "[burst transmission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burst_transmission)" technique. A burst transmission takes only a fraction of a second, minimizing a submarine's risk of detection.

To communicate with other submarines, a system known as Gertrude is used. Gertrude is basically a [sonar telephone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underwater_telephone). Voice communication from one submarine is transmitted by low power speakers into the water, where it is detected by passive sonars on the receiving submarine. The range of this system is probably very short, and using it radiates sound into the water, which can be heard by the enemy.

Civilian submarines can use similar, albeit less powerful systems to communicate with support ships or other submersibles in the area.

### Life support systems

With [nuclear power](22151.md) or [air-independent propulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air-independent_propulsion), submarines can remain submerged for months at a time. Conventional diesel submarines must periodically resurface or run on [snorkel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_snorkel) to recharge their batteries. Most modern military submarines generate breathing [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen) by [electrolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrolysis) of fresh water (using a device called an "[Electrolytic Oxygen Generator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elektron_\(ISS\)#Elektron)"). Emergency oxygen can be produced by burning [sodium chlorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_chlorate) candles.[^cite_note-Smarter_every_day_251-102] Atmosphere control equipment includes a [Carbon dioxide scrubber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide_scrubber), which uses a spray of [monoethanolamine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amine_gas_treating) (MEA) absorbent to remove the gas from the air, after which the MEA is heated in a boiler to release the CO<sub>2</sub> which is then pumped overboard. Emergency scrubbing can also be done with lithium hydroxide, which is consumable.[^cite_note-Smarter_every_day_251-102] A machine that uses a [catalyst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalyst) to convert [carbon monoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide) into carbon dioxide (removed by the CO<sub>2</sub> scrubber) and bonds [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) produced from the ship's storage battery with oxygen in the atmosphere to produce water, is also used. An atmosphere monitoring system samples the air from different areas of the ship for [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen), oxygen, hydrogen, [R-12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dichlorodifluoromethane) and [R-114](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1,2-Dichlorotetrafluoroethane) refrigerants, carbon dioxide, [carbon monoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide), and other gases.[^cite_note-Smarter_every_day_251-102] Poisonous gases are removed, and oxygen is replenished by use of an oxygen bank located in a main ballast tank. Some heavier submarines have two oxygen bleed stations (forward and aft). The oxygen in the air is sometimes kept a few percent less than atmospheric concentration to reduce fire risk.

Fresh water is produced by either an evaporator or a [reverse osmosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reverse_osmosis) unit. The primary use for fresh water is to provide feedwater for the reactor and steam propulsion plants. It is also available for showers, sinks, cooking and cleaning once propulsion plant needs have been met. Seawater is used to flush toilets, and the resulting ["blackwater"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackwater_\(waste\)) is stored in a sanitary tank until it is blown overboard using pressurized air or pumped overboard by using a special sanitary pump. The blackwater-discharge system requires skill to operate, and isolation valves must be closed before discharge.[^cite_note-Smarter_every_day_256-103] The German [Type VIIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_VII_submarine) boat [*U-1206*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_submarine_U-1206) was lost with casualties because of [human error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_error) while using this system.[^cite_note-104] Water from showers and sinks is stored separately in "[grey water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greywater)" tanks and discharged overboard using drain pumps.

Trash on modern large submarines is usually disposed of using a tube called a Trash Disposal Unit (TDU), where it is compacted into a galvanized steel can. At the bottom of the TDU is a large ball valve. An ice plug is set on top of the ball valve to protect it, the cans atop the ice plug. The top breech door is shut, and the TDU is flooded and equalized with sea pressure, the ball valve is opened and the cans fall out assisted by scrap iron weights in the cans. The TDU is also flushed with seawater to ensure it is completely empty and the ball valve is clear before closing the valve.

## Crew



The interior of a [British E-class submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_E-class_submarine). An officer supervises submerging operations, c. 1914–1918.

A typical nuclear submarine has a crew of over 80; conventional boats typically have fewer than 40. The conditions on a submarine can be difficult because crew members must work in isolation for long periods of time, without family contact, and in cramped conditions.[^cite_note-105] Submarines normally maintain [radio silence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_silence) to avoid detection. Operating a submarine is dangerous, even in peacetime, and many submarines have been lost in accidents.[^cite_note-106]

### Women



[Midshipmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midshipman) learn to pilot [USS *West Virginia*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_West_Virginia_\(SSBN-736\)) (2010)

Most navies prohibited women from serving on submarines, even after they had been permitted to serve on surface warships. The [Royal Norwegian Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Norwegian_Navy) became the first navy to allow women on its submarine crews in 1985. The [Royal Danish Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Danish_Navy) allowed female submariners in 1988.[^cite_note-fesub-107] Others followed suit including the [Swedish Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Navy) (1989),[^cite_note-swedfem-108] the [Royal Australian Navy](26327.md) (1998), the [Spanish Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Navy) (1999),[^cite_note-109][^cite_note-110] the [German Navy](12960.md) (2001) and the [Canadian Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Canadian_Navy) (2002). In 1995, [Solveig Krey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solveig_Krey) of the Royal Norwegian Navy became the first female officer to assume command on a military submarine, [HNoMS *Kobben*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kobben-class_submarine).[^cite_note-111]

On 8 December 2011, British [Defence Secretary](302332.md) [Philip Hammond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Hammond) announced that the [UK](31717.md)'s ban on women in submarines was to be lifted from 2013.[^cite_note-Guardian_women-112] Previously there were fears that women were more at risk from a build-up of carbon dioxide in the submarine. But a study showed no medical reason to exclude women, though pregnant women would still be excluded.[^cite_note-Guardian_women-112] Similar dangers to the pregnant woman and her fetus barred women from submarine service in Sweden in 1983, when all other positions were made available for them in the Swedish Navy. Today, pregnant women are still not allowed to serve on submarines in Sweden. However, the policymakers thought that it was discriminatory with a general ban and demanded that women should be tried on their individual merits and have their suitability evaluated and compared to other candidates. Further, they noted that a woman complying with such high demands is unlikely to become pregnant.[^cite_note-swedfem-108] In May 2014, three women became the RN's first female submariners.[^cite_note-113]

Women have served on US Navy surface ships since 1993, and as of 2011–2012, began serving on submarines for the first time. Until presently, the Navy allowed only three exceptions to women being on board military submarines: female civilian technicians for a few days at most, women [midshipmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midshipman) on an overnight during summer training for Navy [ROTC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ROTC) and [Naval Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Naval_Academy), and family members for one-day dependent cruises.[^cite_note-114] In 2009, senior officials, including then-Secretary of the Navy [Ray Mabus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Mabus), Joint Chief of Staff Admiral [Michael Mullen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Mullen), and Chief of Naval Operations Admiral [Gary Roughead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Roughead), began the process of finding a way to implement women on submarines.[^cite_note-Women_should_serve-115] The US Navy rescinded its "no women on subs" policy in 2010.[^cite_note-women-on-virginia-116]

Both the US and British navies operate nuclear-powered submarines that deploy for periods of six months or longer. Other navies that permit women to serve on submarines operate conventionally powered submarines, which deploy for much shorter periods—usually only for a few months.[^cite_note-117] Prior to the change by the US, no nation using nuclear submarines permitted women to serve on board.[^cite_note-118]

In 2011, the first class of female submarine officers graduated from Naval Submarine School's Submarine Officer Basic Course (SOBC) at the [Naval Submarine Base New London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_Submarine_Base_New_London).[^cite_note-119] Additionally, more senior ranking and experienced female supply officers from the surface warfare specialty attended SOBC as well, proceeding to fleet Ballistic Missile (SSBN) and Guided Missile (SSGN) submarines along with the new female submarine line officers beginning in late 2011.[^cite_note-120] By late 2011, several women were assigned to the *Ohio*-class ballistic missile submarine [USS *Wyoming*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Wyoming_\(SSBN-742\)).[^cite_note-121] On 15 October 2013, the US Navy announced that two of the smaller *Virginia*-class attack submarines, [USS *Virginia*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Virginia_\(SSN-774\)) and [USS *Minnesota*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Minnesota_\(SSN-783\)), would have female crew-members by January 2015.[^cite_note-women-on-virginia-116]

In 2020, Japan's national naval submarine academy accepted its first female candidate.[^cite_note-122]

### Abandoning the vessel



Submarine Escape Immersion Equipment suit Mk 10



A submarine escape suit with rebreather

In an emergency, submarines can contact other ships to assist in rescue, and pick up the crew when they abandon ship. The crew can use [escape sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escape_set) such as the [Submarine Escape Immersion Equipment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_Escape_Immersion_Equipment) to abandon the submarine via an [escape trunk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escape_trunk), which is a small [airlock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airlock) compartment that provides a route for crew to escape from a downed submarine at ambient pressure in small groups, while minimising the amount of water admitted to the submarine.[^cite_note-NSMRL1205-123] The crew can avoid lung injury from over-expansion of air in the lungs due to the pressure change known as [pulmonary barotrauma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barotrauma#Pulmonary_barotrauma) by maintaining an open airway and exhaling during the ascent.[^cite_note-pmid10642066-124] Following escape from a pressurized submarine, in which the air pressure is higher than atmospheric due to water ingress or other reasons, the crew is at risk of developing [decompression sickness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decompression_sickness) on return to surface pressure.[^cite_note-Weathersby1999-125]

An alternative escape means is via a [deep-submergence rescue vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep-submergence_rescue_vehicle) that can dock onto the disabled submarine, establish a seal around the escape hatch, and transfer personnel at the same pressure as the interior of the submarine. If the submarine has been pressurised the survivors can lock into a [decompression chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decompression_chamber) on the [submarine rescue ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_rescue_ship) and [transfer under pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfer_under_pressure) for safe [surface decompression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_decompression).[^cite_note-NSMRL1021-126]
