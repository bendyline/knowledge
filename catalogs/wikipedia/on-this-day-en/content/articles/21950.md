**Khyber Pakhtunkhwa**,[^cite_note-7] commonly abbreviated **KP** or **KPK** and formerly known as the [North-West Frontier Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North-West_Frontier_Province) (NWFP), is a [province](446017.md) of [Pakistan](23235.md). Located in the [northwestern region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Pakistan) of the country, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa is the fourth largest province of Pakistan by land area and the third-largest province by population. It is bordered by [Balochistan](4687.md) to the south; [Punjab](24751.md), [Islamabad Capital Territory](15294.md), and [Azad Kashmir](2745.md) to the east; and [Gilgit-Baltistan](18807336.md) to the north and northeast. It shares an [international border](203570.md) with [Afghanistan](737.md) to the west. Khyber Pakhtunkhwa has a varied geography of rugged mountain ranges, valleys, rolling foothills, and dense agricultural farms.&#x20;

The history of the present province of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa is characterized by frequent invasions by various empires, largely due to its geographical proximity to the historically important [Khyber Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khyber_Pass).[^cite_note-8] It was the site of the ancient [Gandhara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gandhara), and was historically a [stronghold of Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gandharan_Buddhism). [Islam](6037917.md) became dominant in the region after the 11th-century conquest of the [Hindu Shahi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_Shahi) kingdom by the [Ghaznavids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghaznavids). The predecessor of the present province was constituted in 1901, under the [British Raj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Raj), when the North-West Frontier Province was created by bifurcating the northwestern districts of the erstwhile [Punjab Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punjab_Province_\(British_India\)). In 1969, the princely states of [Chitral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Chitral), [Swat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swat_\(princely_state\)) and [Dir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dir_\(princely_state\)) were merged into the province, while on 24 May 2018, the [FATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federally_Administered_Tribal_Areas) as well as the [PATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provincially_Administered_Tribal_Areas) were merged into Khyber Pakhtunkhwa after the [25th Constitutional Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twenty-fifth_Amendment_to_the_Constitution_of_Pakistan).[^cite_note-9]&#x20;

Although it is colloquially known by a variety of other names, the present name was brought into effect for the North-West Frontier Province in April 2010, following the enactment of the [18th Constitutional Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eighteenth_Amendment_to_the_Constitution_of_Pakistan). While it is the third-largest Pakistani province in terms of both its population and [its economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Khyber_Pakhtunkhwa), it is geographically the smallest.[^cite_note-T\&F-10] Khyber Pakhtunkhwa is a multiethnic province that makes up 16.9% of Pakistan's total population. Numerous ethnolinguistic and ethnic groups reside across different parts of the province.[^cite_note-11]&#x20;

## Etymology

The present name *Khyber Pakhtunkhwa* means the "[Khyber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khyber_Pass) side of the land of the [Pashtuns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pashtuns)",[^cite_note-USDS-12] where the word *Pakhtunkhwa* is variously translated as the "land of Pashtuns",[^cite_note-13] or "Pashtun culture and society".[^cite_note-14] For over a hundred years after its creation as a province of [British Raj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Raj) in 1901, it was known as the North-West Frontier Province until 2010 due to its relative location being in the northwest of the nation.[^cite_note-15][^cite_note-16] After the independence of Pakistan, it continued with the name but a Pashtun political party, [Awami National Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Awami_National_Party) based in the province demanded that the province name be changed to "Pakhtunkhwa".[^cite_note-17] They reasoned that names of other provinces, namely [Punjab](24751.md), [Sindh](28972.md) and [Balochistan](4687.md) denoted their dominant ethnicities but that was not the case for Pashtun people.[^cite_note-Pakhtunkhwadotcom-18][^cite_note-19][^cite_note-20]&#x20;

Although [Pakistan Muslim League (N)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan_Muslim_League_\(N\)) (PMLN) and [Pakistan People's Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan_People%27s_Party) (PPP), the largest political parties in the 1990s, supported the name amendment proposed by ANP, Pakistan Muslim League wanted a neutral name which did not carry an exclusively Pashtun identity in it, as it argued that there were other ethnic communities living in the province, such as the [Hazarewals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazarewals) of the [Hazara region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazara_region), which opposed the proposed name.[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-22][^cite_note-23][^cite_note-24] The NWFP chief minister, [Sardar Mehtab Ahmed Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sardar_Mehtab_Ahmed_Khan), called for a referendum on the issue as a way of determining the name in 1997, but this was rejected by ANP who threatened to withdraw from national politics.[^cite_note-ghazali.net2-25]&#x20;

In early 2010, the process of renaming proceeded and the [Pakistani Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistani_Senate) confirmed the name change to *Khyber Pakhtunkhwa* in the [18th amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eighteenth_Amendment_to_the_Constitution_of_Pakistan) to the [Constitution of Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Pakistan) with unanimous voting on 15 April 2010.[^cite_note-\:3-26] The name change was met with protests by other ethnic minorities, who feared increased dominance by Pashtuns. On 10 April 2010, the [Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Police](21591049.md) acting on the direction of ANP government opened fire at unarmed protesters, killing seven and injuring dozens, and protests against the name change and for [a separate province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazara_Province_Movement) were suppressed through [police violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_brutality).[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28][^cite_note-29][^cite_note-Brulliard-30]&#x20;

The word *Khyber* was introduced in the name as it is the name of [a major pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khyber_Pass) which connects Pakistan to Afghanistan.[^cite_note-khyber.org-31][^cite_note-ghazali.net2-25][^cite_note-Pakhtunkhwadotcom2-32] The province has had various names throughout history.[^cite_note-\:42-33][^cite_note-ghazali.net2-25][^cite_note-\:5-34] The name [Gandhāra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gandhara), of an ancient civilization centered in the present-day province, was also proposed by PMLN, as a neutral name for the province.[^cite_note-\:52-35][^cite_note-36][^cite_note-37] Other names used or proposed for the province include Sarhad, Afghania, Pashtunistan, Pathanistan, Sarhad, Abaseen, Khyber,[^cite_note-\:42-33][^cite_note-\:52-35][^cite_note-RiazKhan-38][^cite_note-Khan/Toosi-39] or a combination of these names, such as Hazara-Pakhtunkhwa and Gandhara-Pakhtunkhwa.[^cite_note-\:0-40][^cite_note-\:1-41][^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43]&#x20;

## History

Main article: [History of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Khyber_Pakhtunkhwa)

### Ancient history

The [Indus Valley Civilisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Valley_Civilisation) (3300 BCE – 1700 BCE) was one of the earliest human civilisations to emerge in the region. Considered a [cradle of civilisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cradle_of_civilization), its decline was followed by migration of [Indo-Iranian peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Iranians) from [Central Asia](6742.md),[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-humshehri.org-45] which gave rise to [Gandhara grave culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gandhara_grave_culture), later developing into the [Gandhara civilisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gandhara_civilisation). Gandhara features prominently in the [Vedic mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic_mythology),[^cite_note-auto12-46][^cite_note-auto3-47][^cite_note-auto22-48] and was known for [its art and sculptures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Buddhist_art), as well as [Buddhist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhists) culture. The people of Gandhara spoke [Gandhari language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gandhari_language).[^cite_note-Rigveda-49][^cite_note-Macdonell1997-50][^cite_note-51] Gandhara was conquered by [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great), who crossed the [Hindu Kush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_Kush) in the spring of 327 BCE and defeated the tribes of [Assakenoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assakenoi) and [Gouraios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gawri_people).[^cite_note-IG148-52][^cite_note-IG149-53]&#x20;



The [*Standing Buddha from Gandhara*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standing_Buddha_from_Gandhara_\(Tokyo\)), a 1st century CE [Gandharan Buddhist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gandharan_Buddhism) representation of the Buddha.

[^cite_note-books.google.com-54]

&#x20;The Macedonian rule did not remain for long, as soon [Chandragupta Maurya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandragupta_Maurya) entered into conflict with [Seleucus I Nicator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucus_I_Nicator), the successor of Alexander in Persia. A short [war between the two emperors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucid%E2%80%93Mauryan_War) was followed by a peace treaty in 302 BCE; Seleucus ceded the lands of [Aria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariana), [Arachosia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arachosia), Gandhara, and [Gedrosia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gedrosia) to Chandragupta in exchange for a matrimonial alliance and 500 elephants.[^cite_note-55] [Ashoka the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashoka), the grandson of Chandragupta, spent his youth as viceroy in the region. He converted to [Buddhism](3267529.md) and made it the official religion of the empire. His [rock-inscriptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edicts_of_Ashoka) are found at [Shahbazgarhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shahbazgarhi) and [Mansehra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mansehra_Rock_Edicts).[^cite_note-IG149-53]



Greco-Buddhist representation of the Buddha, seated to the left of a depiction of [Vajrapani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vajrapani) in the guise of the Hellenic god [Heracles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heracles).

[^cite_note-books.google.com-54]

The decline of Mauryas allowed [Greco-Bactrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Bactrian_Kingdom) to expand into Gandhara under [Demetrius I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demetrius_I_of_Bactria). [Menander I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menander_I) (reigned 155–130 BCE), the greatest of the [Indo-Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Greek_Kingdom), drove the Greco-Bactrians out of Gandhara and beyond the Hindu Kush, becoming king shortly after his victory.[^cite_note-IG149-53]&#x20;

&#x20;The last known Indo-Greek ruler was [Theodamas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodamas), from the [Bajaur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bajaur) area of Gandhara, mentioned on a 1st-century CE signet ring, bearing the Kharoṣṭhī inscription *"Su Theodamasa"* (*Su* was the Greek transliteration of the [Kushan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kushan_Empire) royal title *Shau* "[Shah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shah)" or "King"). The migrating [Indo-Scythian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Scythians) tribes from Central Asia displaced the Indo-Greeks and ruled a kingdom that stretched from Gandhara to [Mathura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathura,_Uttar_Pradesh) from the middle of the 2nd century BCE to the 1st century BCE.[^cite_note-56][^cite_note-57] They were replaced by yet another group of central Asian origins, the [Indo-Parthians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Parthian_Kingdom), who built the Buddhist monastery of [Takht-i-Bahi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takht-i-Bahi). They had [Taxila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxila) in Punjab as their residence, but during their last few years of existence the capital shifted between Kabul and Peshawar.



Ancient [Buddhist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist) [monastery](45856.md) [Takht-i-Bahi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takht-i-Bahi) (a [UNESCO World Heritage Site](44940.md)) constructed by the Indo-Parthians.

&#x20;In the first century CE, the [Yuezhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuezhi) nomads were forced southwards by the nomadic [Xiongnu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xiongnu). One group, known as the [Kushan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kushan_Empire), took the lead, and its chief [Kadphises I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kujula_Kadphises) seized vast territories extending south to the [Kabul](16826.md) valley. Their greatest ruler was [Kanishka the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanishka_the_Great), who had his capital at Peshawar and like Ashoka patronised Buddhism.[^cite_note-IG149-53][^cite_note-IG150-58]



The [Kanishka stupa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanishka_stupa) once kept sacred Buddhist relics in the [Kanishka casket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanishka_casket).

Kushans were followed by various groups of [Huna people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huna_people). The reign of [Alchon Hun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alchon_Hun) ruler [Mihirakula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mihirakula) in the 6th century was particularly disastrous for the Buddhism, as he prosecuted Buddhists and destroyed their monasteries, resulting in the rise of Hinduism in Gandhara.&#x20;

### Medieval history



Horseman on a coin of Spalapati, i.e. the "War-lord" of the [Hindu Shahis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_Shahis). The headgear has been interpreted as a [turban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turban).

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERehman1976187_and_Pl._V_B."the_horseman_is_shown_wearing_a_turban-like_head-gear_with_a_small_globule_on_the_top"-59]

The [Turk Shahis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turk_Shahis) ruled Gandhara until 870, when they were overthrown by the [Hindu Shahis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_Shahis). Of [Brahman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmin) or [Kshatriya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kshatriya) origins,[^cite_note-60] the Hindu Shahis are suggested by some scholars to have originated from [Oddiyana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oddiyana) (Swat).[^cite_note-61][^cite_note-62] Their capital was at Udabandhapura, near the modern village of [Hund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hund_\(village\)).[^cite_note-63][^cite_note-64][^cite_note-65][^cite_note-66] At its zenith, the kingdom stretched from Kabul and Gandhara to western Punjab under [Jayapala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jayapala) (r. 964–1001).[^cite_note-Wynbrandt2009-67] Jayapala fought a series of battles against the invading [Ghaznavids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghaznavids); Ghaznavid ruler [Mahmud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahmud_of_Ghazni) finally annexed Gandhara by defeating Jayapala at the [battle of Peshawar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Peshawar_\(1001\)) in 1001.[^cite_note-Lewis2-68][^cite_note-Ferishta-69] Hindu Shahis continued their struggle for independence but were unsuccessful. They eventually exiled themselves to the Kashmir [Siwalik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siwalik) hills.[^cite_note-Ferishta-69]&#x20;

[Dilazak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dilazak) was the first Pashtun tribe to migrate to [Peshawar Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peshawar_Valley) during the Ghaznavid period. Mahmud of Ghazni and his descendants reigned till 1179, when [Muhammad of Ghor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_of_Ghor) took Peshawar, making it part of his expanding [Ghurid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghurid_Empire).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWynbrandt200952–55-70][^cite_note-\:6-71][^cite_note-\:8-72]&#x20;

Following the disintegration of the Ghurid Empire, the [Delhi Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delhi_Sultanate) rose in [the subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_subcontinent).[^cite_note-sen2-73] Throughout 13th and 14th centuries Peshawar and surrounding areas remained the scene of battles between Delhi sultans and [numerous invading Mongol armies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_invasions_of_India). In the 15th century [Yousafzais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yusufzai) migrated to Peshawar and Swat, displacing Dilazaks and [Swatis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swati_people_\(Pakistan\)).[^cite_note-bosworth-74]&#x20;

### Modern period



The white-marble façade of the [Mohabbat Khan Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohabbat_Khan_Mosque) was founded by Mughal governor Mohabbat Khan in 1630.

In the early 16th-century, Peshawar, Kohat and surrounding areas were annexed by the Mughal emperor [Babur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babur).[^cite_note-Miers2-75][^cite_note-Richards2-76] Babur married [Bibi Mubarika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibi_Mubarika), the daughter of Yousafzai chief. He noted the town of Peshawar as Bagram. His grandson, [Akbar the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akbar_the_Great) carried out administrative reforms in the region in 1580. During the [Mughal period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire), [Hazara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazara_region) was a part of Punjab while much of rest of the present-day province was a part of Kabul Subah, which had provincial capitals at Peshawar and Kabul.[^cite_note-77]&#x20;

Akbar faced rebellion by the Yousafzai tribes, as did his great-grandson [Aurangzeb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurangzeb), when they rose against Mughals in 1667,[^cite_note-Richards2-76] and engaged in pitched-battles with Mughal battalions in Peshawar and [Attock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attock).[^cite_note-Richards2-76] [Afridi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afridi) tribe also rebelled against Aurangzeb in 1670s.[^cite_note-Richards2-76] The Afridis massacred a Mughal battalion in the [Khyber Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khyber_Pass) in 1672 and shut the pass to lucrative trade routes.[^cite_note-richards-78] Following another massacre in the winter of 1673, Mughal armies led by emperor Aurangzeb himself regained control of the entire area in 1674,[^cite_note-Richards2-76] and enticed tribal leaders with various awards in order to pacify tribes.[^cite_note-Richards2-76]&#x20;



[Bala Hissar fort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bala_Hissar,_Peshawar) in Peshawar was used as a royal residence for the [Durrani Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durrani_Empire).

On 18 November 1738, Peshawar was captured by the [Afsharid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afsharid_dynasty) armies during the [Persian invasion of the Mughal Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nader_Shah%27s_invasion_of_the_Mughal_Empire) under [Nader Shah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nader_Shah).[^cite_note-\:8-72][^cite_note-\:6-71] Peshawar was invaded subsequently by [Ahmad Shah Durrani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmad_Shah_Durrani), founder of the [Durrani Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durrani_Empire), in 1748.[^cite_note-79][^cite_note-80] Ahmad Shah expanded his rule over Kashmir, Sindh and much of Punjab.[^cite_note-81][^cite_note-82] His rule was briefly interrupted by an invasion of the [Marathas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maratha_Confederacy), who ruled over Peshawar for eleven months from 1758 till early 1759 when the Durrani rule was re-established.[^cite_note-Schofield,_Victoria_2003_page_47-83] The northern regions of [Kohistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kohistan,_Pakistan) and [Chitral](988583.md), inhabited by [Dardic peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dardic_languages), remained outside the Durrani sphere of influence.&#x20;

Under [Timur Shah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timur_Shah_Durrani), the Mughal practice of using Kabul as a summer capital and Peshawar as a winter capital was reintroduced.[^cite_note-bosworth-74][^cite_note-Hanifi2011-84] The [Bala Hissar Fort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bala_Hissar,_Peshawar) served as the residence of Durrani kings during their winter stay in Peshawar.[^cite_note-UNESCO-85] In 1809, the British sent an emissary to the court of [Shah Shujah Durrani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shah_Shujah_Durrani) in Peshawar, marking the first diplomatic meeting between the British and Durrani rulers.[^cite_note-UNESCO-85]&#x20;

[Ranjit Singh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranjit_Singh) captured Peshawar in 1818, but left it under control of Jahandad Khan in return of paying tribute. In 1823, his armies decisively defeated [Azim Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azim_Khan) at the [Battle of Nowshera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Nowshera), and he appointed Yar Muhammad Khan as the vassal.[^cite_note-gupta-86] From 1826 to 1830 Peshawar became a centre of activities of [Syed Ahmad Barelvi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syed_Ahmad_Barelvi), who had declared [jihad](16203.md) against the Sikh Empire. [Sultan Muhammad Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultan_Mohammad_Khan) rendered valuable services for Ranjit Singh against Ahmed Barelvi, and massacred thousands of his followers in November 1830, forcing Ahmed Barelvi to relocate to Hazara.[^cite_note-\:02-87] Ultimately Peshawar was directly annexed by [Hari Singh Nalwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hari_Singh_Nalwa) in 1834. Shah Shuja Durrani recognised Peshawar as a part of kingdom of Ranjit Singh under the tripartite treaty signed in 1838.[^cite_note-88]&#x20;

### British colonial period



A colonial era lithograph of the Khyber Pass, made in 1848 by James Rattray.

The [British East India Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_East_India_Company) defeated the Sikh Empire in the [Second Anglo-Sikh War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Anglo-Sikh_War) in 1849, and incorporated western portions of the present-day province into [British Raj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Raj), which remained part of the [Province of Punjab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punjab_Province_\(British_India\)) until 1901. During the [1857 war of independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutiny_of_1857), Pashtun tribes throughout the region were generally neutral or supportive of the British,[^cite_note-humshehri.org-45] in contrast to other parts of Raj which rose up in revolt against the British. The British raised 35,000 soldiers from among Pashtun tribes, which proved instrumental in putting down the rebellion.[^cite_note-89]&#x20;

British had inherited Kohat, Bannu, Peshawar, Hazara, Mardan and Dera Ismail Khan from the Sikh Empire, which they declared settled districts.[^cite_note-\:9-90] In the other parts of what would later become North-Western Frontier Province, the British control was tenuous. The defeat of the [Kingdom of Kabul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Kabul) in the [Second Anglo-Afghan War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Anglo-Afghan_War) in 1878 was followed by further expansion of British suzerainty in the tribal areas of Kurram, Waziristan and Khyber. In 1893, the [boundary with Afghanistan](203570.md) was finalised under a treaty agreed to by the Afghan king, [Abdur Rahman Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdur_Rahman_Khan). British awarded parts of Chitral in [Asmar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asmar,_Afghanistan), as well as [Nuristan](457267.md), to Afghanistan under the terms of treaty.[^cite_note-91] The Durand treaty also established the [Wakhan Corridor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wakhan_Corridor), recognised as a part of Afghanistan to act as buffer zone between British and [Russian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire).[^cite_note-LoC-92]&#x20;

In 1895, the British [campaign against Chitral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chitral_Expedition) resulted in Chitral being formally brought under British suzerainty. In the same year, the colonial government established the [Malakand Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malakand_Agency) to supervise Chitral as well as the tribes of Swat and Dir. Earlier, these areas had been under the jurisdiction of [Gilgit Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilgit_Agency).[^cite_note-93] The tribes of Waziristan rose in rebellion in 1897 against British; the rebellions by [Waziris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wazir_\(Pashtun_tribe\)) continued to flare up until 1947.&#x20;

In 1901, [North-West Frontier Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North-West_Frontier_Province) was created by separating the five districts of Punjab, namely Peshawar, [Kohat](4466450.md), [Bannu](988574.md), [Hazara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazara_District) and [Dera Ismail Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dera_Ismail_Khan_District), by the colonial government for the administrative ease and supervision of northwestern borders.[^cite_note-\:9-90] Following the [Third Anglo-Afghan War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Anglo-Afghan_War) in 1919, Afghan King [Amanullah Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amanullah_Khan) signed the [Treaty of Rawalpindi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Rawalpindi), in which Afghanistan recognised the Durand Line as official border between the two powers while British recognised sovereignty of Afghanistan.[^cite_note-94]&#x20;

In 1918, [Swat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swat_\(princely_state\)) was recognised as a [princely state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princely_state) by British Indian government, the last of the princely states to be recognised as such.[^cite_note-95] The British government transferred [Indus Kohistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Kohistan) from the Gilgit Agency to NWFP in November 1934.[^cite_note-96]&#x20;

Concurrently, the British continued their large public works projects in the region, and extended the [Great Indian Peninsula Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Indian_Peninsula_Railway) into the region, which connected the modern Khyber Pakhtunkhwa region to the plains of India to the east. Other projects, such as the [Attock Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attock_Bridge), [Islamia College University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamia_College_University), [Khyber Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khyber_train_safari), and establishment of [cantonments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantonments) in Peshawar, Kohat, [Mardan](1844876.md), and [Nowshera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nowshera_Cantonment) further cemented British rule in the region.&#x20;

During colonial period, North-West Frontier Province was a "scene of repeated outrages on Hindus."[^cite_note-97] There were other tensions in the area as well, particularly those that involved agitations by Pashtun tribesmen against the Imperial government. For example, in 1936, a British Indian court ruled against the marriage of a Hindu girl allegedly converted to Islam in Bannu, after the girl's family filed a case of abduction and forced conversion.[^cite_note-Yousef_Aboul-Enein-98] The ruling was based on the fact that the girl was a minor and was asked to make her decision of conversion and marriage after she reaches the age of majority, till then she was asked to live with a third party.[^cite_note-Yousef_Aboul-Enein-98] After the girl's family filed a case, the court ruled in the family's favour, angering the local Muslims who had later gone on to lead attacks against the [Bannu Brigade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bannu_Brigade).[^cite_note-Yousef_Aboul-Enein-98] Such controversies stirred up anti-Hindu sentiments amongst the province's Muslim population.[^cite_note-99] By 1947 the majority of the ulama in the province began supporting the [Muslim League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-India_Muslim_League)'s idea of Pakistan.[^cite_note-100]&#x20;

After [1946 elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1946_Indian_provincial_elections) a [Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_party_of_India)-led ministry was established in the province under [Khan Abdul Jabbar Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khan_Abdul_Jabbar_Khan), a brother of [Abdul Ghaffar Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdul_Ghaffar_Khan). The two politicians were opposed to [partition of British India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_of_British_India).[^cite_note-101] When the partition became inevitable, in June 1947, [Khudai Khidmatgars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khudai_Khidmatgars) led by [Mirzali Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirzali_Khan), Abdul Ghaffar Khan, and other demanded from British government the choice of a separate independent state for Pashtuns, which was refused as the principles laid for their departure required regions under their control to choose either to join India or Pakistan, with no third option.[^cite_note-102][^cite_note-103][^cite_note-104]&#x20;

The [1947 North-West Frontier Province referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1947_North-West_Frontier_Province_referendum) to allow voters to choose between joining India or Pakistan was held on 6 July 1947 and the referendum results were made public on 20 July 1947. According to the official results, there were 572,798 registered voters, out of which 289,244 (99.02%) votes were cast in favour of Pakistan, while 2,874 (0.98%) were cast in favour of India.[^cite_note-\:2-105] The referendum was boycotted by Khudai Khidmatgars;[^cite_note-Dust_of_Empire-106][^cite_note-107] their appeal for boycott had an effect, as according to an estimate, the total turnout for the referendum was 15% lower than the total turnout in the 1946 elections,[^cite_note-108] although over half of all eligible voters had voted for Pakistan.[^cite_note-\:2-105]&#x20;

Abdul Ghaffar Khan pledged allegiance to the new state of Pakistan in 1947, and thereafter abandoned his goals of an independent Pashtunistan and a united India in favour of supporting increased autonomy for the NWFP within Pakistan.[^cite_note-humshehri.org-45] He was subsequently arrested several times for his opposition to the strong centralized rule.[^cite_note-109] He later claimed that "Pashtunistan was never a reality. The idea of Pashtunistan never helped Pashtuns and it only caused suffering for them." He further claimed that the "successive governments of Afghanistan only exploited the idea for their own political goals".[^cite_note-110]&#x20;

### Post-independence

After the independence of Pakistan in 1947, Afghanistan was the sole member of the [United Nations](31769.md) to vote against Pakistan's accession to the UN because of Kabul's claim to the Pashtun territories on the Pakistani side of the [Durand Line](203570.md).[^cite_note-pakafgh-111][^cite_note-112] This led to border tensions with Pakistan. Afghanistan's governments have periodically refused to recognize Pakistan's inheritance of past treaties regarding the Pak-Afghan border.[^cite_note-pakafgh-111][^cite_note-113][^cite_note-114][^cite_note-115] During the 1950s, Afghanistan supported the [Pashtunistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pashtunistan) movement, a secessionist movement that failed to gain substantial support amongst the tribes of the North-West Frontier Province.[^cite_note-116]&#x20;

After the Afghan-Soviet War, North-West Frontier Province became one of the areas of top focus for the [War against Terror](13425800.md). The province was reported to struggle with the social and infrastructure issues even as other parts of the country continue to receive funding.[^cite_note-117]&#x20;

In 2010, the name of the province changed to "Khyber Pakhtunkhwa". Protests arose among the locals of the Hazara division due to this name change, as they began to demand their own province.[^cite_note-\:0b-118] Seven people were killed and 100 injured on 11 April 2010 by Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Police to suppress the protests.[^cite_note-\:0b-118]&#x20;

The [Provincial Assembly of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provincial_Assembly_of_Khyber_Pakhtunkhwa) approved a bill on 28 May 2018 to merge the [FATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federally_Administered_Tribal_Areas) as well as the [Provincially Administered Tribal Areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provincially_Administered_Tribal_Areas) into Khyber Pakhtunkhwa following the approval of the [Twenty-fifth Amendment to the Constitution of Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twenty-fifth_Amendment_to_the_Constitution_of_Pakistan) by the national legislature;[^cite_note-\:0a-119] it was signed into law on 31 May by then [President of Pakistan](419460.md) [Mamnoon Hussain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamnoon_Hussain), which officially completed the administrative merger process.[^cite_note-\:1-41][^cite_note-120]&#x20;

## Geography



Northern parts of the province feature forests and dramatic mountain scenery, as in [Swat District](1236283.md).

Khyber Pakhtunkhwa sits primarily on the [Iranian plateau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_plateau) and comprises the junction where the slopes of the [Hindu Kush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_Kush) mountains on the [Eurasian Plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasian_Plate) give way to the Indus-watered hills approaching [South Asia](21566765.md). This situation has led to seismic activity in the past.[^cite_note-121] The well-known [Khyber Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khyber_Pass) links the province to Afghanistan, while the [Kohalla Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kohalla_Bridge) in [Circle Bakote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_Bakote) Abbottabad is a major crossing point over the [Jhelum River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jhelum_River) in the east.&#x20;

Geographically the province can be divided into two zones: the northern zone extending from the ranges of the [Hindu Kush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_Kush) to the borders of the Peshawar basin and the southern zone extending from Peshawar to the [Derajat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derajat) basin.&#x20;

The northern zone is cold and snowy in winters with heavy rainfall and pleasant summers with the exception of the Peshawar basin, which is hot in summer and cold in winter. It has moderate rainfall.&#x20;

The southern zone is arid with hot summers and relatively cold winters and scanty rainfall.[^cite_note-122] The Sheikh Badin Hills, a spur of clay and [sandstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandstone) hills that stretch east from the Sulaiman Mountains to the [Indus River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_River), separates [Dera Ismail Khan District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dera_Ismail_Khan_District) from the *Marwat* plains of the [Lakki Marwat](3828439.md). The highest peak in the range is the limestone Sheikh Badin Mountain, which is protected by the [Sheikh Badin National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheikh_Badin_National_Park). Near the Indus River, the terminus of the Sheikh Badin Hills is a spur of [limestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limestone) hills known as the *Kafir Kot* hills, where the ancient Hindu complex of [Kafir Kot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kafir_Kot) is located.[^cite_note-123]&#x20;

The major rivers that run in the province are Kabul, Swat, Chitral, Kunar, Siran, Panjkora, Bara, Kurram, Dor, Haroo, Gomal, and Zhob.&#x20;

### Climate

The [climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate) of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa varies immensely for a region of its size, encompassing most of the many climate types found in Pakistan. The province stretching southwards from the [Baroghil Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baroghil_Pass) in the [Hindu Kush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_Kush) covers almost six degrees of latitude; it is mainly a mountainous region. [Dera Ismail Khan](696749.md) is one of the hottest places in South Asia while in the mountains to the north the weather is mild in the summer and intensely cold in the winter. The air is generally very dry; consequently, the daily and annual range of temperature is quite large.[^cite_note-124]&#x20;

Rainfall also varies widely. Although large parts of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa are typically dry, the province also contains the wettest parts of Pakistan in its eastern fringe especially in [monsoon](57630.md) season from mid-June to mid-September.&#x20;



[Ghabral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghabral), [Swat Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swat_Valley)

#### Upper and Lower Chitral Districts

[Upper Chitral District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Chitral_District) and [Lower Chitral District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Chitral_District), due to their location, are completely sheltered from the [monsoon](57630.md) that controls the weather in eastern Pakistan, owing to its relatively westerly location and the shielding effect of the [Nanga Parbat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanga_Parbat) massif. In many ways, they have more in common regarding climate with [Central Asia](6742.md) than South Asia.[^cite_note-Trekking-125] The winters are generally cold even in the valleys, and heavy [snow](28191.md) during the winter blocks passes and isolates the region. In the valleys, however, summers can be hotter than on the windward side of the mountains due to lower cloud cover: Chitral can reach 40 °C (104 °F) frequently during this period.[^cite_note-126] However, the humidity is extremely low during these hot spells and, as a result, the summer climate is less torrid than in the rest of the Indian subcontinent.&#x20;

Most precipitation falls as thunderstorms or snow during winter and spring, so that the climate at the lowest elevations is [classed as](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification) [Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_climate) (*Csa*), [continental Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_Mediterranean_climate) (*Dsa*) or [semi-arid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-arid_climate) (*BSk*). Summers are extremely dry in the north of Chitral district and receive only a little rain in the south around [Drosh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drosh).&#x20;

At elevations above 5,000 metres (16,400 ft), as much as a third of the snow which feeds the large Karakoram and Hindukush [glaciers](12463.md) comes from the monsoon since these elevations are too high to be shielded from its moisture.[^cite_note-Trekking-125]&#x20;

The seasonality of rainfall in central Khyber Pakhtunkhwa shows very marked gradients from east to west. At Dir, March remains the wettest month due to frequent frontal cloud bands, whereas in Hazara more than half the rainfall comes from the monsoon.[^cite_note-Wernsted-129] This creates a unique situation characterized by a bimodal rainfall regime, which extends into the southern part of the province described below.[^cite_note-Wernsted-129]&#x20;

Since cold air from the [Siberian High](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siberian_High) loses its chilling capacity upon crossing the vast Karakoram and Himalaya ranges, winters in central Khyber Pakhtunkhwa are somewhat milder than in Chitral. [Snow](28191.md) remains very frequent at high altitudes but rarely lasts long on the ground in the major towns and agricultural valleys. Outside of winter, temperatures in central Khyber Pakhtunkhwa are not so hot as in Chitral.&#x20;

Significantly higher humidity when the monsoon is active means that heat discomfort can be greater. However, even during the most humid periods the high altitudes typically allow for some relief from the heat overnight.[^cite_note-130]&#x20;

#### Southern Khyber Pakhtunkhwa

As one moves further away from the foothills of the Himalaya and Karakoram ranges, the climate changes from the humid subtropical climate of the foothills to the typically [arid climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arid_climate) of [Sindh](28972.md), [Balochistan](4687.md) and southern [Punjab](24751.md). As in central Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, the seasonality of precipitation shows a very sharp gradient from west to east, but the whole region very rarely receives significant monsoon rainfall. Even at high elevations, annual rainfall is less than 400 millimetres (16 in) and in some places as little as 200 millimetres (8 in).&#x20;

Temperatures in southern Khyber Pakhtunkhwa are extremely hot: Dera Ismail Khan in the southernmost district of the province is known as one of the hottest places in the world with temperatures known to have reached 50 °C (122 °F).[^cite_note-131] In the cooler months, nights can be cold and frosts remain frequent; snow is very rare, and daytime temperatures remain comfortably warm with abundant sunshine.&#x20;

### Ethnicity

Khyber Pakhtunkhwa is one of Pakistan's most diverse provinces. It is home to numerous ethnolinguistic and ethnic groups that live across different parts of KPK.[^cite_note-\:KPCensus-155] Four main [ethnolinguistic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnolinguistic_group) of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa are Pashtuns, [Hindkowans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindkowans), [Saraikis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saraiki_people) and [Kohistanis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Kohistani_people). One-third of the province's population is non-Pashtun and mainly concentrated in the northern areas.[^cite_note-\:ShaheenAli-156][^cite_note-\:minorityrights-157]&#x20;

#### Main ethnic groups

- [Pashtun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pashtun): The largest ethnic group in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa is the [Pashtuns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pashtuns).

The Pashtuns of KPK observe tribal code of conduct called [Pashtunwali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pashtunwali) which has four high value components called *nang* (honour), *badal* (revenge), *melmastiya* (hospitality) and *nanawata* (rights to refuge).

  [^cite_note-T\&F3-160]

- [Awan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Awan_\(tribe\)): They are mainly found in Hazara division and speak Hindko as their mother tongue. They are the second main ethnic group and are mainly found in [Hazara division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazara_Division) of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa.

They are the third main ethnic group of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa

#### Refugees

As per 2008 [United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees](31964.md) report, around 1.5 million [Afghan refugees](22663356.md) also remain in the province,[^cite_note-175] the majority of whom are Pashtuns. Despite having lived in the province for over two decades, they are registered as [citizens of Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographics_of_Afghanistan).[^cite_note-176]&#x20;

### Languages

Languages of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (2023 Census)

[^cite_note-177]

1. [Pashto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pashto) (81.0%)

2. [Hindko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindko) (9.39%)

3. [Saraiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saraiki_language) (3.17%)

4. [Kohistani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Kohistani_language) (2.45%)

5. [Urdu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urdu) (0.64%)

6. [Punjabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punjabi_Language) (0.24%)

7. [Shina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shina_language) (0.17%)

8. Others (2.94%)

According to the [2023 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_Pakistani_census), the most widely spoken language is [Pashto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pashto), native to 81% of the population and spoken throughout the province.[^cite_note-2023censuslanguage2-178] Other main languages include [Hindko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindko) spoken by 9.39%, [Saraiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saraiki_language) by 3.17%, and [Kohistani languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kohistani_languages) by 2.45%.[^cite_note-2023censuslanguage2-178]&#x20;

[Urdu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urdu) is spoken by 0.64%, [Punjabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punjabi_language) by 0.24%, and [Shina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shina_language) by 0.17% of the population. Hindko is primarily spoken in the [Hazara Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazara_Division) in the northeast, and Saraiki-speakers are found in [Dera Ismail Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dera_Ismail_Khan_District) and [Tank district](4592102.md)s in the far south of the province.[^cite_note-\:72-179][^cite_note-\:7-180] Punjabi is mainly spoken in Hazara division, as well as in the cities of [Nowshera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nowshera,_Khyber_Pakhtunkhwa), [Peshawar](66230.md), and [Kohat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kohat).[^cite_note-\:KPLangs-181] Kohistani languages are mainly spoken in Kohistan region and [Upper Swat district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Swat_District). [Gujari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gujari) language is spoken in all major cities of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and in the northern half of the province.[^cite_note-\:KPLangs-181] Shina is mainly spoken in Kohistan region, while Khowar is spoken in [Upper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Chitral_District) and [Lower Chitral Districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Chitral_District).[^cite_note-\:KPLangs-181]&#x20;

Urdu, the national and official language, serves as a [lingua franca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lingua_franca). It is used as a common language between different ethnic groups. In communities that speak other [indigenous languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_language), people often use Pashto and Urdu as their second and third languages.[^cite_note-T\&F2-182]&#x20;

Kohistani languages is an umbrella term encompassing several languages spoken in the north of the province, including [Indus Kohistani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Kohistani), [Bateri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bateri_language), [Chilisso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chilisso_language), [Gawri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gawri_language), [Gawro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gowro_language), [Torwali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torwali_language), and [Mankiyali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mankiyali_language).[^cite_note-183][^cite_note-184] In 2011 the [provincial government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_government) approved in principle the introduction of Pashto, Saraiki, Hindko, Khowar and Kohistani as compulsory subjects for schools in the areas where they are spoken.[^cite_note-185]&#x20;

#### Other languages

Around 1 million people selected the "Other" category, as most of these languages were not listed in the census.[^cite_note-2023censuslanguage2-178][^cite_note-\:72-179] Other languages make up 2.94% of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa including Gujari and Khowar. [Gujari language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gujari), along with [Burushaski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burushaski), [Wakhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wakhi_language), and [Kalkoti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalkoti_language) is spoken by 1% of the population.[^cite_note-\:Akbar-186] Gujari is spoken in all major cities and the northern half of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa,[^cite_note-\:KPLangs-181] while others are spoken near border areas of Gilgit-Baltistan. Khowar, along with [Kalasha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalasha_language), [Phalura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palula_language), [Dameli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dameli_language), [Gawar-Bati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gawar-Bati_language), [Balti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balti_language), [Yidgha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yidgha_language), and [Shekhai](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Shekhai_language\&action=edit\&redlink=1), is spoken by 1% of the population, mainly in the Upper and Lower Chitral Districts.[^cite_note-\:Akbar-186][^cite_note-\:KPLangs-181]&#x20;

Remaining 0.94% speak other minority languages. In which [Afghan refugee](22663356.md) speak mainly [Dari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dari), [Hazaragi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazaragi_dialects), and [Tajik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tajik_language), [dialects of Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialects_of_Persian).[^cite_note-\:KPLangs-181]&#x20;

The overwhelming majority of the residents of the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa follows and professes [Sunni Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam) while there is a significant amount of Shia Muslims in areas such as Kurram, Kohat, Hangu, Orakzai, Dera Ismail Khan, Mardan, and many other districts throughout central-southern KPK. Apart from Twelver Shias there are [Isma'ilis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ismaili_Islam) in the Chitral district.[^cite_note-A_Brief_History_of_Pakistan-192] The tribe of [Kalasha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalash_people) in southern Chitral still retain an ancient form of [Polytheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytheism) mixed with [Animism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animism), a faith once dominant in the mountainous upper northeast of the district.[^cite_note-A_Brief_History_of_Pakistan-192] There are a small numbers of residents who are adherents of the [Roman Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_Pakistan) [denomination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_denomination) of [Christianity](5211.md), [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism_in_Pakistan), and [Sikhism](27964.md), mainly living in Peshawar and other urban centres.[^cite_note-193][^cite_note-194]&#x20;

## Government and politics

### Political leanings and the legislative branch

Main article: [Provincial Assembly of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provincial_Assembly_of_Khyber_Pakhtunkhwa)

In rural areas of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, especially in the west and south, people often vote based on [tribe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribe), [biradari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baradari_\(brotherhood\)), or family connections rather that political party policies.[^cite_note-\:KpPolitics-205] This is called vertical alignment. Horizontal alignment means people support a party because of its ideology. Pashtuns dominant local politics of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa.[^cite_note-206] Other groups such as Sayeds, [Gujjars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gurjars_in_Khyber_Pakhtunkhwa), and [Swatis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swati_people_\(Pakistan\)) are influential in the local politics of Malakand and Hazara divisions. [Awan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Awan_\(tribe\)), [Abbasi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhund_\(tribe\)) and [Tanoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanoli) are also politically influential in the Hazara division. In [Mansehra District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mansehra_District), tribal leaders won the [National Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Assembly_of_Pakistan) seats in the 1990, 1993, and 1997 elections. There was also competition between Gurjars and [Sayeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayyid). Because Gurjars had a larger population, the [Pakistan Muslim League – Nawaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan_Muslim_League_%E2%80%93_Nawaz) gave the election ticket to a Gujjars leader, who then won the seat.[^cite_note-\:KpPolitics-205][^cite_note-207]&#x20;

The [Provincial Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provincial_Assembly_of_Khyber_Pakhtunkhwa) is a [unicameral](204304.md) legislature, which consists of 145 members elected to serve for a constitutionally bounded term of five years. Historically, the province perceived to be a stronghold of the [Awami National Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Awami_National_Party) (ANP); a [pro-Russian](2541825.md), by [procommunist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procommunism), [left-wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism_in_Pakistan) and nationalist party.[^cite_note-Rug_Pandits,_Yasir-208][^cite_note-Rug_Pandits,_Yasir_Sheikh-209] Since the 1970s, the [Pakistan Peoples Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan_Peoples_Party) (PPP) also enjoyed considerable support in the province due to its [socialist agenda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism_in_Pakistan).[^cite_note-Rug_Pandits,_Yasir-208] Khyber Pakhtunkhwa was thought to be another leftist region of the country after [Sindh](28972.md).[^cite_note-Rug_Pandits,_Yasir_Sheikh-209]&#x20;

After the nationwide [general elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2002_Pakistani_general_election) held in 2002, a [plurality voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plurality_voting) [swing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swing_\(politics\)) in the province elected one of Pakistan's only religiously based provincial governments led by the [ultra-conservative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Islam) [Muttahida Majlis-e-Amal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muttahida_Majlis-e-Amal) (MMA) during the administration of President [Pervez Musharraf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pervez_Musharraf). The [American involvement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_invasion_of_Afghanistan) in neighbouring Afghanistan contributed towards the electoral victory of the Islamic coalition led by [Jamaat-e-Islami Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamaat-e-Islami_Pakistan) (JeI) whose social policies made the province a ground-swell of [anti-Americanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-American_sentiment_in_Pakistan).[^cite_note-Time-210] The electoral victory of MMA was also in context of [guided democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guided_democracy) in the Musharraff administration that barred the mainstream political parties, the leftist Pakistan Peoples Party and the [centre-right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre-right) [Pakistan Muslim League (N)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan_Muslim_League_\(N\)) (PML(N)), whose chairmen and presidents having been barred from participation in the elections.[^cite_note-211]&#x20;

Policy enforcement of a range of social restrictions, though the implementation of strict [Shariah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shariah) was introduced by the Muttahida Majlis-e-Amal government. But the law was never fully enacted due to objections of the [Governor of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governor_of_Khyber_Pakhtunkhwa) backed by the [Musharraf administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pervez_Musharraf).[^cite_note-Time-210] Restrictions on public musical performances were introduced, as well as a ban prohibiting music to be played in public places as part of the "Prohibition of Dancing and Music Bill, 2005" – which led to the creation of a thriving underground music scene in Peshawar.[^cite_note-212] The Islamist government also attempted to enforce compulsory *[hijab](68301.md)* on women,[^cite_note-Clarke-213] and wished to enforce gender segregation in the province's educational institutions.[^cite_note-Clarke-213] The coalition further tried to prohibit male doctors from performing ultrasounds on women,[^cite_note-Clarke-213] and tried to close the province's cinemas.[^cite_note-Clarke-213] In 2005, the coalition successfully passed the "Prohibition of Use of Women in Photograph Bill, 2005," leading to the removal of all public advertisements that featured women.[^cite_note-214]&#x20;

At the height of [Taliban insurgency in Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taliban_insurgency_in_Pakistan), the religious coalition lost its grip in the [general elections held in 2008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Pakistani_general_election), and the religious coalition was swept out of power by the leftist Awami National Party which also witnessed the resignation of President Musharraf in 2008.[^cite_note-Time-210] The ANP government eventually led the initiatives to repeal the major Islamist's social programs, with the backing of the [federal government](1897833.md) led by PPP in Islamabad.[^cite_note-215] Public disapproval of ANP's leftist program integrated in [civil administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Khyber_Pakhtunkhwa) with the sounded allegations of corruption as well as popular opposition against [religious program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_Conservatism) promoted by the [MMA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muttahida_Majlis-e-Amal) swiftly shifted the province's leniency away from the left in 2012.[^cite_note-Rug_Pandits,_Yasir-208] In 2013, the provincial politics shifted towards [populism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Populism) and [nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalism) when the PTI, led by [Imran Khan](413184.md), was able to form the [minority government](271434.md) in coalition with the [JeI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamaat-e-Islami); the province now serves as the stronghold of the PTI and is perceived as one of the more right wing areas of the country.[^cite_note-rugpundits,_Yasir-216] After the [2018 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Khyber_Pakhtunkhwa_provincial_election), PTI increased their seat share and formed a majority government.&#x20;

In non-Pashtun areas, such as [Abbottabad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbottabad_District), and [Hazara Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazara_Division), the [PML(N)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan_Muslim_League_\(N\)), the [centre-right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre-right) party, enjoys considerable public support over economical and public policy issues and has a substantial vote bank.[^cite_note-rugpundits,_Yasir-216]&#x20;

### Executive branch

Main article: [Government of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Khyber_Pakhtunkhwa)

The [executive branch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Khyber_Pakhtunkhwa) of the Kyber Pakhtunkhwa is led by the [Chief Minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_Minister_of_Khyber_Pakhtunkhwa) elected by popular vote in the [Provincial assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provincial_Assembly_of_Khyber_Pakhtunkhwa)[^cite_note-217] while the Governor, a ceremonial figure representing the [federal government](1897833.md) in Islamabad, is appointed from the necessary advice of the [Prime Minister of Pakistan](461896.md) by the [President of Pakistan](419460.md).[^cite_note-218]&#x20;

The provincial cabinet is then appointed by the Chief Minister who takes the [Oath of office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oath_of_office) from the Governor.[^cite_note-219] In matters of civil administration, the [Chief Secretary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_Secretary_\(Pakistan\)) assists the Chief Minister on executing its right to ensure the writ of the government and the constitution.[^cite_note-A_Brief_History_of_Pakistan-192][^cite_note-220]&#x20;

### Judicial branch

Main article: [Peshawar High Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peshawar_High_Court)

The [Peshawar High Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peshawar_High_Court) is the province's [highest court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Court) of law whose judges are appointed by the approval of the [Supreme Judicial Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Judicial_Council_of_Pakistan) in [Islamabad](51153.md), interpreting the laws and overturn those they find unconstitutional.&#x20;

## Economy

Main article: [Economy of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Khyber_Pakhtunkhwa)



Khyber Pakhtunkhwa's dominance: forestry

Khyber Pakhtunkhwa has the [third largest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Pakistani_provinces_by_GDP) provincial economy in Pakistan. Khyber Pakhtunkhwa's share of Pakistan's GDP has historically comprised 10.5%, although the province accounts for 11.9% of Pakistan's total population. The part of the economy that Khyber Pakhtunkhwa dominates is forestry, where its share has historically ranged from a low of 34.9% to a high of 81%, giving an average of 61.56%.[^cite_note-221] Currently, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa accounts for 10% of Pakistan's GDP,[^cite_note-222] 20% of Pakistan's mining output[^cite_note-223] and, since 1972, it has seen its economy grow in size by 3.6 times.[^cite_note-224]&#x20;

Agriculture remains important and the main cash crops include wheat, maize, tobacco (in Swabi), rice, sugar beets, as well as fruits are grown in the province.&#x20;

Some manufacturing and high-tech investments in Peshawar have helped improve job prospects for many locals, while trade in the province involves nearly every product. The bazaars in the province are renowned throughout Pakistan. Unemployment has been reduced due to the establishment of industrial zones.&#x20;

Workshops throughout the province support the manufacture of small arms and weapons. The province accounts for at least 78% of the [marble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marble) production in Pakistan.[^cite_note-225]&#x20;

## Social issues

The [Awami National Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Awami_National_Party) sought to rename the province "Pakhtunkhwa", which translates to "Land of Pakhtuns" in the [Pashto language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pashto_language).[^cite_note-227] This was opposed by some non-Pashtuns in the province and political parties such as the [Pakistan Muslim League-N](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan_Muslim_League-N) (PML-N) and [Muttahida Majlis-e-Amal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muttahida_Majlis-e-Amal) (MMA), due to the PML-N deriving its support in the province from primarily non-Pashtun [Hazara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazara_people) regions.&#x20;

In 2010, the announcement that the province would have a new name led to a wave of protests in the Hazara region.[^cite_note-228] On 15 April 2010, [Pakistan's senate](761025.md) officially named the province "Khyber Pakhtunkhwa" with 80 senators in favour and 12 opposed.[^cite_note-rename-229] The MMA, who until the elections of 2008 had a majority in the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa government, had proposed "Afghania" as a compromise name.[^cite_note-230]&#x20;

After the [2008 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Pakistani_general_election), the Awami National Party formed a coalition provincial government with the [Pakistan Peoples Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan_Peoples_Party).[^cite_note-231] The Awami National Party has its strongholds in the Pashtun areas of Pakistan, particularly in the Peshawar valley, while [Karachi](17123.md) in [Sindh](28972.md) has one of the largest Pashtun populations in the world—around 7 million by some estimates.[^cite_note-232] In the 2008 election, the ANP won two Sindh assembly seats in Karachi. The Awami National Party has been instrumental in fighting the Taliban. In [the 2013 general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2013_Pakistani_general_election) Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf won a majority in the [provincial assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khyber_Pakhtunkhwa_Assembly) and has now formed their government in coalition with [Jamaat-e-Islami Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamaat-e-Islami_Pakistan).[^cite_note-233]&#x20;

## Folk music and culture

See also: [Khyber Pakhtunkhwa clothing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khyber_Pakhtunkhwa_clothing) and [Peshawari chappal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peshawari_chappal)

### Music

Pashto folk music is popular in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and has a rich tradition going back hundreds of years. The main instruments are the [rubab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubab_\(instrument\)), [mangey](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Mangey\&action=edit\&redlink=1) and [harmonium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonium_\(hand-pumped\)). Khowar folk music is popular in Chitral and northern Swat. The tunes of Khowar music are very different from those of Pashto, and the main instrument is the Chitrali sitar. A form of band music composed of clarinets (Surnai) and drums is popular in Chitral. It is played at polo matches and dances. The same form of band music is played in the neighbouring [Northern Areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Areas).[^cite_note-236]&#x20;

### Literature

There is an important literature produced in the province, mainly in Pashto but also in Urdu and in Hindko, and in 2022 more than 25,000 books were published in these three languages.[^cite_note-237]&#x20;

Khyber Pakhtunkhwa has traditionally had a very low literacy rate, although this is changing in recent times. As of the 2017 census, the literacy rate for Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (including FATA) is 51.66%. In rural areas, the literacy rate is 48.44% of the population while in urban areas it is 66.86%. Khyber Pakhtunkhwa has a huge gap in literacy rate between sexes – for men it is 66.67% while the female literacy rate is 34.58%, just over half the male literacy rate. This gap is particularly prominent in the overwhelmingly-Pashto rural areas, where traditional gender norms have generally limited education of women. As of 2021, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KP) has the highest literacy growth rate in the whole country ([Pakistan](23235.md)).[^cite_note-startuppakistan.com.pk-244][^cite_note-propakistani.pk-245]&#x20;

## Sports and tourism

### Sports

[Cricket](25675557.md) is the main sport played in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. It has produced world-class sportsmen like [Shahid Afridi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shahid_Afridi), [Younis Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Younis_Khan), [Khushdil Shah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khushdil_Shah), [Fakhar Zaman](48434549.md), [Naseem Shah](58376233.md) and [Umar Gul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umar_Gul). Besides producing cricket players, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa has the honour of being the birthplace of many world-class [squash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squash_\(sport\)) players, including greats like Hashim Khan, Qamar Zaman, [Jahangir Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jahangir_Khan) and [Jansher Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jansher_Khan).&#x20;

Total responses in all other modern–era (1951, 1961, 1981, 1998, 2017, 2023) census counts are an amalgamation of the [North-West Frontier Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North-West_Frontier_Province) and [Federally Administered Tribal Areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federally_Administered_Tribal_Areas). Both administrative divisions would ultimately merge to form Khyber Pakhtunkhwa in 2018.
