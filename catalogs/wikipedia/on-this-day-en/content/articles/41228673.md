An **internal combustion engine** (**ICE** or **IC engine**) is a [heat engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_engine) in which the [combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion) of a [fuel](21188370.md) occurs with an [oxidizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidizer) (usually air) in a [combustion chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion_chamber) that is an integral part of the [working fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Working_fluid) flow circuit. In an internal combustion engine, the expansion of the high-[temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature) and high-[pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure) gases produced by combustion applies direct [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force) to components of the engine. The force is typically applied to [pistons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piston) ([piston engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocating_engine)), [turbine blades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbine_blade) ([gas turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_turbine)), a [rotor (Wankel engine)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wankel_engine), or a [nozzle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propulsive_nozzle) ([jet engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_engine)). This force moves the component over a distance. This process transforms [chemical energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_energy) into [kinetic energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_energy) which is used to propel, move or power whatever the engine is attached to.

The first commercially successful internal combustion engines were invented in the mid-19th century. The first modern internal combustion engine, the [Otto engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_engine), was designed in 1876 by the German engineer [Nicolaus Otto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaus_Otto).[^cite_note-EB1-1] The term *[internal combustion engine](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/internal%20combustion%20engine)* usually refers to an engine in which combustion is [intermittent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermittency), such as the more familiar [two-stroke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-stroke) and [four-stroke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-stroke) piston engines, along with variants, such as the [six-stroke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six-stroke_engine) piston engine and the [Wankel rotary engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wankel_engine). A second class of internal combustion engines use continuous combustion: [gas turbines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_turbine), [jet engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_engine) and most [rocket engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_engine), each of which are internal combustion engines on the same principle as previously described.[^cite_note-EB1-1][^cite_note-pulkrabek-2] In contrast, in [external combustion engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/External_combustion_engine), such as [steam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_engine) or [Stirling engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stirling_engine), energy is delivered to a working fluid not consisting of, mixed with, or contaminated by combustion products. Working fluids for *external* combustion engines include air, hot water, [pressurized water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressurized_water_reactor) or even [boiler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boiler)-heated [liquid sodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium-cooled_fast_reactor).

While there are many stationary applications, most ICEs are used in mobile applications and are the primary power supply for [vehicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vehicle) such as [cars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cars), [aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft) and [boats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boats). ICEs are typically powered by [hydrocarbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon)-based fuels like [natural gas](22131.md), [gasoline](23639.md), [diesel fuel](81761.md), or [ethanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethanol). [Renewable fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renewable_fuels) like [biodiesel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiesel) are used in compression ignition (CI) engines and [bioethanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethanol_fuel) or [ETBE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETBE) (ethyl tert-butyl ether) produced from bioethanol in spark ignition (SI) engines. As early as 1900 the inventor of the diesel engine, [Rudolf Diesel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Diesel), was using peanut oil to run his engines.[^cite_note-3] Renewable fuels are commonly blended with fossil fuels. [Hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen), which is rarely used, can be obtained from either fossil fuels or renewable energy.

## History

Main article: [History of the internal combustion engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_internal_combustion_engine)

A vintage mechanical device known as the Brayton walking beam engine from the year 1872

Brayton walking beam engine from 1872

Various [scientists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientist) and [engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineer) contributed to the development of internal combustion engines. In 1791, [John Barber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Barber_\(engineer\)) developed the [gas turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_turbine). In 1794 Thomas Mead patented a [gas engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_engine). Also in 1794, Robert Street patented an internal combustion engine, which was also the first to use [liquid fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_fuel), and built an engine around that time. In 1798, [John Stevens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Stevens_\(inventor,_born_1749\)) built the first American internal combustion engine. In 1807, [French](5843419.md) engineers [Nicéphore Niépce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nic%C3%A9phore_Ni%C3%A9pce) (who went on to invent [photography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photography)) and [Claude Niépce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Ni%C3%A9pce) ran a prototype internal combustion engine, using controlled dust explosions, the [Pyréolophore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyr%C3%A9olophore), which was granted a patent by [Napoleon Bonaparte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon). This engine powered a boat on the [Saône](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sa%C3%B4ne) river in France.[^cite_note-Nicéphore_Niépce's_House_Museum_2021-4][^cite_note-Paléo-Energétique_2019-5] In the same year, [Swiss](26748.md) engineer [François Isaac de Rivaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_Isaac_de_Rivaz) invented a hydrogen-based internal combustion engine with ignition by electric spark. In 1808, De Rivaz fitted his invention to a primitive working vehicle – "the world's first internal combustion powered automobile".[^cite_note-6] In 1823, [Samuel Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Brown_\(engineer\)) patented the first internal combustion engine to be applied industrially.

In 1854, in the UK, the Italian inventors [Eugenio Barsanti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugenio_Barsanti) and [Felice Matteucci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felice_Matteucci) obtained the certification: "Obtaining Motive Power by the Explosion of Gases". In 1857 the Great Seal Patent Office conceded them patent No.1655 for the invention of an "Improved Apparatus for Obtaining Motive Power from Gases".[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10] Barsanti and Matteucci obtained other patents for the same invention in France, Belgium and Piedmont between 1857 and 1859.[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12] In 1860, [Belgian](3343.md) engineer [Jean Joseph Etienne Lenoir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etienne_Lenoir) produced a gas-fired internal combustion engine.[^cite_note-Encyclopedia_Britannica_Étienne_Lenoir-13] In 1864, [Nicolaus Otto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaus_Otto) patented the first atmospheric gas engine. In 1872, American [George Brayton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Brayton) invented the first commercial liquid-fueled internal combustion engine. In 1876, [Nicolaus Otto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaus_Otto) began working with [Gottlieb Daimler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottlieb_Daimler) and [Wilhelm Maybach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Maybach), patented the compressed charge, four-cycle engine. In 1879, [Karl Benz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Benz) patented a reliable [two-stroke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-stroke) gasoline engine. Later, in 1886, Benz began the first commercial production of motor vehicles with an internal combustion engine, in which a three-wheeled, four-cycle engine and chassis formed a single unit.[^cite_note-The_Library_of_Congress-14] In 1892, [Rudolf Diesel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Diesel) developed the first compressed charge, compression ignition engine. In 1926, [Robert Goddard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Goddard_\(scientist\)) launched the first liquid-fueled rocket. In 1939, the [Heinkel He 178](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinkel_He_178) became the world's first [jet aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_aircraft).[^cite_note-15]

## Etymology

In the past, the word *engine* (via [Old French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_French), from [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) *[ingenium](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/ingenium#Latin)*, "ability") meant any piece of [machinery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machinery)—a sense that persists in expressions such as *[siege engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_engine)*. A "motor" (from Latin *[motor](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/motor#Latin)*, "mover") is any machine that produces mechanical [power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_\(physics\)). Traditionally, [electric motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_motor) are not referred to as "engines"; however, combustion engines are often referred to as "motors". (An *[electric engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_locomotive)* refers to a [locomotive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locomotive) operated by electricity.)

In boating, an internal combustion engine that is installed in the hull is referred to as an engine, but the engines that sit on the transom are referred to as motors.[^cite_note-16]

## Applications



[Reciprocating engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocating_engine) of a car



[Diesel generator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diesel_generator) for backup power

Reciprocating piston engines are by far the most common power source for land and water [vehicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vehicle), including [automobiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automobile), [motorcycles](19876.md), [ships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship) and to a lesser extent, [locomotives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locomotive) (some are electrical but most use diesel engines[^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18]). Rotary engines of the Wankel design are used in some automobiles, aircraft and motorcycles. These are collectively known as internal-combustion-engine vehicles (ICEV).[^cite_note-DeLuchi1991-19]

Where high power-to-weight ratios are required, internal combustion engines appear in the form of [combustion turbines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion_turbine), or sometimes Wankel engines. [Powered aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powered_aircraft) typically use an ICE which may be a reciprocating engine. Airplanes can instead use [jet engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_engine) and [helicopters](8286923.md) can instead employ [turboshafts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turboshaft); both of which are types of turbines. In addition to providing propulsion, aircraft may employ a separate ICE as an [auxiliary power unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auxiliary_power_unit). Wankel engines are fitted to many [unmanned aerial vehicles](58900.md).

ICEs drive large electric generators that power electrical grids. They are found in the form of [combustion turbines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion_turbine) with a typical electrical output in the range of some 100 MW. [Combined cycle power plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combined_cycle_power_plant) use the high temperature exhaust to boil and superheat water steam to run a [steam turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_turbine). Thus, the efficiency is higher because more energy is extracted from the fuel than what could be extracted by the combustion engine alone. Combined cycle power plants achieve efficiencies in the range of 50–60%. In a smaller scale, [stationary engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stationary_engine) like [gas engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_engine) or [diesel generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diesel_generator) are used for backup or for providing electrical power to areas not connected to an [electric grid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_grid).

[Small engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_engine) (usually [2‐stroke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-stroke_engine) [single cylinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-cylinder_engine) gasoline/petrol engines) are a common power source for [lawnmowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawnmower), [string trimmers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_trimmer), [chainsaws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chainsaw), [leaf blowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf_blower), [pressure washers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_washer), [radio-controlled cars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio-controlled_car), [snowmobiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snowmobile), [jet skis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_ski), [outboard motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outboard_motor), [mopeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moped), and [motorcycles](19876.md).

### Structure



Bare cylinder block of a V8 engine



Piston, piston ring, gudgeon pin and connecting rod

The base of a reciprocating internal combustion engine is the [engine block](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engine_block), which is typically made of [cast iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cast_iron) (due to its good wear resistance and low cost)[^cite_note-Heywood_2018_p._11-23] or [aluminum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminum). In the latter case, the cylinder liners are made of cast iron or steel,[^cite_note-Denton_2011_p._109-24] or a coating such as [nikasil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikasil) or [alusil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alusil). The engine block contains the [cylinders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_\(engine\)). In engines with more than one cylinder they are usually arranged either in 1 row ([straight engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straight_engine)) or 2 rows ([boxer engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boxer_engine) or [V engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V_engine)); 3 or 4 rows are occasionally used ([W engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W_engine)) in contemporary engines, and other [engine configurations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engine_configuration) are possible and have been used. [Single-cylinder engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-cylinder_engine) (or *thumpers*) are common for motorcycles and other small engines found in light machinery. On the outer side of the cylinder, passages that contain cooling fluid are cast into the engine block whereas, in some heavy duty engines, the passages are the types of removable cylinder sleeves which can be replaceable.[^cite_note-Heywood_2018_p._11-23] Water-cooled engines contain passages in the engine block where cooling fluid circulates (the [water jacket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_jacket)). Some small engines are air-cooled, and instead of having a water jacket the cylinder block has fins protruding away from it to cool the engine by directly transferring heat to the air. The cylinder walls are usually finished by [honing to obtain a cross hatch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honing_\(metalworking\)#Cross-hatch_finish), which is able to retain more oil. A too rough surface would quickly harm the engine by excessive wear on the piston.

The [pistons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piston) are short cylindrical parts which seal one end of the cylinder from the high pressure of the compressed air and combustion products and slide continuously within it while the engine is in operation. In smaller engines, the pistons are made of aluminum; while in larger applications, they are typically made of cast iron.[^cite_note-Heywood_2018_p._11-23] In performance applications, pistons can also be titanium or forged steel for greater strength. The top surface of the piston is called its *crown* and is typically flat or concave. Some two-stroke engines use pistons with a [deflector head](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deflector_piston). Pistons are open at the bottom and hollow except for an integral reinforcement structure (the piston web). When an engine is working, the gas pressure in the combustion chamber exerts a force on the piston crown which is transferred through its web to a [gudgeon pin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gudgeon_pin). Each piston has [rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piston_ring) fitted around its circumference that mostly prevent the gases from leaking into the crankcase or the oil into the combustion chamber.[^cite_note-Yamagata_2005_p._6-25] A [ventilation system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crankcase_ventilation_system) drives the small amount of gas that escapes past the pistons during normal operation (the blow-by gases) out of the crankcase so that it does not accumulate contaminating the oil and creating corrosion.[^cite_note-Heywood_2018_p._11-23] In two-stroke gasoline engines the crankcase is part of the air–fuel path and due to the continuous flow of it, two-stroke engines do not need a separate crankcase ventilation system.



Valve train above a diesel engine cylinder head. This engine uses rocker arms but no pushrods.

The [cylinder head](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_head) is attached to the engine block by numerous [bolts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolt_\(fastener\)) or [studs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stud_\(fastener\)). It has several functions. The cylinder head seals the cylinders on the side opposite to the pistons; it contains short ducts (the *ports*) for intake and exhaust and the associated intake [valves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valves) that open to let the cylinder be filled with fresh air and exhaust valves that open to allow the combustion gases to escape. The valves are often [poppet valves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poppet_valve)[^cite_note-auto-26][^cite_note-27] but they can also be [rotary valves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotary_valve)[^cite_note-28] or [sleeve valves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sleeve_valve).[^cite_note-auto-26] However, 2-stroke crankcase scavenged engines connect the gas ports directly to the cylinder wall without poppet valves; the piston controls their opening and occlusion instead. The cylinder head also holds the [spark plug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spark_plug) in the case of spark ignition engines and the [injector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injector) for engines that use direct injection. All CI (compression ignition) engines use fuel injection, usually direct injection but some engines instead use [indirect injection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indirect_injection). SI (spark ignition) engines can use a [carburetor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carburetor) or fuel injection as port injection or [direct injection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gasoline_direct_injection). Most SI engines have a single spark plug per cylinder but [some have 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_ignition). A [head gasket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_gasket) prevents the gas from leaking between the cylinder head and the engine block. The opening and closing of the valves is controlled by one or several [camshafts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camshaft) and springs—or in some engines—a [desmodromic mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desmodromic_valve) that uses no springs. The camshaft may press directly the stem of the valve or may act upon a [rocker arm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocker_arm), again, either directly or through a [pushrod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pushrod).



Engine block seen from below. The cylinders, oil spray nozzle and half of the main bearings are clearly visible.

The crankcase is sealed at the bottom with a [sump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wet_sump) that collects the falling oil during normal operation to be cycled again. The cavity created between the cylinder block and the sump houses a [crankshaft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crankshaft) that converts the reciprocating motion of the pistons to rotational motion. The crankshaft is held in place relative to the engine block by [main bearings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_bearing), which allow it to rotate. Bulkheads in the crankcase form a half of every main bearing; the other half is a detachable cap. In some cases a single *main bearing deck* is used rather than several smaller caps. A [connecting rod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connecting_rod) is connected to offset sections of the crankshaft (the [crankpins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crankpin)) in one end and to the piston in the other end through the gudgeon pin and thus transfers the force and translates the reciprocating motion of the pistons to the circular motion of the crankshaft. The end of the connecting rod attached to the gudgeon pin is called its small end, and the other end, where it is connected to the crankshaft, the big end. The big end has a detachable half to allow assembly around the crankshaft. It is kept together to the connecting rod by removable bolts.

The cylinder head has an [intake manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intake_manifold) and an [exhaust manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exhaust_manifold) attached to the corresponding ports. The intake manifold connects to the [air filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_filter) directly, or to a carburetor when one is present, which is then connected to the [air filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_filter). It distributes the air incoming from these devices to the individual cylinders. The exhaust manifold is the first component in the [exhaust system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exhaust_system). It collects the exhaust gases from the cylinders and drives it to the following component in the path. The [exhaust system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exhaust_system) of an ICE may also include a [catalytic converter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalytic_converter) and [muffler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muffler). The final section in the path of the exhaust gases is the [tailpipe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tailpipe).

### Four-stroke engines

Main article: [Four-stroke engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-stroke_engine)



Diagram showing the operation of a 4-stroke SI engine. Labels:\
1 ‐ Induction\
2 ‐ Compression\
3 ‐ Power\
4 ‐ Exhaust

The *top dead center* (TDC) of a piston is the position where it is nearest to the valves; *bottom dead center* (BDC) is the opposite position where it is furthest from them. A *stroke* is the movement of a piston from TDC to BDC or vice versa, together with the associated process. While an engine is in operation, the crankshaft rotates continuously at a nearly constant [speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_speed). In a 4-stroke ICE, each piston experiences 2 strokes per crankshaft revolution in the following order. Starting the description at TDC, these are:[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStone19921–2-29][^cite_note-FOOTNOTENunney20075-30]

1. Intake, induction or suction: The intake valves are open as a result of the cam lobe pressing down on the valve stem. The piston moves downward increasing the volume of the combustion chamber and allowing air to enter in the case of a CI engine or an air-fuel mix in the case of SI engines that do not use [direct injection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gasoline_direct_injection). The air or air-fuel mixture is called the *charge* in any case.

2. Compression: In this stroke, both valves are closed and the piston moves upward reducing the combustion chamber volume which reaches its minimum when the piston is at TDC. The piston performs [work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Work_\(physics\)) on the charge as it is being compressed; as a result, its pressure, temperature and density increase; an approximation to this behavior is provided by the [ideal gas law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_gas_law). Just before the piston reaches TDC, ignition begins. In the case of a SI engine, the spark plug receives a high voltage pulse that generates the spark which gives it its name and ignites the charge. In the case of a CI engine, the fuel injector quickly injects fuel into the combustion chamber as a spray; the fuel ignites due to the high temperature.

3. Power or working stroke: The pressure of the combustion gases pushes the piston downward, generating more [kinetic energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_energy) than is required to compress the charge. Complementary to the compression stroke, the combustion gases expand and as a result their temperature, pressure and density decreases. When the piston is near to BDC the exhaust valve opens. In the *blowdown*, the combustion gases expand [irreversibly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreversible_process) due to the leftover pressure—in excess of [back pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Back_pressure), the gauge pressure on the exhaust port.

4. Exhaust: The exhaust valve remains open while the piston moves upward expelling the combustion gases. For naturally aspirated engines a small part of the combustion gases may remain in the cylinder during normal operation because the piston does not close the combustion chamber completely; these gases dissolve in the next charge. At the end of this stroke, the exhaust valve closes, the intake valve opens, and the sequence repeats in the next cycle. The intake valve may open before the exhaust valve closes to allow better scavenging.

### Two-stroke engines

Main article: [Two-stroke engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-stroke_engine)

The defining characteristic of this kind of engine is that each piston completes a cycle every crankshaft revolution. The 4 processes of intake, compression, power and exhaust take place in only 2 strokes so that it is not possible to dedicate a stroke exclusively for each of them. Starting at TDC the cycle consists of:

1. Power: While the piston is descending the combustion gases perform work on it, as in a 4-stroke engine. The same [thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamics) for the expansion apply.

2. Scavenging: Around 75° of crankshaft rotation before BDC the exhaust valve or port opens, and blowdown occurs. Shortly thereafter the intake valve or transfer port opens. The incoming charge displaces the remaining combustion gases to the exhaust system and a part of the charge may enter the exhaust system as well. The piston reaches BDC and reverses direction. After the piston has traveled a short distance upwards into the cylinder the exhaust valve or port closes; shortly the intake valve or transfer port closes as well.

3. Compression: With both intake and exhaust closed the piston continues moving upwards compressing the charge and performing work on it. As in the case of a 4-stroke engine, ignition starts just before the piston reaches TDC and the same consideration on the thermodynamics of the compression on the charge apply.

While a 4-stroke engine uses the piston as a [positive displacement pump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_displacement_pump) to accomplish scavenging taking 2 of the 4 strokes, a 2-stroke engine uses the last part of the power stroke and the first part of the compression stroke for combined intake and exhaust. The work required to displace the charge and exhaust gases comes from either the crankcase or a separate blower. For scavenging, expulsion of burned gas and entry of fresh mix, two main approaches are described: Loop scavenging, and Uniflow scavenging. SAE news published in the 2010s that 'Loop Scavenging' is better under any circumstance than Uniflow Scavenging.[^cite_note-first-hand.info_2_stroke_diesel-20]

#### Crankcase scavenged



Diagram of a crankcase scavenged valveless 2-stroke engine in operation

Some SI engines are crankcase scavenged and do not use poppet valves. Instead, the crankcase and the part of the cylinder below the piston is used as a pump. The intake port is connected to the crankcase through a [reed valve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reed_valve) or a rotary disk valve driven by the engine. For each cylinder, a transfer port connects in one end to the crankcase and in the other end to the cylinder wall. The exhaust port is connected directly to the cylinder wall. The transfer and exhaust port are opened and closed by the piston. The reed valve opens when the crankcase pressure is slightly below intake pressure, to let it be filled with a new charge; this happens when the piston is moving upwards. When the piston is moving downwards the pressure in the crankcase increases and the reed valve closes promptly, then the charge in the crankcase is compressed. When the piston is moving downwards, it also uncovers the exhaust port and the transfer port and the higher pressure of the charge in the crankcase makes it enter the cylinder through the transfer port, blowing the exhaust gases. Lubrication is accomplished by adding *[two-stroke oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-stroke_oil)* to the fuel in small ratios. *Petroil* refers to the mix of gasoline with the aforesaid oil. This kind of 2-stroke engine has a lower efficiency than comparable 4-strokes engines and releases more polluting [exhaust gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exhaust_gas) for the following conditions:

- They use a *[total-loss oiling system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total-loss_oiling_system)*: all the lubricating oil is eventually burned along with the fuel.

- There are conflicting requirements for scavenging: On one side, enough fresh charge needs to be introduced in each cycle to displace almost all the combustion gases but introducing too much of it means that a part of it gets in the exhaust.

- They must use the transfer port(s) as a carefully designed and placed nozzle so that a gas current is created in a way that it sweeps the whole cylinder before reaching the exhaust port so as to expel the combustion gases, but minimize the amount of charge exhausted. 4-stroke engines have the benefit of forcibly expelling almost all of the combustion gases because during exhaust the combustion chamber is reduced to its minimum volume. In crankcase scavenged 2-stroke engines, exhaust and intake are performed mostly simultaneously and with the combustion chamber at its maximum volume.

The main advantage of 2-stroke engines of this type is mechanical simplicity and a higher [power-to-weight ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power-to-weight_ratio) than their 4-stroke counterparts. Despite having twice as many power strokes per cycle, less than twice the power of a comparable 4-stroke engine is attainable in practice.

In the US, 2-stroke engines were banned for road vehicles due to the pollution. Off-road only motorcycles are still often 2-stroke but are rarely road legal. However, many thousands of 2-stroke lawn maintenance engines are in use.

#### Blower scavenged



Diagram of uniflow scavenging

Using a separate blower avoids many of the shortcomings of crankcase scavenging, at the expense of increased complexity which means a higher cost and an increase in maintenance requirement. An engine of this type uses ports or valves for intake and valves for exhaust, except [opposed piston engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opposed_piston_engine), which may also use ports for exhaust. The blower is usually of the [Roots-type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roots_blower) but other types have been used too. This design is commonplace in CI engines, and has been occasionally used in SI engines.

CI engines that use a blower typically use *[uniflow scavenging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniflow_scavenging)*. In this design the cylinder wall contains several intake ports placed uniformly spaced along the circumference just above the position that the piston crown reaches when at BDC. An exhaust valve or several like that of 4-stroke engines is used. The final part of the intake manifold is an air sleeve that feeds the intake ports. The intake ports are placed at a horizontal angle to the cylinder wall (I.e: they are in plane of the piston crown) to give a swirl to the incoming charge to improve combustion. The largest reciprocating IC are low speed CI engines of this type; they are used for marine propulsion (see [marine diesel engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_diesel_engine)) or [electric power generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_power_generation) and achieve the highest thermal efficiencies among internal combustion engines of any kind. Some diesel–electric [locomotive engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locomotive_engine) operate on the 2-stroke cycle. The most powerful of them have a brake power of around 4.5 [MW](21347693.md) or 6,000 [HP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horsepower). The [EMD SD90MAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EMD_SD90MAC) class of locomotives are an example of such. The comparable class [GE AC6000CW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GE_AC6000CW), whose prime mover has almost the same brake power, uses a 4-stroke engine.

An example of this type of engine is the [Wärtsilä-Sulzer RTA96-C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W%C3%A4rtsil%C3%A4-Sulzer_RTA96-C) turbocharged 2-stroke diesel, used in large container ships. It is the most efficient and powerful reciprocating internal combustion engine in the world with a [thermal efficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_efficiency) over 50%.[^cite_note-31][^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33] For comparison, the most efficient small four-stroke engines are around 43% thermally-efficient (SAE 900648); size is an advantage for efficiency due to the increase in the ratio of volume to surface area.

See the [external links](#External_links) for an in-cylinder combustion video in a 2-stroke, optically accessible motorcycle engine.

#### Historical design

[Dugald Clerk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dugald_Clerk) developed the first two-cycle engine in 1879. It used a separate cylinder which functioned as a pump in order to transfer the fuel mixture to the cylinder.[^cite_note-first-hand.info_2_stroke_diesel-20]

In 1899 [John Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Day_\(inventor\)) simplified Clerk's design into the type of 2 cycle engine that is very widely used today.[^cite_note-first-hand.info_2_spark_ignition-34] Day cycle engines are crankcase scavenged and port timed. The crankcase and the part of the cylinder below the exhaust port is used as a pump. The operation of the Day cycle engine begins when the crankshaft is turned so that the piston moves from BDC upward (toward the head) creating a vacuum in the crankcase/cylinder area. The carburetor then feeds the fuel mixture into the crankcase through a [reed valve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reed_valve) or a rotary disk valve (driven by the engine). There are cast in ducts from the crankcase to the port in the cylinder to provide for intake and another from the exhaust port to the exhaust pipe. The height of the port in relationship to the length of the cylinder is called the "port timing".

On the first upstroke of the engine there would be no fuel inducted into the cylinder as the crankcase was empty. On the downstroke, the piston now compresses the fuel mix, which has lubricated the piston in the cylinder and the bearings due to the fuel mix having oil added to it. As the piston moves downward it first uncovers the exhaust, but on the first stroke there is no burnt fuel to exhaust. As the piston moves downward further, it uncovers the intake port which has a duct that runs to the crankcase. Since the fuel mix in the crankcase is under pressure, the mix moves through the duct and into the cylinder.

Because there is no obstruction in the cylinder of the fuel to move directly out of the exhaust port prior to the piston rising far enough to close the port, early engines used a high domed piston to slow down the flow of fuel. Later the fuel was "resonated" back into the cylinder using an expansion chamber design. When the piston rose close to TDC, a spark ignited the fuel. As the piston is driven downward with power, it first uncovers the exhaust port where the burned fuel is expelled under high pressure and then the intake port where the process has been completed and will keep repeating.

Later engines used a type of porting devised by the Deutz company to improve performance. It was called the [Schnurle Reverse Flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schnuerle_porting) system. In 1932, [Auto Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auto_Union) AG acquired an exclusive license for high-speed two-stroke engines. Later that same year, the DKW Block 350 became the first motorcycle model to be converted to the newly acquired technology. The further development of the reverse scavenging system by Auto Union AG revolutionized the construction of small two-stroke engines.[^cite_note-Rauch,_pp._56–63-35]

### Ignition

Internal combustion engines require ignition of the mixture, either by [spark ignition (SI)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spark-ignition_engine) or [compression ignition (CI)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diesel_engine). Before the invention of reliable electrical methods, hot tube and flame methods were used. Experimental engines with [laser ignition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_ignition) have been built.[^cite_note-36]

#### Spark ignition process



Bosch magneto

Main article: [Spark-ignition engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spark-ignition_engine)



Points and coil ignition

The spark-ignition engine was a refinement of the early engines which used Hot Tube ignition. When Bosch developed the [magneto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignition_magneto) it became the primary system for producing electricity to energize a spark plug.[^cite_note-37] Many small engines still use magneto ignition. Small engines are started by hand cranking using a [recoil starter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recoil_start) or hand crank. Prior to [Charles F. Kettering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_F._Kettering) of Delco's development of the automotive starter all gasoline engined automobiles used a hand crank.[^cite_note-38]

Larger engines typically power their [starting motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starter_\(engine\)) and [ignition systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignition_system) using the electrical energy stored in a [lead–acid battery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead%E2%80%93acid_battery). The battery's charged state is maintained by an [automotive alternator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternator#Automotive_alternators) or (previously) a generator which uses engine power to create electrical energy storage.

The battery supplies electrical power for starting when the engine has a [starting motor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starter_\(engine\)) system, and supplies electrical power when the engine is off. The battery also supplies electrical power during rare run conditions where the alternator cannot maintain more than 13.8 volts (for a common 12 V automotive electrical system). As alternator voltage falls below 13.8 volts, the lead-acid storage battery increasingly picks up electrical load. During virtually all running conditions, including normal idle conditions, the alternator supplies primary electrical power.

Some systems disable alternator field (rotor) power during wide-open throttle conditions. Disabling the field reduces alternator pulley mechanical loading to nearly zero, maximizing crankshaft power. In this case, the battery supplies all primary electrical power.

Gasoline engines take in a mixture of air and gasoline and compress it by the movement of the piston from bottom dead center to top dead center when the fuel is at maximum compression. The reduction in the size of the swept area of the cylinder and taking into account the volume of the combustion chamber is described by a ratio. Early engines had [compression ratios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compression_ratio) of 6 to 1. As compression ratios were increased, the efficiency of the engine increased as well.

With early induction and ignition systems the compression ratios had to be kept low. With advances in fuel technology and combustion management, high-performance engines can run reliably at 12\:1 ratio. With low octane fuel, a problem would occur as the compression ratio increased as the fuel was igniting due to the rise in temperature that resulted. [Charles Kettering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Kettering) developed a [lead additive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetraethyllead) which allowed higher compression ratios, which was progressively [abandoned for automotive use](23639.md#Additives) from the 1970s onward, partly due to [lead poisoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_poisoning) concerns.

The fuel mixture is ignited at different progressions of the piston in the cylinder. At low rpm, the spark is timed to occur close to the piston achieving top dead center. In order to produce more power, as rpm rises the spark is advanced sooner during piston movement. The spark occurs while the fuel is still being compressed progressively more as rpm rises.[^cite_note-39]

The necessary high voltage, typically 10,000 volts, is supplied by an [induction coil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Induction_coil) or transformer. The induction coil is a fly-back system, using interruption of electrical primary system current through some type of synchronized interrupter. The interrupter can be either contact points or a power transistor. The problem with this type of ignition is that as RPM increases the availability of electrical energy decreases. This is especially a problem, since the amount of energy needed to ignite a more dense fuel mixture is higher. The result was often a high RPM misfire.

[Capacitor discharge ignition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capacitor_discharge_ignition) was developed. It produces a rising voltage that is sent to the spark plug. CD system voltages can reach 60,000 volts.[^cite_note-40] CD ignitions use step-up [transformers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformer). The step-up transformer uses energy stored in a capacitance to generate [electric spark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_spark). With either system, a mechanical or electrical control system provides a carefully timed high-voltage to the proper cylinder. This spark, via the spark plug, ignites the air-fuel mixture in the engine's cylinders.

While gasoline internal combustion engines are much easier to start in cold weather than diesel engines, they can still have cold weather starting problems under extreme conditions. For years, the solution was to park the car in heated areas. In some parts of the world, the oil was actually drained and heated overnight and returned to the engine for cold starts. In the early 1950s, the gasoline Gasifier unit was developed, where, on cold weather starts, raw gasoline was diverted to the unit where part of the fuel was burned causing the other part to become a hot vapor sent directly to the intake valve manifold. This unit was quite popular until electric [engine block heaters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engine_block_heater) became standard on gasoline engines sold in cold climates.[^cite_note-41]

#### Compression ignition process

Main article: [Diesel engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diesel_engine)

For ignition, diesel, [PPC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partially_premixed_combustion) and [HCCI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneous_charge_compression_ignition) engines rely solely on the high temperature and pressure created by the engine in its compression process. The compression level that occurs is usually twice or more than a gasoline engine. Diesel engines take in air only, and shortly before peak compression, spray a small quantity of diesel fuel into the cylinder via a fuel injector that allows the fuel to instantly ignite. HCCI type engines take in both air and fuel, but continue to rely on an unaided auto-combustion process, due to higher pressures and temperature. This is also why diesel and HCCI engines are more susceptible to cold-starting issues, although they run just as well in cold weather once started. Light duty diesel engines with [indirect injection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indirect_injection) in automobiles and light trucks employ [glowplugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glow_plug_\(diesel_engine\)) (or other pre-heating: see [Cummins ISB#6BT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cummins_B_Series_engine)) that pre-heat the [combustion chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion_chamber) just before starting to reduce no-start conditions in cold weather. Most diesels also have a battery and charging system; nevertheless, this system is secondary and is added by manufacturers as a luxury for the ease of starting, turning fuel on and off (which can also be done via a switch or mechanical apparatus), and for running auxiliary electrical components and accessories. Most new engines rely on electrical and electronic [engine control units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engine_control_unit) (ECU) that also adjust the combustion process to increase efficiency and reduce emissions.

### Lubrication



Diagram of an engine using pressurized lubrication

Wikimedia Commons logo

Wikimedia Commons has media related to [Internal combustion piston engine lubrication systems](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category\:Internal%20combustion%20piston%20engine%20lubrication%20systems).

Surfaces in contact and relative motion to other surfaces require [lubrication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lubrication) to reduce wear, noise and increase efficiency by reducing the power wasting in overcoming [friction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friction), or to make the mechanism work at all. Also, the lubricant used can reduce excess heat and provide additional cooling to components. At the very least, an engine requires lubrication in the following parts:

- Between pistons and cylinders

- Small bearings

- Big end bearings

- Main bearings

- Valve gear (The following elements may not be present):&#x20;

  - Tappets

  - Rocker arms

  - Pushrods

  - Timing chain or gears. Toothed belts do not require lubrication.

In 2-stroke crankcase scavenged engines, the interior of the crankcase, and therefore the crankshaft, connecting rod and bottom of the pistons are sprayed by the [two-stroke oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-stroke_oil) in the air-fuel-oil mixture which is then burned along with the fuel. The valve train may be contained in a compartment flooded with lubricant so that no [oil pump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_pump_\(internal_combustion_engine\)) is required.

In a *splash lubrication system* no oil pump is used. Instead the crankshaft dips into the oil in the sump and due to its high speed, it splashes the crankshaft, connecting rods and bottom of the pistons. The connecting rod big end caps may have an attached scoop to enhance this effect. The valve train may also be sealed in a flooded compartment, or open to the crankshaft in a way that it receives splashed oil and allows it to drain back to the sump. Splash lubrication is common for small 4-stroke engines.

In a *forced* (also called *pressurized*) *lubrication system*, lubrication is accomplished in a closed-loop which carries [motor oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motor_oil) to the surfaces serviced by the system and then returns the oil to a reservoir. The auxiliary equipment of an engine is typically not serviced by this loop; for instance, an [alternator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternator_\(automotive\)) may use [ball bearings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ball_bearing) sealed with their own lubricant. The reservoir for the oil is usually the sump, and when this is the case, it is called a *[wet sump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wet_sump)* system. When there is a different oil reservoir the crankcase still catches it, but it is continuously drained by a dedicated pump; this is called a *[dry sump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dry_sump)* system.

On its bottom, the sump contains an oil intake covered by a mesh filter which is connected to an oil pump then to an [oil filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_filter) outside the crankcase. From there it is diverted to the crankshaft main bearings and valve train. The crankcase contains at least one *oil gallery* (a conduit inside a crankcase wall) to which oil is introduced from the oil filter. The main bearings contain a groove through all or half its circumference; the oil enters these grooves from channels connected to the oil gallery. The crankshaft has drillings that take oil from these grooves and deliver it to the big end bearings. All big end bearings are lubricated this way. A single main bearing may provide oil for 0, 1 or 2 big end bearings. A similar system may be used to lubricate the piston, its gudgeon pin and the small end of its connecting rod; in this system, the connecting rod big end has a groove around the crankshaft and a drilling connected to the groove which distributes oil from there to the bottom of the piston and from then to the cylinder.

Other systems are also used to lubricate the cylinder and piston. The connecting rod may have a nozzle to throw an oil jet to the cylinder and bottom of the piston. That nozzle is in movement relative to the cylinder it lubricates, but always pointed towards it or the corresponding piston.

Typically forced lubrication systems have a lubricant flow higher than what is required to lubricate satisfactorily, in order to assist with cooling. Specifically, the lubricant system helps to move heat from the hot engine parts to the cooling liquid (in water-cooled engines) or fins (in air-cooled engines) which then transfer it to the environment. The lubricant must be designed to be chemically stable and maintain suitable viscosities within the temperature range it encounters in the engine.

### Cylinder configuration

Common cylinder configurations include the [straight or inline configuration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straight_engine), the more compact [V configuration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V_engine), and the wider but smoother [flat or boxer configuration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_engine). [Aircraft engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_engine) can also adopt a [radial configuration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radial_engine), which allows more effective cooling. More unusual configurations such as the [H](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H_engine), [U](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U_engine), [X](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X_engine), and [W](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W_engine) have also been used.

Multiple cylinder engines have their valve train and crankshaft configured so that pistons are at different parts of their cycle. It is desirable to have the pistons' cycles uniformly spaced (this is called *even firing*) especially in forced induction engines; this reduces torque pulsations[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENunney200715-42] and makes [inline engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straight_engine) with more than 3 cylinders statically [balanced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engine_balance) in its primary forces. However, some [engine configurations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engine_configuration) require odd firing to achieve better balance than what is possible with even firing. For instance, a 4-stroke [I2 engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straight-twin_engine) has better balance when the angle between the crankpins is 180° because the pistons move in opposite directions and inertial forces partially cancel, but this gives an odd firing pattern where one cylinder fires 180° of crankshaft rotation after the other, then no cylinder fires for 540°. With an even firing pattern, the pistons would move in unison and the associated forces would add.

Multiple crankshaft configurations do not necessarily need a [cylinder head](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_head) at all because they can instead have a piston at each end of the cylinder called an [opposed piston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opposed_piston) design. Because fuel inlets and outlets are positioned at opposed ends of the cylinder, one can achieve uniflow scavenging, which, as in the four-stroke engine is efficient over a wide range of engine speeds. Thermal efficiency is improved because of a lack of cylinder heads. This design was used in the [Junkers Jumo 205](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junkers_Jumo_205) diesel aircraft engine, using two crankshafts at either end of a single bank of cylinders, and most remarkably in the [Napier Deltic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napier_Deltic) diesel engines. These used three crankshafts to serve three banks of [double-ended cylinders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opposed-piston_engine) arranged in an equilateral triangle with the crankshafts at the corners. It was also used in single-bank [locomotive engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locomotive_engine), and is still used in [marine propulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_propulsion) engines and marine auxiliary generators.

### Diesel cycle

Main article: [Diesel cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diesel_cycle)



[p–V diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%E2%80%93V_diagram) for the ideal diesel cycle. The cycle follows the numbers 1–4 in clockwise direction.

Most truck and automotive diesel engines use a cycle reminiscent of a four-stroke cycle, but with temperature increase by compression causing ignition, rather than needing a separate ignition system. This variation is called the diesel cycle. In the diesel cycle, diesel fuel is injected directly into the cylinder so that combustion occurs at constant pressure, as the piston moves.

### Otto cycle

The [Otto cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_cycle) is the most common cycle for most cars' internal combustion engines that use gasoline as a fuel. It consists of the same major steps as described for the four-stroke engine: Intake, compression, ignition, expansion and exhaust.

### Five-stroke engine

In 1879, [Nicolaus Otto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaus_Otto) manufactured and sold a double expansion engine (the double and triple expansion principles had ample usage in steam engines), with two small cylinders at both sides of a low-pressure larger cylinder, where a second expansion of exhaust stroke gas took place; the owner returned it, alleging poor performance. In 1906, the concept was incorporated in a car built by EHV ([Eisenhuth Horseless Vehicle Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eisenhuth_Horseless_Vehicle_Company));[^cite_note-43] and in the 21st century [Ilmor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilmor) designed and successfully tested a 5-stroke double expansion internal combustion engine, with high power output and low SFC (Specific Fuel Consumption).[^cite_note-44]

### Six-stroke engine

The [six-stroke engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six-stroke_engine) was invented in 1883. Four kinds of six-stroke engines use a regular piston in a regular cylinder (Griffin six-stroke, Bajulaz six-stroke, Velozeta six-stroke and Crower six-stroke), firing every three crankshaft revolutions. These systems capture the waste heat of the [four-stroke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-stroke) Otto cycle with an injection of air or water.

The [Beare Head](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beare_Head) and "piston charger" engines operate as [opposed-piston engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opposed-piston_engine), two pistons in a single cylinder, firing every two revolutions rather than every four like a four-stroke engine.

### Other cycles

The first internal combustion engines did not compress the mixture. The first part of the piston downstroke drew in a fuel-air mixture, then the inlet valve closed and, in the remainder of the down-stroke, the fuel-air mixture fired. The exhaust valve opened for the piston upstroke. These attempts at imitating the principle of a [steam engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_engine) were very inefficient. There are a number of variations of these cycles, most notably the [Atkinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atkinson_cycle) and [Miller cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller_cycle).

[Split-cycle engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split-cycle_engine) separate the four strokes of intake, compression, combustion and exhaust into two separate but paired cylinders. The first cylinder is used for intake and compression. The compressed air is then transferred through a crossover passage from the compression cylinder into the second cylinder, where combustion and exhaust occur. A split-cycle engine is really an [air compressor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_compressor) on one side with a combustion chamber on the other.

Previous split-cycle engines have had two major problems—poor breathing (volumetric efficiency) and low thermal efficiency. However, new designs are being introduced that seek to address these problems. The [Scuderi Engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scuderi_Engine) addresses the breathing problem by reducing the clearance between the piston and the cylinder head through various turbocharging techniques. The Scuderi design requires the use of outwardly opening valves that enable the piston to move very close to the cylinder head without the interference of the valves. Scuderi addresses the low thermal efficiency via firing after top dead center (ATDC).

Firing ATDC can be accomplished by using high-pressure air in the transfer passage to create sonic flow and high turbulence in the power cylinder.

## Combustion turbines

### Jet engine

Main article: [Jet engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_engine)



Turbofan jet engine

Jet engines use a number of rows of fan blades to compress air which then enters a [combustor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustor) where it is mixed with fuel (typically JP fuel) and then ignited. The burning of the fuel raises the temperature of the air which is then exhausted out of the engine creating thrust. A modern [turbofan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbofan) engine can operate at as high as 48% efficiency.[^cite_note-45]

There are six sections to a turbofan engine:

- Fan

- Compressor

- Combustor

- Turbine

- Mixer

- Nozzle

### Gas turbines

Main article: [Gas turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_turbine)



Turbine power plant

A gas turbine compresses air and uses it to turn a [turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbine). It is essentially a jet engine which directs its output to a shaft. There are three stages to a turbine: 1) air is drawn through a compressor where the temperature rises due to compression, 2) fuel is added in the [combustor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustor), and 3) hot air is exhausted through turbine blades which rotate a shaft connected to the compressor.

A gas turbine is a rotary machine similar in principle to a [steam turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_turbine) and it consists of three main components: a compressor, a combustion chamber, and a turbine. The temperature of the air, after being compressed in the compressor, is increased by burning fuel in it. The heated air and the products of combustion expand in a turbine, producing work output. About 2⁄3 of the work drives the compressor: the rest (about 1⁄3) is available as useful work output.[^cite_note-GePowerGen-47]

Gas turbines are among the most efficient internal combustion engines. The General Electric 7HA and 9HA turbine [combined cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combined_cycle) electrical plants are rated at over 61% efficiency.[^cite_note-GasTurbineWorld-48]

### Brayton cycle

Main article: [Brayton cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brayton_cycle)



Brayton cycle

A gas turbine is a rotary machine somewhat similar in principle to a steam turbine. It consists of three main components: compressor, combustion chamber, and turbine. The air is compressed by the compressor where a temperature rise occurs. The temperature of the compressed air is further increased by combustion of injected fuel in the combustion chamber which expands the air. This energy rotates the turbine which powers the compressor via a mechanical coupling. The hot gases are then exhausted to provide thrust.

Gas turbine cycle engines employ a continuous combustion system where compression, combustion, and expansion occur simultaneously at different places in the engine—giving continuous power. Notably, the combustion takes place at constant pressure, rather than with the Otto cycle, constant volume.

## Wankel engines



The Wankel rotary cycle. The shaft turns three times for each rotation of the rotor around the lobe and once for each [orbital revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_revolution) around the eccentric shaft.

Main article: [Wankel engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wankel_engine)

The Wankel engine (rotary engine) does not have piston strokes. It operates with the same separation of phases as the four-stroke engine with the phases taking place in separate locations in the engine. In [thermodynamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamics) terms it follows the [Otto engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_engine) cycle, so may be thought of as a "four-phase" engine. While it is true that three power strokes typically occur per rotor revolution, due to the 3\:1 revolution ratio of the rotor to the eccentric shaft, only one power stroke per shaft revolution actually occurs. The drive (eccentric) shaft rotates once during every power stroke instead of twice (crankshaft), as in the Otto cycle, giving it a greater power-to-weight ratio than piston engines. This type of engine was most notably used in the [Mazda RX-8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mazda_RX-8), the earlier [RX-7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mazda_RX-7), and other vehicle models. The engine is also used in unmanned aerial vehicles, where the small size and weight and the high power-to-weight ratio are advantageous.

## Forced induction

Main article: [Forced induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forced_induction)

Forced induction is the process of delivering compressed air to the intake of an internal combustion engine. A forced induction engine uses a [gas compressor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_compressor) to increase the pressure, temperature and [density of the air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density_of_air). An engine without forced induction is considered a [naturally aspirated engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturally_aspirated_engine).

Forced induction is used in the automotive and aviation industry to increase engine power and efficiency. It particularly helps aviation engines, as they need to operate at high altitude.

Forced induction is achieved by a [supercharger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercharger), where the compressor is directly powered from the engine shaft or, in the [turbocharger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbocharger), from a turbine powered by the engine exhaust.

## Fuels and oxidizers

All internal combustion engines depend on [combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion) of a [chemical fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_fuel), typically with oxygen from the air (though it is possible to inject [nitrous oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrous_oxide) to do more of the same thing and gain a power boost). The combustion process typically results in the production of a great quantity of thermal energy, as well as the production of steam and carbon dioxide and other chemicals at very high temperature; the [temperature reached](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adiabatic_flame_temperature) is determined by the chemical make up of the fuel and oxidizers (see [stoichiometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoichiometry)), as well as by the compression and other factors.

### Fuels

The most common modern fuels are made up of [hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon) and are derived mostly from [fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel) ([petroleum](23195.md)). Fossil fuels include [diesel fuel](81761.md), [gasoline](23639.md) and [petroleum gas](361384.md), and the rarer use of [propane](23643.md). Except for the fuel delivery components, most internal combustion engines that are designed for gasoline use can run on [natural gas](22131.md) or liquefied petroleum gases without major modifications. Large diesels can run with air mixed with gases and a pilot diesel fuel ignition injection. Liquid and gaseous [biofuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biofuel), such as [ethanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethanol_fuel) and [biodiesel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiesel) (a form of diesel fuel that is produced from crops that yield [triglycerides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triglyceride) such as [soybean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soybean) oil), can also be used. Engines with appropriate modifications can also run on [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) gas, [wood gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood_gas), or [charcoal gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood_gas), as well as from so-called [producer gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood_gas) made from other convenient biomass. Experiments have also been conducted using powdered solid fuels, such as the [magnesium injection cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium_injection_cycle).

#### Hydrogen

Main article: [Hydrogen internal combustion engine vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_internal_combustion_engine_vehicle)

Hydrogen could eventually [replace conventional fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_economy) in traditional internal combustion engines. Alternatively [fuel cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_cell) technology may come to deliver its promise and the use of the internal combustion engines could even be phased out.

Although there are multiple ways of producing free hydrogen, those methods require converting combustible molecules into hydrogen or consuming electric energy. Unless that electricity is produced from a renewable source—and is not required for other purposes—hydrogen does not solve any [energy crisis](261316.md). In many situations the disadvantage of hydrogen, relative to carbon fuels, is [its storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_economy#Storage). [Liquid hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_hydrogen) has extremely low density (14 times lower than water) and requires extensive insulation—whilst gaseous hydrogen requires heavy tankage. Even when liquefied, hydrogen has a higher [specific energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_energy) but the volumetric energetic storage is still roughly five times lower than gasoline. However, the energy density of hydrogen is considerably higher than that of electric batteries, making it a serious contender as an energy carrier to replace fossil fuels. The 'Hydrogen on Demand' process (see [direct borohydride fuel cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_borohydride_fuel_cell)) creates hydrogen as needed, but has other issues, such as the high price of the [sodium borohydride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_borohydride) that is the raw material.

## Cooling

Main article: [Internal combustion engine cooling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_combustion_engine_cooling)

Cooling is required to remove excessive heat—high temperature can cause engine failure, usually from wear (due to high-temperature-induced failure of lubrication), cracking or warping. Two most common forms of engine cooling are [air-cooled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air-cooled_engine) and [water-cooled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water-cooled#Automotive_usage). Most modern automotive engines are both water and air-cooled, as the water/liquid-coolant is carried to air-cooled fins and/or fans, whereas larger engines may be singularly water-cooled as they are stationary and have a constant supply of water through water-mains or fresh-water, while most power tool engines and other small engines are air-cooled. Some engines (air or water-cooled) also have an [oil cooler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_cooling). In some engines, especially for [turbine engine blade cooling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbine_blade#Cooling) and [liquid rocket engine cooling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regenerative_cooling_\(rocket\)), fuel is used as a coolant, as it is simultaneously preheated before injecting it into a combustion chamber.

## Starting

Main article: [Starter motor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starter_motor)

Hand-cranking a boat diesel motor in [Inle Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inle_Lake) ([Myanmar](19457.md))



Electric starter as used in automobiles

Internal combustion engines must have their cycles started. In reciprocating engines this is accomplished by turning the crankshaft (Wankel Rotor Shaft) which induces the cycles of intake, compression, combustion, and exhaust. The first engines were started with a turn of their [flywheels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flywheel), while the first vehicle (the Daimler Reitwagen) was started with a hand crank. All ICE engined automobiles were started with hand cranks until [Charles Kettering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Kettering) developed the electric starter for automobiles.[^cite_note-51] This method is now the most widely used, even among non-automobiles.

As diesel engines have become larger and their mechanisms heavier, [air starters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air-start_system) have come into use.[^cite_note-52] This is due to the lack of torque in electric starters. Air starters work by pumping compressed air into the cylinders of an engine to start it turning.

Two-wheeled vehicles may have their engines started in one of four ways:

- By pedaling, as on a bicycle

- By pushing the vehicle and then engaging the clutch, known as "run-and-bump starting"

- By kicking downward on a single pedal, known as "kick starting"

- By an electric starter, as in cars

There are also starters where a spring is compressed by a crank motion and then used to start an engine.

Some small engines use a pull-rope mechanism called "recoil starting", as the rope rewinds itself after it has been pulled out to start the engine. This method is commonly used in pushed lawn mowers and other settings where only a small amount of torque is needed to turn an engine over.

Turbine engines are frequently started by an electric motor or by compressed air.

## Measures of engine performance

Engine types vary greatly in a number of different ways:

- [energy efficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_efficiency)

- fuel/propellant consumption ([brake specific fuel consumption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brake_specific_fuel_consumption) for shaft engines, [thrust specific fuel consumption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrust_specific_fuel_consumption) for jet engines)

- [power-to-weight ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power-to-weight_ratio)

- [thrust to weight ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrust_to_weight_ratio)

- [torque curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_torque) (for shaft engines), [thrust lapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrust_lapse) (jet engines)

- [compression ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compression_ratio) for piston engines, [overall pressure ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overall_pressure_ratio) for jet engines and gas turbines

### Energy efficiency

Once ignited and burnt, the [combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion) products—hot gases—have more available [thermal energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_energy) than the original compressed fuel-air mixture (which had higher [chemical energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_energy)). This available energy is manifested as a higher [temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature) and [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure) that can be converted into [kinetic energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_energy) by the engine. In a reciprocating engine, the high-pressure gases inside the cylinders drive the engine's pistons.

Once the available energy has been removed, the remaining hot gases are [vented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exhaust_gas) (often by opening a [valve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poppet_valve) or exposing the exhaust outlet) and this allows the piston to return to its previous position (top dead center, or TDC). The piston can then proceed to the next phase of its cycle, which varies between engines. Any thermal energy that is not translated into work is normally considered a waste product and is removed from the engine either by an air or liquid cooling system.

Internal combustion engines are considered [heat engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_engine) (since the release of chemical energy in combustion has the same effect as [heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat) transfer into the engine) and as such their theoretical efficiency can be approximated by idealized [thermodynamic cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_cycle). The thermal efficiency of a theoretical cycle cannot exceed that of the [Carnot cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnot_cycle), whose efficiency is determined by the difference between the lower and upper [operating temperatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operating_temperature) of the engine. The upper operating temperature of an engine is limited by two main factors; the thermal operating limits of the materials, and the auto-ignition resistance of the fuel. All [metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal) and [alloys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alloy) have a thermal operating limit, and there is significant research into [ceramic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceramic) materials that can be made with greater thermal stability and desirable structural properties. Higher thermal stability allows for a greater temperature difference between the lower (ambient) and upper operating temperatures, hence greater thermodynamic efficiency. Also, as the cylinder temperature rises, the fuel becomes more prone to auto-ignition. This is caused when the cylinder temperature nears the flash point of the charge. At this point, ignition can spontaneously occur before the spark plug fires, causing excessive cylinder pressures. Auto-ignition can be mitigated by using fuels with high auto-ignition resistance ([octane rating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octane_rating)), however it still puts an upper bound on the allowable peak cylinder temperature.

The [thermodynamic limits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_limit) assume that the engine is operating under ideal conditions: a frictionless world, ideal gases, perfect insulators, and operation for infinite time. Real world applications introduce complexities that reduce efficiency. For example, a real engine runs best at a specific load, termed its [power band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_band). The engine in a car cruising on a highway is usually operating significantly below its ideal load, because it is designed for the higher loads required for rapid acceleration. In addition, factors such as [wind resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_resistance) reduce overall system efficiency. Vehicle [fuel economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_economy_in_automobiles) is measured in [miles per gallon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miles_per_gallon) or in liters per 100 kilometers. The volume of hydrocarbon assumes a standard energy content.

Even when aided with turbochargers and stock efficiency aids, most engines retain an *average* efficiency of about 18–20%.[^cite_note-53] However, the latest technologies in [Formula One engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formula_One_engines) have seen a boost in thermal efficiency past 50%.[^cite_note-54] There are many inventions aimed at increasing the efficiency of IC engines. In general, practical engines are always compromised by trade-offs between different properties such as efficiency, weight, power, heat, response, exhaust emissions, or noise. Sometimes economy also plays a role in the cost to manufacture an engine, and to manufacture and distribute the fuel. Increasing engine efficiency brings better fuel economy, but only if the fuel cost per energy content is the same.

### Measures of fuel efficiency and propellant efficiency

For stationary and shaft engines including propeller engines, fuel consumption is measured by calculating the [brake specific fuel consumption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brake_specific_fuel_consumption), which measures the mass flow rate of fuel consumption divided by the power produced.

For internal combustion engines in the form of jet engines, the power output varies drastically with airspeed and a less variable measure is used: [thrust specific fuel consumption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrust_specific_fuel_consumption) (TSFC), which is the mass of propellant needed to generate [impulses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impulse_\(physics\)) that is measured in either pound force-hour or the grams of propellant needed to generate an impulse that measures one kilonewton-second.

For rockets, TSFC can be used, but typically other equivalent measures are traditionally used, such as [specific impulse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_impulse) and [effective exhaust velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_exhaust_velocity).

### Air pollution

Internal combustion engines such as reciprocating internal combustion engines produce [air pollution](10934212.md) emissions, due to incomplete combustion of [carbonaceous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon) fuel. The main derivatives of the process are [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) CO<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub>, water and some [soot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soot)—also called [particulate matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_particulate_matter) (PM).[^cite_note-55] The effects of inhaling particulate matter have been studied in humans and animals and include asthma, lung cancer, cardiovascular issues, and premature death.[^cite_note-56] There are, however, some additional products of the combustion process that include [nitrogen oxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_oxide) and [sulfur](27127.md) and some uncombusted hydrocarbons, depending on the operating conditions and the fuel-air ratio.

Carbon dioxide emissions from internal combustion engines (particularly ones using [fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel) such as gasoline and diesel) contribute to human-induced [climate change](5042951.md). Increasing the engine's [fuel efficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_efficiency) can reduce, but not eliminate, the amount of CO<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub> emissions as carbon-based fuel combustion produces CO<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub>. Since removing CO<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub> from engine exhaust is impractical, there is increasing interest in alternatives. Sustainable fuels such as [biofuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biofuel), [synfuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synfuel), and [electric motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_motor) powered by batteries are examples.

Not all of the fuel is completely consumed by the combustion process. A small amount of fuel is present after combustion, and some of it reacts to form oxygenates, such as [formaldehyde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formaldehyde) or [acetaldehyde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetaldehyde), or hydrocarbons not originally present in the input fuel mixture. Incomplete combustion usually results from insufficient [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen) to achieve the perfect [stoichiometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoichiometric) ratio. The flame is "quenched" by the relatively cool cylinder walls, leaving behind unreacted fuel that is expelled with the exhaust. When running at lower speeds, quenching is commonly observed in diesel (compression ignition) engines that run on natural gas. Quenching reduces efficiency and increases knocking, sometimes causing the engine to stall. Incomplete combustion also leads to the production of [carbon monoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide) (CO). Further chemicals released are [benzene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzene) and [1,3-butadiene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1,3-butadiene) that are also [hazardous air pollutants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazardous_air_pollutant).

Increasing the amount of air in the engine reduces emissions of incomplete combustion products, but also promotes reaction between oxygen and [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen) in the air to produce [nitrogen oxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_oxide) (NO<sub>*x*</sub>). NO<sub>*x*</sub> is hazardous to both plant and animal health, and leads to the production of [ozone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone) (O<sup></sup>\
<sub>3</sub>). Ozone is not emitted directly; rather, it is a secondary air pollutant, produced in the atmosphere by the reaction of NO<sub>*x*</sub> and [volatile organic compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volatile_organic_compound) in the presence of sunlight. Ground-level ozone is harmful to human health and the environment. Though the same chemical substance, ground-level ozone should not be confused with [stratospheric ozone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratospheric_ozone), or the [ozone layer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone_layer), which protects the earth from harmful ultraviolet rays.

Carbon fuels containing sulfur produce [sulfur monoxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfur_monoxide) (SO) and [sulfur dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfur_dioxide) (SO<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub>) contributing to [acid rain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_rain).

In the United States, nitrogen oxides, [PM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particulates), carbon monoxide, sulfur dioxide, and ozone, are regulated as [criteria air pollutants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criteria_air_pollutant) under the [Clean Air Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clean_Air_Act_\(United_States\)) to levels where human health and welfare are protected. Other pollutants, such as benzene and 1,3-butadiene, are regulated as [hazardous air pollutants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazardous_air_pollutant) whose emissions must be lowered as much as possible depending on technological and practical considerations.

NO<sub>*x*</sub>, carbon monoxide and other pollutants are frequently controlled via [exhaust gas recirculation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exhaust_gas_recirculation) which returns some of the exhaust back into the engine intake. [Catalytic converters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalytic_converter) are used to convert exhaust chemicals to CO<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub> (a [greenhouse gas](21350772.md)), H<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub>O (water vapour, also a greenhouse gas) and N<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub> (nitrogen).

See also: [Air suction valve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_suction_valve)

#### Non-road engines

Main article: [Non-road engine § Emission standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-road_engine#Emission_standards)

The emission standards used by many countries have special requirements for [non-road engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-road_engine) which are used by equipment and vehicles that are not operated on the public roadways. The standards are separated from the road vehicles.[^cite_note-57]

### Noise pollution

Significant contributions to [noise pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noise_pollution) are made by internal combustion engines. Automobile and truck traffic operating on highways and street systems produce noise, as do aircraft flights due to jet noise, particularly supersonic-capable aircraft. Rocket engines create the most intense noise.

### Idling

Main article: [Idle reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idle_reduction)

Internal combustion engines continue to consume fuel and emit pollutants while idling. Idling is reduced by [stop-start systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Start-stop_system).

## Carbon dioxide formation

A good way to estimate the mass of carbon dioxide that is released when one litre of diesel fuel (or gasoline) is combusted can be found as follows:[^cite_note-58]

As a good approximation the chemical formula of diesel is C<sup></sup>\
<sub>n</sub>H<sup></sup>\
<sub>2n</sub>. In reality diesel is a mixture of different molecules. As carbon has a molar mass of 12 g/mol and hydrogen (atomic) has a molar mass of about 1 g/mol, the fraction by weight of carbon in diesel is roughly 12⁄14.

The reaction of diesel combustion is given by:

2C<sup></sup>\
<sub>n</sub>H<sup></sup>\
<sub>2n</sub> + 3nO<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub> ⇌ 2nCO<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub> + 2nH<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub>O

Carbon dioxide has a molar mass of 44 g/mol as it consists of 2 atoms of oxygen (16 g/mol) and 1 atom of carbon (12 g/mol). So 12 g of carbon yields 44 g of carbon dioxide.

Diesel has a density of 0.838 kg per litre.

Putting everything together the mass of carbon dioxide that is produced by burning 1 litre of diesel can be calculated as:

&#x20;   0.838 k g  /  L ⋅   12 14   ⋅   44 12   = 2.63 k g  /  L   {\displaystyle 0.838kg/L\cdot {\frac {12}{14}}\cdot {\frac {44}{12}}=2.63kg/L}  {\displaystyle 0.838kg/L\cdot {\frac {12}{14}}\cdot {\frac {44}{12}}=2.63kg/L}

The figure obtained with this estimation is close to the values found in the literature.

For gasoline, with a density of 0.75 kg/L and a ratio of carbon to hydrogen atoms of about 6 to 14, the estimated value of carbon dioxide emission from burning 1 litre of gasoline is:

&#x20;   0.75 k g  /  L ⋅     6 ⋅ 12   6 ⋅ 12 + 14    ⋅ 1  ⋅   44 12   = 2.3 k g  /  L   {\displaystyle 0.75kg/L\cdot {{\frac {6\cdot 12}{6\cdot 12+14}}\cdot 1}\cdot {\frac {44}{12}}=2.3kg/L}  {\displaystyle 0.75kg/L\cdot {{\frac {6\cdot 12}{6\cdot 12+14}}\cdot 1}\cdot {\frac {44}{12}}=2.3kg/L}

## Parasitic loss

The term *parasitic loss* is often applied to devices that take energy from the engine in order to enhance the engine's ability to create more energy or convert energy to motion. In the internal combustion engine, almost every mechanical component, including the [drivetrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drivetrain), causes parasitic loss and could thus be characterized as a parasitic load.

### Examples

[Bearings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bearing_\(mechanical\)), oil pumps, [piston rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piston_ring), valve springs, [flywheels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flywheel), [transmissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmission_\(mechanics\)), [driveshafts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Driveshaft), and [differentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_\(mechanics\)) all act as parasitic loads that rob the system of power. These parasitic loads can be divided into two categories: those inherent to the working of the engine and those drivetrain losses incurred in the systems that transfer power from the engine to the road (such as the transmission, driveshaft, differentials and axles).

For example, the former category (engine parasitic loads) includes the oil pump used to lubricate the engine, which is a necessary parasite that consumes power from the engine (its host). Another example of an engine parasitic load is a [supercharger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercharger), which derives its power from the engine and creates more power for the engine. The power that the supercharger consumes is parasitic loss and is usually expressed in [kilowatt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilowatt) or [horsepower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horsepower). While the power that the supercharger consumes in comparison to what it generates is small, it is still measurable or calculable. One of the desirable features of a [turbocharger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbocharger) over a supercharger is the lower parasitic loss of the former.[^cite_note-59]

Drivetrain parasitic losses include both steady state and dynamic loads. Steady state loads occur at constant speeds and may originate in discrete components such as the [torque converter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torque_converter), the transmission [oil pump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_pump_\(internal_combustion_engine\)), and/or [clutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clutch) drag, and in seal/bearing drag, churning of lubricant and gear [windage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windage)/[friction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friction) found throughout the system. Dynamic loads occur under acceleration and are caused by [inertia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertia) of rotating components and/or increased friction.[^cite_note-\:0-60]

### Measurement

While rules of thumb such as a 15% power loss from drivetrain parasitic loads have been commonly repeated, the actual loss of energy due to parasitic loads varies between systems. It can be influenced by powertrain design, lubricant type and temperature and many other factors.[^cite_note-\:0-60][^cite_note-61] In automobiles, drivetrain loss can be quantified by measuring the difference between power measured by an [engine dynamometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engine_test_stand) and a [chassis dynamometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chassis_dynamometer). However, this method is primarily useful for measuring steady state loads and may not accurately reflect losses due to dynamic loads.[^cite_note-\:0-60] More advanced methods can be used in a laboratory setting, such as measuring in-cylinder pressure measurements, flow rate and temperature at certain points, and testing of individual parts or sub-assemblies to determine friction and pumping losses.[^cite_note-\:2-62]

For example, in a dynamometer test by [*Hot Rod* magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hot_Rod_\(magazine\)), a [Ford Mustang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_Mustang) equipped with a modified 357ci [small-block Ford V8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_small_block_engine) engine and an automatic transmission had a measured drivetrain power loss averaging 33%. In the same test, a [Buick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buick) equipped with a modified 455ci V8 engine and a 4-speed manual transmission was measured to have an average drivetrain power loss of 21%.[^cite_note-\:1-63]

Laboratory testing of a heavy-duty diesel engine determined that 1.3% of the fuel energy input was lost to parasitic loads of engine accessories such as water and oil pumps.[^cite_note-\:2-62]

### Reduction

Automotive engineers and tuners commonly make design choices that reduce parasitic loads in order to improve efficiency and power output. These may involve the choice of major engine components or systems, such as the use of [dry sump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dry_sump) lubrication system over a [wet sump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wet_sump) system. Alternately, this can be effected through substitution of minor components available as aftermarket modifications, such as exchanging a directly engine-driven fan for one equipped with a [fan clutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fan_clutch) or an electric fan.[^cite_note-\:1-63] Another modification to reduce parasitic loss, usually seen in track-only cars, is the replacement of an engine-driven water pump for an electrical water pump.[^cite_note-64] The reduction in parasitic loss from these changes may be due to reduced friction or many other variables that cause the design to be more efficient.
