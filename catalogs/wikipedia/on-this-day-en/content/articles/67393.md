**West Africa**, also known as **Western Africa**, is the westernmost region of [Africa](5334607.md). The [United Nations defines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_geoscheme_for_Africa#Western_Africa) Western Africa as the 16 countries of [Benin](3459.md), [Burkina Faso](3470.md), [Cape Verde](18962637.md), [The Gambia](65656.md), [Ghana](12067.md), [Guinea](12176.md), [Guinea-Bissau](12186.md), [Ivory Coast](57744.md), [Liberia](17791.md), [Mali](19127.md), [Mauritania](18778516.md), [Niger](21373.md), [Nigeria](21383.md), [Senegal](27278.md), [Sierra Leone](27308.md), and [Togo](30138.md), as well as [Saint Helena, Ascension and Tristan da Cunha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Helena,_Ascension_and_Tristan_da_Cunha) ([a United Kingdom Overseas Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_Overseas_Territories)).[^cite_note-UNSD_Country_and_Area_Codes-7][^cite_note-Paul_R._Masson_2001-8] As of 2021, the population of West Africa is estimated at 419 million,[^cite_note-UN_WPP-1][^cite_note-UN_WPP_2022-2] and approximately 382 million in 2017, of which 189.7 million were female and 192.3 million male.[^cite_note-UN_Wt_Af-3] The region is one of the fastest growing in Africa, both demographically[^cite_note-9] and economically.[^cite_note-10] West Africa is a culturally and demographically diverse region, home to many ethnic groups, including [Hausa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hausa_people), [Yoruba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoruba_people), [Igbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igbo_people), [Fulani](102383.md), [Akan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akan_people), [Mandé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mand%C3%A9), [Wolof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolof_people), [Songhai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songhai_people), [Kanuri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanuri_people), [Zarma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zarma_people) and [Tuareg](103054.md). The most common religions practiced in the region are [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_Africa), [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_Africa) and [traditional African religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_African_religions).

Historically, West Africa was home to several powerful states and empires that controlled regional trade routes, including the [Mali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mali_Empire) and [Gao Empires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gao_Empire). Positioned at a crossroads of trade between [North Africa](21714.md) and South Sahel Africa, the region supplied goods such as [gold](12240.md), [ivory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory), and advanced [iron-working](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron-working). During [European exploration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_exploration_of_Africa), local economies were incorporated into the [Atlantic slave trade](49005.md), which expanded existing systems of slavery. Even after the [end of the slave trade in the early 19th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blockade_of_Africa), colonial powers – especially [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_West_Africa) and [Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_West_Africa) — continued to [exploit the region through colonial relationships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scramble_for_Africa). For example, they continued exporting [extractive goods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extractive_industries) like [cocoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocoa_bean), [coffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffee), [tropical timber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_timber), and mineral resources. Since gaining independence, several West African nations, such as the Ivory Coast, Ghana, Nigeria and Senegal – have taken active roles in regional and global economies.[^cite_note-11] Nigeria, with [a population](21386.md) of more than 242 million, is the [most populous country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_African_countries_by_population) in Africa, and the [world's sixth-most populous country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_and_dependencies_by_population), and [its economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Nigeria) is the largest in the region and one of the largest in Africa along with Egypt and South Africa. By contrast, other countries in the area, such as Niger and Mali, have among the lowest [human development index in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_Human_Development_Index).

West Africa has a rich [ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecology), with significant [biodiversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity) across various regions. Its climate is shaped by the dry [Sahara](325363.md) to the north and east – producing the [Harmattan winds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmattan) — and by the [Atlantic Ocean](698.md) to the south and west, which brings seasonal monsoons. This climatic mix creates a range of [biomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biome), from [tropical forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_forest) to [drylands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drylands), supporting species such as [pangolin](51124.md), [rhinoceros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhinoceros), and [elephant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elephant). Several countries in West Africa are crossed by the [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel), a [transition zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecotone) between the more humid [Sudanian savannas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudanian_savanna) to its south and the drier Sahara to the north. However, West Africa's environment faces major threats due to [deforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation), [biodiversity loss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity_loss), [overfishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overfishing), [pollution from mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_effects_of_mining), [plastics](11169849.md), and [climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_in_Africa).

## History

Main article: [History of West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_West_Africa)

Further information: [Sub-Saharan Africa § Western Africa](27067.md#Western_Africa), [African empires § West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_empires#West_Africa), and [List of kingdoms in pre-colonial Africa § West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_kingdoms_in_pre-colonial_Africa#West_Africa)

The history of West Africa can be divided into five major periods: first, its prehistory, in which the first [human](682482.md) settlers arrived, developed [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture), and made contact with peoples to the north; the second, the Iron Age empires that consolidated both intra-Africa, and extra-Africa trade, and developed centralized states; third, major polities flourished, which would undergo an extensive history of contact with non-Africans; fourth, the colonial period, in which [Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Britain) and [France](5843419.md) controlled nearly the entire region; and fifth, the post-independence era, in which the current nations were formed.[^cite_note-12]

### Prehistory

Main article: [Prehistoric West Africa § Early Stone Age 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prehistoric_West_Africa#Early_Stone_Age_2)



Representations of [West African hunter-gatherers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_African_hunter-gatherers) from the [Dahomey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahomey) region of [Benin](3459.md)

West African populations were considerably mobile and interacted with one another throughout the [population history of West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_history_of_West_Africa).[^cite_note-Haour_II-13] Acheulean tool-using [archaic humans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaic_humans) may have dwelled throughout West Africa since at least between 780,000 BP and 126,000 BP ([Middle Pleistocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Pleistocene)).[^cite_note-Scerri-14] During the [Pleistocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleistocene), [Middle Stone Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Stone_Age) peoples (e.g., [Iwo Eleru people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iwo_Eleru_skull),[^cite_note-MacDonald-15] possibly [Aterians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aterians)), who dwelled throughout West Africa between [MIS 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIS_4) and [MIS 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIS_2),[^cite_note-Niang-16] were gradually replaced by incoming [Late Stone Age peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_African_foragers), who migrated into West Africa[^cite_note-Schlebusch-17] as an increase in humid conditions resulted in the subsequent expansion of the [West African forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinean_Forests_of_West_Africa).[^cite_note-Scerri_II-18] [West African hunter-gatherers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_African_hunter-gatherers) occupied western [Central Africa](539606.md) (e.g., [Shum Laka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shum_Laka)) earlier than 32,000 BP,[^cite_note-MacDonald-15] dwelled throughout [coastal West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinea_\(region\)) by 12,000 BP,[^cite_note-MacDonald_X-19] and migrated northward between 12,000 BP and 8000 BP as far as Mali, Burkina Faso,[^cite_note-MacDonald_X-19] and Mauritania.[^cite_note-Abd-El-Moniem-20]

During the [Holocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocene), [Niger-Congo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niger-Congo) speakers independently created pottery in [Ounjougou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ounjougou), Mali[^cite_note-Ness-22][^cite_note-Ehret_V-23][^cite_note-Jesse-24] – the earliest [pottery in Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pottery#Africa)[^cite_note-Huysecom_X-25] – by at least 9400 BCE,[^cite_note-Ness-22] and along with their pottery,[^cite_note-Huysecom_X-25] as well as wielding independently invented [bows and arrows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bows_and_arrows),[^cite_note-Blench-26][^cite_note-Roy-27] migrated into the Central Sahara,[^cite_note-Huysecom_X-25] which became their primary region of residence by 10,000 BP.[^cite_note-Blench-26] The emergence and expansion of ceramics in the Sahara may be linked with the origin of Round Head and Kel Essuf rock art, which occupy rockshelters in the same regions (e.g., [Djado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Djado), [Acacus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tadrart_Acacus), [Tadrart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tadrart_Rouge)).[^cite_note-Achrati-28] Hunters in the Central Sahara farmed, stored, and cooked undomesticated central Saharan [flora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flora),[^cite_note-Mercuri-29] underwent domestication of [antelope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antelope),[^cite_note-Aïn-Séba-30] and [domesticated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domesticated) and shepherded [Barbary sheep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbary_sheep).[^cite_note-Mercuri-29] After the [Kel Essuf Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kel_Essuf_Period) and [Round Head Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Round_Head_Period) of the Central Sahara, the [Pastoral Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastoral_Period) followed.[^cite_note-Soukopova_I-31] Some of the hunter-gatherers who created the Round Head rock art may have adopted pastoral culture, and others may have not.[^cite_note-Soukopova_X-32] As a result of increasing [aridification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aridification) of the [Green Sahara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Sahara), Central Saharan [hunter-gatherers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter-gatherers) and cattle [herders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herders) may have used seasonal [waterways](174851.md) as the migratory route taken to the [Niger River](21437.md) and [Chad Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chad_Basin) of West Africa.[^cite_note-Soukopova_II-33] In 2000 BCE, "[Thiaroye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thiaroye) Woman",[^cite_note-Metropolitan_Museum_of_Art-34] also known as the "[Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus_figurine) of Thiaroye",[^cite_note-Thiam-35][^cite_note-Metropolitan_Museum_of_Art-34] may have been the earliest [statuette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statuette) created in [Sub-Saharan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub-Saharan) West Africa; it may have particularly been a [fertility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertility_rite) statuette, created in the region of [Senegambia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senegambia),[^cite_note-Thiam-35] and may be associated with the emergence of [complexly organized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_society) [pastoral societies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastoral_period) in [West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_West_Africa#Iron_Age) between 4000 BCE and 1000 BCE.[^cite_note-LaGamma-36] Though possibly developed as early as 5000 BCE,[^cite_note-Akpan-37] [Nsibidi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nsibidi) may have also developed in 2000 BCE,[^cite_note-Hales-38][^cite_note-Akpan-37] as evidenced by depictions of the [West African script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_African_script) on [Ikom monoliths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ikom_monoliths) at [Ikom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ikom), in [Nigeria](21383.md).[^cite_note-Akpan-37] Migration of Saharan peoples south of the Sahelian region resulted in seasonal interaction with and gradual absorption of West African hunter-gatherers, who primarily dwelt in the [savannas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Sudanian_savanna) and [forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinean_Forests_of_West_Africa) of West Africa.[^cite_note-MacDonald_X-19] In West Africa, which may have been a major regional cradle in Africa for the [domestication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestication) of crops and animals,[^cite_note-Shen-39][^cite_note-Scarcelli-40] Niger-Congo speakers domesticated the [helmeted guineafowl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmeted_guineafowl)[^cite_note-Murunga-41] between 5500 BP and 1300 BP;[^cite_note-Shen-39] domestication of field crops occurred throughout various locations in West Africa, such as [yams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yam_\(vegetable\)) (d. praehensilis) in the Niger River basin between eastern Ghana and western Nigeria (northern Benin), [rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice) ([oryza glaberrima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oryza_glaberrima)) in the [Inner Niger Delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_Niger_Delta) region of Mali, [pearl millet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_millet#Africa) ([cenchrus americanus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cenchrus_americanus)) in northern Mali and Mauritania, and [cowpeas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cowpeas) in northern Ghana.[^cite_note-Scarcelli-40] After having persisted as late as 1000 BP,[^cite_note-MacDonald_X-19] or some period of time after 1500 CE,[^cite_note-Van_Beek-42] remaining West African hunter-gatherers, many of whom dwelt in the [forest-savanna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinean_forest-savanna_mosaic) region, were ultimately acculturated and admixed into the larger groups of West African [agriculturalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculturalists), akin to the migratory [Bantu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantu_peoples)-speaking agriculturalists and their [encounters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantu_expansion#Central_Africa) with [Central African hunter-gatherers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_African_hunter-gatherers).[^cite_note-MacDonald_X-19]

### Empires

Main article: [History of West Africa § Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_West_Africa#Iron_Age)

Further information: [History of Africa § West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Africa#West_Africa), [History of Africa § West Africa 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Africa#West_Africa_2), and [Blacksmiths of western Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blacksmiths_of_western_Africa)

See also: [Jews of Bilad el-Sudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews_of_Bilad_el-Sudan)



[Mansa Musa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mansa_Musa) depicted holding a [gold nugget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_nugget) from a 1395 map of [Africa](5334607.md) and [Europe](9239.md)

The development of the region's economy allowed more centralized states and civilizations to form, beginning with [Dhar Tichitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhar_Tichitt) that began in 1600 B.C. followed by [Djenné-Djenno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Djenn%C3%A9-Djenno) beginning in 300 B.C. This was then succeeded by the [Ghana Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghana_Empire) that first flourished roughly between the 2nd and 12th centuries C.E., which later gave way to the [Mali Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mali_Empire). In current-day Mauritania, there exist archaeological sites in the towns of [Tichit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tichit) and [Oualata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oualata) that were initially constructed around 2000 B.C., and were found to have originated from the [Soninke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soninke_people) branch of the [Mandé peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mand%C3%A9_peoples). Also, based on the archaeology of the city of [Kumbi Saleh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kumbi_Saleh) in modern-day Mauritania, the Mali empire came to dominate much of the region until its defeat by [Almoravid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almoravid) invaders in 1052.

Three great kingdoms were identified in *Bilad al-Sudan* by the ninth century. They included Ghana, [Gao](1255721.md) and [Kanem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanem_Empire).[^cite_note-43]

The [Sosso Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sosso_Empire) sought to fill the void but was defeated (c. 1240) by the [Mandinka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandinka_people) forces of [Sundiata Keita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sundiata_Keita), founder of the new Mali Empire. The Mali Empire continued to flourish for several centuries, most particularly under Sundiata's grandnephew [Musa I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mansa_Musa), before a succession of weak rulers led to its collapse under [Mossi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mossi_people), [Tuareg](103054.md) and [Songhai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songhai_people) invaders. In the 15th century, the Songhai would form a new dominant state based on [Gao](1255721.md), in the [Songhai Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songhai_Empire), under the leadership of [Sonni Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonni_Ali) and [Askia Mohammed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Askia_Mohammed).



13th-century Africa – map of the main trade routes and states, kingdoms and empires

Meanwhile, south of Sudan, strong city-states arose in [Igboland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igboland), such as the 10th-century [Kingdom of Nri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Nri), which helped birth the arts and customs of the [Igbo people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igbo_people), [Bono state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bono_state) in the 11th century, which gave birth to the numerous [Akan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akan_people) States, while [Ife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ife) rose to prominence around the 12th century. Further east, [Oyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oyo_Empire) arose as the dominant [Yoruba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoruba_people) state and the [Aro Confederacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aro_Confederacy) as a dominant Igbo state in modern-day Nigeria.

The Kingdom of Nri was a West African medieval state in present-day southeastern Nigeria and a subgroup of the Igbo people. The Kingdom of Nri was unusual in the history of world government in that its leader exercised no military power over his subjects. The kingdom existed as a sphere of religious and political influence over a third of Igboland and was administered by a priest-king called an [Eze Nri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eze_Nri). The Eze Nri managed trade and diplomacy on behalf of the Nri people and possessed divine authority in religious matters.

The [Oyo Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oyo_Empire) was a [Yoruba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoruba_people) empire of what is today Western, North Central [Nigeria](21383.md) and Southern Republic of [Benin](3459.md). Established in the 14th century, the Oyo Empire grew to become one of the largest West African states. It rose through the outstanding organizational skills of the Yoruba, wealth gained from trade and its powerful [cavalry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavalry). The Oyo Empire was the most politically important state in the region from the mid-17th to the late 18th century, holding sway not only over most of the other kingdoms in [Yorubaland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yorubaland), but also over nearby African states, notably the [Fon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fon_people) [Kingdom of Dahomey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Dahomey) in the modern Republic of Benin to the west.

The Benin Empire was a [post-classical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-classical) empire located in what is now southern [Nigeria](21383.md). Its capital was Edo, now known as [Benin City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benin_City), [Edo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo_State). It should not be confused with the modern-day country called [Benin](3459.md), formerly called [Dahomey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Dahomey). The Benin Empire was "one of the oldest and most highly developed states in the coastal hinterland of West Africa, dating perhaps to the eleventh century CE". The Benin Empire was governed by a sovereign Emperor with hundreds of thousands of soldiers and a powerful council rich in resources, wealth, ancient science and technology with cities described as beautiful and large as [Haarlem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haarlem). "[Olfert Dapper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olfert_Dapper), a Dutch writer, describing Benin in his book *[Description of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Description_of_Africa_\(Olfert_Dapper\))* (1668) ". Its craft was the most adored and treasured bronze casting in the history of Africa. It was annexed by the [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire) in 1897 during the invasion and scramble of Africa.

### European contact and slave trade

Main article: [Atlantic slave trade](49005.md)



West Africa c. 1875

Portuguese traders began establishing settlements along the coast in 1445, followed by the [French](5843419.md), [English](9316.md), [Spanish](26667.md), [Danish](76972.md) and [Dutch](21148.md); the [African slave trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_slave_trade) began not long after, which over the following centuries would debilitate the region's economy and population.[^cite_note-44] The slave trade also encouraged the formation of states such as the [Bono state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bono_state), [Bambara Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bambara_Empire) and [Dahomey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahomey), whose economic activities include but not limited to exchanging slaves for European [firearms](11966.md).[^cite_note-45]

### Colonialism

Further information: [Colonisation of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonisation_of_Africa)



French in West Africa c. 1913

In the early 19th century, a series of Fulani reformist [jihads](16203.md) swept across Western Africa. The most notable include [Usman dan Fodio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usman_dan_Fodio)'s [Fulani Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fulani_Empire), which replaced the [Hausa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hausa_people) city-states, [Seku Amadu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seku_Amadu)'s [Massina Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massina_Empire), which defeated the Bambara, and El Hadj [Umar Tall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umar_Tall)'s [Toucouleur Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toucouleur_Empire), which briefly conquered much of modern-day Mali.

However, the [French](5843419.md) and [British](31717.md) continued to advance in the [Scramble for Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scramble_for_Africa), subjugating kingdom after kingdom. With the fall of [Samory Ture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samory)'s established [Wassoulou Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wassoulou_Empire) in 1898 and the [Ashanti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashanti_people) queen [Yaa Asantewaa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaa_Asantewaa) in 1902, most West African military resistance to colonial rule resulted in failure.

Part of the West African regions underwent an increase in the [numeracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numeracy) level throughout the 19th century. The reason for such a growth was predetermined by a number of factors. Namely, the [peanut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peanut) production and trade, which was boosted by the [demand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demand) of the [colonial states](7299.md). Importantly, the rise of numeracy was higher in the regions which were less [hierarchical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hierarchy) and had less dependence on the [slavery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery) trade (e.g. Sine and Salum). Whereas areas with the opposite trends illustrated opposite tendencies (e.g. central and northern Senegal). Those patterns were further even more stimulated by the French colonial campaign.[^cite_note-46]

Britain controlled the Gambia, Sierra Leone, Ghana, and Nigeria throughout the colonial era, while France unified Senegal, Guinea, Mali, Burkina Faso, Benin, Ivory Coast, and Niger into [French West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_West_Africa). [Portugal](23033.md) founded the colony of [Guinea-Bissau](12186.md), while [Germany](11867.md) claimed [Togoland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Togoland), but was forced to divide it between France and Britain following [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War) due to the [Treaty of Versailles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Versailles). Only [Liberia](17791.md) retained its independence, at the price of major territorial concessions.

Since independence, many West African nations have been submerged under political instability, with notable civil wars in Nigeria, Sierra Leone, Liberia, and Ivory Coast, and a succession of military coups in [Ghana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Ghana) and [Burkina Faso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Burkina_Faso).

### Nature



A [rhinoceros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhinoceros) in Bandia Nature Reserve, [Senegal](27278.md)



[African bush elephants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_bush_elephants) in [Yankari National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yankari_National_Park), Nigeria

Before [European colonisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scramble_for_Africa), West African countries such as those from the [Senegambia region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senegambia) (Senegal and the Gambia) used to have a diverse [wildlife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildlife) including [lions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lion), [hippopotamus](37764.md), [elephants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elephant), [antelopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antelope), [leopards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopard) etc.[^cite_note-55] However, during colonization, the European colonizers such as the French and British killed most of the wildlife particularly the lions, using their body parts as trophies. By the turn of the 20th century, the Senegambia region had lost most of its lion population and other exotic animals due to [poaching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poaching). By the 1930s, the Gambian elephant population became extinct. That phenomenon was not only limited to the Senegambia region but affected much of West Africa as the region lost much of its "natural resources once tied so closely to its cultural identity. Poaching has stolen most of its wildlife." The British issued poaching licenses, and although they would later try to reverse the damage that had been done by attempting to preserve what was left of the local wildlife, but by that time, it was too late.[^cite_note-New_York_Times-56][^cite_note-Somerville-57] During the 1930s, the elephant population in the [Gold Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_Coast_\(region\)) was about 300, and Sierra Leone between 500 and 600. Although a small number of elephants survived in Nigeria, hunting, [agricultural expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_expansion) and [clearing of forest in that country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation_in_Nigeria) drastically affected its wildlife population, particularly elephants.[^cite_note-Somerville-57]

### Deforestation

Further information: [Deforestation in Nigeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation_in_Nigeria)

West Africa is greatly affected by [deforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation) and has one of the worst deforestation rate.[^cite_note-ThoughtCo-65] Even "the beloved baobab tree" which is viewed as sacred by some West African cultures are under threat due to [climate change](5042951.md), [urbanization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urbanization) and population growth. "Huge swaths of forest are being razed to clear space for palm oil and cocoa plantations. Mangroves are being killed off by pollution. Even wispy [acacias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acacia) are hacked away for use in cooking fires to feed growing families."[^cite_note-New_York_Times-56] Nigeria, Liberia, Guinea, Ghana and the Ivory Coast, have lost large areas of their [rainforest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainforest).[^cite_note-66][^cite_note-67] In 2005, the [Food and Agriculture Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_and_Agriculture_Organization) of the United Nations ranked [Nigeria as the state with the worst deforestation rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation_in_Nigeria) in the entire world. Causes include logging, [subsistence agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsistence_agriculture), and the collection of fuelwoods.[^cite_note-68]

According to a [ThoughtCo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ThoughtCo) publication authored Steve Nix (2018), almost 90 percent of West Africa's original rainforest has been destroyed, and the rest is "heavily fragmented and in a degraded state, being poorly used."[^cite_note-ThoughtCo-65]

### Overfishing

[Overfishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overfishing) is a major issue in West Africa. Besides reducing fish stocks in the region, it also threatens [food security](216361.md) and the livelihoods of many coastal communities that largely depend on [artisanal fishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artisanal_fishing). The overfishing generally comes from [foreign trawlers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trawling) operating in the region.[^cite_note-69]

To combat the overfishing, [Greenpeace](12233.md) has recommended countries reduce the number of registered trawlers operating in African waters, increase the monitoring and control and set up regional fisheries organizations.[^cite_note-70] Some steps have already been taken in the form of [WARFP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WARFP?action=edit\&redlink=1) (the World Bank's West Africa Regional Fisheries Program which empowers west-African countries (i.e. Liberia, Sierra Leone, Cape Verde, and Senegal) with information, training and monitoring systems. Furthermore, Liberia enacted a fisheries regulations Act in 2010[^cite_note-71] and installed a satellite-based monitoring system and Senegal enacted a fisheries code in 2015. In Cape Verde, the fishermen communities of Palmiera and Santa Maria have organized themselves to protect fishing zones. Mozambique finally created a [conservation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_biology) area, including a coastline. [^cite_note-72][^cite_note-73]

The northwest African region of [Mauritania](18778516.md) periodically suffers country-wide plagues of [locusts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locust) which consume water, salt and crops on which the human population relies.[^cite_note-76]

#### Background

West Africa is west of an imagined north–south axis lying close to [10° east longitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/10th_meridian_east).[^cite_note-autogenerated1-74] The Atlantic [Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Atlantic_Ocean) forms the western as well as the southern borders of the West African region.[^cite_note-autogenerated1-74] The northern border is the [Sahara Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahara_Desert), with the Ranishanu Bend generally considered the northernmost part of the region.[^cite_note-77] The eastern border is less precise, with some placing it at the [Benue Trough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benue_Trough), and others on a line running from [Mount Cameroon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Cameroon) to [Lake Chad](59314.md).

Colonial boundaries are reflected in the modern boundaries between contemporary West African states, cutting across ethnic and cultural lines, often dividing single ethnic groups between two or more states.[^cite_note-78]

In contrast to most of Central, Southern, and Southeast Africa, West Africa is not populated by [Bantu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantu_languages)-speaking peoples.[^cite_note-79]

### Climate change



The [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel) has a [hot semi-arid climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-arid_climate#Hot_semi-arid_climates).

This section is an excerpt from [Climate change in Africa § West Africa and the Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_in_Africa#West_Africa_and_the_Sahel).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Climate_change_in_Africa\&action=edit)]

The West African region can be divided into four climatic sub-regions namely the Guinea Coast, Soudano-Sahel, Sahel (extending eastward to the Ethiopian border) and the Sahara,[^cite_note-80] each with different climatic conditions. The seasonal cycle of rainfall is mainly driven by the south–north movement of the [Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intertropical_Convergence_Zone) (ITCZ) which is characterised by the confluence between moist southwesterly monsoon winds and the dry northeasterly Harmattan.[^cite_note-81]

Based on the inter-annual rainfall variability, three main climatic periods have been observed over the Sahel: the wet period from 1950 to the early 1960s followed by a dry period from 1972 to 1990 and then the period from 1991 onwards which has seen a partial rainfall recovery.[^cite_note-82][^cite_note-83][^cite_note-84] During the dry period, the Sahel experienced a number of particularly severe drought events, with devastating effects.[^cite_note-Climate_change_in_Africa_Funk-85][^cite_note-86] The recent decades, have also witnessed a moderate increment in annual rainfall since the beginning of 1990s. However, total annual rainfall remains significantly below that observed during the 1950s.[^cite_note-87][^cite_note-Climate_change_in_Africa_Funk-85]

Some have identified the two recent decades as a recovery period.[^cite_note-88] Others refer to this as a period of "hydrological intensification" with much of the annual rainfall increase coming from more severe rain events and sometimes flooding rather than more frequent rainfall, or similarly other works[^cite_note-89][^cite_note-90] underline the continuity of the drought even though the rainfall has increased. Since 1985, 54 percent of the population has been affected by five or more floods in the 17 Sahel region countries.[^cite_note-Climate_change_in_Africa_Sahel2011-91] In 2012, severe [drought conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Sahel_drought) in the Sahel were reported. Governments in the region responded quickly, launching strategies to address the issue.[^cite_note-92]

The region is projected to experience changes in rainfall regime, with climate models suggesting that decreases in wet season rainfall are more likely in the western Sahel, and increases more likely in the central to east Sahel, although opposite trends cannot yet be ruled out.[^cite_note-93][^cite_note-Climate_change_in_Africa_sb-94][^cite_note-Climate_change_in_Africa_Kendon-2019-95] These trends will affect the frequency and severity of floods, droughts, [desertification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desertification), sand and dust storms, desert locust plagues and water shortages.[^cite_note-96][^cite_note-97]

However, irrespective of the changes in seasonal mean rain, the most intense storms are expected to become more intense, amplifying flood frequency.[^cite_note-Climate_change_in_Africa_sb-94][^cite_note-Climate_change_in_Africa_Kendon-2019-95] Enhanced carbon emissions and global warming may also lead to an increase in dry spells especially across the Guinea Coast associated with a reduction of the wet spells under both 1.5 °C and 2 °C global warming level.[^cite_note-98]

Fifteen percent of [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel) region population has also experienced a temperature increase of more than 1 °C from 1970 to 2010. The Sahel region, in particular, will experience higher average temperatures over the course of the 21st century and changes in rainfall patterns, according to the [Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change](15030.md) (IPCC).

## Transport

### Rail transport

Main article: [ECOWAS rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ECOWAS_rail)

A Trans-ECOWAS project, established in 2007, plans to upgrade railways in this zone. One of the goals of the [Economic Community of West African States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_Community_of_West_African_States) (ECOWAS) is the development of an integrated [railroad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railroad) network.[^cite_note-Proposed_Ecowas_railway-99] Aims include the extension of railways in member countries, the interconnection of previously isolated railways and the standardization of gauge, brakes, couplings, and other parameters. The first line would connect the cities and ports of [Lagos](85232.md), [Cotonou](56647.md), [Lomé](57067.md) and [Accra](56824.md) and would allow the largest container ships to focus on a smaller number of large ports, while efficiently serving a larger [hinterland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinterland). This line connects [3 ft 6 in](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3_ft_6_in_gauge_railways) (1,067 mm) gauge and 1,000 mm (3 ft 3+3⁄8 in) [metre gauge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre_gauge) systems, which would require four rail [dual gauge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_gauge), which can also provide [standard gauge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_gauge).[^cite_note-Proposed_Ecowas_railway-99]

## Health

Main article: [History of West Africa § Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_West_Africa#Health)

West Africa has made considerable improvement in the health outcomes of its populations, despite the challenges posed by pervasive poverty, epidemic diseases, and food insecurity. The traditional communicable diseases of HIV/AIDS, malaria, and tuberculosis are still the major reasons for mortality. Primary health care is the best answer to curing diseases, as it provides the basic preventive strategies and it reduce the rate of child and maternal morbidity and mortality—two of the most preventable outcomes that can prolong life expectancy at birth.[^cite_note-102] Recently, mental health problems are on the rise in West Africa, as they are in many other world regions. However, the subject is largely a taboo, and professional treatment is still rare.[^cite_note-103]

## Culture



[Emir of Kano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kano_Emirate_Council) in Nigeria

Despite the wide variety of cultures in West Africa, from [Nigeria](21383.md) through to [Senegal](27278.md), there are general similarities in [dress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boubou_\(clothing\)), [cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_African_cuisine), [music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_West_Africa) and culture that are not shared extensively with groups outside the geographic region. This long history of cultural exchange predates the colonization era of the region and can be approximately placed at the time of the [Ghana Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghana_Empire) (proper: [Wagadou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wagadou) Empire), [Mali Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mali_Empire) or perhaps before these empires. West Africa varies a series of tribes and cultures that have combined a diverse regional subculture.[^cite_note-104]

### Art

Main article: [African art § West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_art#West_Africa)

### Traditional architecture

Further information: [Architecture of Africa § West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture_of_Africa#West_Africa)



A street and airport in [Timbuktu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timbuktu), Mali, showing the Sudano-Sahelian architectural style of the West African interior

The main traditional styles of building (in conjunction with modern styles) are the distinct [Sudano-Sahelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudano-Sahelian_architecture) style in inland areas, and the coastal forest styles more reminiscent of other sub-Saharan areas. They differ greatly in construction due to the demands made by the variety of climates in the area, from tropical humid forests to arid grasslands and deserts. Despite the architectural differences, buildings perform similar functions, including the [compound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compound_\(enclosure\)) structure central to West African family life or the strict distinction between the private and public worlds needed to maintain taboos or social etiquette.[^cite_note-105]

### Clothing

Further information: [Folk costume § Western Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_costume#Western_Africa)



[Philip Emeagwali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Emeagwali) wearing the [boubou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boubou_\(clothing\)) (or *agbada*), a traditional robe symbolic of West Africa

In contrast to other parts of the continent south of the [Sahara Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahara_Desert), the concepts of [hemming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hem) and [embroidering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embroidered) clothing have been traditionally common to West Africa for centuries, demonstrated by the production of various [breeches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breeches), [shirts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shirts), [tunics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunic) and [jackets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackets). As a result, the people of the region's diverse nations wear a wide variety of clothing with underlying similarities. Typical pieces of West African formal attire include the knee-to-ankle-length, flowing [boubou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boubou_\(clothing\)) robe, [dashiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dashiki), and [Senegalese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senegalese) [kaftan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaftan) (also known as *agbada* and *babariga*), which has its origins in the clothing of nobility of various West African empires in the 12th century. Traditional half-sleeved, hip-long, woven smocks or tunics (known as *fugu* in Gurunsi, *riga* in Hausa) – worn over a pair of baggy trousers—are another popular garment.[^cite_note-106] In the coastal regions stretching from southern Ivory Coast to Benin, a huge rectangular cloth is wrapped under one arm, draped over a shoulder, and held in one of the wearer's hands—coincidentally, reminiscent of [Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome)' [togas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toga). The best-known of these toga-like garments is the [Kente](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kente) (made by the [Akan people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akan_people) of [Ghana](12067.md) and [Ivory Coast](57744.md)), who wear them as a gesture of national pride.

### Cuisine

Main article: [West African cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_African_cuisine)

Further information: [History of West Africa § Cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_West_Africa#Cuisine), [African cuisine § West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_cuisine#West_Africa), and [List of African cuisines § West African cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_African_cuisines#West_African_cuisine)



[Jollof rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jollof_rice) or *benachin*, one of many Pan–West African dishes found only in West Africa

Scores of foreign visitors to West African nations (e.g., [traders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merchant), [historians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historians), [emigrants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emigrant), colonists, missionaries) have benefited from its citizens' generosity, and even left with a piece of its cultural heritage, via its foods. West African cuisines have had a significant influence on those of [Western civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_culture) for centuries; several dishes of West African origin are currently enjoyed in the [Caribbean](18956035.md) (such as the [West Indies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Indies) and [Haiti](13373.md)); Australia; the US (particularly [Louisiana](18130.md), [Virginia](32432.md), [North](21650.md) and [South Carolina](27956.md)); Italy; and other countries. Although some of these recipes have been altered to suit the sensibilities of their adopters, they retain a distinct West African essence.[^cite_note-107]

West African cuisines include fish (especially among the coastal areas), meat, vegetables, and fruits—most of which are grown by the nation's local farmers. In spite of the obvious differences among the various local cuisines in this multinational region, the foods display more similarities than differences. The small difference may be in the ingredients used. Most foods are cooked via boiling or frying. Commonly featured, starchy vegetables include [yams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yam_\(vegetable\)), [plantains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plantain_\(cooking\)), [cassava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassava), and sweet potatoes.[^cite_note-108] Rice is also a staple food, as is the [Serer people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serer_people)'s [sorghum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorghum) [couscous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Couscous) (called "*chereh*" in [Serer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serer_language)) particularly in Senegal and [the Gambia](65656.md).[^cite_note-109] [Jollof rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jollof_rice)—originally from the [Kingdom of Jolof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Jolof) (now part of modern-day Senegal) but has spread to the Wolofs of Gambia—is also enjoyed in many Western nations, as well;[^cite_note-110] [Mafé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maafe) (proper: "*tigh-dege-na"* or *domodah*) from [Mali](19127.md) (via the [Bambara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bambara_people) and [Mandinka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandinka_people))[^cite_note-111]—a peanut-butter stew served with rice;[^cite_note-112][^cite_note-113] [Akara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akara) (fried bean balls seasoned with spices served with sauce and bread) from Nigeria is a favorite breakfast for Gambians and Senegalese, as well as a favorite side snack or side dish in [Brazil](3383.md) and the [Caribbean](18956035.md) just as it is in West Africa. It is said that its exact origin may be from [Yorubaland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yorubaland) in Nigeria.[^cite_note-114][^cite_note-115] [Fufu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fufu) (from the [Twi language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twi_language), a dough served with a spicy stew or sauce—for example, [okra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okra) stew) from [Ghana](12067.md) is enjoyed throughout the region and beyond even in [Central Africa](539606.md) with their own versions of it.[^cite_note-116] Dishes such as [taguella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taguella) and [eghajira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eghajira). are popular among the [Tuareg people](103054.md).[^cite_note-117]

The [talking drum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talking_drum) is an instrument unique to West Africa.

[Mbalax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mbalax), [Highlife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highlife), [Fuji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuji_music), [Afrobeat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afrobeat), and [Afrobeats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afrobeats) are modern musical genres of West Africa and its diaspora. #### Griot artists



Kora-playing [griots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Griots) in [Senegal](27278.md), 1900. Both the [Kora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kora_\(instrument\)), a 21-stringed harp-lute, and the griot musical caste are unique to West Africa.

[Griot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Griot) artists and praise-singing is an important musical tradition related to the oral history of West African culture. Traditionally, musical and oral history as conveyed over generations by griots are typical of West African culture in [Mande](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mand%C3%A9_peoples), [Wolof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolof_people), [Songhay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songhai_people), [Serer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serer_people) and, to some extent, [Fula](102383.md) areas in the far west. A hereditary caste occupying the fringes of society, the griots were charged with memorizing the histories of local rulers and personages and the caste was further broken down into music-playing griots (similar to [bards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bard)) and non-music-playing griots. Like Praise-singers, the griot's main profession was musical acquisition and prowess, and patrons were the sole means of financial support. Modern griots enjoy higher status in the patronage of rich individuals in places such as [Mali](19127.md), [Senegal](27278.md), [Gambia](65656.md), [Mauritania](18778516.md) and [Guinea](12176.md), and to some extent make up the vast majority of musicians in these countries. Examples of modern popular griot artists include [Youssou N'Dour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Youssou_N'Dour), [Mamadou Diabate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamadou_Diabate), [Sona Jobareteh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sona_Jobarteh), and [Toumani Diabate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toumani_Diabate).

In other areas of West Africa, primarily among the [Hausa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hausa_people), [Mossi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mossi_people), [Dagomba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dagomba_people) and [Yoruba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoruba_people) in the area encompassing [Burkina Faso](3470.md), northern [Ghana](12067.md), [Nigeria](21383.md) and [Niger](21373.md), the traditional profession of non-hereditary praise-singers, [minstrels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minstrels), bards and poets play a vital role in extending the public show of power, lineage and prestige of traditional rulers through their exclusive patronage. Like the griot tradition, praise singers are charged with knowing the details of specific historical events and royal lineages, but more importantly need to be capable of poetic improvisation and creativity, with knowledge of traditional songs directed towards showing a patron's financial and political or religious power. Competition between Praise-singing ensembles and artists is high, and artists responsible for any extraordinarily skilled prose, musical compositions, and panegyric songs are lavishly rewarded with money, clothing, provisions and other luxuries by patrons who are usually politicians, rulers, Islamic clerics and merchants; these successful praise-singers rise to national stardom. Examples include [Mamman Shata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamman_Shata), [Souley Konko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Souley_Konko?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Fati Niger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fati_Niger), [Saadou Bori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saadou_Bori?action=edit\&redlink=1) and [Dan Maraya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Maraya). In the case of Niger, numerous praise songs are composed and shown on television in praise of local rulers, Islamic clerics, and politicians.

### Film industry

[Nollywood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nollywood) of Nigeria, is the main film industry of West Africa. The Nigerian cinema industry is the second largest film industry in terms of number of annual film productions, ahead of the [American film industry](418334.md) in [Hollywood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hollywood,_Los_Angeles).[^cite_note-123] [Senegal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinema_of_Senegal) and [Ghana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinema_of_Ghana) also have long traditions of producing films. ## Religion

West Africa along with North Africa are the only regions in Africa which have Muslim majority populations, and [Nigeria](21383.md) has the largest Muslim population in Africa.[^cite_note-auto1-124] In addition to Islam, Christianity and traditional religions are also practiced.

### Christianity

Further information: [Christianity in Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_Africa) and [Religion in Africa § Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_Africa#Christianity)



Cathedral [Basilica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica) of the Most [Holy Trinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity), [Onitsha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onitsha), Nigeria

[Christianity](5211.md) is the second largest religion practiced in West Africa.[^cite_note-125] Christianity was largely introduced from the late 18th century onward, when missionaries from European countries brought the religion to the region.[^cite_note-126] West African Christians are predominantly [Roman Catholic](606848.md) or [Anglican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglicanism); some [Evangelical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelical_Christianity) churches have also been established. Christianity is the predominant religion in [Ghana](12067.md), [Togo](30138.md), [Benin Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benin_Republic), [Liberia](17791.md) to coastal parts of [Sierra Leone](27308.md) and southern part of [Nigeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_Nigeria) and [Ivory Coast](57744.md) . elements of traditional African religion are mixed with Christianity.[^cite_note-127]

### Islam

Further information: [Islam in Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_Africa) and [Religion in Africa § Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_Africa#Islam)



The 13th-century [Great Mosque of Djenné](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Mosque_of_Djenn%C3%A9) is a superb example of the indigenous [Sahelian architectural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahelian_architecture) style prevalent in the Savannah and Sahelian interior of West Africa. It is listed an [UNESCO World Heritage Site](44940.md).

[Islam](6037917.md) is largely practiced in the West African interior and the far west coast of the continent and was introduced to the region by traders in the 8th century. Islamic rules on livelihood, values, dress and practices had a profound effect on the populations and cultures in their predominant areas, so much so that the concept of tribalism is less observed by Islamized groups like the [Wolof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolof_people), [Hausa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hausa_people), [Fula](102383.md), [Songhai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songhai_people), [Zarma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zarma_people) or [Soninke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soninke_people), than they are by non-Islamized groups.[^cite_note-128] Ethnic intermarriage and shared cultural icons are established through a superseded commonality of belief or community, known as [ummah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ummah).[^cite_note-129] Traditional Muslim areas include [Senegal](27278.md), [Gambia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gambia), [Mali](19127.md), [Mauritania](18778516.md), [Guinea](12176.md), [Niger](21373.md); the upper coast of [Sierra Leone](27308.md) the western, northern and far-eastern regions of [Burkina Faso](3470.md); and the northern half of [Nigeria](21383.md) and [Ivory Coast](57744.md).[^cite_note-130]

### African traditional

Further information: [Traditional African religion § West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_African_religion#West_Africa), [West African Vodun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_African_Vodun), and [Religion in Africa § African Traditional Religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_Africa#African_Traditional_Religion)



Voodoo altar with several fetishes in [Abomey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abomey), Benin

[Traditional African religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_African_religion) (noting the many different belief systems) are the oldest belief systems among the populations of this region, and include [Akan religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akan_religion), [Yoruba religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoruba_religion), [Odinani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odinani)-[Igbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igbo_people), and [Serer religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serer_religion). They are [spiritual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirituality) [creeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creed) that also perform other functions such as preserving the historical and cultural heritage of the people,[^cite_note-131] and "West African tribal groups" blend social and [religious rituals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_ritual) together to the point where there is usually not "much distinction" between them.[^cite_note-\:02-132] Although traditional beliefs vary from one place to the next, there are more similarities than differences.[^cite_note-133]

Most traditional religious organizations "do not have a formal hierarchy of [priests](23707.md)."[^cite_note-\:02-132] Group rituals are usually overseen by tribal elders who, "within many cultures", "serve as the main religious figures and determine the time, nature, and intricacies of rituals", or [shaman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shamanism) priests who can use magic to heal, control fate, and connect to the spirit world.[^cite_note-\:02-132]

## Demographics and languages

Further information: [Demographics of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographics_of_Africa), [List of ethnic groups of Africa § West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_ethnic_groups_of_Africa#West_Africa), [List of African countries by population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_African_countries_by_population), [African diaspora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_diaspora), and [Writing systems of Africa § West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writing_systems_of_Africa#West_Africa)



Language family map of West Africa



[Hausa people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hausa_people) are native to West Africa

Native West Africans primarily speak [Niger–Congo languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niger%E2%80%93Congo_languages), belonging mostly, though not exclusively, to its non-Bantu branches, though some [Nilo-Saharan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilo-Saharan) and [Afro-Asiatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afroasiatic_languages) speaking groups are also found in West Africa. The Niger–Congo-speaking [Yoruba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoruba_people), [Igbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igbo_people), [Fulani](102383.md), [Akan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akan_people) and [Wolof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolof_people) ethnic groups are the largest and most influential. In the central Sudan/Sahel, [Mandinka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandinka_people) or [Mande](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mand%C3%A9_peoples) groups are most significant. Chadic-speaking groups, most prominently including the [Hausa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hausa_people), and Nilo-Saharan-speaking communities, such as the [Songhai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songhai_people), [Kanuri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanuri_people) and [Zarma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zarma_people), are found in the eastern parts of the Sahel bordering [Central Africa](539606.md). In [Mali](19127.md), [Niger](21373.md), and [Burkina Faso](3470.md), the nomadic [Tuareg](103054.md) speak the [Tuareg language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuareg_language), a Berber language. The population of West Africa is estimated at 419 million[^cite_note-UN_WPP-1][^cite_note-UN_WPP_2022-2] people as of 2021.

Colonial languages also play a pivotal cultural and political role, being adopted as the official languages of most countries in the region, as well as *linguae franca* in communication between the region's various ethnic groups. For historical reasons, Western European languages such as [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language), [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language) and [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_language) predominate in Southern and Coastal subregions, whilst [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language) (in its [Maghrebi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maghrebi_Arabic) varieties) spreads inland northwards.

## Economic and regional organizations



Map of [petroleum](23195.md) and [natural gas](22131.md) within West Africa

### Economic Community of West Africa

This section is an excerpt from [ECOWAS](36848.md).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=ECOWAS\&action=edit)]

The Economic Community of West African States ([ECOWAS](36848.md); also known as CEDEAO in French and Portuguese) is a regional [political](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_union) and [economic union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_union) of twelve countries of West Africa. Collectively, the present and former members comprise an area of 5,114,162 km<sup>2</sup> (1,974,589 sq mi) and have an estimated population of over 424.34 million.[^cite_note-134]

Considered one of the pillar [regional blocs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_Economic_Communities) of the continent-wide [African Economic Community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Economic_Community) (AEC), the stated goal of ECOWAS is to achieve "collective [self-sufficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-sustainability)" for its member states by creating a single large trade bloc by building a full economic and trading union. Additionally, ECOWAS aims to raise [living standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_of_living) and promote [economic development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_development).[^cite_note-135] The union was established on 28 May 1975, with the signing of the [Treaty of Lagos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Lagos),[^cite_note-136] with its stated mission to promote economic integration across the region. A revised version of the [treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty) was agreed and signed on 24 July 1993 in [Cotonou](56647.md), the largest city in Benin.[^cite_note-137]

ECOWAS's published principles include equality and inter-dependence of member states, solidarity, self-reliance, cooperation and harmonization of policies, nonaggression, promotion of [human rights](13831.md), economic and [social justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_justice), and democratic governance.[^cite_note-138]

Notably among ECOWAS's protocols and plans are the ECOWAS Free Movement of Persons, Residences and Establishment Protocol and the Ecotour Action Plan 2019–2029. The *Free Movement of Persons Protocol* permits citizens the right to enter and reside in any member state's territory,[^cite_note-ECOWAS_\:4-139] and the *Ecotour Action Plan* aims to develop and integrate the tourist industry of each member state.[^cite_note-ECOWAS_\:6-140]

ECOWAS also serves as a [peacekeeping](84617.md) force in the region, with member states occasionally sending joint [military forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_forces) to intervene in the bloc's member countries at times of political instability and unrest.[^cite_note-ECOWAS_deployment_to_liberia_2003_08_06_janes_defense_weekly-141][^cite_note-ECOWAS_yahoomilint-142]

In 2024, the [military governments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coup_Belt) of [Niger](21373.md), [Burkina Faso](3470.md), and [Mali](19127.md) jointly announced their withdrawal from the bloc, after having been suspended following respective military takeovers in these countries.[^cite_note-143][^cite_note-144] The withdrawal took effect on 29 January 2025.[^cite_note-145] The three later went on to form the [Alliance of Sahel States](74840743.md), with the end goal of establishing a federation.[^cite_note-146][^cite_note-147]

### West African Monetary Union

The [West African Monetary Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_African_Monetary_Union) (or UEMOA from its name in French, *Union économique et monétaire ouest-africaine*) is limited to the eight, mostly Francophone countries that employ the [CFA franc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CFA_franc) as their common currency. The [Liptako–Gourma Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liptako%E2%80%93Gourma_Authority) of Mali, Niger, and Burkina Faso seeks to jointly develop the contiguous areas of the three countries.

### Women's peace movement

Since the adoption of the [United Nations Security Council Resolution 1325](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Security_Council_Resolution_1325) in 2000, women have been engaged in rebuilding war-torn Africa. Starting with the [Women of Liberia Mass Action for Peace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_of_Liberia_Mass_Action_for_Peace) and Women in Peacebuilding Network (WIPNET), the [peace movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_movement) has grown to include women across West Africa.

Established on 8 May 2006, [Women Peace and Security Network – Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_Peace_and_Security_Network_%E2%80%93_Africa) (WIPSEN-Africa), is a women-focused, women-led [Pan-African](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-African) non-governmental organization based in [Ghana](12067.md).[^cite_note-About_WIPSEN-148] The organization focuses on empowering women to have a role in political and peace governance in Africa.[^cite_note-About_WIPSEN-148] It has a presence in Ghana, [Nigeria](21383.md), [Ivory Coast](57744.md), [Liberia](17791.md) and [Sierra Leone](27308.md). Regional leaders of [nonviolent resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonviolent_resistance) include [Leymah Gbowee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leymah_Gbowee),[^cite_note-149] [Comfort Freeman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comfort_Freeman), and [Aya Virginie Toure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aya_Virginie_Toure).

*[Pray the Devil Back to Hell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pray_the_Devil_Back_to_Hell)* is a documentary film about the origin of this peace movement. The film has been used as an advocacy tool in post-conflict zones like [Sudan](27421.md) and [Zimbabwe](34399.md), mobilizing African women to petition for peace and security.[^cite_note-150]
