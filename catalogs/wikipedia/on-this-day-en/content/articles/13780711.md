# Peripheral tolerance

In [immunology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunology), **peripheral tolerance** is the second branch of [immunological tolerance](5024592.md), after [central tolerance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_tolerance). It takes place in [lymph nodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lymph_nodes) and tissues other than the [thymus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thymus) and [bone marrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bone_marrow) (after [T](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T_cell) and [B cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B_cell) egress from [primary lymphoid organs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_lymphoid_organ)). Its main purpose is to ensure that [self-reactive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reactive_lymphocyte) T and B cells which escaped central tolerance do not cause [autoimmune disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autoimmune_disease).[^cite_note-Janeway-1] Peripheral tolerance can also serve a purpose in preventing an immune response to harmless food [antigens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antigen) and [allergens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allergen).[^cite_note-2] [Mary E. Brunkow](81273907.md), [Fred Ramsdell](60424610.md), and [Shimon Sakaguchi](48010182.md) were awarded the [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](52502.md) in 2025 "for their discoveries concerning peripheral immune tolerance."[^cite_note-3]&#x20;

Self-reactive cells are subject to [clonal deletion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clonal_deletion) or clonal diversion. Both processes of peripheral tolerance control the presence and production of self-reactive immune cells.[^cite_note-4] Deletion of self-reactive T cells in the [thymus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thymus) is only 60–70% efficient, and [naive T cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naive_T_cell) repertoire contains a significant portion of low-avidity self-reactive T cells. These cells can trigger an autoimmune response, and there are several mechanisms of peripheral tolerance to prevent their activation.[^cite_note-\:3-5] Antigen-specific mechanisms of peripheral tolerance include persistence of T cell in quiescence, ignorance of antigen and direct inactivation of effector T cells by either clonal deletion, conversion to [regulatory T cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulatory_T_cell) (Tregs) or induction of [anergy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clonal_anergy).[^cite_note-\:0-6][^cite_note-\:3-5] Tregs, which are also generated during thymic T cell development, further suppress the effector functions of conventional lymphocytes in the periphery.[^cite_note-\:2-7] [Dendritic cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dendritic_cell) (DCs) participate in the negative selection of autoreactive T cells in the thymus, but they also mediate peripheral immune tolerance through several mechanisms.[^cite_note-\:7-8]&#x20;

Dependence of a particular antigen on either central or peripheral tolerance is determined by its abundance in the organism.[^cite_note-\:1-9] B cells have a lower probability that they will express cell surface markers to pose the threat of causing an autoimmune attack.[^cite_note-10] What little is known about peripheral tolerance of B cells indicate that it is largely mediated by B cell dependence on T cell help. However, as B cells adjust their specificities in the [germinal center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germinal_center), the self-reacting ones do tend to be selected against through unknown means.[^cite_note-11]&#x20;

## Cells mediating peripheral tolerance

### Regulatory T cells

Tregs are the central mediators of immune suppression, and they play a key role in maintaining peripheral tolerance. The master regulator of Treg phenotype and function is [Foxp3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foxp3). Natural Tregs (nTregs) are generated in the thymus during the [negative selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_selection_\(immunology\)). [TCR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-cell_receptor) of nTregs shows a high affinity for self-peptides. Induced Tregs (iTreg) develop from conventional naive helper T cells after antigen recognition in presence of [TGF-β](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TGF-%CE%B2) and [IL-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interleukin_2). iTregs are enriched in the gut to establish tolerance to commensal microbiota and harmless food antigens.[^cite_note-12] Regardless of their origin, once present, Tregs use several different mechanisms to suppress autoimmune reactions. These include depletion of IL-2 from the environment, secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines [IL-10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interleukin_10), TGF-β and [IL-35](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interleukin_35),[^cite_note-13] and induction of apoptosis of effector cells. [CTLA-4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CTLA-4) is a surface molecule present on Tregs which can prevent [CD28](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CD28)-mediated co-stimulation of T cells after TCR antigen recognition.[^cite_note-\:2-7]&#x20;

### Tolerogenic DCs

[Dendritic cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dendritic_cell) (DCs) are a major cell population responsible for the initiation of the adaptive immune response. They present short peptides on [MHCII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MHC_class_II), which are recognized by specific TCR. After encountering an antigen with recognition [danger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damage-associated_molecular_pattern)- or [pathogen-associated molecular patterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathogen-associated_molecular_pattern), DCs start the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, express costimulatory molecules [CD80](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CD80) and [CD86](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CD86) and migrate to the [lymph nodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lymph_node) to activate naive T cells.[^cite_note-Janeway-1] However, immature DCs (iDCs) are able to induce both [CD4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CD4) and [CD8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CD8) tolerance. The immunogenic potential of iDCs is weak, because of the low expression of costimulatory molecules and a modest level of MHCII. iDCs perform endocytosis and phagocytosis of foreign antigens and apoptotic cells, which occurs physiologically in peripheral tissues. Antigen-loaded iDCs migrate to the lymph nodes, secrete IL-10, TGF-β and present antigen to the naive T cells without [co-stimulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Co-stimulation). If the T cell recognizes the antigen, it is turned into the anergic state, depleted or converted to Treg.[^cite_note-\:4-14] iDCs are more potent Treg inducers than lymph node–resident DCs.[^cite_note-\:7-8] [BTLA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BTLA) is a crucial molecule for DCs-mediated Treg conversion.[^cite_note-15] Tolerogenic DCs express [FasL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fas_ligand) and [TRAIL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TRAIL) to directly induce apoptosis of responding T cells. They also produce [indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indoleamine_2,3-dioxygenase) (IDO) to prevent T cell proliferation. Retinoic acid is secreted to support iTreg differentiation, too.[^cite_note-\:5-16] Nonetheless, upon maturation (for example, during the infection), DCs largely lose their tolerogenic capabilities.[^cite_note-\:4-14]&#x20;

### LNSCs

Aside from dendritic cells, additional cell populations were identified that are able to induce antigen-specific T cell tolerance. These are mainly the members of [lymph node stromal cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lymph_node_stromal_cell) (LNSCs). LNSCs are generally divided into several subpopulations based on the expression of gp38 ([PDPN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PDPN)) and [CD31](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CD31) surface markers.[^cite_note-17] Among those, only fibroblastic reticular cells and lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs) were shown to play a role in peripheral tolerance. Both of those populations are able to induce CD8 T cell tolerance by the presentation of the endogenous antigens on [MHCI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MHC_class_I) molecules.[^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19] LNSCs lack expression of the [autoimmune regulator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autoimmune_regulator), and the production of autoantigens depends on transcription factor [Deaf1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DEAF1). LECs express [PD-L1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PD-L1) to engage [PD-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Programmed_cell_death_protein_1) on CD8 T cells to restrict self-reactivity.[^cite_note-\:6-20] LNSCs can drive the CD4 T cell tolerance by the presentation of the peptide-MHCII complexes, which they acquired from the DCs.[^cite_note-21] On the other hand, LECs can serve as a self-antigen reservoir and can transport self-antigens to DCs to direct self-peptide-MHCII presentation to CD4 T cells. In [mesenteric lymph nodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superior_mesenteric_lymph_nodes) (mLN), LNSCs can induce Tregs directly by secretion of TGF-β or indirectly by imprinting mLN–resident DCs.[^cite_note-\:6-20]&#x20;

## Intrinsic mechanisms of T cell peripheral tolerance

Although the majority of self-reactive T cell clones are deleted in the thymus by the mechanisms of [central tolerance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_tolerance), low-affinity self-reactive T cells continuously escape to the immune periphery.[^cite_note-\:1-9] Therefore, additional mechanisms exist to prevent self-reactive and unrestrained T cell responses.&#x20;

### Quiescence

When naive T cells exit the [thymus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thymus), they are in a quiescent state. That means they are in the non-proliferative, G0 stage of the [cell cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_cycle) and they have low metabolic, transcriptional and translational activities, but still retain the capacity to enter the cell cycle.[^cite_note-22] Quiescence can prevent [naive T cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naive_T_cell) activation after tonic signaling, meaning that T cells may be constitutively activated when not in the presence of a ligand.[^cite_note-23] After antigen exposure and co-stimulation, naive T cells start the process called quiescence exit, which results in proliferation and effector differentiation.[^cite_note-24]&#x20;

Naive cells must enter and exit a quiescent state at the proper timing in their life cycle. If T cells exit a quiescence prematurely, there is a lack of tolerance to potential self-reactive cells. T cells rely on negative regulators to keep them in a quiescence state until they are ready for exit; the [downregulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downregulation_and_upregulation) of negative regulators increases T cell activation. Premature and overactivation of T cells can lead to harmful downstream responses and possibly trigger an autoimmune response.[^cite_note-\:03-25]&#x20;

As cells exit a quiescent state, they will up regulate enzymes that are responsible for production of essential pathways (nucleic acids, proteins, carbohydrates, etc.).[^cite_note-\:03-25] At this stage, the T cell will enter the cell cycle and continue to be metabolically active.&#x20;

### Ignorance

When self-reactive T cells escape thymic deletion, they may enter an ignorant state.[^cite_note-26] Self-reactive T cells can fail to initiate immune response after recognition of self-antigen. These T cells are not classified as dysfunctional members of the immune response; rather, they are antigen-inexperienced naive cells that will remain in circulation.[^cite_note-\:23-27] These cells retain the ability to become activated if in the presence of the correct stimuli.&#x20;

Ignorance can be seen in situations where there is not a high enough concentration of antigen to trigger activation. The intrinsic mechanism of ignorance is when the affinity of TCR to antigen is too low to elicit T cell activation. There is also an extrinsic mechanism. Antigens, which are present in generally low numbers, cannot stimulate T cells sufficiently.[^cite_note-Janeway-1] Additionally, there are anatomical barriers that prohibit the activation of these T cells. These specialized mechanisms ensuring ignorance by the immune system have developed in so-called [immune privileged organs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immune_privilege).[^cite_note-\:0-6]&#x20;

T cells can overcome ignorance through a sufficient signal from signaling molecules (cytokines, infection, inflammatory stimuli, etc.) and induce an autoimmune response.[^cite_note-\:23-27] In the inflammatory context, T cells can override ignorance and induce autoimmune disease.[^cite_note-\:3-5]&#x20;

### Anergy

Anergy is a state of functional unresponsiveness induced upon self-antigen recognition.[^cite_note-28] T cells can be made nonresponsive to antigens presented if the T cell engages an MHC molecule on an antigen presenting cell (signal 1) without engagement of costimulatory molecules (signal 2). Co-stimulatory molecules are upregulated by cytokines (signal 3) in the context of acute inflammation. Without pro-inflammatory cytokines, co-stimulatory molecules will not be expressed on the surface of the antigen presenting cell, and so anergy will result if there is an MHC-TCR interaction between the T cell and the APC.[^cite_note-\:0-6] TCR stimulation leads to translocation of [NFAT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NFAT) into the nucleus. In the absence of co-stimulation, there is no [MAPK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MAPK) signaling in T cells and translocation of transcription factor [AP-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AP-1_transcription_factor) into the nucleus is impaired. This disbalance of transcription factors in T cells results in the expression of several genes involved in forming an anergic state.[^cite_note-29] Anergic T cells show long-lasting epigenetic programming that silences effector cytokine production. Anergy is reversible and T cells can recover their functional responsiveness in the absence of the antigen.[^cite_note-\:3-5]&#x20;

### Peripheral deletion

Before release into the periphery, T cells are subjected to thymic deletion if they prove to have the capacity to react with self. Peripheral deletion is the disposal of potential self-reactive T cells that escaped thymic deletion.[^cite_note-Herndon_4098–4104-30]&#x20;

After T cell response to co-stimulation–deficient antigen, a minor population of T cells develop anergy and a large proportion of T cells are rapidly lost by apoptosis. This cell death can be mediated by intrinsic pro-apoptotic family member [BIM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BCL2L11). The balance between proapoptotic BIM and the antiapoptotic mediator [BCL-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bcl-2) determine the eventual fate of the tolerized T cell.[^cite_note-\:3-5] There are also extrinsic mechanisms of deletion mediated by the cytotoxic activity of [Fas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fas_receptor)/FasL or TRAIL/[TRAILR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_receptor_4) interaction.[^cite_note-\:5-16] Cell death can be mediated by intrinsic of extrinsic methods as mentioned. In most instances, there is an upregulation of death markers or the presence of Bcl-2 proteins, which are essential proteins in facilitating programmed cell death.[^cite_note-Herndon_4098–4104-30]&#x20;

## Immunoprivileged organs

Immunoprivileged organs evolved mechanisms in which specialized tissue cells and immune cells can mount an appropriate response without disturbing the specialized tissue.[^cite_note-31] Immunopathogenic disturbances are not present in a variety of specialized organs such as the eyes, reproductive organs, and the central nervous system. These areas are protected by several mechanisms: [Fas-ligand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fas_ligand) expression binds, Fas on lymphocytes inducing apoptosis, anti-inflammatory cytokines (including TGF-beta and [interleukin 10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interleukin_10)) and blood-tissue barrier with [tight junctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tight_junctions) between endothelial cells.&#x20;

## Split tolerance

Split tolerance describes how some antigens can trigger an immune response in one aspect of the immune system and the same antigen could not trigger a response in another set of immune cells. Since many pathways of immunity are interdependent, they do not all need to be tolerized. For example, tolerized T cells will not activate auto-reactive B cells. Without this help from [CD4 T cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T_helper_cell), the B cells will not be activated.[^cite_note-Janeway-1]&#x20;
