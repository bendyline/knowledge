# Sugar

This article is about the class of sweet-flavoured substances used as food. For common table sugar, see [Sucrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sucrose). For other uses, see [Sugar (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_\(disambiguation\)).



Sugars (clockwise from top-left): white refined, unrefined, [brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_sugar), unprocessed cane sugar&#x20;

**Sugar** is a class of [sweet-tasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweetness), soluble [carbohydrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbohydrate), many of which are used in food. Simple sugars, also called [monosaccharides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monosaccharide), include [glucose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucose), [fructose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fructose) and [galactose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galactose). Compound sugars, also called [disaccharides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disaccharide) or double sugars, are molecules made of two [bonded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glycosidic_bond) monosaccharides; common examples are [sucrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sucrose) (glucose + fructose), [lactose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactose) (glucose + galactose) and [maltose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maltose) (two molecules of glucose). [White sugar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_sugar) is almost pure sucrose. During digestion, compound sugars are [hydrolysed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrolysed) into simple sugars.

Longer chains of saccharides are not regarded as sugars, and are called [oligosaccharides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oligosaccharide) or [polysaccharides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polysaccharide). [Starch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starch) is a glucose polymer found in plants – the most abundant source of energy in [human food](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_food). Some other chemical substances, such as [ethylene glycol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethylene_glycol), [glycerol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glycerol) and [sugar alcohols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_alcohol), may have a sweet taste, but are not classified as sugar.

Sugars are found in the [tissues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_tissue) of most plants. [Honey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honey) and fruits are abundant natural sources of simple sugars. Sucrose is especially concentrated in [sugarcane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugarcane) and [sugar beet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_beet), making them efficient for commercial [extraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extract) to make refined sugar. In 2016 the combined world production of those two crops was about two billion [tonnes](31185.md). Maltose may be produced by [malting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malt) grain. Lactose is the only sugar that cannot be extracted from plants, as it occurs only in milk, including human breast milk, and in some [dairy products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dairy_product). A cheap source of sugar is [corn syrup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corn_syrup), industrially produced by converting [corn starch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corn_starch) into sugars, such as maltose, fructose and glucose.

Sucrose is used in prepared foods (e.g., cookies and cakes), is [added](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Added_sugar) to commercially available [ultra-processed food](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultra-processed_food) and beverages, and is used as a sweetener for foods (e.g., toast and cereal) and beverages (e.g., coffee and tea). Globally, on average, a person consumes about 24 kilograms (53 pounds) of sugar each year. North and South Americans consume up to 50 kg (110 lb), and Africans consume under 20 kg (44 lb).[^cite_note-1]

The use of added sugar in food and beverage manufacturing is a concern for elevated [calorie intake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calorie), which is associated with an increased risk of several diseases, such as [obesity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obesity), [diabetes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diabetes) and [cardiovascular disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiovascular_disease).[^cite_note-huang-2] In 2015 the [World Health Organization](33583.md) recommended that adults and children should reduce their intake of free sugars to less than 10% of their total [energy intake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_homeostasis), encouraging a reduction to below 5%.[^cite_note-who-nlm-3]

## Etymology

The [etymology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etymology) of *sugar* reflects the commodity's spread. From [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit) *_śarkarā_*, meaning "ground or candied sugar", came [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language) *_shakar_* and Arabic *sukkar*. The Arabic word was borrowed in [Medieval Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Latin) as *succarum*, whence came the 12th-century [Old French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_French) *sucre* and the English *sugar*. Sugar was introduced into Europe by the Arabs in [Sicily](27619.md) and Spain.[^cite_note-oed-4]

The English word *[jaggery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaggery)*, a coarse [brown sugar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_sugar) made from [date palm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Date_palm) sap or sugarcane juice, has a similar etymological origin: Portuguese *_jágara_* from the Malayalam *_cakkarā_*, which is from the Sanskrit *_śarkarā_*.[^cite_note-5]

## History

Main article: [History of sugar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_sugar)



[Sugar cane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_cane) plantation

Sugar was first produced from sugar cane in the Indian subcontinent.[^cite_note-Moxham2002-6] Diverse species of sugar cane seem to have originated from India (*[Saccharum barberi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saccharum_barberi)* and *[S. edule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saccharum_edule)*) and New Guinea (*[S. officinarum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saccharum_officinarum)*).[^cite_note-Kiple-7][^cite_note-Sharpe-8] Sugarcane is described in Chinese manuscripts dating to the 8th century BCE, which state that the use of sugarcane originated in India.[^cite_note-gr1-9]

[Nearchus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nearchus) (admiral of [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great)), the Greek physician [Pedanius Dioscorides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dioscorides) and the Roman [Pliny the Elder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliny_the_Elder) also described sugar.[^cite_note-faas-10] In the mid-15th century, sugar was introduced into [Madeira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madeira) and the [Canary Islands](5717.md), where it was mass-produced. [Christopher Columbus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Columbus) introduced it to the [New World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_World), leading to sugar industries in [Cuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Captaincy_General_of_Cuba) and [Jamaica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Jamaica) by the 1520s.[^cite_note-duke-11] The Portuguese took sugar cane to Brazil.&#x20;

Beet sugar, the starting point for the modern sugar industry,[^cite_note-12] was a German invention.[^cite_note-13][^cite_note-TUBe-14] Beet sugar was first produced industrially in 1801 in [Cunern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konary,_Wo%C5%82%C3%B3w_County), [Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Prussia).[^cite_note-TUBe-14]

Sugar became a household item by the 19th century, and this evolution of taste and demand for sugar as an essential food ingredient resulted in major economic and social changes.[^cite_note-mintz-15] Demand drove, in part, the colonisation and industrialisation of previously under-developed lands. It was also intimately associated with slavery.[^cite_note-mintz-15] World consumption increased more than 100 times from 1850 to 2000, led by the United Kingdom, where it increased from about 2 pounds per head per year in 1650 to 90 pounds by the early 20th century.

## Chemistry



[Sucrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sucrose): a disaccharide of [glucose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucose) (left) and [fructose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fructose) (right)



Sugars (e.g. glucose) and their polymers (such as starch, cellulose).

Scientifically, *sugar* loosely refers to a number of compounds typically with the formula (CH<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>n</sub>. Some large classes of sugars, ranked in increasing order of molecular weight are [monosaccharides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monosaccharide), [disaccharides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disaccharide), or oligosaccharides.&#x20;

### Monosaccharides

Main article: [monosaccharide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monosaccharide)

Monosaccharides are also called "simple sugars", the most important being glucose. Most monosaccharides have a formula that conforms to C<sup></sup>\
<sub>n</sub>H<sup></sup>\
<sub>2n</sub>O<sup></sup>\
<sub>n</sub> with n between 3 and 7 ([deoxyribose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deoxyribose) being an exception). [Glucose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucose) has the [molecular formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_formula) C<sup></sup>\
<sub>6</sub>H<sup></sup>\
<sub>12</sub>O<sup></sup>\
<sub>6</sub>. The names of typical sugars end with -*ose*, as in "glucose" and "[fructose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fructose)". Such labels may also refer to any types of these compounds. Fructose, galactose and glucose are all simple sugars, monosaccharides, with the general formula C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>12</sub>O<sub>6</sub>. They have five hydroxyl groups (−OH) and a carbonyl group (C=O) and are cyclic when dissolved in water. They each exist as several [isomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomer) with dextro- and laevo-rotatory forms that cause polarised light to diverge to the right or the left.[^cite_note-Manual-16]

- [Fructose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fructose), or fruit sugar, occurs naturally in fruits, some root vegetables, cane sugar and honey and is the sweetest of the sugars. It is one of the components of sucrose or table sugar. It is used as a [high-fructose syrup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-fructose_corn_syrup), which is manufactured from hydrolysed corn starch that has been processed to yield [corn syrup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corn_syrup), with enzymes then added to convert part of the glucose into fructose.

  [^cite_note-17]

- [Galactose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galactose) generally does not occur in the free state but is a constituent with glucose of the disaccharide lactose or milk sugar. It is less sweet than glucose. It is a component of the antigens found on the surface of [red blood cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_blood_cell) that determine [blood groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABO_blood_group_system).

  [^cite_note-Raven_and_Johnson-18]

- [Glucose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucose) occurs naturally in fruits and plant juices and is the primary product of [photosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis). [Starch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starch) is converted into glucose during digestion, and glucose is the form of sugar that is transported around the bodies of animals in the bloodstream. Although in principle there are two [enantiomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enantiomer) of glucose (mirror images one of the other), naturally occurring glucose is D-glucose. This is also called *dextrose*, or *grape sugar* because drying grape juice produces crystals of dextrose that can be [sieved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sieve) from the other components.

  [^cite_note-19]

The [acyclic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-chain_compound) monosaccharides (and disaccharides) contain either [aldehyde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldehyde) groups or [ketone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ketone) groups. These [carbon-oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonyl) double bonds (C=O) are the reactive centres. All [saccharides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saccharide_\(disambiguation\)) with more than one ring in their structure result from two or more monosaccharides joined by [glycosidic bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glycosidic_bond) with the resultant loss of a molecule of water (H<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub>O) per bond.[^cite_note-Pigman-20]

### Disaccharides

Main article: [disaccharide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disaccharide)

Lactose, maltose and sucrose are disaccharides, also called "compound sugars". They share the formula C<sub>12</sub>H<sub>22</sub>O<sub>11</sub>. They are formed by the condensation of two monosaccharide molecules with the expulsion of a molecule of water.[^cite_note-Manual-16]

- [Lactose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactose) is the naturally occurring sugar found in milk. A molecule of lactose is formed by the combination of a molecule of galactose with a molecule of glucose. It is broken down when consumed into its constituent parts by the enzyme [lactase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactase) during digestion. Children have this enzyme, but some adults no longer form it and they are unable to digest lactose.

  [^cite_note-21]

- [Maltose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maltose) is formed during the germination of certain grains, the most notable being [barley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barley), which is converted into [malt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malt), the source of the sugar's name. A molecule of maltose is formed by the combination of two molecules of glucose. It is less sweet than glucose, fructose or sucrose.

  [^cite_note-Manual-16]

  &#x20;It is formed in the body during the digestion of starch by the enzyme [amylase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amylase) and is itself broken down during digestion by the enzyme [maltase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maltase).

  [^cite_note-22]

- [Sucrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sucrose) is found in the stems of sugarcane and roots of sugar beet. It also occurs naturally alongside fructose and glucose in other plants, in particular fruits and some roots such as carrots. The different proportions of sugars found in these foods determines the range of sweetness experienced when eating them.

  [^cite_note-Manual-16]

  &#x20;A molecule of sucrose is formed by the combination of a molecule of glucose with a molecule of fructose. After being eaten, sucrose is split into its constituent parts during digestion by a number of enzymes known as [sucrases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sucrase).

  [^cite_note-23]

Longer than disaccharides are oligosaccharides and polysaccharides. [Cellulose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellulose) and [chitin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chitin) are polymers, often crystalline, found in diverse plants and insects, respectively. Sugar cane accounted for around 21% of the global crop production over the 2000–2021 period. The Americas was the leading region in the production of sugar cane (52% of the world total).[^cite_note-28] Global production of [sugarcane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugarcane) in 2022 was 1.9 billion tonnes, with Brazil producing 38% of the world total and India 23% (table).

Sugarcane is any of several species, or their hybrids, of giant grasses in the genus *[Saccharum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saccharum)* in the family [Poaceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poaceae). They have been cultivated in tropical climates in the Indian subcontinent and Southeast Asia over centuries for the sucrose found in their stems.[^cite_note-Moxham2002-6]



World production of raw sugar, main producers

[^cite_note-29]

Sugar cane requires a frost-free climate with sufficient rainfall during the growing season to make full use of the plant's substantial growth potential. The crop is harvested mechanically or by hand, chopped into lengths and conveyed rapidly to the processing plant (commonly known as a [sugar mill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_mill)) where it is either milled and the juice extracted with water or extracted by diffusion.[^cite_note-skil2-30] The juice is clarified with [lime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium_hydroxide) and heated to destroy [enzymes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzyme). The resulting thin syrup is concentrated in a series of evaporators, after which further water is removed. The resulting [supersaturated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supersaturation) solution is seeded with sugar crystals, facilitating crystal formation and drying.[^cite_note-skil2-30] [Molasses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molasses) is a by-product of the process and the fibre from the stems, known as [bagasse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bagasse),[^cite_note-skil2-30] is burned to provide energy for the sugar extraction process. The crystals of raw sugar have a sticky brown coating and either can be used as they are, can be bleached by [sulfur dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfur_dioxide), or can be treated in a [carbonatation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonatation) process to produce a whiter product.[^cite_note-skil2-30] About 2,500 litres (660 US gal) of irrigation water is needed for every one kilogram (2.2 pounds) of sugar produced.[^cite_note-31]

In 2022 global production of sugar beets was 260 million [tonnes](31185.md), led by Russia with 18.8% of the world total (table).

Sugar beet became a major source of sugar in the 19th century when methods for extracting the sugar became available. It is a [biennial plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biennial_plant),[^cite_note-32] a [cultivated variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultivar) of *[Beta vulgaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_vulgaris)* in the [family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_\(taxonomy\)) [Amaranthaceae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amaranthaceae), the tuberous root of which contains a high proportion of sucrose. It is cultivated as a root crop in temperate regions with adequate rainfall and requires a fertile soil. The crop is harvested mechanically in the autumn and the crown of leaves and excess soil removed. The roots do not deteriorate rapidly and may be left in the field for some weeks before being transported to the processing plant where the crop is washed and sliced, and the sugar extracted by diffusion.[^cite_note-skil-33] [Milk of lime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milk_of_lime) is added to the raw juice with [calcium carbonate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium_carbonate). After water is evaporated by boiling the syrup under a vacuum, the syrup is cooled and seeded with sugar crystals. The [white sugar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_sugar) that crystallises can be separated in a centrifuge and dried, requiring no further refining.[^cite_note-skil-33]

### Refining

See also: [Sugar refinery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_refinery), [Non-centrifugal cane sugar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-centrifugal_cane_sugar), and [White sugar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_sugar)

Refined sugar is made from raw sugar that has undergone a [refining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refining) process to remove the molasses.[^cite_note-agrifinasi-34][^cite_note-kompas-35] Raw sugar is sucrose which is extracted from sugarcane or sugar beet. While raw sugar can be consumed, the refining process removes unwanted tastes and results in refined sugar or white sugar.[^cite_note-western-36][^cite_note-tebu-37]

The sugar may be transported in bulk to the country where it will be used and the refining process often takes place there. The first stage is known as affination and involves immersing the sugar crystals in a concentrated syrup that softens and removes the sticky brown coating without dissolving them. The crystals are then separated from the liquor and dissolved in water. The resulting syrup is treated either by a [carbonatation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonatation) or by a phosphatation process. Both involve the precipitation of a fine solid in the syrup and when this is filtered out, many of the impurities are removed at the same time. Removal of colour is achieved by using either a granular [activated carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activated_carbon) or an [ion-exchange resin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion-exchange_resin). The sugar syrup is concentrated by boiling and then cooled and seeded with sugar crystals, causing the sugar to crystallise out. The liquor is spun off in a centrifuge and the white crystals are dried in hot air and ready to be packaged or used. The surplus liquor is made into refiners' molasses.[^cite_note-38]

The [International Commission for Uniform Methods of Sugar Analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Commission_for_Uniform_Methods_of_Sugar_Analysis) sets standards for the measurement of the purity of refined sugar, known as ICUMSA numbers; lower numbers indicate a higher level of purity in the refined sugar.[^cite_note-39]

Refined sugar is widely used for industrial needs for higher quality. Refined sugar is purer (ICUMSA below 300) than raw sugar (ICUMSA over 1,500).[^cite_note-tebu_2-40] The level of purity associated with the colours of sugar, expressed by standard number [ICUMSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Commission_for_Uniform_Methods_of_Sugar_Analysis), the smaller ICUMSA numbers indicate the higher purity of sugar.[^cite_note-tebu_2-40]

## Forms and uses

### Crystal size

See also: [Rock candy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_candy), [Sucrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sucrose), and [Powdered sugar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powdered_sugar)



Misri crystals



[Rock candy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_candy) coloured with green dye

- [Coarse-grain sugar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coarse-grain_sugar), also known as sanding sugar, composed of reflective crystals with grain size of about 1 to 3 mm, similar to [kitchen salt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitchen_salt). Used atop baked products and candies, it will not dissolve when subjected to heat and moisture.

  [^cite_note-TSA-41]

- Granulated sugar (about 0.6 mm crystals), also known as table sugar or regular sugar, is used at the table, to sprinkle on foods and to sweeten hot drinks (coffee and tea), and in home baking to add sweetness and texture to baked products (cookies and cakes) and desserts (pudding and ice cream). It is also used as a preservative to prevent micro-organisms from growing and perishable food from spoiling, as in candied fruits, jams, and [marmalades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marmalade).

  [^cite_note-42]

- Milled sugars such as [powdered sugar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powdered_sugar) (icing sugar) are ground to a fine powder. They are used for dusting foods and in baking and confectionery.

  [^cite_note-BS-43]

  [^cite_note-TSA-41]

- Screened sugars such as [caster sugar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caster_sugar) are crystalline products separated according to the size of the grains. They are used for decorative table sugars, for blending in dry mixes and in baking and confectionery.

  [^cite_note-BS-43]

### Densities

The densities of culinary sugars varies owing to differences in particle size and inclusion of moisture:[^cite_note-44]

- Beet sugar 0.80 g/mL

- Dextrose sugar 0.62 g/mL ( = 620 kg/m^3)

- Granulated sugar 0.70 g/mL

- Powdered sugar 0.56 g/mL

### Shapes



Sugar cubes

- [Cube sugar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cube_sugar) (sometimes called sugar lumps) are white or brown granulated sugars lightly steamed and pressed together in block shape. They are used to sweeten drinks.

  [^cite_note-BS-43]

- [Sugarloaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugarloaf) was the usual [cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cone)-form in which refined sugar was produced and sold until the late 19th century.

  [^cite_note-45]

### Brown sugars

Main article: [Brown sugar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_sugar)



Brown sugar examples: Muscovado (top), dark brown (left), light brown (right)

Brown sugars are granulated sugars, either containing residual molasses, or with the grains deliberately coated with molasses to produce a light- or dark-coloured sugar such as [muscovado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muscovado) and [turbinado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbinado). They are used in baked goods, confectionery, and [toffees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toffee).[^cite_note-BS-43] Their darkness is due to the amount of molasses they contain. They may be classified based on their darkness or country of origin.[^cite_note-TSA-41]

### Liquid sugars



A jar of honey with a [dipper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honey_dipper) and a [biscuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_biscuit)&#x20;

- [Glucose syrup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucose_syrup) and corn syrup are widely used in the manufacture of foodstuffs. They are manufactured from starch by [enzymatic hydrolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzymatic_hydrolysis).

  [^cite_note-46]

  &#x20;For example, corn syrup, which is produced commercially by breaking down [maize starch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize_starch), is one common source of purified dextrose.

  [^cite_note-47]

  &#x20;Such [syrups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrup) are use in producing beverages, [hard candy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_candy), [ice cream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_cream), and [jams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fruit_preserves).

  [^cite_note-BS-43]

- [Inverted sugar syrup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverted_sugar_syrup), commonly known as invert syrup or invert sugar, is a mixture of two simple sugars – glucose and fructose – that is made by heating granulated sugar in water. It is used in breads, cakes and beverages for adjusting sweetness, aiding moisture retention and avoiding crystallisation of sugars.

  [^cite_note-BS-43]

- Molasses and [treacle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treacle) are obtained by removing sugar from sugarcane or sugar beet juice, as a byproduct of sugar production. They may be blended with the above-mentioned syrups to enhance sweetness and used in a range of baked goods and confectionery including toffees and [licorice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Licorice).

  [^cite_note-BS-43]

- In [winemaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winemaking), [fruit sugars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugars_in_wine) are converted into alcohol by a [fermentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermentation_\(wine\)) process. If the [must](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Must) formed by pressing the fruit has a low sugar content, additional sugar may be added to raise the alcohol content of the wine in a process called [chaptalisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaptalization). In the production of sweet wines, fermentation may be halted before it has run its full course, leaving behind some [residual sugar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweetness_of_wine) that gives the wine its sweet taste.

  [^cite_note-Oxford_pg_665-666-48]

### Burnt sugars and caramels

Heating sugar to near 200 °C for several minutes yields a product called burnt sugar. Often additives are used to modify the resulting caramels, e.g. [alkali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkali) or [sulfites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfite). Several volatile products evolve in the heating process including [butanone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butanone), several [furans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Furan) ([2-Acetylfuran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-Acetylfuran), [furanone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Furanone), [hydroxymethyl furfural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroxymethyl_furfural)), and [levoglucosan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levoglucosan) and more.[^cite_note-UllmannSugar-49]

Because sugars burn easily when exposed to flame, the handling of sugar powders risks [dust explosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_explosion).[^cite_note-50] The [2008 Georgia Imperial sugar refinery explosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Georgia_Imperial_Sugar_refinery_explosion), which killed 14 people and injured 36, and destroyed most of the refinery, was caused by the ignition of sugar dust.[^cite_note-51]

### Other sweeteners

See also: [Saccharin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saccharin)

- Low-calorie sweeteners are often made of [maltodextrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maltodextrin) with added sweeteners. Maltodextrin is an easily digestible synthetic [polysaccharide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polysaccharide) consisting of short chains of three or more glucose molecules and is made by the partial [hydrolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrolysis) of starch.

  [^cite_note-52]

  &#x20;Strictly, maltodextrin is not classified as sugar as it contains more than two glucose molecules, although its structure is similar to [maltose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maltose), a molecule composed of two joined glucose molecules.

- [Polyols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyol) are [sugar alcohols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_alcohol) and are used in chewing gums where a sweet flavour is required that lasts for a prolonged time in the mouth.

  [^cite_note-Union1990-53]

## Consumption

Worldwide sugar provides 10% of the daily calories (based on a 2000 kcal diet).[^cite_note-54] In 1750 the average Briton got 72 calories a day from sugar. In 1913 this had risen to 395. In 2015, sugar still provided around 14% of the calories in British diets.[^cite_note-55] According to one source, *per capita* consumption of sugar in 2016 was highest in the United States, followed by Germany and the Netherlands.[^cite_note-56]

## Health effects

The World Health Organization and other clinical associations recommend that reducing the consumption of *free sugar* (sugar sources added during manufacturing) to less than 10% of total energy needs can help to lower disease risk.[^cite_note-huang-2][^cite_note-who-nlm-3] This amount of sugar consumption is equivalent to about 50 g (1.8 oz) or 12 [teaspoons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teaspoon) of added sugar per day.[^cite_note-diab-can-61] As of 2025, the [American Heart Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Heart_Association) recommends that free sugar intake should be limited to 6% of total daily energy needs, or 36 g (1.3 oz) (9 teaspoons) for adult males, and 25 g (0.88 oz) (6 teaspoons) for women.[^cite_note-aha-add-62] In many countries, the source and amount of added sugars can be viewed among ingredients on the [labels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_food_labeling_regulations) of packaged foods.[^cite_note-aha-add-62] Added sugars provide no nutritional benefit, but are a source of excess calories that can lead to overweight and increased disease risk.[^cite_note-huang-2][^cite_note-who-nlm-3][^cite_note-diab-can-61][^cite_note-aha-add-62]

### Obesity and metabolic syndrome

Main article: [Diet and obesity § Sugar consumption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diet_and_obesity#Sugar_consumption)

A 2003 technical report by the World Health Organization provided evidence that high intake of sugary drinks (including [fruit juice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fruit_juice)) increases the risk of obesity by adding to overall energy intake.[^cite_note-WHO2003-63] By itself, sugar is not a factor causing obesity and [metabolic syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolic_syndrome), but rather its excessive consumption adds to caloric burden, which [meta-analyses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meta-analysis) showed could increase the risk of developing [type 2 diabetes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diabetes_mellitus_type_2) and [metabolic syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolic_syndrome) in adults and children.[^cite_note-64][^cite_note-65]

### Cancer

Sugar consumption does not directly cause cancer.[^cite_note-Australia-66][^cite_note-67][^cite_note-CRUK-68] [Cancer Council Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cancer_Council_Australia) have stated that "there is no evidence that consuming sugar makes cancer cells grow faster or cause cancer".[^cite_note-Australia-66] There is an indirect relationship between sugar consumption and obesity-related cancers through increased risk of excess body weight.[^cite_note-CRUK-68][^cite_note-Australia-66][^cite_note-69]

The [American Institute for Cancer Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Institute_for_Cancer_Research) and [World Cancer Research Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Cancer_Research_Fund) recommend that people should limit sugar consumption.[^cite_note-70][^cite_note-71]

### Cognition

Despite some studies suggesting that sugar consumption causes hyperactivity, the quality of evidence is low[^cite_note-72] and it is generally accepted within the scientific community that the notion of children's "sugar rush" is a myth.[^cite_note-73][^cite_note-74] A 2019 meta-analysis found that sugar consumption does not improve [mood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mood_\(psychology\)), but can lower alertness and increase fatigue within an hour of consumption.[^cite_note-75] One review of low-quality studies of children consuming high amounts of [energy drinks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_drink) showed association with higher rates of unhealthy behaviours, including smoking and excessive alcohol use, and with hyperactivity and [insomnia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insomnia), although such effects could not be specifically attributed to sugar over other components of those drinks such as [caffeine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caffeine).[^cite_note-76]

### Tooth decay

The WHO, [Action on Sugar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_on_Sugar) and the Scientific Advisory Committee on Nutrition (SACN) state dental caries, also known as tooth decay/cavities, "can be prevented by avoiding dietary free sugars".[^cite_note-who-nlm-3][^cite_note-WHO_2017-77][^cite_note-78][^cite_note-79]

A review of human studies showed that the incidence of caries is lower when sugar intake is less than 10% of total energy consumed.[^cite_note-80] [Sugar-sweetened beverage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar-sweetened_beverage) consumption is associated with an increased risk of tooth decay.[^cite_note-81]

### Nutritional displacement

The "[empty calories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empty_calories)" argument states that a diet high in [added](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Added_sugar) (or 'free') sugars will reduce consumption of foods that contain [essential nutrients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essential_nutrient).[^cite_note-82] This nutrient displacement occurs if sugar makes up more than 25% of daily energy intake,[^cite_note-dietary_reference-83] a proportion associated with poor diet quality and risk of obesity.[^cite_note-who-nlm-3] Displacement may occur at lower levels of consumption.[^cite_note-dietary_reference-83]

### Recommended dietary intake

The WHO recommends that both adults and children reduce the intake of free sugars to less than 10% of total energy intake.[^cite_note-who-nlm-3] "Free sugars" include monosaccharides and disaccharides added to foods, and sugars found in fruit juice and concentrates, as well as in honey and syrups.[^cite_note-who-nlm-3][^cite_note-aha-add-62]

On 20 May 2016, the United States [Food and Drug Administration](11632.md) announced changes to the Nutrition Facts panel displayed on all foods, to be effective by July 2018. New to the panel is a requirement to list "added sugars" by weight and as a percentage of Daily Value (DV). For vitamins and minerals, the intent of DVs is to indicate how much should be consumed. For added sugars, the guidance is that 100% DV should not be exceeded. 100% DV is defined as 50 grams. For a person consuming 2000 calories a day, 50 grams is equal to 200 calories and thus 10% of total calories – the same guidance as the WHO.[^cite_note-84] To put this in context, most 12-US-fluid-ounce (355 ml) cans of soda contain 39 grams of sugar. In the United States, a government survey on food consumption in 2013–14 reported that, for men and women aged 20 and older, the average total sugar intakes – naturally occurring in foods and added – were, respectively, 125 and 99 grams per day.[^cite_note-85] The American Heart Association recommends even lower daily consumption of added sugars: 36 grams for men and 25 grams for women.[^cite_note-aha-add-62]

## Society and culture

Manufacturers of sugary products, such as soft drinks and candy, and the [Sugar Research Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_Association) have been accused of trying to influence consumers and medical associations in the 1960s and 1970s by creating doubt about the potential health hazards of sucrose overconsumption, while promoting [saturated fat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturated_fat) as the main dietary risk factor in [cardiovascular diseases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiovascular_disease).[^cite_note-jama2016-86] In 2016, the criticism led to recommendations that diet [policymakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Policymaker) emphasise the need for high-quality research that accounts for multiple [biomarkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomarker) on development of cardiovascular diseases.[^cite_note-jama2016-86]

Originally, no sugar was white; the anthropologist [Sidney Mintz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidney_Mintz) writes that white likely became understood as the ideal after groups who associated the colour white with purity transferred their value to sugar.[^cite_note-Mintz-2015-87] In India, sugar frequently appears in religious observances. For ritual purity, such sugar cannot be white.[^cite_note-Mintz-2015-87]

## In interstellar space

See also: [List of interstellar and circumstellar molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_interstellar_and_circumstellar_molecules)

As sugar could not have been easily formed under the extreme environment of early Earth, it has been suggested that certain sugars may have originated from space.[^cite_note-serra-88][^cite_note-nyt-89][^cite_note-CNN-90] [Astronomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy) postulate that [asteroids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid) delivered sugars to Earth, or sugars were always present in the [Solar System](26903.md).[^cite_note-serra-88][^cite_note-AP-91]

In 2000, astronomers detected [glycolaldehyde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glycolaldehyde)—a sugar-like molecule—in [interstellar space](177602.md#interstellar_space).[^cite_note-serra-88][^cite_note-nyt-89][^cite_note-92] True sugar was first collected from an asteroid in 2019.[^cite_note-93] Sugar was also detected in a sample collected from the asteroid [101955 Bennu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/101955_Bennu) in 2020.[^cite_note-CNN-90]

In 2026, [erythrulose](1302410.md)—a [tetrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrose) [monosaccharide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monosaccharide)—was detected in dust grains of a [molecular cloud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_cloud) near the center of the [Milky Way](2589714.md), marking the first time a sugar molecule was found in interstellar space.[^cite_note-serra-88] The identification of erythrulose in [galactic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy) dust indicates its possible involvement in forming sugar-containing [nucleic acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleic_acid) during the [origin of life on Earth](19179706.md) about four billion years ago.[^cite_note-serra-88]
