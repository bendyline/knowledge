**Spanish** (*español*) or **Castilian** (*castellano*) is a [Romance language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_languages) of the [Indo-European language family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European_languages) that evolved from the [Vulgar Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulgar_Latin) spoken on the [Iberian Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_Peninsula) of [Europe](9239.md). It originated in the [Kingdom of Castile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Castile), a kingdom in north-central Iberia.[^cite_note-4] Today, it is a [global language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_language) with 519 million native speakers, mainly in the [Americas](29833.md) and [Spain](26667.md), and about 636 million speakers total, including [second-language speakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_language). Spanish is the official language of [21 countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_where_Spanish_is_an_official_language), as well as one of the [six official languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Official_languages_of_the_United_Nations) of the [United Nations](31769.md).[^cite_note-un1-5][^cite_note-6] Spanish is the world's [second-most spoken native language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_languages_by_number_of_native_speakers) after [Mandarin Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandarin_Chinese);[^cite_note-size-7][^cite_note-8] the world's [fourth-most spoken language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_languages_by_total_number_of_speakers) overall after [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language), Mandarin Chinese, and [Hindustani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindustani_language) ([Hindi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindi)-[Urdu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urdu)); and the world's most widely spoken Romance language. The country with the largest population of native speakers is [Mexico](3966054.md),[^cite_note-9] with 130M speakers, almost triple that of the [United States of America](3434750.md).&#x20;

Spanish is part of the [Ibero-Romance language group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_Romance_languages), which evolved from several dialects of Vulgar Latin in Iberia after the [collapse of the Western Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collapse_of_the_Western_Roman_Empire) in the 5th century. The oldest Latin texts with traces of Spanish come from mid-northern Iberia in the 9th century,[^cite_note-10] and the first systematic written use of the language happened in [Toledo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toledo_\(Spain\)), a prominent city of the [Kingdom of Castile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Castile), in the 13th century. Spanish colonialism in the [early modern period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_modern_period) spurred the introduction of the language to overseas locations, most notably to the Americas.[^cite_note-11]

As a Romance language, Spanish is a descendant of Latin. About 75% of modern Spanish vocabulary is Latin in origin, including Latin borrowings from Ancient Greek.[^cite_note-12] Alongside English and [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language), it is also one of the most taught foreign languages throughout the world.[^cite_note-13] Spanish is well represented in the [humanities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanities) and [social sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_science).[^cite_note-14] Spanish is also the third most used language on the internet by number of users after English and Chinese[^cite_note-15] and the second most used language by number of websites after English.[^cite_note-16]

Spanish is used as an official language by [many international organizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_and_territories_where_Spanish_is_an_official_language#International_organizations), including the United Nations, [European Union](9317.md), [Organization of Ibero-American States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organization_of_Ibero-American_States), [Organization of American States](22788.md), [Union of South American Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_of_South_American_Nations), [Community of Latin American and Caribbean States](26313243.md), [African Union](10634933.md), and others.[^cite_note-un1-5]

## Name of the language and etymology

Main article: [Names given to the Spanish language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Names_given_to_the_Spanish_language)

### Name of the language

In Spain and some other parts of the Spanish-speaking world, Spanish is called not only *[español](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/espa%C3%B1ol#Spanish)* but also *[castellano](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/castellano#Spanish)* (Castilian), the language from the [Kingdom of Castile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Castile), contrasting it with other [languages spoken in Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Spain) such as [Galician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galician_language), [Basque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basque_language), [Asturian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asturian_language), [Catalan/Valencian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalan_language), [Aragonese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aragonese_language), [Occitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occitan_language) and other minor languages.

The [Spanish Constitution of 1978](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Constitution_of_1978) uses the term *castellano* to define the [official language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Official_language) of the whole of Spain, in contrast to *las demás lenguas españolas* (lit. 'the other [Spanish languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_languages)'). Article III reads as follows:

> *El castellano es la lengua española oficial del Estado. ... Las demás lenguas españolas serán también oficiales en las respectivas Comunidades Autónomas...*
>
> &#x20;Castilian is the official Spanish language of the State. ... The other Spanish languages shall also be official in their respective Autonomous Communities...

The [Royal Spanish Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_Academia_Espa%C3%B1ola) (*Real Academia Española*), on the other hand, currently uses the term *español* in its publications. However, from 1713 to 1923, it called the language *castellano*.[^cite_note-17]

The *[Diccionario panhispánico de dudas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diccionario_panhisp%C3%A1nico_de_dudas)* (a language guide published by the Royal Spanish Academy) states that, although the Royal Spanish Academy prefers to use the term *español* in its publications when referring to the Spanish language, both terms—*español* and *castellano*—are regarded as synonymous and equally valid.[^cite_note-18]

### Etymology

The term *castellano* is related to [Castile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Castile) (*Castilla* or archaically *Castiella*), the kingdom where the language was originally spoken. The name *Castile*, in turn, is usually assumed to be derived from *castillo* ('castle').

In the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), the language spoken in Castile was generically referred to as *Romance* and later also as *Lengua vulgar*.[^cite_note-espania-19] Later in the period, it gained geographical specification as *Romance castellano* (*romanz castellano*, *romanz de Castiella*), *lenguaje de Castiella*, and ultimately simply as *castellano* (noun).[^cite_note-espania-19]

Different etymologies have been suggested for the term *español* (Spanish). According to the Royal Spanish Academy, *español* derives from the [Occitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occitan_language) word *espaignol* and that, in turn, derives from the [Vulgar Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulgar_Latin) \**hispaniolus* ('of Hispania').[^cite_note-20] [Hispania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispania) was the Roman name for the entire [Iberian Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_Peninsula).

There are other hypotheses apart from the one suggested by the Royal Spanish Academy. Spanish philologist [Ramón Menéndez Pidal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ram%C3%B3n_Men%C3%A9ndez_Pidal) suggested that the classic *hispanus* or *hispanicus* took the suffix *-one* from [Vulgar Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulgar_Latin), as happened with other words such as *bretón* (Breton) or *sajón* (Saxon).

## History

Main article: [History of the Spanish language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Spanish_language)



The [Cartularies of Valpuesta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartularies_of_Valpuesta), written in a late form of Latin, were declared in 2010 by the Royal Spanish Academy as the record of the earliest words written in Castilian, predating those of the [Glosas Emilianenses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glosas_Emilianenses).

[^cite_note-21]

Like the other [Romance languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_languages), the Spanish language evolved from [Vulgar Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulgar_Latin), which was brought to the [Iberian Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_Peninsula) by the [Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republic) during the [Second Punic War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Punic_War), beginning in 210 BC. Several pre-Roman languages (also called [Paleohispanic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleohispanic_languages))—some distantly related to Latin as [Indo-European languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European_languages), and some that are not related at all—were previously spoken in the Iberian Peninsula. These languages included [Proto-Basque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Basque_language), [Iberian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_language), [Lusitanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lusitanian_language), [Celtiberian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtiberian_language) and [Gallaecian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallaecian_language).

The first documents to show traces of what is today regarded as the precursor of modern Spanish are from the 9th century. Throughout the Middle Ages and into the [modern era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_era), the most important [influences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_contact) on the Spanish lexicon came from neighboring [Romance languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_languages)—[Mozarabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andalusi_Romance) ([Andalusi Romance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andalusi_Romance)), [Navarro-Aragonese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navarro-Aragonese), [Leonese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonese_language), [Catalan/Valencian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalan_language), [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_language), [Galician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galician_language), [Occitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occitan_language), and later, [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language) and [Italian](14708.md). Spanish also [borrowed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loanword) a considerable number of words from [Andalusi Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andalusi_Arabic), and a few from [Basque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basque_language). In addition, many more words were borrowed from [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) through the influence of written language and the liturgical language of the Church. The loanwords were taken from both [Classical Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Latin) and [Renaissance Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_Latin), the form of Latin in use at that time.

According to the theories of [Ramón Menéndez Pidal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ram%C3%B3n_Men%C3%A9ndez_Pidal), local [sociolects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociolect) of Vulgar Latin evolved into Spanish, in the north of Iberia, in an area centered in the city of [Burgos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burgos), and this dialect was later brought to the city of [Toledo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toledo,_Spain), where the written standard of Spanish was first developed, in the 13th century.[^cite_note-Penny1p16-22] In this formative stage, Spanish developed a strongly differing variant from its close cousin, [Leonese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonese_language), and, according to some authors, was distinguished by a heavy Basque influence (see [Iberian Romance languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_Romance_languages)). This distinctive dialect spread to southern Spain with the advance of the *[Reconquista](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconquista)*, and meanwhile gathered a sizable lexical influence from the [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic) of [Al-Andalus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Andalus), much of it indirectly, through the Romance [Mozarabic dialects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andalusi_Romance) (some 4,000 [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic)-derived words, make up about 8% of the language today).[^cite_note-23] The written standard for this new language was developed in the cities of [Toledo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toledo,_Spain), in the 13th to 16th centuries, and [Madrid](41188263.md), from the 1570s.[^cite_note-Penny1p16-22]

The development of the [Spanish sound system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_phonology) from that of [Vulgar Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulgar_Latin) exhibits most of the changes that are typical of [Western Romance languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Romance_languages), including [lenition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenition) of intervocalic consonants (thus Latin *vīta* > Spanish *vida*). Spanish is marked by [palatalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palatalization_\(sound_change\)) of the Latin double consonants ([geminates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geminate)) *nn* and *ll* (thus Latin *annum* > Spanish *año*, and Latin *anellum* > Spanish *anillo*).

The consonant written *u* or *v* in Latin and pronounced \[w] in Classical Latin had probably "[fortified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortition)" to a bilabial fricative /β/ in Vulgar Latin. In early Spanish (but not in Catalan or Portuguese) it merged with the consonant written *b* (a bilabial with plosive and fricative allophones). In modern Spanish, there is [no difference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betacism) between the pronunciation of orthographic *b* and *v*.

Typical of Spanish (as also of neighboring [Gascon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gascon_language) extending as far north as the [Gironde estuary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gironde_estuary), and found in a small area of [Calabria](44772.md)), attributed by some scholars to a Basque [substratum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substrata_\(linguistics\)) was the mutation of Latin initial *f* into *h-* whenever it was followed by a vowel that did not diphthongize. The *h-*, still preserved in spelling, is now silent in most varieties of the language, although in some Andalusian and Caribbean dialects, it is still aspirated in some words. Because of borrowings from Latin and neighboring Romance languages, there are many *f*-/*h*- [doublets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doublet_\(linguistics\)) in modern Spanish: *Fernando* and *Hernando* (both Spanish for "Ferdinand"), *ferrero* and *herrero* (both Spanish for "smith"), *fierro* and *hierro* (both Spanish for "iron"), and *fondo* and *hondo* (both words pertaining to depth in Spanish, though *fondo* means "bottom", while *hondo* means "deep"); additionally, *hacer* ("to make") is [cognate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognate) to the root word of *satisfacer* ("to satisfy"), and *hecho* ("made") is similarly cognate to the root word of *satisfecho* ("satisfied").

In the 15th and 16th centuries, Spanish underwent [a dramatic change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonological_history_of_Spanish_coronal_fricatives) in the pronunciation of its [sibilant consonants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sibilant_consonants), known in Spanish as the *reajuste de las sibilantes*, which resulted in the distinctive [velar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velar_consonant) \[x] pronunciation of the letter ⟨j⟩ and—in a large part of Spain—the characteristic [interdental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interdental_consonant) \[θ] ("th-sound") for the letter ⟨z⟩ (and for ⟨c⟩ before ⟨e⟩ or ⟨i⟩). See [History of Spanish (Modern development of the Old Spanish sibilants)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Spanish#Modern_development_of_the_Old_Spanish_sibilants) for details.

The *[Gramática de la lengua castellana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gram%C3%A1tica_de_la_lengua_castellana)*, written in [Salamanca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salamanca) in 1492 by [Elio Antonio de Nebrija](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_de_Nebrija), was the first grammar written for a modern European language.[^cite_note-25] According to a popular anecdote, when Nebrija presented it to [Queen Isabella I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isabella_I_of_Castile), she asked him what was the use of such a work, and he answered that language is the instrument of empire.[^cite_note-26] In his introduction to the grammar, dated 18 August 1492, Nebrija wrote that "... language was always the companion of empire."[^cite_note-27]

From the 16th century onwards, the language was taken to the Spanish-discovered [America](29833.md) and the [Spanish East Indies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_East_Indies) via [Spanish colonization of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_colonization_of_the_Americas). [Miguel de Cervantes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miguel_de_Cervantes), author of *[Don Quixote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Don_Quixote)*, is such a well-known reference in the world that Spanish is often called *la lengua de Cervantes* ("the language of Cervantes").[^cite_note-28]

In the 20th century, Spanish was introduced to [Equatorial Guinea](9366.md) and the [Western Sahara](33220.md), and to areas of the United States that had not been part of the Spanish Empire, such as [Spanish Harlem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Harlem) in [New York City](645042.md). For details on borrowed words and other external influences upon Spanish, see [Influences on the Spanish language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Influences_on_the_Spanish_language).

## Geographical distribution

See also: [Hispanophone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispanophone)

Spanish is the primary language in 20 countries worldwide. As of 2025, it is estimated that about 519 million people speak Spanish as a [native language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_language), making it the second [most spoken language by number of native speakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_languages_by_number_of_native_speakers).[^cite_note-29] An additional 117 million speak Spanish as a second or [foreign language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_as_a_foreign_language), making it the fourth [most spoken language in the world overall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_languages_by_total_number_of_speakers) after English, Mandarin Chinese, and Hindi with a total number of 636 million speakers.[^cite_note-30] Spanish is also the third [most used language on the Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_used_on_the_Internet), after English and Chinese.[^cite_note-31]

Spanish is the official language of [Spain](26667.md). Upon the emergence of the [Castilian Crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castilian_Crown) as the dominant power in the Iberian Peninsula by the end of the Middle Ages, the Romance vernacular associated with this polity became increasingly used in instances of prestige and influence, and the distinction between "Castilian" and "Spanish" started to become blurred.[^cite_note-32] Hard policies imposing the language's hegemony in an intensely centralising Spanish state were established from the 18th century onward.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMar-Molinero200021-33]

Other European territories in which it is also widely spoken include [Gibraltar](7607314.md) and [Andorra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andorra).[^cite_note-34]

Spanish is also spoken by immigrant communities in other European countries, such as the [United Kingdom](31717.md), [France](5843419.md), [Italy](14532.md), and [Germany](11867.md).[^cite_note-35] Spanish is the most widely studied [Romance language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_language) in Europe. According to Eurostat data, about 27% of secondary school students in the European Union study Spanish, making it the second most taught foreign language after English and the most studied Romance language on the continent.[^cite_note-36] Spanish is an official language of the [European Union](9317.md).

### Americas

#### Hispanic America

Main article: [Spanish language in the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_language_in_the_Americas)

Today, the majority of the Spanish speakers live in [Hispanic America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispanic_America). #### United States

Main article: [Spanish language in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_language_in_the_United_States)

See also: [Spanish language in California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_language_in_California), [New Mexican Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Mexican_Spanish), and [Isleño Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isle%C3%B1o_Spanish)

The Spanish language has a long history in the territory of the current-day United States dating back to the 16th century.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELamboySalgado-Robles20201-40] In the wake of the [1848 Guadalupe Hidalgo Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Guadalupe_Hidalgo), hundreds of thousands of Spanish speakers became a minoritized community in the United States.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELamboySalgado-Robles20201-40] The 20th century saw further massive growth of Spanish speakers in areas where they had been hitherto scarce.[^cite_note-41]

According to the 2020 census, over 60 million people of the U.S. population were of [Hispanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispanic) or [Hispanic American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispanic_America) by origin.[^cite_note-42] In turn, 41.8 million people in the United States aged five or older speak Spanish at home, or about 13% of the population.[^cite_note-43] Spanish predominates in the unincorporated territory of [Puerto Rico](23041.md), where it is also an official language along with English.

Spanish is by far the most common second language in the country, with over 50 million total speakers if non-native or second-language speakers are included.[^cite_note-44] While English is the de facto national language of the country, Spanish is often used in public services and notices at the federal and state levels. Spanish is also used in administration in the state of [New Mexico](21649.md).[^cite_note-45] The language has a strong influence in major metropolitan areas such as those of [Los Angeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Los_Angeles_area), [San Diego](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Diego%E2%80%93Tijuana), [Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami_metropolitan_area), [San Antonio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Antonio_metropolitan_area), [New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_metropolitan_area), [San Francisco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_Bay_Area), [Dallas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dallas%E2%80%93Fort_Worth_metroplex), [Tucson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Tucson) and [Phoenix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenix_metropolitan_area) of the [Arizona Sun Corridor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arizona_Sun_Corridor), as well as more recently, [Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_metropolitan_area), [Las Vegas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Las_Vegas_Valley), [Boston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Boston), [Denver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Denver), [Houston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Houston), [Indianapolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Indianapolis), [Oklahoma City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_City_metropolitan_area), [Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Philadelphia), [Cleveland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Cleveland), [Salt Lake City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Salt_Lake_City), [Atlanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Atlanta), [Nashville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Nashville), [Orlando](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Orlando), [Tampa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Tampa), [Raleigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Raleigh) and [Baltimore-Washington, D.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltimore%E2%80%93Washington_metropolitan_area) due to 20th- and 21st-century immigration.

#### Rest of the Americas

Although Spanish has no official recognition in the former [British colony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_overseas_territories) of [Belize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belize) (known until 1973 as [British Honduras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Honduras)) where English is the sole official language, according to the 2022 census, 54% of the total population are able to speak the language.[^cite_note-46]

Due to its proximity to Spanish-speaking countries and small existing [native Spanish speaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinidadian_Spanish) minority, [Trinidad and Tobago](3565457.md) has implemented Spanish language teaching into its education system. The Trinidadian and Tobagonian government launched the *Spanish as a First Foreign Language* (SAFFL) initiative in March 2005.[^cite_note-47]

Spanish has historically had a significant presence on the [Dutch Caribbean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Caribbean) islands of [Aruba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aruba), [Bonaire](108737.md) and [Curaçao](65205.md) ([ABC Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABC_islands_\(Leeward_Antilles\))) throughout the centuries and in present times. The majority of the populations of each island (especially Aruba) speak Spanish at varying although often high degrees of fluency.[^cite_note-48] The local language [Papiamentu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papiamento) (or Papiamento in Aruba) is heavily influenced by Venezuelan Spanish.

In addition to sharing most of its borders with Spanish-speaking countries, the creation of [Mercosur](25869317.md) in the early 1990s induced a favorable situation for the promotion of Spanish language teaching in [Brazil](3383.md).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEValleVilla2006376-49][^cite_note-50] In 2005, the [National Congress of Brazil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Congress_of_Brazil) approved a bill, signed into law by the [President](273638.md), making it mandatory for [schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School) to offer Spanish as an alternative foreign language course in both public and private secondary schools in Brazil.[^cite_note-51] In September 2016 this law was revoked by [Michel Temer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Temer) after the [impeachment of Dilma Rousseff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impeachment_of_Dilma_Rousseff).[^cite_note-52] In many border towns and villages along Paraguay and Uruguay, a [mixed language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_language) known as [Portuñol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riverense_Portu%C3%B1ol) is spoken.[^cite_note-53]

### Africa

#### Sub-Saharan Africa

See also: [Equatoguinean Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equatoguinean_Spanish)



Spanish language signage in [Malabo](48823.md), capital city of [Equatorial Guinea](9366.md)

[Equatorial Guinea](9366.md) is the only Spanish-speaking country located entirely in Africa, with the language introduced during the [Spanish colonial period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Guinea).[^cite_note-54] Enshrined in the constitution as an official language (alongside French and Portuguese), Spanish features prominently in the Equatoguinean education system and is the primary language used in government and business.[^cite_note-55] Spanish is spoken as a native language by a small minority in Equatorial Guinea, primarily in larger cities.[^cite_note-56][^cite_note-57] The [Instituto Cervantes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instituto_Cervantes) estimates that 87.7% of the population is fluent in Spanish.[^cite_note-58] The proportion of proficient Spanish speakers in Equatorial Guinea exceeds the proportion of proficient speakers in other West and Central African nations of their respective colonial languages.[^cite_note-59]

Spanish is spoken by very small communities in [Angola](701.md) due to Cuban influence from the [Cold War](325329.md) and in [South Sudan](32350676.md) among South Sudanese natives that relocated to Cuba during the Sudanese wars and returned for their country's independence.[^cite_note-60]

#### North Africa and Macaronesia

See also: [Canarian Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canarian_Spanish) and [Saharan Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saharan_Spanish)

Spanish is also spoken in the integral territories of Spain in Africa, namely the cities of [Ceuta](6443.md) and [Melilla](20226.md) and the [Canary Islands](5717.md), located in the Atlantic Ocean some 100 km (62 mi) off the northwest of the African mainland. The [Spanish spoken in the Canary Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canarian_Spanish) traces its origins back to the [Castilian conquest in the 15th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conquest_of_the_Canary_Islands), and, in addition to a resemblance to Western Andalusian speech patterns, it also features strong influence from the Spanish varieties spoken in the Americas,[^cite_note-61] which in turn have also been influenced historically by Canarian Spanish.[^cite_note-62] The Spanish spoken in North Africa by native bilingual speakers of Arabic or Berber who also speak Spanish as a second language features characteristics involving the variability of the vowel system.[^cite_note-63]

While far from its heyday during the [Spanish protectorate in Morocco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_protectorate_in_Morocco), the Spanish language has some presence in northern [Morocco](19291.md), stemming for example from the availability of certain Spanish-language media.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEVicente201167-64] According to a 2012 survey by Morocco's Royal Institute for Strategic Studies (IRES), penetration of Spanish in Morocco reaches 4.6% of the population.[^cite_note-65] Many northern Moroccans have rudimentary knowledge of Spanish,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEVicente201167-64] with Spanish being particularly significant in areas adjacent to Ceuta and Melilla.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFernández_Vítores201832–46-66] Spanish also has a presence in the education system of the country (through either selected education centers implementing Spain's education system, primarily located in the North, or the availability of Spanish as foreign language subject in secondary education).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEVicente201167-64]

In [Western Sahara](33220.md), formerly [Spanish Sahara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Sahara), a primarily [Hassaniya Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hassaniya_Arabic)-speaking territory, Spanish was officially spoken as the language of the colonial administration during the late 19th and 20th centuries. Today, Spanish is present in the partially-recognized [Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic](38762388.md) as its secondary official language,[^cite_note-67] and in the [Sahrawi refugee camps in Tindouf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahrawi_refugee_camps) ([Algeria](358.md)), where the Spanish language is still taught as a second language, largely by Cuban educators.[^cite_note-68][^cite_note-69][^cite_note-70]

Spanish is also an official language of the [African Union](10634933.md).[^cite_note-71]

### Asia

See also: [Chavacano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chavacano), [Philippine Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippine_Spanish), and [Spanish language in the Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_language_in_the_Philippines)



An 1892 issue of&#x20;*&#x20;[La Solidaridad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Solidaridad)*, a Spanish-language newspaper on the [colonial Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Captaincy_General_of_the_Philippines) published in [Barcelona](4443.md) by Filipino exiles and international students

Spanish was an official language of the [Philippines](23440.md) from the beginning of Spanish administration in 1565 to a constitutional change in 1973. During [Spanish colonization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Philippines_\(1521%E2%80%931898\)), it was the language of government, trade, and education, and was spoken as a first language by Spaniards and educated Filipinos (*[Ilustrados](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilustrados)*). Despite a public education system set up by the colonial government, by the end of Spanish rule in 1898, only about 10% of the population had knowledge of Spanish, mostly those of Spanish descent or elite standing.[^cite_note-72]



Map of the [Chavacano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chavacano) language in various [provinces of the Philippines](165100.md), as well as [Sabah](28678.md) in [Malaysia](3607937.md) (where it is spoken by immigrants)

Spanish continued to be official and used in Philippine literature and press during the early years of [American administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insular_Government_of_the_Philippine_Islands) after the [Spanish–American War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish%E2%80%93American_War) but was eventually replaced by English as the primary language of administration and education by the 1920s.[^cite_note-73] Nevertheless, despite a significant decrease in influence and speakers, Spanish remained an official language of the Philippines upon independence in 1946, alongside English and [Filipino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filipino_language), a standardized version of [Tagalog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tagalog_language).

Spanish was briefly removed from official status in 1973 but reimplemented under the administration of [Ferdinand Marcos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Marcos) two months later.[^cite_note-74] It remained an official language until the ratification of the present constitution in 1987, in which it was re-designated as a voluntary and optional auxiliary language.[^cite_note-75] Additionally, the constitution, in its Article XIV, stipulates that [the government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_the_Philippines) shall provide the people of the Philippines with a Spanish-language translation of the country's constitution.[^cite_note-76] In recent years changing attitudes among non-Spanish speaking Filipinos have helped spur a revival of the language,[^cite_note-77][^cite_note-78] and starting in 2009 Spanish was reintroduced as part of the basic education curriculum in a number of public high schools, becoming the largest foreign language program offered by the public school system,[^cite_note-79] with over 7,000 students studying the language in the 2021–2022 school year alone.[^cite_note-80] The [local business process outsourcing industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_process_outsourcing_in_the_Philippines) has also helped boost the language's economic prospects.[^cite_note-81] Today, while the actual number of proficient Spanish speakers is about 400,000, or under 0.5% of the population,[^cite_note-82] a new generation of Spanish speakers in the Philippines has likewise emerged, though speaker estimates vary widely.[^cite_note-83]

Aside from standard Spanish, a Spanish-based creole language called [Chavacano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chavacano) developed in the southern Philippines. However, it is not mutually intelligible with Spanish.[^cite_note-84] The number of Chavacano-speakers was estimated at 1.2 million in 1996.[^cite_note-85] The local [languages of the Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_the_Philippines) also retain significant Spanish influence, with many words derived from [Mexican Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Spanish), owing to the administration of the islands by Spain through [New Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Spain) until 1821, until direct governance from Madrid afterwards to 1898.[^cite_note-86][^cite_note-87]

### Oceania



Announcement in Spanish on [Easter Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Island), welcoming visitors to [Rapa Nui National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapa_Nui_National_Park)

Spanish is the official and most spoken language on [Easter Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Island), which is geographically part of [Polynesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesia) in Oceania and politically part of [Chile](5489.md). However, Easter Island's traditional language is [Rapa Nui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapa_Nui_language), an [Eastern Polynesian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_languages).

As a legacy of comprising the former [Spanish East Indies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_East_Indies), Spanish loan words are present in the local languages of [Guam](11974.md), [Northern Mariana Islands](21402.md), [Palau](23372.md), [Marshall Islands](19147.md) and [Micronesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federated_States_of_Micronesia).[^cite_note-88][^cite_note-89]

In addition, in Australia and New Zealand, there are native Spanish communities, resulting from emigration from Spanish-speaking countries (mainly from the [Southern Cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Cone)).[^cite_note-90]

[Miguel de Cervantes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miguel_de_Cervantes), considered by many the greatest author of Spanish literature, and author of *[Don Quixote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Don_Quixote)*, widely considered the first modern European novel

Most of the grammatical and [typological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistic_typology) features of Spanish are shared with the other [Romance languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_languages). Spanish is a [fusional language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fusional_language). The [noun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_nouns) and [adjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_adjectives) systems exhibit two [genders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_gender) and two [numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_number). In addition, articles and some [pronouns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_pronouns) and [determiners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_determiners) have a neuter gender in their singular form. There are about fifty [conjugated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_conjugation) forms per [verb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verb), with 3 tenses: past, present, future; 2 [aspects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_aspect) for past: [perfective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfective_aspect), [imperfective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperfective_aspect); 4 [moods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_mood): indicative, subjunctive, conditional, imperative; 3 persons: first, second, third; 2 numbers: singular, plural; 3 [verboid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verboid) forms: infinitive, gerund, and past participle. The indicative mood is the [unmarked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markedness) one, while the subjunctive mood [expresses uncertainty or indetermination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subjunctive_mood_in_Spanish), and is commonly paired with the conditional, which is a mood used to express "would" (as in, "I would eat if I had food"); the imperative is a mood to express a command, commonly a one word phrase – "¡Di!" ("Talk!").

Verbs express [T–V distinction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%E2%80%93V_distinction) by using different persons for formal and informal addresses. (For a detailed overview of verbs, see [Spanish verbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_verbs) and [Spanish irregular verbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_irregular_verbs).)

Spanish [syntax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntax) is considered [right-branching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Branching_\(linguistics\)), meaning that subordinate or [modifying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_modifier) [constituents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constituent_\(linguistics\)) tend to be placed after head words. The language uses [prepositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preposition_and_postposition) (rather than postpositions or inflection of nouns for [case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_case)), and usually—though not always—places [adjectives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjective) after [nouns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noun), as do most other Romance languages.

Spanish is classified as a [subject–verb–object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subject%E2%80%93verb%E2%80%93object) language; however, as in most Romance languages, constituent order is highly variable and governed mainly by [topicalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topicalization) and [focus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Focus_\(linguistics\)). It is a "[pro-drop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pro-drop_language)", or "[null-subject](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null-subject_language)" language—that is, it allows the deletion of subject pronouns when they are [pragmatically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatics) unnecessary. Spanish is described as a "[verb-framed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verb_framing)" language, meaning that the *direction* of motion is expressed in the verb while the *mode* of locomotion is expressed adverbially (e.g. *subir corriendo* or *salir volando*; the respective English equivalents of these examples—'to run up' and 'to fly out'—show that English is, by contrast, "satellite-framed", with mode of locomotion expressed in the verb and direction in an adverbial modifier).

## Phonology

Spanish as spoken in Spain

Main article: [Spanish phonology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_phonology)

The Spanish phonological system evolved from that of [Vulgar Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulgar_Latin). Its development exhibits some traits in common with other [Western Romance languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Romance_languages), others with the neighboring Hispanic varieties—especially [Leonese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonese_dialect) and [Aragonese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aragonese_language)—as well as other features unique to Spanish. Spanish is alone among its immediate neighbors in having undergone frequent aspiration and eventual loss of the Latin initial /f/ sound (e.g. Cast. *harina* vs. Leon. and Arag. *farina*).[^cite_note-249] The Latin initial consonant sequences *pl-*, *cl-*, and *fl-* in Spanish typically merge as *ll-* (originally pronounced \[ʎ]), while in Aragonese they are preserved in most dialects, and in Leonese they present a variety of outcomes, including \[tʃ], \[ʃ], and \[ʎ]. Where Latin had *-li-* before a vowel (e.g. *filius*) or the ending *-iculus*, *-icula* (e.g. *auricula*), Old Spanish produced \[ʒ], that in Modern Spanish became the velar fricative \[x] (*hijo*, *oreja*), whereas neighboring languages have the palatal lateral \[ʎ] (e.g. Portuguese *filho*, *orelha*; Catalan *fill*, *orella*).

### Segmental phonology



Spanish vowel chart, from [Ladefoged & Johnson (2010](#CITEREFLadefogedJohnson2010)\:227)

The Spanish [phonemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoneme) inventory consists of five vowel phonemes (/a/, /e/, /i/, /o/, /u/) and 17 to 19 consonant phonemes (the exact number depending on the dialect[^cite_note-250]). The main [allophonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allophone) variation among vowels is the reduction of the high vowels /i/ and /u/ to glides—\[j] and \[w] respectively—when unstressed and adjacent to another vowel. Some instances of the mid vowels /e/ and /o/, determined lexically, alternate with the diphthongs /je/ and /we/ respectively when stressed, in a process that is better described as [morphophonemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphophonology) rather than phonological, as it is not predictable from phonology alone.

The Spanish consonant system is characterized by (1) three [nasal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasal_stop) phonemes, and one or two (depending on the dialect) [lateral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lateral_consonant) phoneme(s), which in syllable-final position [lose their contrast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archiphonemic) and are subject to [assimilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assimilation_\(linguistics\)) to a following consonant; (2) three [voiceless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voicelessness) [stops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plosive) and the [affricate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affricate_consonant) /tʃ/; (3) three or four (depending on the dialect) [voiceless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voicelessness) [fricatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fricative_consonant); (4) a set of voiced [obstruents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obstruent)—/b/, /d/, /ɡ/, and sometimes /ʝ/—which alternate between [approximant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Approximant_consonant) and [plosive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plosive) allophones depending on the environment; and (5) a phonemic distinction between the "[tapped](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flap_consonant)" and "[trilled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trill_consonant)" *r*-sounds (single ⟨r⟩ and double ⟨rr⟩ in orthography).

In the following table of consonant phonemes, /ʎ/ is marked with an asterisk (\*) to indicate that it is preserved only in some dialects. In most dialects it has been merged with /ʝ/ in the merger called *[yeísmo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ye%C3%ADsmo)*. Similarly, /θ/ is also marked with an asterisk to indicate that most dialects do not distinguish it from /s/ (see *[seseo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seseo)*), although this is not a true merger but an outcome of different evolution of sibilants in southern Spain.

The phoneme /ʃ/ is in parentheses () to indicate that it appears only in [loanwords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loanwords). Each of the voiced obstruent phonemes /b/, /d/, /ʝ/, and /ɡ/ appears to the right of a *pair* of voiceless phonemes, to indicate that, while the *voiceless* phonemes maintain a phonemic contrast between plosive (or affricate) and fricative, the *voiced* ones alternate [allophonically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allophone) (i.e. without phonemic contrast) between plosive and approximant pronunciations.

### Prosody

Spanish is classified by its [rhythm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isochrony) as a [syllable-timed language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isochrony#syllable_timing): each syllable has approximately the same duration regardless of stress.[^cite_note-252][^cite_note-253]

Spanish [intonation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intonation_\(linguistics\)) varies significantly according to dialect but generally conforms to a pattern of falling tone for declarative sentences and [wh-questions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Question#Open_questions) (who, what, why, etc.) and rising tone for [yes/no questions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yes%E2%80%93no_question).[^cite_note-254][^cite_note-255] There are no syntactic markers to distinguish between questions and statements and thus, the recognition of declarative or interrogative depends entirely on intonation.

Stress most often occurs on any of the last three syllables of a word, with some rare exceptions at the fourth-to-last or earlier syllables. Stress tends to occur as follows:[^cite_note-256]

- in words that end with a [monophthong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monophthong), on the penultimate syllable

- when the word ends in a [diphthong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diphthong), on the final syllable.

- in words that end with a consonant, on the last syllable, with the exception of two grammatical endings: *-n*, for third-person-plural of verbs, and *-s*, for plural of nouns and adjectives or for second-person-singular of verbs. However, even though a significant number of nouns and adjectives ending with *-n* are also stressed on the penult (*joven*, *virgen*, *mitin*), the great majority of nouns and adjectives ending with *-n* are stressed on their last syllable (*capitán*, *almacén*, *jardín*, *corazón*).

- stress on the fourth-to-last syllable occurs rarely, only on verbs with [clitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clitic) pronouns attached (e.g. *guardándoselos* 'saving them for him/her/them/you').

In addition to the many exceptions to these tendencies, there are numerous [minimal pairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_pair) that contrast solely on stress such as *sábana* ('sheet') and *sabana* ('savannah'); *límite* ('boundary'), *limite* ('he/she limits') and *limité* ('I limited'); *líquido* ('liquid'), *liquido* ('I sell off') and *liquidó* ('he/she sold off').

The orthographic system unambiguously reflects where the stress occurs: in the absence of an accent mark, the stress falls on the last syllable unless the last letter is ⟨n⟩, ⟨s⟩, or a vowel, in which case the stress falls on the next-to-last (penultimate) syllable. Exceptions to those rules are indicated by an acute accent mark over the vowel of the stressed syllable. (See [Spanish orthography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_orthography).)

## Speaker population

Spanish is the official, or national language in [18 countries and one "Unincorporated" territory in the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispanic_America), [Spain](26667.md), [Equatorial Guinea](9366.md) and co-official in the non-self-governing territory with limited recognition of [Western Sahara](33220.md) ([RASD](38762388.md)) With a population of over 410 million, [Hispanophone America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_language_in_the_Americas) accounts for the vast majority of Spanish speakers, of which [Mexico](3966054.md) is the most populous Spanish-speaking country. In the [European Union](9317.md), Spanish is the [mother tongue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_language) of 8% of the population, with an additional 7% speaking it as a second language.[^cite_note-257] Additionally, Spanish is the second most spoken language in the [United States](3434750.md) and is by far the most popular foreign language among students.[^cite_note-258] In 2015, it was estimated that over 50 million U.S.citizens, about 41 million of whom were native speakers.[^cite_note-259] With continued immigration and increased use of the language domestically in public spheres and media, the number of Spanish speakers in the United States is expected to continue growing over the forthcoming decades.[^cite_note-260]

## Dialectal variation



A world map attempting to identify the main dialects of Spanish

Main article: [Spanish dialects and varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_dialects_and_varieties)

While being mutually intelligible, there are important variations ([phonological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonology), [grammatical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammar), and [lexical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexicon)) in the spoken Spanish of the various regions of Spain and throughout the Spanish-speaking areas of the Americas.

The national variety with the most speakers is [Mexican Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Spanish). It is spoken by more than twenty percent of the world's Spanish speakers (more than 112 million of the total of more than 500 million, according to the table above). One of its main features is the [reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vowel_reduction) or loss of [unstressed vowels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unstressed_vowel), mainly when they are in contact with the sound /s/.[^cite_note-261][^cite_note-262]

In Spain, northern dialects are popularly thought of as closer to the standard, although positive attitudes toward southern dialects have increased significantly in the last 50 years. The speech from the educated classes of Madrid is the standard variety for use on radio and television in Spain and it is indicated by many as the one that has most influenced the written standard for Spanish.[^cite_note-263] Central (European) Spanish speech patterns have been noted to be in the process of merging with more innovative southern varieties (including Eastern Andalusian and Murcian), as an emerging interdialectal levelled *koine* buffered between the Madrid's traditional national standard and the Seville speech trends.[^cite_note-264]

### Phonology

See also: [Phonetic change "f → h" in Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonetic_change_%22f_%E2%86%92_h%22_in_Spanish)

The four main phonological divisions are based respectively on (1) the phoneme /[θ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiceless_dental_fricative)/, (2) the [debuccalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debuccalization) of syllable-final /s/, (3) the sound of the spelled ⟨s⟩, (4) and the phoneme /[ʎ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiced_palatal_lateral_approximant)/.

- The phoneme /θ/ (spelled *c* before *e* or *i* and spelled ⟨z⟩ elsewhere), a [voiceless dental fricative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiceless_dental_fricative) as in English ***th**ing*, is maintained by a majority of Spain's population, especially in the northern and central parts of the country. In other areas (some parts of southern Spain, the [Canary Islands](5717.md), and the Americas), /θ/ does not exist and /s/ occurs instead. The maintenance of phonemic contrast is called *[distinción](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distinci%C3%B3n)* in Spanish, while the merger is generally called *[seseo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seseo)* (in reference to the usual realization of the merged phoneme as \[s]) or, occasionally, *[ceceo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceceo)* (referring to its interdental realization, \[θ], in some parts of southern Spain). In most of Hispanic America, the spelled ⟨c⟩ before ⟨e⟩ or ⟨i⟩, and spelled ⟨z⟩ is always pronounced as a [voiceless dental sibilant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiceless_alveolar_sibilant#Dentalized_laminal_alveolar).

- The debuccalization (pronunciation as \[h], or loss) of syllable-final /s/ is associated with the southern half of Spain and lowland Americas: Central America (except central Costa Rica and Guatemala), the Caribbean, coastal areas of southern Mexico, and South America except Andean highlands. Debuccalization is frequently called "aspiration" in English, and *aspiración* in Spanish. When there is no debuccalization, the syllable-final /s/ is pronounced as [voiceless "apico-alveolar" sibilant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiceless_alveolar_sibilant#Retracted_alveolar) or as a [voiceless dental sibilant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiceless_alveolar_sibilant#Dentalized_laminal_alveolar) in the same fashion as in the next paragraph.

- The sound that corresponds to the letter ⟨s⟩ is pronounced in northern and central Spain as a [voiceless "apico-alveolar" sibilant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiceless_alveolar_sibilant#Retracted_alveolar) \[s̺] (also described acoustically as "[grave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grave_and_acute)" and articulatorily as "retracted"), with a weak "hushing" sound reminiscent of [retroflex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retroflex_consonant) fricatives. In [Andalusia](2736.md), [Canary Islands](5717.md) and most of Hispanic America (except in the [Paisa region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colombian_Spanish#Paisa_dialect) of Colombia) it is pronounced as a [voiceless dental sibilant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiceless_alveolar_sibilant#Dentalized_laminal_alveolar) \[s], much like the most frequent pronunciation of the /s/ of English.

- The phoneme /ʎ/, spelled ⟨ll⟩, a [palatal lateral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palatal_lateral_approximant) consonant that can be approximated by the sound of the ⟨lli⟩ of English *million*, tends to be maintained in less-urbanized areas of northern Spain and in the [highland areas of South America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andean_Spanish), as well as in [Paraguay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraguayan_Spanish) and [lowland Bolivia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolivian_Spanish#Dialects). Meanwhile, in the speech of most other Spanish speakers, it is merged with /ʝ/ ("curly-tail *j*"), a non-lateral, usually voiced, usually fricative, palatal consonant, sometimes compared to English /j/ (*yod*) as in ***y**acht* and spelled ⟨y⟩ in Spanish. As with other forms of allophony across world languages, the small difference of the spelled ⟨ll⟩ and the spelled ⟨y⟩ is usually not perceived (the difference is not heard) by people who do not produce them as different phonemes. Such a phonemic merger is called *[yeísmo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ye%C3%ADsmo)* in Spanish. In [Rioplatense Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rioplatense_Spanish), the merged phoneme is generally pronounced as a postalveolar fricative, either voiced \[ʒ] (as in English *measure* or the French ⟨j⟩) in the central and western parts of the dialectal region (*zheísmo*), or voiceless \[ʃ] (as in the French ⟨ch⟩ or Portuguese ⟨x⟩) in and around Buenos Aires and Montevideo (*sheísmo*).

  [^cite_note-265]

### Morphology

The main [morphological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphology_\(linguistics\)) variations between dialects of Spanish involve differing uses of pronouns, especially those of the second [person](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_person) and, to a lesser extent, the [object pronouns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_pronoun) of the third [person](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_person).

#### Voseo

Main article: [Voseo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voseo)



An examination of the dominance and stress of the *voseo* feature in Hispanic America. Data generated as illustrated by the [Association of Spanish Language Academies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_of_Spanish_Language_Academies). The darker the area, the stronger its dominance.

Virtually all dialects of Spanish make the [distinction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%E2%80%93V_distinction) between a formal and a familiar [register](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Register_\(sociolinguistics\)) in the [second-person](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_person) [singular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_number) and thus have two different [pronouns](24985.md) meaning "you": *usted* in the formal and either *tú* or *vos* in the familiar (and each of these three pronouns has its associated verb forms), with the choice of *tú* or *vos* varying from one dialect to another. The use of *vos* and its verb forms is called *[voseo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voseo)*. In a few dialects, all three pronouns are used, with *usted*, *tú*, and *vos* denoting respectively formality, familiarity, and intimacy.[^cite_note-rae.es_site-266]

In *voseo*, *vos* is the [subject](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subject_\(grammar\)) form (*vos decís*, "you say") and the form for the object of a [preposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preposition_and_postposition) (*voy con vos*, "I am going with you"), while the direct and indirect [object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_\(grammar\)) forms, and the [possessives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Possessive_adjective), are the same as those associated with *tú*: *Vos sabés que tus amigos te respetan* ("You know your friends respect you").

The verb forms of the general *voseo* are the same as those used with *tú* except in the present [tense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_tense) ([indicative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indicative) and [imperative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperative_mood)) verbs. The forms for *vos* generally can be derived from those of *vosotros* (the traditional second-person familiar *plural*) by deleting the [glide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semivowel) \[i̯], or /d/, where it appears in the ending: *vosotros pensá**i**s* > *vos pensás*; *vosotros volvé**i**s* > *vos volvés*, *pensa**d**!* (*vosotros*) > *pensá!* (*vos*), *volve**d**!* (*vosotros*) > *volvé!* (*vos*).[^cite_note-267]

##### Distribution in Spanish-speaking regions of the Americas

Although *vos* is not used in Spain, it occurs in many Spanish-speaking regions of the Americas as the primary spoken form of the second-person singular familiar pronoun, with wide differences in social consideration.[^cite_note-269] Generally, it can be said that there are zones of exclusive use of *tuteo* (the use of *tú*) in the following areas: almost all of [Mexico](3966054.md), the [West Indies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Indies), [Panama](22997.md), most of [Colombia](5222.md), [Peru](170691.md), [Venezuela](32374.md) and coastal [Ecuador](9334.md).

*Tuteo* as a cultured form alternates with *voseo* as a popular or rural form in [Bolivia](3462.md), in the north and south of Peru, in Andean Ecuador, in small zones of the Venezuelan Andes (and most notably in the Venezuelan state of [Zulia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zulia)), and in a large part of Colombia. Some researchers maintain that *voseo* can be heard in some parts of eastern Cuba, and others assert that it is absent from the island.[^cite_note-270]

*Tuteo* exists as the second-person usage with an intermediate degree of formality alongside the more familiar *voseo* in [Chile](5489.md), in the Venezuelan state of [Zulia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zulia), on the Caribbean coast of [Colombia](5222.md), in the [Azuero Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azuero_Peninsula) in Panama, in the Mexican state of [Chiapas](6787.md), and in parts of Guatemala.

Areas of generalized *voseo* include [Argentina](18951905.md), [Nicaragua](21362.md), eastern [Bolivia](3462.md), [El Salvador](9356.md), [Guatemala](17238567.md), [Honduras](13394.md), [Costa Rica](5551.md), [Paraguay](23420.md), [Uruguay](31843.md) and the Colombian departments of [Antioquia](185251.md), [Caldas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caldas_Department), [Risaralda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risaralda_Department), [Quindio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quindio) and [Valle del Cauca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valle_del_Cauca).[^cite_note-rae.es_site-266]

*Usted* is the usual second-person singular pronoun in a formal context, but it is used jointly with the third-person singular voice of the verb. It is used to convey respect toward someone who is a generation older or is of higher authority ("you, sir"/"you, ma'am"). It is also used in a *familiar* context by many speakers in Colombia and Costa Rica and in parts of Ecuador and Panama, to the exclusion of *tú* or *vos*. This usage is sometimes called *[ustedeo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ustedeo?action=edit\&redlink=1)* in Spanish.

In Central America, especially in Honduras, *usted* is often used as a formal pronoun to convey respect between the members of a romantic couple. *Usted* is also used that way between parents and children in the Andean regions of Ecuador, Colombia and Venezuela.

#### Third-person object pronouns

Most speakers use (and the *[Real Academia Española](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_Academia_Espa%C3%B1ola)* prefers) the pronouns *lo* and *la* for [direct objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_\(grammar\)) (masculine and feminine respectively, regardless of [animacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animacy), meaning "him", "her", or "it"), and *le* for [indirect objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_\(grammar\)) (regardless of [gender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_gender) or [animacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animacy), meaning "to him", "to her", or "to it"). The usage is sometimes called "etymological", as these direct and indirect object pronouns are a continuation, respectively, of the [accusative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accusative_case) and [dative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dative_case) pronouns of Latin, the ancestor language of Spanish.

A number of dialects (more common in Spain than in the Americas) use additional rules for the pronouns, such as animacy, or [count noun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Count_noun) vs. [mass noun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_noun), rather than just direct vs. indirect object. The ways of using the pronouns in such varieties are called "*[leísmo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le%C3%ADsmo)*", "*[loísmo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lo%C3%ADsmo)*", or "*[laísmo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La%C3%ADsmo)*", according to which respective pronoun, *le*, *lo*, or *la*, covers more than just the etymological usage (*le* as a direct object, or *lo* or *la* as an indirect object).

### Vocabulary

Some words can be significantly different in different Hispanophone countries. Most Spanish speakers can recognize other Spanish forms even in places where they are not commonly used, but Spaniards generally do not recognize specifically American usages. For example, Spanish *mantequilla*, *aguacate* and *albaricoque* (respectively, 'butter', 'avocado', 'apricot') correspond to *manteca* (word used for [lard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lard) in [Peninsular Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peninsular_Spanish)), *palta*, and *damasco*, respectively, in Argentina, Chile (except *manteca*), Paraguay, Peru (except *manteca* and *damasco*), and Uruguay. In the healthcare context, an assessment of the Spanish translation of the [QWB-SA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quality_of_well-being_scale) identified some regional vocabulary choices and US-specific concepts, which cannot be successfully implemented in Spain without adaptation.[^cite_note-271]

## Vocabulary

Spanish vocabulary has been influenced by several languages. As in other European languages, [Classical Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Greek) words (Hellenisms) are abundant in the terminologies of several fields, including art, science, politics, nature, etc.[^cite_note-272] Its vocabulary has also been [influenced by Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Influence_of_Arabic_on_Spanish), having developed during the [Al-Andalus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Andalus) era in the [Iberian Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_Peninsula), with about 8% of its vocabulary having [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language) lexical roots.[^cite_note-273][^cite_note-274][^cite_note-275][^cite_note-Dworkin83-276] It may have also been influenced by [Basque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basque_language), [Iberian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_language), [Celtiberian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtiberian_language), [Visigothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_language), and other neighboring Ibero-Romance languages.[^cite_note-277][^cite_note-Dworkin83-276] Additionally, it has absorbed vocabulary from other languages, particularly other Romance languages such as [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language), [Mozarabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mozarabic_language), [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_language), [Galician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galician_language), [Catalan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalan_language), [Occitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occitan_language), and [Sardinian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sardinian_language), as well as from [Quechua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quechua_language), [Nahuatl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nahuatl_language), and [other indigenous languages of the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Spanish_words_of_Indigenous_American_Indian_origin).[^cite_note-278] In the 18th century, words taken from French referring above all to fashion, cooking and bureaucracy were added to the Spanish lexicon. In the 19th century, new loanwords were incorporated, especially from English and German, but also from Italian in areas related to music, particularly opera and cooking. In the 20th century, the pressure of English in the fields of technology, computing, science and sports was greatly accentuated.

In general, [Hispanic America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispanic_America) is more susceptible to loanwords from English or [Anglicisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglicism). For example: [mouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_mouse) (computer mouse) is used in Hispanic America, in [Spain](26667.md) *ratón* is used. This happens largely due to closer contact with the [United States](3434750.md). For its part, Spain is known by the use of [Gallicisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallicism) or words taken from neighboring [France](5843419.md) (such as the Gallicism *ordenador* in European Spanish, in contrast to the Anglicism *computador* or *computadora* in American Spanish).

## Relation to other languages

Further information: [Comparison of Portuguese and Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparison_of_Portuguese_and_Spanish)

Spanish is closely related to the other [West Iberian Romance languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Iberian_languages), including [Asturian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asturian_language), [Aragonese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aragonese_language), [Galician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galician_language), [Ladino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ladino_language), [Leonese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonese_language), [Mirandese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirandese_language) and [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_language). It is somewhat less similar, to varying degrees, from other members of the [Romance language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_language) family.

It is generally acknowledged that Portuguese and Spanish speakers can communicate in written form, with varying degrees of mutual intelligibility.[^cite_note-279][^cite_note-280][^cite_note-281][^cite_note-282] [Mutual intelligibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutual_intelligibility) of the *written* Spanish and Portuguese languages is high, lexically and grammatically. *[Ethnologue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnologue)* gives estimates of the [lexical similarity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexical_similarity) between related languages in terms of precise percentages. For Spanish and Portuguese, that figure is 89%, although phonologically the two languages are quite dissimilar. Italian on the other hand, is phonologically similar to Spanish, while sharing lower lexical and grammatical similarity of 82%. Mutual intelligibility between Spanish and [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language) or between Spanish and [Romanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_language) is lower still, given lexical similarity ratings of 75% and 71% respectively.[^cite_note-283][^cite_note-284] Comprehension of Spanish by French speakers who have not studied the language is much lower, at an estimated 45%. In general, thanks to the common features of the writing systems of the Romance languages, interlingual comprehension of the written word is greater than that of oral communication.

### Judaeo-Spanish

Further information: [Judaeo-Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaeo-Spanish)



The [Rashi script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashi_script), originally used to print Judaeo-Spanish



An original letter in Haketia, written in 1832

Judaeo-Spanish, also known as Ladino,[^cite_note-Ladino-286] is a variety of Spanish which preserves many features of medieval Spanish and some old Portuguese and is spoken by descendants of the [Sephardi Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sephardi_Jews) who were [expelled from Spain in the 15th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alhambra_decree).[^cite_note-Ladino-286] While in Portugal the conversion of Jews occurred earlier and the assimilation of [New Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Christians) was overwhelming, in Spain the Jews kept their language and identity. The relationship of Ladino and Spanish is therefore comparable with that of the [Yiddish language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yiddish_language) to [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language). Ladino speakers today are almost exclusively [Sephardi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sephardim) Jews, with family roots in Turkey, Greece, or the Balkans, and living mostly in Israel, Turkey, and the United States, with a few communities in Hispanic America.[^cite_note-Ladino-286] Judaeo-Spanish lacks the [Native American vocabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amerindian_languages) which was acquired by standard Spanish during the [Spanish colonial period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Empire), and it retains many archaic features which have since been lost in standard Spanish. It contains, however, other vocabulary which is not found in standard Spanish, including vocabulary from [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language), French, Greek and [Turkish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_language), and other languages spoken where the Sephardim settled.

Judaeo-Spanish is in serious danger of extinction because many native speakers today are elderly as well as elderly *olim* (immigrants to [Israel](9282173.md)) who have not transmitted the language to their children or grandchildren. However, it is experiencing a minor revival among Sephardi communities, especially in music. In Hispanic American communities, the danger of extinction is also due to assimilation by modern Spanish.

A related dialect is [Haketia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haketia), the Judaeo-Spanish of northern [Morocco](19291.md). This too, tended to assimilate with modern Spanish, during the Spanish occupation of the region.

Spanish is written in the [Latin script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_script), with the addition of the character ⟨[ñ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%91)⟩ (*eñe*, representing the phoneme /[ɲ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palatal_nasal)/, a letter distinct from ⟨n⟩, although typographically composed of an ⟨n⟩ with a [tilde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tilde)). Formerly the [digraphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digraph_\(orthography\)) ⟨ch⟩ (*che*, representing the phoneme /t͡ʃ/) and ⟨ll⟩ (*elle*, representing the phoneme /[ʎ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palatal_lateral_approximant)/ or /ʝ/), were also considered single letters. However, the digraph ⟨rr⟩ (*erre fuerte*, 'strong r', *erre doble*, 'double r', or simply *erre*), which also represents a distinct phoneme /r/, was not similarly regarded as a single letter. Since 1994 ⟨ch⟩ and ⟨ll⟩ have been treated as letter pairs for [collation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collation) purposes, though they remained a part of the alphabet until 2010. Words with ⟨ch⟩ are now alphabetically sorted between those with ⟨cg⟩ and ⟨ci⟩, instead of following ⟨cz⟩ as they used to. The situation is similar for ⟨ll⟩.[^cite_note-287][^cite_note-288]

Thus, the Spanish alphabet has the following 27 letters:

*A, B, C, D, E, F, G, H, I, J, K, L, M, N, Ñ, O, P, Q, R, S, T, U, V, W, X, Y, Z.*

Since 2010, none of the digraphs (*ch, ll, rr, gu, qu*) are considered letters by the Royal Spanish Academy.[^cite_note-289]

The letters *k* and *w* are used only in words and names coming from foreign languages (*kilo, folklore, whisky, kiwi*, etc.).

With the exclusion of a very small number of regional terms such as *México* (see [Toponymy of Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toponymy_of_Mexico#Phonetic_evolution)), pronunciation can be entirely determined from spelling. Under the orthographic conventions, a typical Spanish word is stressed on the [syllable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syllable) before the last if it ends with a vowel (not including ⟨y⟩) or with a vowel followed by ⟨n⟩ or an ⟨s⟩; it is stressed on the last syllable otherwise. Exceptions to this rule are indicated by placing an [acute accent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_accent) on the [stressed vowel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_\(linguistics\)).

The acute accent is used, in addition, to distinguish between certain [homophones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homophone), especially when one of them is a stressed word and the other one is a [clitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clitic): compare *el* ('the', masculine singular definite article) with *él* ('he' or 'it'), or *te* ('you', object pronoun) with *té* ('tea'), *de* (preposition 'of') versus *dé* ('give' \[formal imperative/third-person present subjunctive]), and *se* (reflexive pronoun) versus *sé* ('I know' or imperative 'be').

The interrogative pronouns (*qué*, *cuál*, *dónde*, *quién*, etc.) also receive accents in direct or indirect questions, and some demonstratives (*ése*, *éste*, *aquél*, etc.) can be accented when used as pronouns. Accent marks used to be omitted on capital letters (a widespread practice in the days of [typewriters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typewriter) and the early days of computers when only lowercase vowels were available with accents), although the *Real Academia Española* advises against this and the orthographic conventions taught at schools enforce the use of the accent.

When *u* is written between *g* and a front vowel *e* or *i*, it indicates a "[hard g](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_and_soft_G)" pronunciation. A [diaeresis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diaeresis_\(diacritic\)) *ü* indicates that it is not silent as it normally would be (e.g., *cigüeña*, 'stork', is pronounced \[θiˈɣweɲa]; if it were written \**cigueña*, it would be pronounced \*\[θiˈɣeɲa]).

Interrogative and exclamatory clauses are introduced with [inverted question and exclamation marks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverted_question_and_exclamation_marks) (*¿* and *¡*, respectively) and closed by the usual question and exclamation marks.

## Organizations

### Royal Spanish Academy

Main article: [Royal Spanish Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Spanish_Academy)

The Royal Spanish Academy (*Real Academia Española*), founded in 1713,[^cite_note-290] together with the 21 other national ones (see [Association of Spanish Language Academies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_of_Spanish_Language_Academies)), exercises a standardizing influence through its publication of dictionaries and widely respected grammar and style guides.[^cite_note-291] Because of influence and for other sociohistorical reasons, a standardized form of the language ([Standard Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Spanish)) is widely acknowledged for use in literature, academic contexts and the media.

### Association of Spanish Language Academies

Main article: [Association of Spanish Language Academies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_of_Spanish_Language_Academies)



Member states of the ASALE

[^cite_note-292]

The Association of Spanish Language Academies (*Asociación de Academias de la Lengua Española*, or *ASALE*) is the entity which regulates the Spanish language. It was created in Mexico in 1951 and represents the union of all the separate academies in the Spanish-speaking world. It comprises the academies of 23 countries, ordered by date of academy foundation: [Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_Academia_Espa%C3%B1ola) (1713),[^cite_note-293] [Colombia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academia_Colombiana_de_la_Lengua) (1871),[^cite_note-294] [Ecuador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academia_Ecuatoriana_de_la_Lengua) (1874),[^cite_note-295] ### Cervantes Institute

Main article: [Instituto Cervantes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instituto_Cervantes)

The Instituto Cervantes ('Cervantes Institute') is a worldwide nonprofit organization created by the Spanish government in 1991. This organization has branches in 45 countries, with 88 centers devoted to the Spanish and Hispanic American cultures and Spanish language.[^cite_note-316] The goals of the Institute are to promote universally the education, the study, and the use of Spanish as a second language, to support methods and activities that help the process of Spanish-language education, and to contribute to the advancement of the Spanish and Hispanic American cultures in non-Spanish-speaking countries. The institute's 2015 report "El español, una lengua viva" (Spanish, a living language) estimated that there were 559 million Spanish speakers worldwide. Its latest annual report "El español en el mundo 2018" (Spanish in the world 2018) counts 577 million Spanish speakers worldwide. Among the sources cited in the report is the [U.S. Census Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Census_Bureau), which estimates that the U.S. will have 138 million Spanish speakers by 2050, making it the biggest Spanish-speaking nation on earth, with Spanish the mother tongue of almost a third of its citizens.[^cite_note-317]
