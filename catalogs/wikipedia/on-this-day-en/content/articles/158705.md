An **oil spill** is the release of a liquid [petroleum](23195.md) [hydrocarbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon) into the environment, especially the [marine ecosystem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_ecosystem), due to human activity, and is a form of [pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollution). The term usually refers to marine oil spills, where oil is released into the ocean or [coastal waters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coastal_waters), but spills may also occur on land. Oil spills can result from the release of [crude oil](23195.md) from [tankers](15923603.md), [offshore platforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_platform), [drilling rigs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drilling_rig), and [wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_well). They may also involve spills of [refined petroleum products](195137.md), such as [gasoline](23639.md) and [diesel fuel](81761.md), as well as their by-products. Additionally, heavier fuels used by large ships, such as [bunker fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunker_fuel), or spills of any oily refuse or [waste oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waste_oil), contribute to such incidents. These spills can have severe environmental and economic consequences.

Oil spills penetrate into the structure of the [plumage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plumage) of birds and the [fur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fur) of mammals, reducing its insulating ability, and making them more vulnerable to temperature fluctuations and much less [buoyant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buoyancy) in the water. Cleanup and recovery from an oil spill is difficult and depends upon many factors, including the type of oil spilled, the temperature of the water (affecting evaporation and biodegradation), and the types of shorelines and beaches involved.[^cite_note-common_dreams_1-1] Spills may take weeks, months or even years to clean up.[^cite_note-2]

Oil spills can have disastrous consequences for society; economically, environmentally, and socially. As a result, oil spill accidents have initiated intense media attention and political uproar, bringing many together in a political struggle concerning government response to oil spills and what actions can best prevent them from happening.[^cite_note-autogenerated2-3]

## Human impacts

Oil spills increase fire hazards and can cause some health problems. The fire resulting from the [Lac-Mégantic derailment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lac-M%C3%A9gantic_derailment) killed 47 and destroyed half of the town's centre.[^cite_note-4]

Spilled oil can also contaminate drinking water supplies. For example, in 2013 two oil spills contaminated water supplies for 300,000 in [Miri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miri,_Malaysia), [Malaysia](3607937.md);[^cite_note-5] 80,000 people in [Coca, Ecuador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coca,_Ecuador).[^cite_note-6] In 2000, springs were contaminated by an oil spill in [Clark County, Kentucky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clark_County,_Kentucky).[^cite_note-7]

### Animals

The threat posed to birds, fish, shellfish and crustaceans from spilled oil was known in England in the 1920s, largely through observations made in [Yorkshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yorkshire).[^cite_note-8] The subject was also explored in a scientific paper produced by the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences) in the US in 1974 which considered impacts to fish, crustaceans and molluscs. The paper was limited to 100 copies and was described as a draft document, not to be cited.[^cite_note-9]

In general, spilled oil can affect animals and plants in two ways: direct from the oil and from the response or cleanup process.[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-Sarbatly_R._2016_8–16-11][^cite_note-12][^cite_note-13] Oil penetrates into the structure of the plumage of birds and the fur of mammals, reducing their insulating ability, and making them more vulnerable to temperature fluctuations and much less buoyant in the water.

Animals who rely on scent to find their babies or mothers cannot do so due to the strong scent of the oil. This causes a baby to be rejected and abandoned, leaving the babies to starve and eventually die. Oil can impair a bird's ability to fly, preventing it from foraging or escaping from predators. As they [preen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_grooming#In_animals), birds may ingest the oil coating their feathers, irritating the [digestive tract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digestive_tract), altering [liver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liver) function, and causing [kidney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kidney) damage. Together with their diminished foraging capacity, this can rapidly result in [dehydration](155056.md) and [metabolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolism) imbalance. Some birds exposed to petroleum also experience changes in their hormonal balance, including changes in their [luteinizing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luteinizing) protein.[^cite_note-14] The majority of birds affected by oil spills die from complications without human intervention.[^cite_note-15][^cite_note-16] Some studies have suggested that less than one percent of oil-soaked birds survive, even after cleaning,[^cite_note-17] although the survival rate can also exceed ninety percent, as in the case of the [MV *Treasure* oil spill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MV_Treasure_oil_spill).[^cite_note-18] Oil spills and oil dumping events have been impacting sea birds since at least the 1920s[^cite_note-19][^cite_note-20][^cite_note-21] and was understood to be a global problem in the 1930s.[^cite_note-22]

Heavily furred [marine mammals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_mammal) exposed to oil spills are affected in similar ways. Oil coats the fur of [sea otters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_otters) and [seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinniped), reducing its insulating effect, leading to fluctuations in [body temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_temperature) and [hypothermia](146879.md). Oil can also blind an animal, leaving it defenseless. The ingestion of oil causes dehydration and impairs the digestive process. Animals can be poisoned, and may die from oil entering the lungs or liver.

### Air

In addition, oil spills can also harm air quality.[^cite_note-23] The chemicals in crude oil are mostly hydrocarbons that contains toxic chemicals such as [benzenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzene), [toluene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toluene), [poly-aromatic hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyaromatic_hydrocarbon) and oxygenated [polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polycyclic_aromatic_hydrocarbon). These chemicals can introduce adverse health effects when being inhaled into the human body. In addition, these chemicals can be oxidized by oxidants in the atmosphere to form fine particulate matter after they evaporate into the atmosphere.[^cite_note-24] These particulates can penetrate lungs and carry toxic chemicals into the human body. Burning surface oil can also be a source for pollution such as soot particles. During the cleanup and recovery process, it will also generate air pollutants such as nitric oxides and ozone from ships. Lastly, bubble bursting can also be a generation pathway for particulate matter during an oil spill.[^cite_note-25] During the [Deepwater Horizon oil spill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deepwater_Horizon_oil_spill), significant air quality issues were found on the Gulf Coast, which is downwind of the DWH oil spill. Air quality monitoring data showed that criteria pollutants had exceeded the health-based standard in the coastal regions.[^cite_note-26]

## Sources and rate of occurrence

Oil spills can be caused by human error, natural disasters, technical failures or deliberate releases.[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28] It is estimated that 30–50% of all oil spills are directly or indirectly caused by human error, with approximately 20–40% of oil spills being attributed to equipment failure or malfunction.[^cite_note-\:0-29] Causes of oil spills are further distinguished between deliberate releases, such as operational discharges or acts of war and accidental releases. Accidental oil spills are in the focus of the literature, although some of the largest oil spills ever recorded, the [Gulf War Oil Spill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_War_oil_spill) (sea based) and [Kuwaiti Oil Fires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuwaiti_Oil_Fires) (land based) were deliberate acts of war.[^cite_note-\:1-30] The academic study of sources and causes of oil spills identifies vulnerable points in oil transportation infrastructure and calculates the likelihood of oil spills happening. This can then guide prevention efforts and regulation policies[^cite_note-31]

### Natural seeps

Around 40–50% of all oil released into the oceans stems from natural seeps from seafloor rocks. This corresponds to approximately 600,000 tons annually on a global level. While natural seeps are the single largest source of oil spills, they are considered less problematic because ecosystems have adapted to such regular releases. For instance, on sites of natural oil seeps, ocean bacteria have evolved to digest oil molecules.[^cite_note-\:2-32][^cite_note-\:3-33][^cite_note-\:1-30]

### Oil tankers and vessels

Vessels can be the source of oil spills either through operational releases of oil or in the case of [oil tanker](15923603.md) accidents. Operational discharges from vessels were estimated to account for 21% of oil releases from vessels between 1990 and 1999, and tanker spills about 15% of total annual oil entering the sea 1970-2004 (excluding natural seeps).[^cite_note-\:3-33] They occur as a consequence of failure to comply with regulations or arbitrary discharges of waste oil and water containing such oil residues.[^cite_note-\:4-34] Such operational discharges are regulated through the [MARPOL convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MARPOL_73/78).[^cite_note-35] Operational releases are frequent, but small in the amount of oil spilled per release, and are often not in the focus of attention regarding oil spills.[^cite_note-\:3-33] There has been a steady decrease of operational discharges of oil, with an additional decrease of around 50% since the 1990s.[^cite_note-\:1-30]

As of 2007, accidental oil tank vessel spills accounted for approximately 8–13% of all oil spilled into the oceans.[^cite_note-\:3-33][^cite_note-\:5-36] The main causes of oil tank vessel spills were collision (29%), grounding (22%), mishandling (14%) and sinking (12%), among others.[^cite_note-\:3-33][^cite_note-37] Oil tanker spills are considered a major ecological threat due to the large amount of oil spilled per accident and the fact that major sea traffic routes are close to [Large Marine Ecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_marine_ecosystem).[^cite_note-\:3-33] Around 90% of the world's oil transportation is through oil tankers, and the absolute amount of seaborne oil trade is steadily increasing.[^cite_note-\:5-36] However, there has been a reduction of the number of spills from oil tankers and of the amount of oil released per oil tanker spill.[^cite_note-\:5-36][^cite_note-\:1-30] In 1992, [MARPOL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MARPOL_73/78) was amended and made it mandatory for large tankers (5,000 dwt and more) to be fitted with [double hulls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_hull).[^cite_note-38] This is considered to be a major reason for the reduction of oil tanker spills, alongside other innovations such as [GPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Positioning_System), sectioning of vessels and [sea lanes](2018420.md) in narrow straits.[^cite_note-\:1-30][^cite_note-\:3-33]

In 2023, the International Tanker Owners Pollution Federation (ITOPF) documented a significant oil spill incident of over 700 tonnes and nine medium spills ranging between 7 and 700 tonnes. The major spill occurred in Asia involving heavy fuel oil, and the medium spills were scattered across Asia, Africa, Europe, and America, involving various oil types.[^cite_note-\:10-39]

The total volume of oil released from these spills in 2023 was approximately 2,000 tonnes. This contributes to a trend of decreased oil spill volumes and frequencies over the decades. Comparatively, the 1970s averaged 79 significant spills per year, which drastically reduced to an average of about 6.3 per year in the 2010s, and has maintained a similar level in the current decade.[^cite_note-\:10-39]

The reduction in oil spill volume has also been substantial over the years. For instance, the 1990s recorded 1,134,000 tonnes lost, mainly from 10 major spills. This figure decreased to 196,000 tonnes in the 2000s and 164,000 tonnes in the 2010s. In the early 2020s, approximately 28,000 tonnes have been lost, predominantly from major incidents.[^cite_note-\:10-39]

### Offshore oil platforms



Chemical dispersants may be deployed from boats, planes, and underwater vehicles in response to an offshore oil spill

Accidental spills from [oil platforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_platform) nowadays account for approximately 3% of oil spills in the oceans.[^cite_note-\:3-33] Prominent offshore oil platform spills typically occurred as a result of a [blowout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blowout_\(well_drilling\)). They can go on for months until relief wells have been drilled, resulting in enormous amounts of oil leaked.[^cite_note-\:1-30] Notable examples of such oil spills are [Deepwater Horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deepwater_Horizon_oil_spill) and [Ixtoc I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ixtoc_I_oil_spill). While technologies for drilling in deep water have significantly improved in the past 30–40 years, oil companies move to drilling sites in more and more difficult places. This ambiguous development results in no clear trend regarding the frequency of offshore oil platform spills.[^cite_note-\:1-30]

### Pipelines

As of 2010, overall, there has been a substantial increase of pipeline oil spills in the past four decades.[^cite_note-\:1-30] Prominent examples include [oil spills of pipelines in the Niger Delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_issues_in_the_Niger_Delta). Pipeline oil spills can be caused by trawling of fishing boats, natural disasters, pipe corrosion, construction defects, sabotage, or an attack,[^cite_note-\:4-34] as with the [Caño Limón-Coveñas pipeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ca%C3%B1o_Lim%C3%B3n%E2%80%93Cove%C3%B1as_pipeline) in Colombia.

[Pipelines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipeline_transport) as sources of oil spills are estimated to contribute 1% of oil pollution to the oceans.[^cite_note-\:3-33] Reasons for this are underreporting, and many oil pipeline leaks occur on land with only fractions of that oil reaching the oceans.

### Other sources

[Recreational boats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recreational_boat) can spill oil into the ocean because of operational or human error and unpreparedness. The amounts are however small, and such oil spills are hard to track due to underreporting.[^cite_note-\:2-32]

Oil can reach the oceans as oil and fuel from land-based sources.[^cite_note-\:0-29] It is estimated that runoff oil and oil from rivers are responsible for 11% of oil pollution to the oceans.[^cite_note-\:3-33] Such pollution can also be oil on roads from land vehicles, which is then flushed into the oceans during rainstorms.[^cite_note-\:2-32] Purely land-based oil spills are different from maritime oil spills in that oil on land does not spread as quickly as in water, and effects thus remain local.[^cite_note-\:0-29]

## Cleanup and recovery

A US Navy oil spill response team drills with a "Harbour Buster high-speed oil containment system".

Cleanup and recovery from an oil spill is difficult and contingent on many factors, including the type of oil spilled, the temperature of the water (affecting evaporation and biodegradation), and the types of shorelines and beaches involved.[^cite_note-common_dreams_1-1] The accurate determination and prediction of the severity and movement of the spill is critical in determining effective response methods.[^cite_note-x430-40] Physical cleanups of oil spills are also very expensive. Until the 1960s, the best method for remediation consisted of putting [straw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straw) on the spill and retrieving the oil-soaked straw manually.[^cite_note-\:9-41] Chemical remediation is the norm as of the early 21st century, using compounds that can herd and thicken oil for physical recovery, disperse oil in the water, or facilitate burning the oil off.[^cite_note-\:9-41] The future of oil cleanup technology is likely the use of microorganisms such as [Fusobacteriota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fusobacteriota) (formerly Fusobacteria), species demonstrate potential for future oil spill cleanup because of their ability to colonize and degrade oil slicks on the sea surface.[^cite_note-\:9-41][^cite_note-pmid27681918-42]

There are three kinds of oil-consuming bacteria. [Sulfate-reducing bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfate-reducing_bacteria) (SRB) and acid-producing bacteria are [anaerobic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaerobic_organism), while general aerobic bacteria (GAB) are [aerobic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerobic_organism). These bacteria occur naturally and will act to remove oil from an ecosystem, and their biomass will tend to replace other populations in the food chain. The chemicals from the oil which dissolve in water, and hence are available to bacteria, are those in the [water associated fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_associated_fraction) of the oil.

## Environmental Sensitivity Index (ESI) mapping

Environmental Sensitivity Indexes (ESI) are tools used to create Environmental Sensitivity Maps (ESM). ESM's are pre-planning tools used to identify sensitive areas and resources prior to an oil spill event in order to set priorities for protection and plan clean-up strategies.[^cite_note-68][^cite_note-69] It is to date the most commonly used mapping tool for sensitive area plotting.[^cite_note-\:6-70] The ESI has three components: A shoreline type ranking system, a biological resources section, and a human-use resource category.[^cite_note-NOAA_2002-71]

### History and development

ESI is the most frequently used sensitivity mapping tool yet. It was first applied in 1979 in response to an oil-spill near Texas in the Gulf of Mexico.[^cite_note-\:6-70] To this time, ESI maps were prepared merely days in advance of one's arrival to an oil spill location. ESMs used to be atlases, maps consisting of thousands of pages that could solely work with spills in the oceans. In the past 3 decades, this product has been transformed into a versatile online tool. This conversion allows sensitivity indexing to become more adaptable and in 1995 by the [US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA)](37876.md) worked on the tool allowing ESI to extended maps to lakes, rivers, and estuary shoreline types.[^cite_note-NOAA_2002-71] ESI maps have since become integral to collecting, synthesizing, and producing data which have previously never been accessible in digital formats. Especially in the United States, the tool has made impressive advancements in developing tidal bay protection strategies, collecting seasonal information and generally in the modelling of sensitive areas.[^cite_note-\:6-70] Together with [Geographic Information System Mapping (GIS)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographic_information_system), ESI integrates their techniques to successfully geographically reference the three different types of resources.[^cite_note-72]

### Usage and application

The ESI depicts environmental stability, coastal resilience to maritime related catastrophes, and the configurations of a stress-response relationship between all things maritime.[^cite_note-73] Created for ecological-related decision making, ESMs can accurately identify sensitive areas and habitats, clean-up responses, response measures and monitoring strategies for oil-spills.[^cite_note-74] The maps allow experts from varying fields to come together and work efficiently during fast-paced response operations. The process of making an ESI atlas involves GIS technology. The steps involve, first zoning the area that is to be mapped, and secondly, a meeting with local and regional experts on the area and its resources.[^cite_note-\:7-75] Following, all the shoreline types, biological, and human use resources need to be identified and their locations pinpointed. Once all this information is gathered, it then becomes digitized. In its digital format, classifications are set in place, tables are produced and local experts refine the product before it gets released.

ESI's current most common use is within contingency planning. After the maps are calculated and produced, the most sensitive areas get picked out and authenticated. These areas then go through a scrutinization process throughout which methods of protection and resource assessments are obtained.[^cite_note-\:7-75] This in-depth research is then put back into the ESMs to develop their accuracy and allowing for tactical information to be stored in them as well. The finished maps are then used for drills and trainings for clean-up efficiency.[^cite_note-\:7-75] Trainings also often help to update the maps and tweak certain flaws that might have occurred in the previous steps.

### ESI Categories

#### Shoreline type

[Shoreline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoreline) type is classified by rank depending on how easy the target site would be to clean up, how long the oil would persist, and how sensitive the shoreline is.[^cite_note-76] The ranking system works on a 10-point scale where the higher the rank, the more sensitive a habitat or shore is. The coding system usually works in colour, where warm colours are used for the increasingly sensitive types and cooler colours are used for robust shores.[^cite_note-\:7-75] For each navigable body of water, there is a feature classifying its sensitivity to oil. Shoreline type mapping codes a large range of ecological settings including [estuarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estuary), [lacustrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lacustrine), and [riverine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riverine) environments.[^cite_note-\:6-70] Floating oil slicks put the shoreline at particular risk when they eventually come ashore, covering the [substrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substrate_\(marine_biology\)) with oil. The differing substrates between shoreline types vary in their response to oiling, and influence the type of cleanup that will be required to effectively decontaminate the shoreline. Hence ESI shoreline ranking helps committees identify which clean-up techniques are approved or detrimental the natural environment. The exposure the shoreline has to wave energy and tides, substrate type, and slope of the shoreline are also taken into account—in addition to biological productivity and sensitivity.[^cite_note-NOAA_2008-77] [Mangroves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mangroves) and marshes tend to have higher ESI rankings due to the potentially long-lasting and damaging effects of both oil contamination and cleanup actions. Impermeable and exposed surfaces with high wave action are ranked lower due to the reflecting waves keeping oil from coming onshore, and the speed at which natural processes will remove the oil.

#### Biological resources

Within the biological resources, the ESI maps protected areas as well as those with bio-diverse importance. These are usually identified through the [UNEP-WCMC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNEP-WCMC) Integrated Biodiversity Assessment Tool. There are varying types of coastal habitats and ecosystems and thus also many endangered species that need to be considered when looking at affected areas post oil spills. The habitats of plants and animals that may be at risk from oil spills are referred to as "elements" and are divided by functional group. Further classification divides each element into species groups with similar life histories and behaviors relative to their vulnerability to oil spills. There are eight element groups: birds, reptiles, amphibians, fish, invertebrates, habitats and plants, wetlands, and marine mammals and terrestrial mammals. Element groups are further divided into sub-groups, for example, the 'marine mammals' element group is divided into [dolphins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolphin), manatees, [pinnipeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinnipeds) (seals, sea lions & walruses), [polar bears](24408.md), [sea otters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_otter) and [whales](33777.md).[^cite_note-NOAA_2002-71][^cite_note-NOAA_2008-77] Necessary when ranking and selecting species is their vulnerability to the oil spills themselves. This not only includes their reactions to such events but also their fragility, the scale of large clusters of animals, whether special life stages occur ashore, and whether any present species is threatened, endangered or rare.[^cite_note-IPIECA_1994-78] The way in which the biological resources are mapped is through symbols representing the species, and polygons and lines to map out the special extent of the species.[^cite_note-\:8-79] The symbols also have the ability to identify the most vulnerable of a species life stages, such as the [molting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moulting), nesting, hatching or migration patterns. This allows for more accurate response plans during those given periods. There is also a division for sub-tidal habitats which are equally important to coastal biodiversity including kelp, coral reefs and sea beds which are not commonly mapped within the shoreline ESI type.[^cite_note-\:8-79]

#### Human-use resources

Human-use resources are also often referred to as socio-economic features, which map inanimate resources that have the potential to be directly impacted by oil pollution. Human-use resources that are mapped within the ESI will have socio-economic repercussions to an oil spill. These resources are divided into four major classifications: [archaeological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeological) importance or cultural resource site, high-use recreational areas or shoreline access points, important protected management areas, and resource origins.[^cite_note-NOAA_2002-71][^cite_note-IPIECA_1994-78] Some examples include airports, diving sites, popular beach sites, marinas, hotels, factories, natural reserves or marine sanctuaries. When mapped, the human-use resources the need protecting must be certified by a local or regional policy maker.[^cite_note-\:7-75] These resources are often extremely vulnerable to seasonal changes due to ex. fishing and tourism. For this category there are also a set of symbols available to demonstrate their importance on ESMs.

Oil spill model systems are used by industry and government to assist in planning and emergency decision making. Of critical importance for the skill of the oil spill model prediction is the adequate description of the wind and current fields. There is a worldwide oil spill modelling (WOSM) program.[^cite_note-81] Tracking the scope of an oil spill may also involve verifying that hydrocarbons collected during an ongoing spill are derived from the active spill or some other source. This can involve sophisticated analytical chemistry focused on finger printing an oil source based on the complex mixture of substances present. Largely, these will be various hydrocarbons, among the most useful being [polyaromatic hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyaromatic_hydrocarbons). In addition, both oxygen and nitrogen heterocyclic hydrocarbons, such as parent and alkyl homologues of [carbazole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbazole), [quinoline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinoline), and [pyridine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyridine), are present in many crude oils. As a result, these compounds have great potential to supplement the existing suite of hydrocarbons targets to fine-tune source tracking of petroleum spills. Such analysis can also be used to follow weathering and degradation of crude spills.[^cite_note-82]

## Largest oil spills

Oil spills at sea are generally much more damaging than those on land, since they can spread for hundreds of nautical miles in a thin [oil slick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_slick) which can cover beaches with a thin coating of oil. ## The economic impact of oil spills

Oil spills can have devastating environmental impacts; however, they often have equally detrimental economic consequences.[^cite_note-AlverniaETAL-120] These disasters do not only pose immediate threats to marine ecosystems, but also leave lasting impacts on local and regional economies. This section will explore the multifaceted economic repercussions of oil spills, specifically considering: the decline in tourism, the reduction in fishing, and the impact on port activity.

### Decline in tourism

In the short term, an oil spill will prevent tourists from partaking in usual recreational activities such as swimming, boating, diving, and angling.[^cite_note-ITOPF-121] As such, the area will witness a decline in tourism. This will negatively impact several industries. Firstly, the hotels, restaurants, and bars in the immediate vicinity will have significantly fewer customers. Local car park owners and shopkeepers will be affected too. Then, this decline in tourists will cause further damage to travel agencies, tour guides, and transport companies.[^cite_note-Economic_Effects-122] The beaches will likely stay shut for several days whilst clean-up operations take place, and there may be disruption caused by an increase in clean-up vehicles.[^cite_note-ITOPF-121] Overall, several businesses will be negatively impacted by the spill in the short term, which can lead to further long-term damage should companies be forced to reduce staff or shut down entirely.

Similarly, tourism in Ibiza was severely impacted in 2007. Just 20 tonnes of oil were spilled from the Don Pedro in July 2007, a relatively limited volume compared with other spills. Whilst this caused just a small amount of environmental damage, the economic damage was disproportionately large. Most beaches were reopened within a week, just a dozen seabirds were affected, and there were no reports of injured sea mammals. Nonetheless, 27 percent of hotels in Ibiza were negatively affected, with two thirds of these being seafront hotels. Thus, 32 claims were made by tourist firms, equating to approximately 1.5 million euros of compensation.[^cite_note-123] This provides a clear example of an oil spill resulting in massive economic disaster. Furthermore, following the world's largest oil spill, the Deepwater Horizon Oil Spill in 2010,[^cite_note-Encyclopedia_Brittania-124] the U.S. Travel Association estimated 23 billion dollars' worth of associated costs for affected tourist infrastructure.[^cite_note-125]

### Reduction in fishing

After the Deepwater Horizon crisis,[^cite_note-Encyclopedia_Brittania-124] the Gulf of Mexico suffered an estimated 1.9-billion-dollar loss in revenue from fishing. This is because fishing closures were imposed due to fears of the safety of seafood,[^cite_note-GraciaETAL-126] there was also a decline in demand, as seafood restaurants and markets suffered such severe losses that many were forced to shut.[^cite_note-ITOPF-121] Usually, the Gulf sees an average of 106,703 fishing trips per day,[^cite_note-127] equating to 1 million metric tonnes of annual fishery landings.[^cite_note-GraciaETAL-126] Therefore, the necessary fishing ban following the disaster was highly damaging. Similarly, following the sinking of the Prestige oil tanker near Galicia, Spain, in November 2002, 77,000 tonnes of crude oil were spilled into the ocean. This disaster has had severe economic consequences, alongside the environmental damage. Large zones were cordoned in which fishing was banned, with these bans lasting for more than eight months. This affected several groups, including fishermen, ship owners, and the companies who bought and sold the fish. Several compensatory actions were introduced, including tax benefits and aid. This resulted in expenses of approximately 113 million euros in an attempt to compensate for the halt in fishing activity.[^cite_note-128] The examples of the Deepwater Horizon and the Prestige clearly illustrate the severe economic consequences when oil spills prevent commercial fishing.

Water pollution due to oil spills can be severe, often resulting in the death or injury of many sea creatures, including birds, sea mammals, fish, algae, and coral.[^cite_note-AlverniaETAL-120] The impact on fish caught in the spill has both immediate and longer-term impacts. Immediately, the fish are tainted with oil, and they cannot be used commercially due to safety reasons. Then, the oil can spread and sink below the water's surface. If fish swallow the oil, they are also inconsumable due to the health risk posed to humans.[^cite_note-AlverniaETAL-120] Therefore, massive economic damage is caused to the fishing industry following an oil spill, as the stock is vastly reduced. Furthermore, the oil can cause damage to the equipment and boats of fishermen. Clean-up operations can also interrupt usual fishing routes, and sometimes fishing bans are imposed.[^cite_note-Economic_Effects-122] This further illustrates the damaging economic effects of oil spills on commercial fishing, which is particularly detrimental for regions whose economy relies heavily on fishing.

### The impact on port activity

Ports are major hubs for economic activity; thus, an oil spill in or near a port can have significant consequences.[^cite_note-OBrien2006-129] During and following a spill, all boats entering or leaving the port must be closely managed in order to prevent further spread. Furthermore, specialist cleaning contractors must be hired to effectively clean the various port structures.[^cite_note-ITOPF-121] Oil spills are relatively regular occurrences in ports, as small spills often happen due to the large volume of boats, and these are not as well documented in the media as larger events are.[^cite_note-OBrien2006-129] However, these spills must still be dealt with, and they can still have damaging economic repercussions.[^cite_note-Modeling_Logistical_&_Economical_Im-130] Both the incident and the response require expensive and time-consuming management which is disruptive to port activity.[^cite_note-Modeling_Logistical_&_Economical_Im-130] Furthermore, special care must be taken during clean-up operations to ensure that the oil does not get stuck under the quayside, as this could act as a continual source of oil contamination.[^cite_note-Modeling_Logistical_&_Economical_Im-130] This can also be seen with sea defenses; should the oil penetrate deep into the structures, they may become a source of secondary pollution.[^cite_note-ITOPF-121] Therefore, it is crucial for ports to manage and mitigate any oil spills, in order to limit the damage to ships and shipping operations. Otherwise, should large disruption occur, the economic damage can be extensive due to costly clean-up processes and delayed shipments.
