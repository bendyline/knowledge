A [GEM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphite-Epoxy_Motor)-40 [strap-on](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_rocket) booster for a [Delta II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_II) launch vehicle.

A **booster** is a [rocket](26301.md) (or [rocket engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_engine)) used either in the first stage of a [multistage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multistage_rocket) [launch vehicle](1306157.md) or in parallel with longer-burning [sustainer rockets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainer_engine) to augment the [space vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_vehicle)'s takeoff thrust and payload capability.[^cite_note-nasa-staging-1][^cite_note-2] Boosters are traditionally necessary to launch [spacecraft](37910.md) into [low Earth orbit](47568.md) (absent a [single-stage-to-orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-stage-to-orbit) design), and are especially important for a space vehicle to go beyond Earth orbit. The booster is dropped to fall back to Earth once its fuel is expended, a point known as *booster engine cut-off* (BECO).[^cite_note-3]

Following [booster separation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separation_event), the rest of the launch vehicle continues flight with its core or upper-stage engines. The booster may be recovered, refurbished and reused, as was the case of the steel casings used for the [Space Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle) [Solid Rocket Boosters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_Solid_Rocket_Booster).[^cite_note-nasa-staging-1]

## Drop-away engines

The [SM-65 Atlas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SM-65_Atlas) rocket used three engines, one of which was fixed to the fuel tank, and two of which were mounted on a skirt which dropped away at BECO. This was used as an [Intercontinental ballistic missile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intercontinental_ballistic_missile) (ICBM); to launch the crewed [Project Mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Mercury) capsule into orbit; and as the first stage of the [Atlas-Agena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas-Agena) and [Atlas-Centaur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas-Centaur) launch vehicles.

## Strap-on

See also: [Modular rocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_rocket)

Several launch vehicles, including the Russian [Soyuz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soyuz_\(rocket_family\)), Indian [LVM3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LVM3) and US [Titan IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titan_IV), employ strap-on boosters. The US [Space Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle) was the first crewed vehicle to use strap-on boosters. Launch vehicles like the US [Delta IV Heavy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_IV_Heavy) and US [Falcon Heavy](31406060.md) employ strap-on [liquid rocket boosters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_rocket_booster).

## Recoverable

The [booster casings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_Solid_Rocket_Booster#Descent_and_recovery) for the [Space Shuttle Solid Rocket Boosters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_Solid_Rocket_Booster) were recovered and refurbished for reuse from 1981 to 2011 as part of the [Space Shuttle program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_program).

In a new development program initiated in 2011, [SpaceX](832774.md) developed [reusable first stages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_reusable_launch_system_development_program) of their [Falcon 9 rocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_rocket). After launching the second stage and the payload, the booster [returns to launch site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Return_to_launch_site) or flies to a [drone ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_spaceport_drone_ship) and [lands vertically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VTVL). After landing multiple boosters both on land and on drone ships in 2015–2016, a landed stage was first reflown in March 2017: [Rocket core B1021](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_booster_B1021) that had been used to launch a re-supply mission to the [ISS](15043.md) when new in April 2016 was subsequently used to launch the satellite [SES-10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SES-10) in March 2017.[^cite_note-4] The program was intended to reduce launch prices significantly, and by 2018, SpaceX had reduced launch prices on a flight-proven boosters to US\$50 million, the [lowest price in the industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_launch_market_competition) for [medium-lift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medium-lift_launch_vehicle) launch services.[^cite_note-nsf20180517-5]

By August 2019, the [recovery and reuse of Falcon 9 boosters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Falcon_9_first-stage_boosters) had become routine, with booster landings/recovery being attempted on more than 90 percent of all SpaceX flights, and successful landings and recoveries occurring 65 times out of 75 attempts. In total 25 recovered boosters have been refurbished and subsequently flown a second time by late 2020, with several having been flown a third time as well.

In late 2020, [Rocket Lab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_Lab) guided the booster of their [Electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_Lab_Electron) rocket for a splashdown in the [Pacific Ocean](23070.md) with a [parafoil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parafoil) after launching the [Return to Sender mission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_Lab), as part of a program to catch the booster with a helicopter and reuse it on later missions.[^cite_note-6]

## Use in aviation

Rocket boosters used on aircraft are known as [jet-assisted take-off (JATO)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JATO) rockets.

Various [missiles](19594.md) also use solid rocket boosters. Examples are:

- [2K11 (SA-4)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2K11_Krug) which uses SRBs as a first stage, and then a [ramjet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramjet).

- [S-200 (SA-5)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-200_Angara/Vega/Dubna) which uses SRBs as the first stage, followed by a [liquid fuel rocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid-propellant_rocket).

- Surface and submarine launched versions of the [turbojet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbojet)-powered [Boeing Harpoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_Harpoon) and many other [cruise missiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cruise_missiles) use an SRB.
