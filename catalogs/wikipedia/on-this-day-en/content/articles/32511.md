**Vietnamese** (*tiếng Việt*) is an [Austroasiatic language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austroasiatic_languages) primarily spoken throughout [Vietnam](202354.md) where it is the [official language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Official_language). It belongs to the [Vietic subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietic_languages) of the Austroasiatic language family.[^cite_note-3] Vietnamese is spoken natively by around 86 million people,[^cite_note-e28|vie|Vietnamese-1] and as a second language by 11 million people,[^cite_note-e28|vie|Vietnamese-1] several times as many as the rest of the Austroasiatic family combined.[^cite_note-4] It is the [native language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_language) of the [Viet people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viet_people) and functions as the [second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_language) or first language for [other ethnicities in Vietnam](153139.md); it is also used by the [Vietnamese diaspora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_diaspora) worldwide.

Like many languages in Southeast Asia and East Asia, Vietnamese is an [isolating language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isolating_language) (highly [analytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_language)) and is [tonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tone_\(linguistics\)). Structurally, Vietnamese is mixed between [head-initial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head-initial) and [head-final](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head-final) directionalities: head-initial is more prominent in clausal structures while head-final may appear more in compounds, and modifiers generally follow the words they modify but sometimes precede them as well. Syntactically, Vietnamese is [topic-prominent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topic-prominent_language) and strictly [subject–verb–object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subject%E2%80%93verb%E2%80%93object). It also uses noun [classifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classifier_\(linguistics\)).&#x20;

Vietnamese morphemes and phonological words are predominantly monosyllabic; however, many multisyllabic words do occur, usually as a result of compounding and reduplication. This aspect of Vietnamese allows for great efficiency in day-to-day writing, communication, and much more.[^cite_note-5] Borrowings from [Middle Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Chinese), known as [Sino-Vietnamese vocabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Vietnamese_vocabulary), make up a significant and important part of the lexicon. The vocabulary has also had influence from French.[^cite_note-6]

Vietnamese currently is written using a [Latin-based](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_script) [alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_alphabet) known as [chữ Quốc ngữ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_alphabet). The [alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_alphabet) largely relies on 17th-century [Portuguese orthography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_orthography), and was officially adopted in the early 20th century during [French rule of Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Indochina). It uses [digraphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digraph_\(orthography\)) and [diacritics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diacritic) to mark [tones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tone_\(linguistics\)) and some [phonemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoneme). Vietnamese was historically written using *[chữ Nôm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%E1%BB%AF_N%C3%B4m)*, a [logographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logographic) script composed of [Chinese characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_characters) (called *[chữ Hán](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%E1%BB%AF_H%C3%A1n)*) and locally created characters to represent native Vietnamese words and sounds.[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8]

## Classification



A 1906 analysis map of [Austroasiatic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austroasiatic_languages) (previously known as Mon-Annam languages) by British linguists [Walter William Skeat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_William_Skeat) and [Charles Otto Blagden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Otto_Blagden). Vietnamese is shown as Annamese.

Early [linguistic work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_method) in the late 19th and early 20th centuries ([Logan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Richardson_Logan) 1852, [Forbes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_James_Forbes_Smith-Forbes?action=edit\&redlink=1) 1881, [Müller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_M%C3%BCller_\(linguist\)) 1888, [Kuhn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Kuhn) 1889, [Schmidt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Schmidt_\(linguist\)) 1905, [Przyluski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Przyluski) 1924, and [Benedict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_K._Benedict) 1942)[^cite_note-9] classified Vietnamese as belonging to the [Mon–Khmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mon%E2%80%93Khmer_languages) branch of the [Austroasiatic language family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austroasiatic_languages) (which also includes the [Khmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khmer_language) language spoken in [Cambodia](334751.md), and various smaller or [regional languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_language), such as the [Munda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munda_languages) and [Khasi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khasi_language) languages spoken in eastern India, and others in [Laos](17752.md), southern China, and parts of Thailand). In 1850, British lawyer [James Richardson Logan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Richardson_Logan) detected striking similarities between the [Korku language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korku_language) in [Central India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_India) and Vietnamese. He suggested that [Korku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korku_language), [Mon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mon_language), and Vietnamese were part of what he termed "Mon–Annam languages" in a paper published in 1856. Later, in 1920, French-Polish linguist [Jean Przyluski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Przyluski) found that [Mường](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muong_language) is more closely related to Vietnamese than other Mon–Khmer languages, and a Viet– Mường subgrouping was established, also including [Thavung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thavung_language), [Chut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chut_language), [Cuoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuoi_language), and others.[^cite_note-10] The term "Vietic" was proposed by Hayes (1992),[^cite_note-Hayes-11] who proposed to redefine Viet–Mường as referring to a subbranch of Vietic containing only Vietnamese and Mường. The term "[Vietic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietic_languages)" is used by [Gérard Diffloth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%A9rard_Diffloth), among others, with a slightly different proposal on subclassification, within which the term "Viet–Mường" refers to a lower subgrouping (within an eastern Vietic branch) consisting of Vietnamese dialects, Mường dialects, and [Nguồn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ngu%E1%BB%93n_language) (of [Quảng Bình Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qu%E1%BA%A3ng_B%C3%ACnh_Province)).[^cite_note-12]

## History

Austroasiatic is believed to have dispersed around 2000 BC.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAlves2020xviii-13] The arrival of the agricultural [Phùng Nguyên culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ph%C3%B9ng_Nguy%C3%AAn_culture) in the [Red River Delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_River_Delta) at that time may correspond to the Vietic branch.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESidwellAlves2021189-14]

This ancestral Vietic was typologically very different from later Vietnamese. In addition to monosyllabic roots, it had [sesquisyllabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sesquisyllabic) roots consisting of a reduced syllable followed by a full syllable, and featured many consonant clusters. Both of these features are found elsewhere in Austroasiatic and in modern conservative Vietic languages south of the Red River area.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAlves2021661-15] The language was non-tonal, but featured glottal stop and voiceless fricative codas.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAlves2021662-16]

Borrowed vocabulary indicates early contact with speakers of [Tai languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tai_languages) in the last millennium BCE, which is consistent with genetic evidence from [Dong Son culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dong_Son_culture) sites.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESidwellAlves2021189-14] Extensive contact with Chinese began during the [Han dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_dynasty) (2nd century BCE).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAlves2020xix-17] At this time, Vietic groups began to expand south from the Red River Delta and into the adjacent uplands, possibly to escape Chinese encroachment.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESidwellAlves2021189-14] The oldest layer of loans from Chinese into northern Vietic (which would become the Viet–Mường subbranch) date from this period.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAlves2021663-18]

The northern Vietic varieties thus became part of the [Mainland Southeast Asia linguistic area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainland_Southeast_Asia_linguistic_area), in which languages from genetically unrelated families converged toward characteristics such as [isolating morphology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isolating_language) and similar syllable structure.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAlves2021659-19] Many languages in this area, including Viet–Mường, underwent a process of [tonogenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonogenesis), in which distinctions formerly expressed by final consonants became phonemic [tonal distinctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tone_\(linguistics\)) when those consonants disappeared. These characteristics have become part of many of the genetically unrelated languages of Southeast Asia; for example, [Tsat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsat_language) (a member of the [Malayo-Polynesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malayo-Polynesian_languages) group within [Austronesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austronesian_languages)) and Vietnamese each developed tones as a phonemic feature.

After expelling the Chinese at the beginning of the 10th century, the [Ngô dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ng%C3%B4_dynasty) adopted [Classical Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Chinese) as the formal medium of government, scholarship, and literature. With the dominance of Chinese came wholesale importation of Chinese vocabulary. The resulting [Sino-Vietnamese vocabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Vietnamese_vocabulary) makes up about a third of the Vietnamese lexicon in all realms, and may account for as much as 60% of the vocabulary used in formal texts.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDeFrancis19778-26]

Vietic languages were confined to the northern third of modern Vietnam until the "southward advance" ([Nam tiến](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nam_ti%E1%BA%BFn)) from the late 15th century.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESidwellAlves2021187-27] The conquest of the ancient nation of [Champa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Champa) and of the [Mekong Delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mekong_Delta) led to an expansion of the Vietnamese people and [language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language), with distinctive local variations emerging.

After France invaded Vietnam in the late 19th century, French gradually replaced Literary Chinese as the official language in education and government. Vietnamese adopted many French terms, such as *đầm* ('dame', from *madame*), *ga* ('train station', from *gare*), *sơ mi* ('shirt', from *chemise*), and *búp bê* ('doll', from *poupée*), resulting in a language that was Austroasiatic but with major Chinese influences and relatively minor French influences from the French colonial era.

### Lenition of medial consonants

As noted above, Proto-Vietic had [sesquisyllabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sesquisyllabic) words with an initial [minor syllable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_syllable) (in addition to, and independent of, initial clusters in the main syllable). When a minor syllable occurred, the main syllable's initial consonant was intervocalic and as a result suffered [lenition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenition), becoming a voiced fricative.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFerlus1992113-34] These fricatives were not present in Proto-Viet–Mường, as indicated by their absence in [Mường](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muong_language), but were present in Vietnamese until the 15th or 16th centuries.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFerlus1992119-35] Subsequent loss of the minor-syllable prefixes phonemicized the fricatives. Ferlus 1992 proposes that originally there were both voiced and voiceless fricatives, corresponding to original voiced or voiceless stops,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFerlus1992-36] but Ferlus 2009 appears to have abandoned that hypothesis, suggesting that stops were softened and voiced at approximately the same time, according to the following pattern:[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFerlus200996-29]

- \*p, \*b > /β/ > *v*. In [Middle Vietnamese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Vietnamese), the outcome of these sounds was written with a hooked *b* (ꞗ), representing a /β/ that was still distinct from *v* (then pronounced /w/).

- \*t, \*d > /ð/ > *d*

- \*c, \*ɟ, \*tʃ > /ʝ/ > *gi*

- \*k, \*ɡ > /ɣ/ > *g/gh*

- \*s > /r̝/ > *r*

### Origin of tones

Proto-Vietic did not have tones. Tones developed later in some of the daughter languages from distinctions in the initial and final consonants. Vietnamese tones developed as follows:[^cite_note-Haudricourt_2017_122–128-37]

| [Register](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Register_\(phonology\)) | Initial consonant | Smooth ending | Glottal ending | Fricative ending |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| High (first) register | Voiceless | A1 *ngang* "level" | B1 *sắc* "sharp" | C1 *hỏi* "asking" |
| Low (second) register | Voiced | A2 *huyền* "deep" | B2 *nặng* "heavy" | C2 *ngã* "tumbling" |

Glottal-ending syllables ended with a glottal stop /ʔ/, while fricative-ending syllables ended with /s/ or /h/. Both types of syllables could co-occur with a resonant (e.g. /m/ or /n/).

At some point, a [tone split](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tone_split) occurred, as in many other [mainland Southeast Asian languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainland_Southeast_Asia_linguistic_area). Essentially, an [allophonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allophonic) distinction developed in the tones whereby those in syllables with voiced initials were pronounced differently from those with voiceless initials. (Approximately speaking, the voiced [allotones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allotone) were pronounced with additional [breathy voice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breathy_voice) or [creaky voice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creaky_voice) and with lowered pitch. The quality difference predominates in today's northern varieties, e.g., in [Hanoi](56667.md), while in the southern varieties the pitch difference predominates, as in [Ho Chi Minh City](66956.md).) Later the plain-voiced stops became voiceless and the allotones became new phonemic tones.

The implosive stops (ɓ, ɗ and ʄ) were unaffected, and in fact developed tonally as if they were unvoiced.[^cite_note-38] (This behavior is common to all East Asian languages with implosive stops.) These stops merged with the corresponding nasals (m, n and ɲ) before the Old Vietnamese period.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFerlus1992117-39][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEShimizu2015152-40]

As noted above, consonants following minor syllables became voiced fricatives. The minor syllables were eventually lost, but not until the tone split had occurred. As a result, words in modern Vietnamese with voiced fricatives occur in all six tones, and the tonal register reflects the voicing of the minor-syllable prefix and not the voicing of the Proto-Vietic main-syllable stop that produced the fricative. For similar reasons, words beginning with /l/ and /ŋ/ occur in both registers. (Thompson 1976 reconstructed voiceless resonants to account for outcomes where resonants occur with a first-register tone,[^cite_note-41] but this is no longer considered necessary, at least by Ferlus.)

A large number of words were borrowed from [Middle Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Chinese), forming part of the [Sino-Vietnamese vocabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Vietnamese_vocabulary). These caused the original introduction of the retroflex sounds /ʂ/ and /ʈ/ (modern *s*, *tr*) into the language.

### Old Vietnamese

Old (or Ancient) Vietnamese separated from Mường around the 9th century. The sources for the reconstruction of Old Vietnamese are [Nom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chu_Nom) texts, such as the 12th-century/1486 Buddhist scripture *Phật thuyết Đại báo phụ mẫu ân trọng kinh* ("Sūtra explained by the Buddha on the Great Repayment of the Heavy Debt to Parents"),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGong201960-42] old inscriptions, and a late 13th-century (possibly 1293) *Annan Jishi*[^cite_note-43] glossary by Chinese diplomat [Chen Fu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chen_Fu?action=edit\&redlink=1) (c. 1259 – 1309).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyen2018162-44]

The *Đại báo* used Chinese characters phonetically where each word, monosyllabic in Modern Vietnamese, is written with two Chinese characters or in a composite character made of two different characters.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGong201958–59-47] This conveys the transformation of the Vietnamese lexicon from sesquisyllabic to fully [monosyllabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monosyllabic_language) under the pressure of Chinese linguistic influence, characterized by linguistic phenomena such as the reduction of minor syllables; loss of affixal morphology drifting towards analytical grammar; simplification of major syllable segments, and the change of suprasegment instruments.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGong201958-48] For example,

- The modern Vietnamese word *trời* 'heaven' was *\*plời* in Old Vietnamese and *blời* in Middle Vietnamese.

  [^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGong201955,_59-49]

- The modern Vietnamese [expressive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideophone) *vỗ về* /vo˧ˀ˦˥ ve˨˩/ 'comfort' was glossed in the *Đại báo* as (阿)寵(阿)弄 *a phổ a phê* /(a)-fo˧˩˧ (a)-fe˧/, which is reconstructed by Shimizu (2015) as \*a¹-ɸo³ a¹-ɸe¹ in Old Vietnamese, and later became voiced as *βo⁴ βe²* in Middle Vietnamese.

  [^cite_note-FOOTNOTEShimizu2015140-50]

Subsequent changes to initial consonants included:[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFerlus1992119-35]

- re-introduction of implosive stops p > ɓ and t > ɗ

- s > ts > t

- tʃ > ɕ

- a merger j > ð

The writing system used for Vietnamese is based closely on the system developed by [Alexandre de Rhodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandre_de_Rhodes) for his 1651 *[Dictionarium Annamiticum Lusitanum et Latinum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictionarium_Annamiticum_Lusitanum_et_Latinum)*. It reflects the pronunciation of the Vietnamese of Hanoi at that time, a stage commonly termed *Middle Vietnamese* (*tiếng Việt trung đại*). The pronunciation of the "rime" of the syllable, i.e. all parts other than the initial consonant (optional /w/ glide, vowel nucleus, tone and final consonant), appears nearly identical between Middle Vietnamese and modern Hanoi pronunciation. On the other hand, the Middle Vietnamese pronunciation of the initial consonant differs greatly from all modern dialects, and in fact is significantly closer to the modern Saigon dialect than the modern Hanoi dialect.

**[^1](#ref_p)** \[p] occurs only at the end of a syllable.\

This *ĕ*, and the /j/ it notated, have disappeared from the modern language.

Note that *b* \[ɓ] and *p* \[p] never contrast in any position, suggesting that they are allophones.

The language also has three clusters at the beginning of syllables, which have since disappeared:

- *tl* /tl/ > modern *tr* – tlước > trước (written in chữ Nôm as 𫏾 (⿰車畧) where 車 represented the initial tl- sound).

- *bl* /ɓl/ > modern *gi* (Northern), *tr* (Southern) – blăng > trăng/giăng (written in chữ Nôm as 𪩮 (⿱巴夌) where 巴 represented the initial bl- sound).

- *ml* /ml/ > *mnh* /mɲ/ > modern *nh* (Northern), l (Southern) – mlời > lời/nhời (written in chữ Nôm as 𠅜 (⿱亠例) where 亠 (simplified from 麻; 𫜗 \[⿱麻例]) represented the initial ml- sound).



de Rhodes's entry for *dĕóu᷃* shows distinct [breves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breve), [acutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_accent) and [apices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_tilde).

Most of the unusual correspondences between spelling and modern pronunciation are explained by Middle Vietnamese. Note in particular:

- de Rhodes' system has two different b letters, ⟨b⟩ and ⟨ꞗ⟩. The latter apparently represented a [voiced bilabial fricative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiced_bilabial_fricative) /β/. Within a century or so, both /β/ and /w/ had merged as /v/, spelled as *v*.

- de Rhodes' system has a second medial glide /j/ that is written *ĕ* and appears in some words with initial *d* and hooked *b*. These later disappear.

- *đ* /ɗ/ was (and still is) [alveolar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alveolar_consonant), whereas *d* /ð/ was dental. The choice of symbols was based on the dental rather than alveolar nature of /d/ and its [allophone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allophone) \[ð] in Spanish and other Romance languages. The inconsistency with the symbols assigned to /ɓ/ vs. /β/ was based on the lack of any such place distinction between the two, with the result that the [stop consonant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stop_consonant) /ɓ/ appeared more "normal" than the fricative /β/. In both cases, the [implosive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implosive_consonant) nature of the stops does not appear to have had any role in the choice of symbol.

- *x* was the [alveolo-palatal fricative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiceless_alveolo-palatal_fricative) /ɕ/ rather than the [dental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiceless_dental_sibilant_fricative) /s/ of the modern language. In 17th-century [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_language), the common language of the Jesuits, *s* was the [apico-alveolar sibilant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiceless_alveolar_fricative) /s̺/ (as still in much of Spain and some parts of Portugal), while *x* was a [palatoalveolar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palato-alveolar_consonant) /ʃ/. The similarity of apicoalveolar /s̺/ to the Vietnamese [retroflex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retroflex_consonant) /ʂ/ led to the assignment of *s* and *x* as above.

De Rhodes's orthography also made use of an [apex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_tilde) diacritic on *o᷃* and *u᷃* to indicate a final [labial-velar nasal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labial-velar_nasal) /ŋ͡m/, an allophone of /ŋ/ that is peculiar to the Hanoi dialect to the present day. An example is *[xao᷃](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/xao%E1%B7%83#Middle_Vietnamese)* /ɕawŋ͡m<sup>A1</sup>/, which later became *[xong](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/xong#Vietnamese)*. This diacritic is often mistaken for a tilde in modern reproductions of early Vietnamese writing.

### Modern era

During [French Indochina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Indochina), a large number of new vocabulary items were introduced into Vietnamese, including ones from Japanese [wasei-kango](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wasei-kango) for Western-originated concepts, adopted through Sino-Vietnamese pronunciation.

Following the defeat of South Vietnam in 1975 by North Vietnam in the [Vietnam War](32611.md), the Vietnamese language within Vietnam has gradually shifted towards the Northern dialect.[^cite_note-\:2-51] [Hanoi](56667.md), the largest city in Northern Vietnam was made the capital of Vietnam in 1976. A study stated that "The gap in vocabulary use between speakers in North and South Vietnam is now much narrower than before. There is little to distinguish between how the generations that were born and grew up in the South after 1975 now speak, compared to their peers in the North. This gap is almost non-existent in newspapers, on radio and television, and in websites."[^cite_note-\:2-51] However, this convergence does not apply to emigrants, in which the study states represent "[culture freeze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_freeze?action=edit\&redlink=1)", a phenomenon that describes when culture among emigrants is frozen in time and does not evolve with culture in their home country once they move to a new country.

Here, culture freeze describes that the use of the language of emigrants from Vietnam has been "frozen" in both vocabulary and pronunciation, and as languages gradually evolve over time, has come to differ slightly from than the present Vietnamese language in Vietnam. Additionally, as immigration to the United States following the Vietnam war was primarily driven due to political reasons, the Southern Vietnamese dialect was initially strongly linked to social identity. During and after the Vietnam War, thousands of Southern Vietnamese immigrated to the United States with the partnership between Saigon and the US.[^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53] In contrast, during and following the Vietnam War, thousands of Northern Vietnamese moved to the Czech Republic due to Hanoi's partnership with the now obsolete [Czechoslovak Socialist Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czechoslovak_Socialist_Republic). As a result, today, the Vietnamese language is generally taught through the Northern dialect in the Czech Republic in contrast with the Southern dialect in the United States.

## Geographic distribution



Global distribution of speakers

As a result of [emigration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overseas_Vietnamese), Vietnamese speakers are also found in other parts of Southeast Asia, East Asia, North America, Europe, and [Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australia_\(continent\)). Vietnamese has also been officially recognized as a minority language in the [Czech Republic](5321.md).[^cite_note-54]

As the national language, Vietnamese is the *[lingua franca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lingua_franca)* in Vietnam. It is also spoken by the [Jing people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jing_people) traditionally residing on three islands (now joined to the mainland) off [Dongxing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dongxing,_Guangxi) in southern [Guangxi Province](47836950.md), China.[^cite_note-55] A large number of Vietnamese speakers also reside in neighboring countries of [Cambodia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_Cambodians) and [Laos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_people_in_Laos).

See also: [Vietnamese language in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_language_in_the_United_States)

In the United States, Vietnamese is the [sixth most spoken language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_language_in_the_United_States), with over 1.5 million speakers, who are concentrated in a handful of states. It is the third-most spoken language in Texas and Washington; fourth-most in Georgia, Louisiana, and Virginia; and fifth-most in Arkansas and California.[^cite_note-Language_Map_Data_Center-56] Vietnamese is the third most spoken language in Australia other than English, after Mandarin and Arabic.[^cite_note-57] In France, it is the most spoken Asian language and the eighth most spoken immigrant language at home.[^cite_note-58]

### Official status

Vietnamese is the sole official and national language of Vietnam. It is the first language of the majority of the Vietnamese population, as well as a first or second language for the country's [ethnic minority groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_groups_in_Vietnam).[^cite_note-Vietnamese_language-59]

In the [Czech Republic](5321.md), Vietnamese has been recognized as one of 14 minority languages, on the basis of communities that have resided in the country either traditionally or on a long-term basis. This status grants the [Vietnamese community in the country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_people_in_the_Czech_Republic) a representative on the Government Council for Nationalities, an advisory body of the Czech Government for matters of policy towards national minorities and their members. It also grants the community the right to use Vietnamese with public authorities and in courts anywhere in the country.[^cite_note-60][^cite_note-61] Vietnamese was also recognized as a national minority language in [Slovakia](26830.md) in 2023.[^cite_note-62]

In the U.S. city of San Francisco, municipal services began to be offered in Vietnamese starting in 2024.[^cite_note-63]

### As a foreign language

Vietnamese is taught in schools and institutions outside of Vietnam, a large part contributed by its [diaspora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overseas_Vietnamese). In countries with Vietnamese-speaking communities Vietnamese language education largely serves as a role to link descendants of Vietnamese immigrants to their ancestral culture. In neighboring countries and vicinities near Vietnam such as Southern China, Cambodia, Laos, and Thailand, Vietnamese as a foreign language is largely due to trade, as well as recovery and growth of the Vietnamese economy.[^cite_note-64][^cite_note-65]

Since the 1980s, Vietnamese language schools (*trường Việt ngữ*) have been established for youth in many Vietnamese-speaking communities around the world such as [in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_Americans),[^cite_note-66] [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_people_in_Germany),[^cite_note-vietteachlearnoverwhelminggermany-67] and [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_people_in_France).[^cite_note-68][^cite_note-69][^cite_note-70]

Front and central vowels (*i*, *ê*, *e*, *ư*, *â*, *ơ*, *ă*, *a*) are [unrounded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roundedness), whereas the back vowels (*u*, *ô*, *o*) are rounded. The vowels *â* \[ɤ̆] and *ă* \[ă] are pronounced very short, much shorter than the other vowels. Thus, *ơ* and *â* are basically pronounced the same except that *ơ* \[ɤ] is of normal length while *â* \[ɤ̆] is short – the same applies to the vowels long *a* \[a] and short *ă* \[ă].[^cite_note-75]

The centering diphthongs are formed with only the three high vowels (*i*, *ư*, *u*). They are generally spelled as *ia*, *ưa*, *ua* when they end a word and are spelled *iê*, *ươ*, *uô*, respectively, when they are followed by a consonant.

In addition to single vowels (or [monophthongs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monophthong)) and centering diphthongs, Vietnamese has closing [diphthongs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diphthong)[^cite_note-76] and [triphthongs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triphthong). The closing diphthongs and triphthongs consist of a main vowel component followed by a shorter semivowel [offglide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offglide) /j/ or /w/.[^cite_note-77] There are restrictions on the high offglides: /j/ cannot occur after a front vowel (*i*, *ê*, *e*) nucleus and /w/ cannot occur after a back vowel (*u*, *ô*, *o*) nucleus.[^cite_note-78]

The correspondence between the orthography and pronunciation is complicated. For example, the offglide /j/ is usually written as *i*; however, it may also be represented with *y*. In addition, in the diphthongs \[ɐj] and \[aj] the letters *y* and *i* also indicate the pronunciation of the main vowel: *ay* = *ă* + /j/, *ai* = *a* + /j/. Thus, *tay* "hand" is \[tɐj] while *tai* "ear" is \[taj]. Similarly, *u* and *o* indicate different pronunciations of the main vowel: *au* = *ă* + /w/, *ao* = *a* + /w/. Thus, *thau* "brass" is \[t̺ʰɐw] while *thao* "raw silk" is \[tʰaw].

Some consonant sounds are written with only one letter (like "*p*"), other consonant sounds are written with a [digraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digraph_\(orthography\)) (like "*ph*"), and others are written with more than one letter or digraph (the velar stop is written variously as "*c*", "*k*", or "*q*"). In some cases, they are based on their Middle Vietnamese pronunciation; since that period, *ph* and *kh* (but not *th*) have evolved from aspirated stops into fricatives (like Greek [phi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phi) and [chi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chi_\(letter\))), while *d* and *gi* have collapsed and converged together (into /z/ in the north and /j/ in the south).

Not all dialects of Vietnamese have the same consonant in a given word (although all dialects use the same spelling in the written language). See the [language variation section](#Language_variation) for further elaboration.

Syllable-final orthographic *ch* and *nh* in Vietnamese has had different analyses. One analysis has final *ch*, *nh* (as [phonemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoneme) /c/, /ɲ/) contrasting with syllable-final *t*, *c* (/t/, /k/) and *n*, *ng* (/n/, /ŋ/) and identifies final *ch* with the syllable-initial *ch* /c/. The other analysis has final *ch* and *nh* as predictable [allophonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allophonic) variants of the velar [phonemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoneme) /k/ and /ŋ/ that occur after the upper front vowels *i* /i/ and *ê* /e/; although they also occur after *a*, but in such cases are believed to have resulted from an earlier *e* /ɛ/ which diphthongized to *ai* (cf. *ach* from *aic*, *anh* from *aing*). (See [Vietnamese phonology: Analysis of final *ch*, *nh*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_phonology#Analysis_of_final_ch.2C_nh) for further details.)

### Tones

Each Vietnamese syllable is pronounced with one of six inherent [tones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tone_\(linguistics\)),[^cite_note-79] centered on the main vowel or group of vowels. Tones differ in:

- length (duration)

- [pitch contour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_contour) (i.e. pitch melody)

- pitch height

- [phonation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonation)

Tone is indicated by diacritics written above or below the vowel (most of the tone diacritics appear above the vowel; except the *nặng* tone dot diacritic goes below the vowel).[^cite_note-80] The six tones in the northern varieties (including Hanoi), with their self-referential Vietnamese names, are:

[Four tones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_tones_\(Middle_Chinese\)) diagram shown in the book *Thiền lâm bảo huấn hợp chú* 禪林寶訓合註 (1858; Tự Đức thập nhất niên 嗣德十一年).

Before Vietnamese switched from a Chinese-based script to a Latin-based script, Vietnamese had used the traditional Chinese system of classifying tones. Using this system, Vietnamese has 8 tones, but modern linguists only count 6 phonemic tones.

Vietnamese tones were classified into two main groups, *bằng* (平; 'level tones') and *trắc* (仄; 'sharp tones'). Some tones such as *ngang* belong to the *bằng* group, while others such as *ngã* belong to the *trắc* group. Then, these tones were further divided in several other categories: *bình* (平; 'even'), *thượng* (上; 'rising'), *khứ* (去; 'departing'), and *nhập* (入; 'entering').

*Sắc* and *nặng* are counted twice in the system, once in *khứ* (去; 'departing') and again in *nhập* (入; 'entering'). The reason for the extra two tones is that syllables ending in the stops /p/, /t/, /c/ and /k/ are treated as having entering tones, but phonetically they are exactly the same.

The tones in the old classification were called *Âm bình* 陰平 (*ngang*), *Dương bình* 陽平 (*huyền*), *Âm thượng* 陰上 (*hỏi*), *Dương thượng* 陽上 (*ngã*), *Âm khứ* 陰去 (*sắc*; for words that do not end in /p/, /t/, /c/ and /k/), *Dương khứ* 陽去 (*nặng*; for words that do not end in /p/, /t/, /c/ and /k/), *Âm nhập* 陰入 (*sắc*; for words that do end in /p/, /t/, /c/ and /k/), and *Dương nhập* 陽入 (*nặng*; for words that do end in /p/, /t/, /c/ and /k/).

## Grammar

Main articles: [Vietnamese grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_grammar) and [Vietnamese morphology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_morphology)

Vietnamese, like Thai and many languages in Southeast Asia, is an [analytic language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_language). Vietnamese does not use [morphological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphology_\(linguistics\)) marking of [case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_case), [gender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_gender), [number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_number) or [tense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_tense) (and, as a result, has no [finite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_verb)/[nonfinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-finite_verb) distinction).[^cite_note-82] Also like other languages in the region, Vietnamese syntax conforms to [subject–verb–object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subject%E2%80%93verb%E2%80%93object) [word order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_order), is [head-initial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head-initial) (displaying modified-[modifier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_modifier) ordering), and has a noun [classifier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classifier_\(linguistics\)) system. The [topic–comment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topic_and_comment) structure is also prevalent in Vietnamese. Additionally, it is [pro-drop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pro-drop), [wh-in-situ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wh-in-situ), and allows [verb serialization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verb_serialization).

### Chinese contact



Old Nôm character for rice noodle soup "phở". The character [米](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E2%BD%B6) on the left means "rice" whilst the character on the right "頗" was used to indicate the sound of the word (*[phở](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pho)*).

Although Vietnamese roots are classified as Austroasiatic, Vietic, and Viet-Mường, [language contact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_contact) with Chinese heavily influenced the Vietnamese language, causing it to diverge from [Viet-Mường](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viet-Muong) around the 10th to 11th century and become Modern Vietnamese. For instance, the Vietnamese word *quản lý*, meaning "management" (noun) or "manage" (verb), likely descended from the same word as *guǎnlǐ* (管理) in Chinese (also *kanri* (管理, かんり) in Japanese and *gwalli* (*gwan+ri*; [Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_language): 관리; [Hanja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanja): 管理) in Korean). Instances of Chinese contact include the historical [Nam Việt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nam_Vi%E1%BB%87t) (aka [Nanyue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanyue)) as well as other periods of influence. Besides English and French, which have made some contributions to the Vietnamese language, Japanese loanwords into Vietnamese are also a more recently studied phenomenon.

Modern linguists describe modern Vietnamese having lost many [Proto-Austroasiatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Mon%E2%80%93Khmer_language) phonological and morphological features that original Vietnamese had.[^cite_note-84] The Chinese influence on Vietnamese corresponds to various periods when Vietnam was under [Chinese rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_under_Chinese_rule) and subsequent influence after Vietnam became independent. Early linguists thought that this meant the Vietnamese lexicon had only two influxes of Chinese words, one stemming from the period under actual Chinese rule and a second from afterwards. These words are grouped together as [Sino-Vietnamese vocabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Vietnamese_vocabulary).

However, according to linguist John Phan, "Annamese Middle Chinese" was already used and spoken in the [Red River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_River_\(Asia\)) Valley by the 1st century CE, and its vocabulary significantly fused with the co-existing Proto-Viet-Mường language, the immediate ancestor of Vietnamese. He lists three major classes of Sino-Vietnamese borrowings:[^cite_note-85][^cite_note-86][^cite_note-87] Early [Sino-Vietnamese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Vietnamese_vocabulary) ([Han dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_dynasty) ca. 1st century CE and [Jin dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jin_dynasty_\(266%E2%80%93420\)) ca. 4th century CE), Late Sino-Vietnamese ([Tang dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tang_dynasty)), and Recent Sino-Vietnamese ([Ming dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ming_dynasty) and afterwards)

Vietnam became a French protectorate/colonial territory in 1883 (until the [Geneva Accords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1954_Geneva_Conference) of 1954), which resulted in significant influence from [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language_in_Vietnam) into the Indochina region (Laos, Cambodia and Vietnam). Examples include:

*Cà phê* in Vietnamese was derived from the French *café* (coffee). Yogurt in Vietnamese is *sữa chua* (lit. 'sour milk'), but it is also calqued from French (*yaourt*) into Vietnamese (*da ua -* /j/a ua). *Phô mai* (cheese) is from the French *fromage*. A necktie (*cravate* in French) is rendered into Vietnamese as *cà vạt*.

In addition, modern Vietnamese pronunciations of French names correspond directly to the original French pronunciations (*Pa-ri* for Paris, *Mác-xây* for [Marseille](40888948.md), *Boóc-đô* for [Bordeaux](4097.md), etc.), whereas pronunciations of other foreign names (Chinese excluded) are generally derived from English.

Some English words were incorporated into Vietnamese as [loan words](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loan_words) – such as "TV", borrowed as "tivi" or just TV, but still officially called *truyền hình*. ### Japanese

Japanese loanwords are a more recently studied phenomenon, with a paper by Nguyễn & Lê (2020) classifying three waves of Japanese influence – with the first two waves being the principal influxes and the third wave coming from the Vietnamese who studied Japanese.[^cite_note-\:0-88] The first wave consisted of Kanji words created by Japanese to represent Western concepts that were not readily available in Chinese or Japanese, where by the end of the 19th century they were imported to other Asian languages.[^cite_note-89] This first influx is called Sino-Vietnamese words of Japanese origins. For example, the Vietnamese term for "association club", *câu lạc bộ*, which was borrowed from Chinese (俱樂部, [pinyin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinyin): *jùlèbù*, [jyutping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jyutping): *keoi1 lok6 bou6*), and then in turn from Japanese ([kanji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanji): 倶楽部, [katakana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katakana): クラブ, [rōmaji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C5%8Dmaji): *kurabu*) which came from the English "*club"*, resulting in indirect borrowing from Japanese.

The second wave was during the brief Japanese occupation of Vietnam from 1940 until 1945. However, Japanese cultural influence in Vietnam started significantly from the 1980s. This newer second wave of Japanese-origin loanwords is distinctive from the Sino-Vietnamese words of Japanese origin in that they were borrowed directly from Japanese. This vocabulary includes words representative of Japanese culture, such as *kimono*, *sumo*, *samurai*, and *bonsai* from modified [Hepburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepburn_romanization) romanisation. These loanwords are coined as "new Japanese loanwords". A significant number of new Japanese loanwords were also of Chinese origin. Sometimes the same concept can be described using both Sino-Vietnamese words of Japanese origin (first wave) and new Japanese loanwords (second wave). For example, judo can be referred to as both *judo* and *nhu đạo*, the Vietnamese reading of 柔道.[^cite_note-\:0-88]

## Slang

Vietnamese slang (tiếng lóng) has changed over time. Vietnamese slang consists of pure Vietnamese words as well as words borrowed from other languages such as [Mandarin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandarin_Chinese) or [Indo-European languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European_languages).[^cite_note-\:02-90] It is estimated that Vietnamese slang originating from Mandarin accounts for a tiny proportion (4.6% of surveyed data in newspapers).[^cite_note-\:02-90] On the other hand, slang originating from Indo-European languages accounts for a more significant proportion (12%) and is much more common in today's usage.[^cite_note-\:02-90] Slang borrowed from these languages can be either [transliteral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transliteration) or [vernacular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vernacular).[^cite_note-\:02-90] Some examples:

| Word | [IPA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Phonetic_Alphabet) | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Ex* | /ɛk̚/, /ejk̚/ | a word borrowed from English used to describe an ex-lover, usually pronounced similarly to *ếch* ("frog"). This is an example of vernacular slang.[^cite_note-\:02-90] |
| *Sô* | /ʂoː/ | a word derived from the English word "sho&#x77;*"* which has the same meaning, usually paired with the word *chạy* ("to run") to make the phrase *chạy sô*, which translates in English to "running shows", but its everyday use has the same connotation as "having to do a lot of tasks within a short amount of time". This is an example of transliteral slang.[^cite_note-\:02-90] |

With the rise of the Internet, new slang is generated and popularized through social media. This modern slang is commonly used in the younger generation's teenspeak in Vietnam. This recent slang is mostly pure Vietnamese, and almost all the words are [homonyms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homonym) or some form of [wordplay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_play). Some slang words may include [profanity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Profanity) swear words ([derogatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pejorative)) or just a [play on words](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Play-on-words).

Some examples with newer and older slang that originate from northern, central, or southern Vietnamese dialects include:

| Word | IPA | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *vãi* | /vǎːj/ | "Vãi" (predominately from northern Vietnamese) is a [profanity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Profanity) word that can be a noun or a verb depending on the context. It refers to a female [Buddhist temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_temple)-goer in its noun form and to "spilling something over" in its verb form. In slang terms, it is commonly used to emphasize an adjective or a verb – for example, *ngon vãi* ("very delicious"), *sợ vãi* ("very scary").[^cite_note-91] Similar uses to the [expletive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Profanity) [bloody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloody). |
| *trẻ trâu* | /ʈɛ̌ːʈəw/ | A noun whose literal translation is "buffalo kid". | *gấu* | /ɣə̆́w/ | A noun meaning "bear". It is also commonly used to refer to someone's lover.[^cite_note-\:12-93] |
| *gà* | /ɣàː/ | A noun meaning "chicken". It is also commonly used to refer to someone's lack of ability to complete or compete in a task.[^cite_note-\:22-92] |
| *cá sấu* | /káːʂə́w/ | A noun meaning "crocodile". It is also commonly used to refer to someone's lack of beauty. The word *sấu* can be pronounced similarly to *xấu* (ugly).[^cite_note-\:12-93] |

| *nha (and other variants)* | /ɲaː/ | Similar to other particles (nhé, nghe, nhỉ, nhá), it can be used to end sentences. "Rửa chén, nhỉ" can mean "Wash the dishes... yeah?"[^cite_note-94] |

Whilst older slang has been used by previous generations, the prevalence of modern slang used by young people in Vietnam (as teenspeak) has made conversations more difficult for older generations to understand. This has become subject for debate. Some believe that incorporating teenspeak or internet slang in daily conversation among teenagers will affect the formality and cadence of their general speech.[^cite_note-97] Others argue that it is not slang that is the problem, but rather the lack of communication techniques for the instant internet messaging era. They believe slang should not be dismissed, but instead, youth should be adequately informed to recognise when to use it and when it is inappropriate.

### Chữ Nôm

Main article: [Chữ Nôm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ch%E1%BB%AF_N%C3%B4m)

From around the 13th century, Vietnamese scholars used their knowledge of the Chinese script to develop the *chữ Nôm* (lit. 'Southern characters') script to record folk literature in Vietnamese. The script used Chinese characters to represent both borrowed [Sino-Vietnamese vocabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Vietnamese_vocabulary) and native words with similar pronunciation or meaning. In addition, thousands of new compound characters were created to write Vietnamese words using a variety of methods, including [phono-semantic compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phono-semantic_compound).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDeFrancis197724–26-100] For example, in the opening lines of the classic poem *[The Tale of Kiều](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tale_of_Kieu)*,

- the Sino-Vietnamese word *mệnh* 'destiny' was written with its original character [命](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%91%BD#Chinese);

- the native Vietnamese word *ta* 'our' was written with the character [些](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E4%BA%9B#Chinese) of the homophonous Sino-Vietnamese word *ta* 'little, few; rather, somewhat';

- the native Vietnamese word *năm* 'year' was written with a new character 𢆥 that is compounded from [南](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%8D%97#Chinese) *nam* and [年](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%B9%B4#Chinese) 'year'.

The oldest example of an early form of the *Nôm* is found in a list of names in the Tháp Miếu Temple Inscription, dating from the early 13th century AD.[^cite_note-Holcombe-101][^cite_note-Kornicki-102] *Nôm* writing reached its zenith in the 18th century when many Vietnamese writers and poets composed their works in *Nôm*, most notably [Nguyễn Du](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguy%E1%BB%85n_Du) and [Hồ Xuân Hương](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%E1%BB%93_Xu%C3%A2n_H%C6%B0%C6%A1ng) (dubbed "the Queen of Nôm poetry"). However, it was only used for official purposes during the brief [Hồ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%E1%BB%93_dynasty) and [Tây Sơn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C3%A2y_S%C6%A1n_dynasty) dynasties (1400–1406 and 1778–1802 respectively).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDeFrancis197732,_38-103]

A [Vietnamese Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_Vietnam), [Nguyễn Trường Tộ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguy%E1%BB%85n_Tr%C6%B0%E1%BB%9Dng_T%E1%BB%99), unsuccessfully petitioned the Court suggesting the adoption of a script for Vietnamese based on Chinese characters.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDeFrancis1977101–105-104][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMarr1984145-105]

### Vietnamese alphabet

Main article: [Vietnamese alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_alphabet)

A [romanisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization) of Vietnamese was codified in the 17th century by the Avignonese [Jesuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_of_Jesus) missionary [Alexandre de Rhodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandre_de_Rhodes) (1591–1660), based on works of earlier [Portuguese missionaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_discoveries), particularly [Francisco de Pina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_de_Pina), Gaspar do Amaral and Antonio Barbosa.[^cite_note-Jacques_2002-106][^cite_note-Tran_2019-107] It reflects a "Middle Vietnamese" dialect close to the Hanoi variety as spoken in the 17th century. Its vowels and final consonants correspond most closely to northern dialects while its initial consonants are most similar to southern dialects. (This is not unlike how [English orthography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_orthography) is based on the Chancery Standard of [Late Middle English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_English#Late_Middle_English), with many spellings retained even after the [Great Vowel Shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Vowel_Shift).)

The Vietnamese alphabet contains 29 letters, supplementing the Latin alphabet with an additional consonant letter (*[đ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D_with_stroke)*) and 6 additional vowel letters (*ă*, *â/ê/ô*, *ơ*, *ư*) formed with [diacritics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diacritic). The Latin letters *f*, *j*, *w* and *z* are not used.[^cite_note-\:1-108][^cite_note-109] The script also represents additional [phonemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoneme) using ten [digraphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digraph_\(orthography\)) (*ch*, *gh*, *gi*, *kh*, *ng*, *nh*, *ph*, *qu*, *th*, and *tr*) and a single [trigraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigraph_\(orthography\)) (*ngh*). Further diacritics are used to indicate the [tone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tone_\(linguistics\)) of each syllable:

| Diacritic | Vietnamese name and meaning |
| --- | --- |
| (no mark) | *ngang* 'level' |
| ◌̀ ([grave accent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grave_accent)) | *huyền* 'deep' |
| ◌́ ([acute accent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_accent)) | *sắc* 'sharp' |
| ◌̉ ([hook above](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hook_above)) | *hỏi* 'questioning' |
| ◌̃ ([tilde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tilde)) | *ngã* 'tumbling' |
| ◌̣ ([dot below](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dot_\(diacritic\))) | *nặng* 'heavy' |

Thus, it is possible for diacritics to be stacked e.g. *ể*, combining letter with diacritic, *ê*, with diacritic for tone, *ẻ*, to make *ể*.

Despite the missionaries' creation of the alphabetic script, *chữ Nôm* remained the dominant script in [Vietnamese Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_Vietnam) literature for more than 200 years.[^cite_note-110] Starting from the late 19th century, the Vietnamese alphabet (*chữ Quốc ngữ* or 'national language script') gradually expanded from its initial usage in Christian writing to become more popular among the general public.

The romanised script became predominant over the course of the early 20th century, when education became widespread and a simpler writing system was found to be more expedient for teaching and communication with the general population. The [French colonial administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Indochina) sought to eliminate Chinese writing, Confucianism, and other Chinese influences from Vietnam.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMarr1984145-105] French superseded Literary Chinese in administration. Vietnamese written with the alphabet became required for all public documents in 1910 by issue of a decree by the French Résident Supérieur of the protectorate of [Tonkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonkin). In turn, Vietnamese reformists and nationalists themselves encouraged and popularized the use of *chữ Quốc ngữ*. By the middle of the 20th century, most writing was done in *chữ Quốc ngữ*, which became the official script on independence.



An excerpt of the [Book of Jonah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Jonah) in Vietnamese.

Nevertheless, *chữ Hán* was still in use during the French colonial period and as late as [World War II](32927.md) was still featured on banknotes,[^cite_note-111][^cite_note-112] but fell out of official and mainstream use shortly thereafter. The education reform by [North Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Vietnam) in 1950 eliminated the use of *chữ Hán* and *chữ Nôm*.[^cite_note-113] Today, only a few scholars and some extremely elderly people are able to read *chữ Nôm* or use it in [Vietnamese calligraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_calligraphy). Priests of the [Jing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gin_people) minority in China (descendants of 16th-century migrants from Vietnam) use songbooks and scriptures written in *chữ Nôm* in their ceremonies.[^cite_note-114]

Currently the [Nguồn language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ngu%E1%BB%93n_language) is considered by the Vietnamese government to be a dialect of Vietnamese, however it is also considered a separate Việt-Mường language or the southernmost dialect of Mường language. Vietnamese has traditionally been divided into three dialect regions: North (45%), Central (10%), and South (45%). [Michel Ferlus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Ferlus) and Nguyễn Tài Cẩn found that there was a separate North-Central dialect for Vietnamese as well. The term *Haut-Annam* refers to dialects spoken from the northern Nghệ An Province to the southern (former) Thừa Thiên Province that preserve archaic features (like consonant clusters and undiphthongized vowels) that have been lost in other modern dialects.

The dialect regions differ mostly in their sound systems (see below) but also in vocabulary (including basic and non-basic vocabulary) and grammar.[^cite_note-116] The North-Central and the Central regional varieties, which have a significant number of vocabulary differences, are generally less [mutually intelligible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutually_intelligible) to Northern and Southern speakers. There is less internal variation within the Southern region than the other regions because of its relatively late settlement by Vietnamese-speakers (around the end of the 15th century). The North-Central region is particularly conservative since its pronunciation has diverged less from Vietnamese orthography than the other varieties, which tend to merge certain sounds. Along the coastal areas, regional variation has been neutralized to a certain extent, but more mountainous regions preserve more variation. As for [sociolinguistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociolinguistic) attitudes, the North-Central varieties are often felt to be "peculiar" or "difficult to understand" by speakers of other dialects although their pronunciation fits the written language the most closely; that is typically because of various words in their vocabulary that are unfamiliar to other speakers (see the example vocabulary table below).

The large movements of people between North and South since the mid-20th century has resulted in a sizable number of Southern residents with a somewhat Northern accent. Following the 1954 [partition of Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_of_Vietnam), about a million [Northerners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%E1%BA%AFc_54), mainly from the Red River Delta, moved south, primarily to [Saigon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saigon) and in large numbers to [Biên Hòa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bi%C3%AAn_H%C3%B2a), [Vũng Tàu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C5%A9ng_T%C3%A0u), and the surrounding areas as part of [Operation Passage to Freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Passage_to_Freedom). About 150,000 moved in the reverse direction (*Tập kết ra Bắc*, literally 'regroup to the North').

After the [Fall of Saigon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Saigon) in 1975, Northern and North-Central speakers from the densely populated Red River Delta and the traditionally-poorer provinces of Nghệ An, Hà Tĩnh, and Quảng Bình have continued to move south to look for better economic opportunities allowed by the new government's New Economic Zones, a program that lasted from 1975 to 1985.[^cite_note-Desbarats-117] The first half of the program (1975–1980) resulted in 1.3 million people sent to the New Economic Zones (NEZs), most of which were relocated to the southern half of the country in previously uninhabited areas, and 550,000 of them were Northerners.[^cite_note-Desbarats-117] The second half (1981–1985) saw almost 1 million Northerners relocated to the New Economic Zones.[^cite_note-Desbarats-117] Government and military personnel from Northern and North-Central Vietnam are also posted to various locations throughout the country that were often away from their home regions. More recently, the growth of the free market system has resulted in increased interregional movement and relations between distant parts of Vietnam through business and travel. The movements have also resulted in some blending of dialects and more significantly have made the Northern dialect more easily understood in the South and vice versa. Most Southerners, when singing modern/old popular Vietnamese songs or addressing the public, do so in the standardized accent if possible, which uses the Northern pronunciation. That is true in both Vietnam and overseas Vietnamese communities.

Modern Standard Vietnamese is based on the Hanoi dialect. Nevertheless, the major dialects are still predominant in their respective areas and have also evolved over time with influences from other areas. Historically, accents have been distinguished by how each region pronounces the letters *d* (\[z] in the Northern dialect and \[j] in the Central and Southern dialect) and *r* (\[z] in the Northern dialect and \[r] in the Central and Southern dialects). Thus, the Central and the Southern dialects can be said to have retained a pronunciation closer to Vietnamese orthography and resemble how Middle Vietnamese sounded, in contrast to the modern Northern (Hanoi) dialect, which has since undergone pronunciation shifts.&#x20;

### Consonants

## Word play

A basic form of [word play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_play) in Vietnamese called *nối từ* involves disyllabic words in which the last syllable forms the first syllable of the next word in the chain. This game involves two members versing each other until the opponent is unable to think of another word. For instance:&#x20;

| Hậu trường (backstage) | → | Trường học (School) | → | Học tập (Study) | → | Tập trung (Concentrate) | → |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Trung tâm (Centre) | → | Tâm lí (Mentality) | → | Lí do (Reason) | → | Etc., until someone cannot form the next word or, if the word play is used as a game, gives up. |  |

Another [language game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_game) known as *nói lái* is used by Vietnamese speakers.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENguyễn199728–29-123] *Nói lái* involves switching, adding or removing the tones in a pair of words and may also involve switching the order of words or the first consonant and the [rime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syllable_rime) of each word. Some examples:

| Original phrase |  | Phrase after *nói lái* transformation | Structural change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *đái dầm* "[peeing oneself](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urinary_incontinence)" | → | *dấm đài* (literal translation "vinegar stage") | word order and tone switch |
| *chửa hoang* "pregnancy out of wedlock" | → | *hoảng chưa* "scared yet?" | word order and tone switch |
| *bầy tôi* "all the king's subjects" | → | *bồi tây* "waiter (of Western origin)" | initial consonant, rime, and tone switch |
| *bí mật* "secrets" | → | *bật mí* "reveal secrets" | initial consonant and rime switch |
| *Tây Ban Nha* "Spain (España)" | → | *Tây Bán Nhà* (literal translation "West Sell House", mainly used to mock [Spain national football team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spain_men's_national_football_team)[^cite_note-124]) | initial consonant, rime, and tone switch |
| *Bồ Đào Nha* "[Portugal](23033.md)" | → | *Nhà Đào Bô* (literal translation "House Dig [Potty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potty_chair)", mainly used to mock [Portugal national football team](576328.md)) | word order and tone switch |

The resulting transformed phrase often has a different meaning but sometimes may just be a nonsensical word pair. *Nói lái* can be used to obscure the original meaning and thus soften the discussion of a socially sensitive issue, as with *dấm đài* and *hoảng chưa* (above), or when implied (and not overtly spoken), to deliver a hidden subtextual message, as with *bồi tây*.[^cite_note-125] Naturally, *nói lái* can be used for a humorous effect.[^cite_note-126]

Another word game somewhat reminiscent of [pig latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pig_latin) is played by children. Here a nonsense syllable (chosen by the child) is prefixed onto a target word's syllables, then their initial consonants and rimes are switched with the tone of the original word remaining on the new switched rime.

| Nonsense syllable | Target word |  | Intermediate form with prefixed syllable |  | Resulting "secret" word |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *la* | *phở* "beef or chicken noodle soup" | → | *la phở* | → | *lơ phả* |
| *la* | *ăn* "to eat" | → | *la ăn* | → | *lăn a* |
| *la* | *hoàn cảnh* "situation" | → | *la hoàn la cảnh* | → | *loan hà lanh cả* |
| *chim* | *hoàn cảnh* "situation" | → | *chim hoàn chim cảnh* | → | *choan hìm chanh kỉm* |

This language game is often used as a "secret" or "coded" language useful for obscuring messages from adult comprehension.
