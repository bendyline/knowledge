The **Boeing B-52 Stratofortress** is an American [nuclear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_nuclear-capable_aircraft)-capable [subsonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsonic_aircraft) jet-powered [strategic bomber](214209.md). The B-52 was designed and built by [Boeing](18933266.md), which has continued to provide support and upgrades. It has been operated by the [United States Air Force](32090.md) (USAF) since 1955 and was flown by [NASA](18426568.md) from 1959 to 2007.[^cite_note-NASA_2023_r581-2] The bomber can carry up to 70,000 pounds (32,000 kg) of weapons[^cite_note-Minot_Fact_Sheet-3] and has a typical [combat range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Range_\(aeronautics\)) of around 8,800 miles (7,600 nmi; 14,200 km) without [aerial refueling](237949.md).[^cite_note-AF_B52-4]

After Boeing won the initial contract in June 1946, the aircraft's design evolved from a [straight-wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straight_wing) aircraft powered by six [turboprop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turboprop) engines to the final prototype YB-52 with eight [turbojet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbojet) engines and [swept wings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swept_wing). The B-52 took its [maiden flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maiden_flight) in April 1952. Built to carry [nuclear weapons](21785.md) for Cold War [deterrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_strategy) missions, the B-52 Stratofortress replaced the [Convair B-36 Peacemaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convair_B-36_Peacemaker). The bombers flew under the [Strategic Air Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_Air_Command) (SAC) until it was disestablished in 1992 and its aircraft absorbed into the [Air Combat Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Combat_Command) (ACC); in 2010, all B-52s were transferred to the new [Air Force Global Strike Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Force_Global_Strike_Command) (AFGSC).

The B-52's official name *Stratofortress* is rarely used; informally, the aircraft is commonly referred to as the ***BUFF*** (**Big Ugly Fat Fucker**/**Fella**).[^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7][^cite_note-9] Superior performance at high subsonic speeds and relatively low operating costs have kept them in service despite the introduction of more advanced strategic bombers, such as the Mach-2+ [Convair B-58 Hustler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convair_B-58_Hustler), the [variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variable-sweep_wing)-geometry [Rockwell B-1 Lancer](4952.md), and the [stealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stealth_aircraft) plane [Northrop Grumman B-2 Spirit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northrop_Grumman_B-2_Spirit). The B-52 has used [conventional munitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conventional_weapon) in the [Vietnam War](32611.md), [Gulf War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_War), [NATO bombing of Yugoslavia](322473.md), [Iraq War](5043324.md), [war against the Islamic State](43517689.md), [war in Afghanistan](19666611.md), and [2026 Iran war](82533582.md).

In its nuclear role, the B-52H is assigned only the [AGM-86B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-86B) cruise missile. Conventionally, it can employ the [AGM-142](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-142), [AGM-154](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-154_Joint_Standoff_Weapon), [AGM-158](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-158_JASSM) air-to-ground weapons, and a variety of [Joint Direct Attack Munitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_Direct_Attack_Munition), and in maritime roles can employ [Harpoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harpoon_\(missile\)) anti-ship missiles and [Quickstrike](22102.md#MK65_Quickstrike) naval mines. As a [mother ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mother_ship), it has deployed the [D-21](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_D-21) reconnaissance [drone](38212231.md), and test-launched the [X-15](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_X-15), [X-24](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Marietta_X-24), [X-38](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA_X-38), and [X-43](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA_X-43) experimental aircraft and various [hypersonic missiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypersonic_missile).

The US Air Force has 76 B-52s in service as of 2024[^cite_note-10] with 58 operated by active forces ([2nd Bomb Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2nd_Bomb_Wing) and [5th Bomb Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/5th_Bomb_Wing)),[^cite_note-Barksdale_Fact_Sheet-11][^cite_note-Minot_Fact_Sheet-3] 18 by reserve forces ([307th Bomb Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/307th_Bomb_Wing)),[^cite_note-12] and about 12 in long-term storage at the [Davis-Monthan AFB Boneyard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/309th_Aerospace_Maintenance_and_Regeneration_Group).[^cite_note-AF_B52-4][^cite_note-13] The operational aircraft received upgrades between 2013 and 2015 and are expected to serve into the 2050s. The [B-52H](#Variants) is the only operational variant, scheduled to be upgraded as the B-52J by 2030.

On 23 November 1945, [Air Materiel Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Materiel_Command) (AMC) issued desired performance characteristics for a new strategic bomber "capable of carrying out the strategic mission without dependence upon advanced and intermediate bases controlled by other countries".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988206–207-15] The aircraft was to have a crew of five or more [turret gunners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_gun_turret), and a six-man relief crew. It was required to [cruise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cruise_\(aeronautics\)) at 300 miles per hour (260 kn; 480 km/h) at 34,000 feet (10,000 m) with a combat radius of 5,000 miles (4,300 nmi; 8,000 km). The armament was to consist of an unspecified number of 20 mm cannons and 10,000 pounds (4,500 kg) of bombs.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988207-16] On 13 February 1946, the [USAAF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USAAF) issued bid invitations for these specifications, with Boeing, [Consolidated Aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consolidated_Aircraft), and [Glenn L. Martin Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glenn_L._Martin_Company) submitting proposals.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988207-16]

On 5 June 1946, Boeing's Model 462, a straight-wing aircraft powered by six [Wright T35](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_J37) turboprops with a gross weight of 360,000 pounds (160,000 kg) and a combat radius of 3,110 miles (2,700 nmi; 5,010 km), was declared the winner.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988207–208-17] On 28 June 1946, Boeing was issued a letter of contract for US\$1.7 million to build a full-scale [mockup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mockup) of the new XB-52 and do preliminary engineering and testing.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200419-18] However, by October 1946, the USAAF was beginning to express concern about the sheer size of the new aircraft and its inability to meet the specified design requirements.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200421-19] In response, Boeing produced the Model 464, a smaller four-engine version with a 230,000-pound (100,000 kg) gross weight, which was briefly deemed acceptable.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200421Knaack1988208-20]

Subsequently, in November 1946, the Deputy Chief of Air Staff for Research and Development, General [Curtis LeMay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtis_LeMay), expressed the desire for a cruising speed of 400 miles per hour (350 kn; 640 km/h), to which Boeing responded with a 300,000-pound (140,000 kg) aircraft.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200422-21] In December 1946, Boeing was asked to change its design to a four-engine bomber with a top speed of 400 miles per hour (350 kn; 640 km/h), range of 12,000 miles (10,000 nmi; 19,000 km), and the ability to carry a [nuclear weapon](21785.md); in total, the aircraft could weigh up to 480,000 pounds (220,000 kg).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200423-22] Boeing responded with two models powered by T35 turboprops. The Model 464-16 was a "nuclear only" bomber with a 10,000-pound (4,500 kg) payload, while the Model 464-17 was a general purpose bomber with a 9,000-pound (4,100 kg) payload.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200423-22] Due to the cost associated with purchasing two specialized aircraft, the USAAF selected Model 464–17 with the understanding that it could be adapted for nuclear strikes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988209-23]

In June 1947, the military requirements were updated and the Model 464-17 met all of them except for the range.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200430-24] It was becoming obvious to the USAAF that, even with the updated performance, the XB-52 would be obsolete by the time it entered production and would offer little improvement over the [Convair B-36 Peacemaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convair_B-36_Peacemaker); as a result, the entire project was postponed for six months.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200434-25] During this time, Boeing continued to perfect the design, which resulted in the Model 464–29 with a top speed of 455 miles per hour (395 kn; 732 km/h) and a 5,000-mile (8,000 km) range.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988210-26] In September 1947, the Heavy Bombardment Committee was convened to ascertain performance requirements for a nuclear bomber. Formalized on 8 December 1947, these requirements called for a top speed of 500 miles per hour (430 kn; 800 km/h) and an 8,000-mile (7,000 nmi; 13,000 km) range, far beyond the capabilities of the 464–29.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200430Knaack1988210–211-27]

The outright cancellation of the Boeing contract on 11 December 1947 was staved off by a plea from its president [William McPherson Allen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_McPherson_Allen) to the Secretary of the Air Force [Stuart Symington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_Symington).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988212-28] Allen reasoned that the design was capable of being adapted to new aviation technology and more stringent requirements.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200435–36-29] In January 1948, Boeing was instructed to thoroughly explore recent technological innovations, including [aerial refueling](237949.md) and the [flying wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flying_wing).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200436–39-30] Noting stability and control problems [Northrop Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northrop_Corporation) was experiencing with its [YB-35](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northrop_YB-35) and [YB-49](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northrop_YB-49) flying wing bombers, Boeing insisted on a conventional aircraft, and in April 1948 presented a US\$30 million (US\$402 million today[^cite_note-inflation-US-31]) proposal for design, construction, and testing of two Model 464-35 prototypes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200440–44-32] Further revisions during 1948 resulted in an aircraft with a top speed of 513 miles per hour (446 kn; 826 km/h) at 35,000 feet (11,000 m), a range of 6,909 miles (6,004 nmi; 11,119 km), and a 280,000-pound (130,000 kg) gross weight, which included 10,000 pounds (4,500 kg) of bombs and 19,875 US gallons (75,240 L) of fuel.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988213Tagg200445–47-33]

### Design effort



XB-52 prototype on flight line ([X-4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northrop_X-4_Bantam) in foreground; [B-36](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convair_B-36_Peacemaker) behind). Note original tandem-seat "[bubble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bubble_canopy)" style canopy, similar to Boeing's earlier [B-47 Stratojet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_B-47_Stratojet).



Side view of YB-52 bomber, still fitted with a tandem cockpit, in common with other jet bombers in US service, such as the [B-45 Tornado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_B-45_Tornado), [B-47 Stratojet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_B-47_Stratojet) and [Martin B-57 Canberra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_B-57_Canberra)

In May 1948, Air Materiel Command asked Boeing to incorporate the previously discarded jet engine, with improvements in [fuel efficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_efficiency), into the design.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200444–45-34] That resulted in the development of yet another revision—in July 1948, Model 464-40 substituted [Westinghouse J40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westinghouse_J40) turbojets for the turboprops.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988214–215-35] The USAF project officer who reviewed the Model 464-40 was favorably impressed, especially since he had already been thinking along similar lines. Nevertheless, the government was concerned about the high fuel consumption rate of the jet engines of the day, and directed Boeing to use the turboprop-powered Model 464–35 as the basis for the XB-52. Although he agreed that turbojet propulsion was the future, General Howard A. Craig, Deputy Chief of Staff for Materiel, was not very enthusiastic about a jet-powered B-52, since he felt that the jet engine had not yet progressed sufficiently to permit skipping an intermediate turboprop stage. However, Boeing was encouraged to continue turbojet studies even without any expected commitment to jet propulsion.[^cite_note-36][^cite_note-37]

On Thursday, 21 October 1948, Boeing engineers [George S. Schairer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_S._Schairer), Art Carlsen, and Vaughn Blumenthal presented the design of a four-engine turboprop bomber to the chief of bomber development, Colonel [Pete Warden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Edward_Warden). Warden was disappointed by the projected aircraft and asked if the Boeing team could produce a proposal for a four-engine turbojet bomber. Joined by Ed Wells, Boeing's vice president of engineering, the engineers worked that night in [The Hotel Van Cleve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hotel_Van_Cleve) in Dayton, Ohio, redesigning Boeing's proposal as a four-engine turbojet bomber. On Friday, Colonel Warden looked over the information and asked for a better design. Returning to the hotel, the Boeing team was joined by Bob Withington and Maynard Pennell, two top Boeing engineers who were in town on other business.[^cite_note-38]

By late Friday night, they had laid out what was an essentially new airplane. The new design (464–49) built upon the basic layout of the [B-47 Stratojet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_B-47_Stratojet) with 35-degree [swept wings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swept_wing), eight engines paired in four underwing [pods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nacelle), and bicycle landing gear with wingtip outrigger wheels.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200448–50-39] A notable feature was the ability to pivot both fore and aft main landing gear up to 20° from the aircraft centerline to increase safety during [crosswind landings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crosswind_landing) (allowing the aircraft to "crab" or roll with a sideways slip angle down the runway).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200458–59-40] After a trip to a hobby shop for supplies, Schairer set to work building a model. The rest of the team focused on weight and performance data. Wells, who was also a skilled artist, completed the aircraft drawings. On Sunday, a stenographer was hired to type a clean copy of the proposal. On Monday, Schairer presented Colonel Warden with a neatly bound 33-page proposal and a 14-inch (36 cm) scale model.[^cite_note-B52org-41][^cite_note-42] The aircraft was projected to exceed all design specifications.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988215–216-43]



B-52H Stratofortress undergoing maintenance to its rudder with its fin folded

Although the full-size mock-up inspection in April 1949 was generally favorable, range again became a concern, since the J40s and early model J57s had excessive fuel consumption.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200457-44] Despite talk of another revision of specifications or even a full design competition among aircraft manufacturers, General LeMay, now in charge of Strategic Air Command, insisted that performance should not be compromised due to delays in engine development.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988217–218Tagg200460-45] In a final attempt to increase range, Boeing created the larger 464–67, stating that once in production, the range could be further increased in subsequent modifications.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988218-46] Following several direct interventions by LeMay,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988217–219-47] Boeing was awarded a production contract for thirteen B-52As and seventeen detachable reconnaissance pods on 14 February 1951.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988219-48] The last major design change—also at General LeMay's insistence—was a switch from the B-47 style tandem seating to a more conventional side-by-side cockpit, which increased the effectiveness of the copilot and reduced crew fatigue.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988221-49] Both XB-52 prototypes featured the original tandem seating arrangement with a framed bubble-type canopy (see above images).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECooke195624–28-50]

Tex Johnston noted, "The B-52, like the B-47, utilized a flexible wing. I saw the wingtip of the B-52 static test airplane travel 32 feet (9.8 m), from the negative 1-G load position to the positive 4-G load position." The flexible structure allowed "... the wing to flex during gust and maneuvering loads, thus relieving high-stress areas and providing a smoother ride." During a 3.5-G pullup, "The wingtips appeared about 35 degrees above level flight position."[^cite_note-51]

### Pre-production and production



First flight of the B-52 Stratofortress on 15 April 1952

During ground testing on 29 November 1951, the XB-52's pneumatic system failed during a full-pressure test; the resulting explosion severely damaged the trailing edge of the wing, necessitating considerable repairs. The YB-52, the second XB-52 modified with more operational equipment, [first flew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maiden_flight) on 15 April 1952 with ["Tex" Johnston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvin_M._Johnston) as the pilot.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDonald1997161–162-52][^cite_note-53] A 2-hour, 21-minute proving flight from [Boeing Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_Field), near [Seattle](11388236.md), Washington, to [Larson Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larson_Air_Force_Base) was undertaken with Boeing test pilot Johnston and USAF Lieutenant Colonel [Guy M. Townsend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guy_M._Townsend).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988222-54] The XB-52 followed on 2 October 1952.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200482-55] The thorough development,[^cite_note-57] including 670 days in the [wind tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_tunnel) and 130 days of [aerodynamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerodynamics) and [aeroelastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeroelasticity) testing, paid off with smooth flight testing. Encouraged, the USAF increased its order to 282 B-52s.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988229-58]

Only three of the thirteen B-52As ordered were built.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200485-67] All were returned to Boeing and used in their test program.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988230-59] On 9 June 1952, the February 1951 contract was updated to order the aircraft under new specifications. The final 10, the first aircraft to enter active service, were completed as B-52Bs.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988230-59] At the roll-out ceremony on 18 March 1954, Air Force Chief of Staff General [Nathan Twining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathan_Farragut_Twining) said:

> &#x20;The [long rifle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_rifle) was the great weapon of its day. ... today this B-52 is the long rifle of the air age.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988229–230-68]

The B-52B was followed by progressively improved bomber and reconnaissance variants, culminating in the B-52G and [turbofan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbofan) B-52H. To allow rapid delivery, production lines were set up both at its main Seattle factory and at Boeing's [Wichita](114932.md) facility. More than 5,000 companies were involved in the huge production effort, with 41% of the airframe being built by subcontractors.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGunston1957776-69] The prototypes and all B-52A, B and C models (90 aircraft)[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELake2003a117–121-70] were built at Seattle. Testing of aircraft built in Seattle caused problems due to jet noise, which led to the establishment of curfews for engine tests. Aircraft were ferried 150 miles (240 km) east on their maiden flights to [Larson Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larson_Air_Force_Base) near [Moses Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses_Lake,_Washington), where they were fully tested.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBowers1989379-71]

As production of the B-47 came to an end, the Wichita factory was phased in for B-52D production, with Seattle responsible for 101 D-models and Wichita 69.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELake2003b100–101-72] Both plants continued to build the B-52E, with 42 built at Seattle and 58 at Wichita,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELake2003b102-73] and the B-52F (44 from Seattle and 45 from Wichita).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELake2003b103-74] For the B-52G, Boeing decided in 1957 to transfer all production to Wichita, which freed up Seattle for other tasks, particularly the production of airliners.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGunston1957778Lake2003b101-75] Production ended in 1962 with the B-52H, with 742 aircraft built, plus the original two prototypes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEden200471-76]

### Upgrades

A proposed variant of the B-52H was the EB-52H, which would have consisted of 16 modified and augmented B-52H airframes with additional [electronic jamming](82272.md) capabilities.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200491Trimble2009-77][^cite_note-78] This variant would have restored USAF airborne jamming capability that it lost on retiring the [EF-111 Raven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EF-111_Raven). The program was canceled in 2005 following the removal of funds for the [stand-off jammer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stand-off_jammer). The program was revived in 2007 and cut again in early 2009.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETrimble2009-79]

In July 2013, the USAF began a fleet-wide technological upgrade of its B-52 bombers called Combat Network Communications Technology (CONECT) to modernize electronics, communications technology, computing, and avionics on the flight deck. CONECT upgrades include software and hardware such as new computer servers, modems, radios, data-links, receivers, and digital workstations for the crew. One update is the [AN/ARC-210](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AN/ARC-210) Warrior beyond-line-of-sight software programmable radio able to transmit voice, data, and information in-flight between B-52s and ground command and control centers, allowing the transmission and reception of data with updated intelligence, mapping, and targeting information; previous in-flight target changes required copying down coordinates. The ARC-210 allows machine-to-machine transfer of data, useful on long-endurance missions where targets may have moved before the arrival of the B-52. The aircraft will be able to receive information through [Link-16](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Link-16). CONECT upgrades will cost US\$1.1 billion overall and take several years. Funding has been secured for 30 B-52s; the USAF hopes for 10 CONECT upgrades per year, but the rate has yet to be decided.[^cite_note-CONECT-80][^cite_note-81]

Weapons upgrades include the 1760 Internal Weapons Bay Upgrade (IWBU), which gives a 66 percent increase in weapons payload using a digital interface ([MIL-STD-1760](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIL-STD-1760)) and rotary launcher. IWBU is expected to cost roughly US\$313 million.[^cite_note-CONECT-80] The 1760 IWBU will allow the B-52 to carry eight[^cite_note-82] [JDAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JDAM) 2,000-pound (910 kg) bombs, [AGM-158B JASSM-ER](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-158_JASSM#Improved_JASSM_versions) cruise missiles and [ADM-160C MALD-J](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ADM-160_MALD) decoy missiles internally. All 1760 IWBUs should be operational by October 2017. Two bombers will have the ability to carry 40 weapons in place of the 36 that three B-52s can carry.[^cite_note-83] The 1760 IWBU allows precision-guided missiles or bombs to be deployed from inside the weapons bay; the previous aircraft carried these munitions externally on the wing [hardpoints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardpoint). This increases the number of guided weapons ([Joint Direct Attack Munition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_Direct_Attack_Munition) or JDAM) a B-52 can carry and reduces the need for guided bombs to be carried on the wings. The first phase will allow a B-52 to carry twenty-four GBU-38 500-pound guided bombs or twenty GBU-31 2,000-pound bombs, with later phases accommodating the JASSM and MALD family of missiles.[^cite_note-84] In addition to carrying more smart bombs, moving them internally from the wings reduces drag and achieves a 15 percent reduction in fuel consumption.[^cite_note-85]

In 2017, the US Air Force Research Lab was investigating defensive laser weapons for the B-52.[^cite_note-86]

The US Air Force extended a 2020 solicitation called the **B-52 Commercial Engine Replacement Program**. Both General Electric and [Rolls-Royce North America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolls-Royce_North_America) submitted proposals; GE with [CF34-10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric_CF34) engines[^cite_note-87] and Rolls-Royce with the [Rolls-Royce F130](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolls-Royce_F130). In September 2021, the F130 was selected.[^cite_note-88] The F130 has 17,000 lbf (75.6 kN) thrust, similar to the existing B-52 engines ([Pratt & Whitney TF33](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_&_Whitney_JT3D)). The USAF intends to purchase 650 engines (608 direct replacements, 42 spares) for its fleet of 75 B-52H aircraft in a \$2.6 billion deal;[^cite_note-89] upgraded aircraft is slated to be redesignated B-52J.[^cite_note-Tirpak_2023-90]

In July 2026, a [US Government Accountability Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Government_Accountability_Office) (GAO) report said that the "Commercial Engine Replacement Program" was running \$3 billion over budget due to cost overruns and delays. The GAO criticised the Air Force for not using modern digital engineering methods to keep the program on track.[^cite_note-91][^cite_note-92]

The B-52 is due to receive a range of upgrades alongside a planned engine retrofit. These upgrades aim to modernize the sensors and displays of the B-52. They include the new variant of the [AN/APG-79](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AN/APG-79) active [electronically scanned array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_electronically_scanned_array) radar designated the [AN/APQ-188](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AN/APQ-188), replacing older mechanically scanned arrays, the streamlining of the nose, and the deletion of blisters housing the forward-looking infrared/electro-optical viewing system. In October 2022, Boeing released new images of what the upgrade would look like.[^cite_note-93][^cite_note-Air-Space_new_designation-94] The upgrades will also include improved communication systems, new pylons, new cockpit displays, and the deletion of one crew station. The changes will carry the designation B-52J.[^cite_note-Air-Space_new_designation-94][^cite_note-95] The B-52J is scheduled to reach Initial operational capability in 2033.[^cite_note-96]

## Design



[B-29 Superfortress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B-29_Superfortress) *[Doc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doc_\(aircraft\))*, [B-17 Flying Fortress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B-17_Flying_Fortress) *[Thunderbird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thunderbird_\(aircraft\))*, and a B-52 Stratofortress flying in formation at the 2017 [Barksdale Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barksdale_Air_Force_Base) Airshow

### Overview

The B-52 shared many technological similarities with the preceding B-47 Stratojet strategic bomber. The two aircraft used the same basic design, such as swept wings and podded jet engines,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETegler2000xiii-97] and the cabin included the crew ejection systems.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETegler200084–85-98] On the B-52D, the pilots and electronic countermeasures (ECM) operator ejected upwards, while the lower deck crew ejected downwards; until the B-52G, the gunner had to jettison the tail gun to [bail out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parachute).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHigham200543–44-99] The tail gunner in early model B-52s was located in the traditional location in the tail of the plane, with both visual and [radar gun laying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire-control_radar) systems; in later models, the gunner was moved to the front of the fuselage, with gun laying carried out by radar alone, much like the B-58 Hustler's tail gun system.[^cite_note-100]

[Structural fatigue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatigue_\(material\)) was accelerated by at least a factor of eight in a low-altitude flight profile over that of high-altitude flying, requiring costly repairs to extend service life. In the early 1960s, the three-phase *High Stress* program was launched to counter structural fatigue, enrolling aircraft at 2,000 flying hours.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200487Knaack1988254–255-101] Follow-up programs were conducted, such as a 2,000-hour service life extension to select airframes in 1966–1968, and the extensive *Pacer Plank* reskinning, completed in 1977.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELake2003b101Knaack1988259-102] The [wet wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wet_wing) introduced on G and H models was even more susceptible to fatigue, experiencing 60% more stress during a flight than the old wing. The wings were modified by 1964 under *ECP 1050*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988276–277-103] This was followed by a fuselage skin and [longeron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longeron) replacement (*ECP 1185*) in 1966, and the *B-52 Stability Augmentation and Flight Control* program (*ECP 1195*) in 1967.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988276–277-103] Fuel leaks due to deteriorating [Marman clamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marman_clamp) continued to plague all variants of the B-52. To this end, all aircraft variants were subjected to *Blue Band* (1957), *Hard Shell* (1958), and finally *QuickClip* (1958) programs. The latter fitted safety straps that prevented catastrophic loss of fuel in case of clamp failure.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988266–267-104] The B-52's service ceiling is officially listed as 50,000 feet (15,000 m), but operational experience shows this is difficult to reach when fully laden with bombs. According to one source: "The optimal altitude for a combat mission was around 43,000 feet (13,000 m), because to exceed that height would rapidly degrade the plane's range."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESorenson1995127-105]

Black-and-white photo of a B-52 in flight with its vertical stabilizer sheared off.

B-52H (AF Ser. No. 61-23), configured at the time as a [testbed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Testbed) to investigate structural failures, still flying after its [vertical stabilizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_stabilizer) sheared off in severe turbulence on 10 January 1964. The aircraft landed safely.

[^cite_note-106]

In September 2006, the B-52 became one of the first US military aircraft to fly using alternative fuel. It took off from [Edwards Air Force Base](107530.md) with a 50/50 blend of [Fischer–Tropsch process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fischer%E2%80%93Tropsch_process) (FT) synthetic fuel and conventional [JP-8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JP-8) jet fuel, which burned in two of the eight engines. On 15 December 2006, a B-52 took off from Edwards with the synthetic fuel powering all eight engines, the first time a USAF aircraft was entirely powered by the blend. The seven-hour flight was considered a success. This program is part of the Department of Defense [Assured Fuel Initiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assured_Fuel_Initiative), which aimed to reduce crude oil usage and obtain half of its aviation fuel from alternative sources by 2016.[^cite_note-107] On 8 August 2007, Air Force Secretary [Michael Wynne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Wynne) certified the B-52H as fully approved to use the FT blend.[^cite_note-108]

### Flight controls

Because of the B-52's mission parameters, only modest maneuvers would be required with no need for [spin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_\(aerodynamics\)) recovery. The aircraft has a relatively small, narrow [chord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chord_\(aeronautics\)) rudder, giving it limited [yaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_principal_axes) control authority. Originally an all-moving [vertical stabilizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_stabilizer) was to be used but was abandoned because of doubts about hydraulic actuator reliability.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAbzugLarrabee2002105–108-109] Because the aircraft has eight engines, [asymmetrical thrust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_engine) due to the loss of an engine in flight would be minimal and correctable with the narrow rudder. To assist with crosswind takeoffs and landings the main [landing gear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landing_gear) can be pivoted 20 degrees to either side from neutral.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJenkinsRogers199015-110] The crew would preset the yaw adjustable crosswind landing gear according to wind observations made on the ground.

Like the rudder, the [elevator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevator_\(aeronautics\)) is also very narrow chord and the B-52 suffers from limited elevator control authority. For long-term pitch trim and airspeed changes the aircraft uses a [stabilator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stabilator) (or all-moving tail) with the elevator used for small adjustments within a stabilizer setting. The stabilizer is adjustable through 13 degrees of movement (nine up, four down) and is crucial to operations during takeoff and landing due to large pitch changes induced by [flap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flap_\(aeronautics\)) application.[^cite_note-aer-111]

B-52s prior to the G models had very small [ailerons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ailerons) with a short span that was approximately equal to their chord. These "feeler ailerons" were used to provide feedback forces to the pilot's control yoke and to fine-tune the roll axes during delicate maneuvers such as aerial refueling. Due to twisting of the thin main wing, conventional outboard flap-type ailerons would lose authority and therefore could not be used. In other words, aileron activation would cause the wing to twist, undermining roll control. Six [spoilerons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoileron) on each wing are responsible for the majority of roll control. The late B-52G models eliminated the ailerons altogether and added an extra spoileron to each wing.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAbzugLarrabee2002105–108-109] Partly because of the lack of ailerons, the B-52G and H models were more susceptible to [Dutch roll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_roll).[^cite_note-aer-111]

### Avionics

Lower deck of a B-52, with instruments and displays featuring dominantly on the aircraft's side wall. Two crew members man this station.

A view of the lower deck of the B-52, dubbed the battle station

Ongoing problems with avionics systems were addressed in the *Jolly Well* program, completed in 1964, which improved components of the AN/ASQ-38 bombing navigational computer and the terrain computer. The MADREC (Malfunction Detection and Recording) upgrade fitted to most aircraft by 1965 could detect failures in avionics and weapons computer systems and was essential in monitoring the [AGM-28 Hound Dog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-28_Hound_Dog) missiles. The [electronic countermeasures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_countermeasure) capability of the B-52 was expanded with *Rivet Rambler* (1971) and *Rivet Ace* (1973).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988279–280-112]

To improve operations at low altitudes, the AN/ASQ-151 Electro-Optical Viewing System (EVS), which consisted of an AN/AVQ-22 [low light level television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_light_level_television) (LLLTV) and an AN/AAQ-6 [forward looking infrared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forward_looking_infrared) (FLIR)[^cite_note-RivetAce-113] system mounted in blisters under the noses of B-52Gs and Hs between 1972 and 1976.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWillis2005b41–43-114] The navigational capabilities of the B-52 were later augmented with the addition of [GPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPS) in the 1980s.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECondor199438-115] The [IBM AP-101](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_AP-101), also used on the [Rockwell B-1 Lancer](4952.md) bomber and the [Space Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle), was the B-52's main computer.[^cite_note-ap101-116]

In 2007, the [LITENING targeting pod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LITENING_targeting_pod) was fitted, which increased the effectiveness of the aircraft in the attack of ground targets with a variety of standoff weapons, using [laser guidance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_guidance), a high-resolution forward-looking infrared sensor ([FLIR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FLIR)), and a [CCD camera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge-coupled_device) used to obtain target imagery.[^cite_note-117] LITENING pods have been fitted to a wide variety of other US aircraft, such as the [McDonnell Douglas F/A-18 Hornet](45998.md), the [General Dynamics F-16 Fighting Falcon](11642.md) and the [McDonnell Douglas AV-8B Harrier II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McDonnell_Douglas_AV-8B_Harrier_II).[^cite_note-118]

### Armament



A B-52D with [anti-flash white](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-flash_white) on the underside

The ability to carry up to 20 [AGM-69 SRAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-69_SRAM) nuclear missiles was added to G and H models, starting in 1971.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988277–278-119] To further improve its offensive ability, air-launched cruise missiles (ALCMs) were fitted.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200489-120] After testing of both the USAF-backed [Boeing AGM-86 Air Launched Cruise Missile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-86_ALCM) and the Navy-backed [General Dynamics AGM-109 Tomahawk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BGM-109_Tomahawk), the AGM-86B was selected for operation by the B-52 (and ultimately by the B-1 Lancer).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPolmar2005529-121] A total of 194 B-52Gs and Hs were modified to carry AGM-86s, carrying 12 missiles on underwing pylons, with 82 B-52Hs further modified to carry another eight missiles on a rotary launcher fitted in the bomb bay. To conform with [SALT II Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_Arms_Limitation_Talks) requirements that cruise missile-capable aircraft be readily identifiable by reconnaissance satellites, the cruise missile-armed B-52Gs were modified with a distinctive [wing root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wing_root) fairing. As all B-52Hs were assumed modified, no visual modification of these aircraft was required.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWillis2005b44–45-122] In 1990, the stealthy [AGM-129 ACM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-129_ACM) cruise missile entered service; although intended to replace the AGM-86, the high cost and the Cold War's end led to only 450 being produced; unlike the AGM-86, no conventional, non-nuclear version was built.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDorrRogers199665–66-123] The B-52 was to have been modified to utilize [Northrop Grumman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northrop_Grumman)'s [AGM-137 TSSAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-137_TSSAM) weapon; however, the missile was canceled due to development costs.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPolmar2005532-124]



B-52H bomb bay: [AGM-69 SRAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-69_SRAM) missiles (front, white) and [B28 nuclear bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B28_nuclear_bomb) (background, gray), as a downloading takes place during Exercise GLOBAL SHIELD '84

Those B-52Gs not converted as cruise missile carriers underwent a series of modifications to improve conventional bombing. They were fitted with a new Integrated Conventional Stores Management System (ICSMS) and new underwing pylons that could hold larger bombs or other stores than the external pylons could. Thirty B-52Gs were further modified to carry up to 12 [AGM-84 Harpoon anti-ship missiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-84_Harpoon) each, while 12 B-52Gs were fitted to carry the [AGM-142 Have Nap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-142_Have_Nap) stand-off air-to-ground missile.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELake2003b108–109-125] When the B-52G was retired in 1994, an urgent scheme was launched to restore an interim Harpoon and Have Nap capability,[^cite_note-127] the four aircraft being modified to carry Harpoon and four to carry Have Nap under the *Rapid Eight* program.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELake2003b114-128]



Members of the Global Strike Challenge nuclear/conventional load crew team lift a GBU-31 Joint Direct Attack Munition into position during a dry run leading up to the 2012 Air Force Global Strike Command Challenge on Barksdale Air Force Base, Louisiana, 20 Aug 2012

The Conventional Enhancement Modification (CEM) program gave the B-52H a more comprehensive conventional weapons capability, adding the modified underwing weapon pylons used by conventional-armed B-52Gs, Harpoon and Have Nap, and the capability to carry new-generation weapons including the [Joint Direct Attack Munition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_Direct_Attack_Munition) (JDAM) and [Wind Corrected Munitions Dispenser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_Corrected_Munitions_Dispenser) guided bombs, the [AGM-154](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-154_Joint_Standoff_Weapon) glide bomb and the [AGM-158 JASSM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-158_JASSM) missile. The CEM program also introduced new radios, integrated [Global Positioning System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Positioning_System) into the aircraft's navigation system, and replaced the under-nose FLIR with a more modern unit. Forty-seven B-52Hs were modified under the CEM program by 1996, with 19 more by the end of 1999.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELake2001290–291DorrRogers199681–82-129]

By around 2010, [US Strategic Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Strategic_Command) stopped assigning [B61](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B61_nuclear_bomb) and [B83](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B83_nuclear_bomb) nuclear gravity bombs to B-52, and later listed only the [B-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northrop_Grumman_B-2_Spirit) as tasked with delivering strategic nuclear bombs in budget requests. Nuclear gravity bombs were removed from the B-52's capabilities because it is no longer considered survivable enough to penetrate modern air defenses, instead relying on nuclear cruise missiles and focusing on expanding its conventional strike role.[^cite_note-130] The 2019 "Safety Rules for U.S. Strategic Bomber Aircraft" manual subsequently confirmed the removal of B61-7 and B83-1 gravity bombs from the B-52H's approved weapons configuration.[^cite_note-B61/B83_removal-131]

Starting in 2016, Boeing is to upgrade the internal rotary launchers to the [MIL-STD-1760](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIL-STD-1760) interface to enable the internal carriage of smart bombs, which previously could be carried only on the wings.[^cite_note-132]

While the B-1 Lancer has a larger theoretical maximum payload of 75,000 pounds (34,000 kg) compared to the B-52's 70,000 pounds (32,000 kg), the bombers are rarely able to carry their full loads. The most the B-52 carries is a full load of AGM-86Bs totaling 62,660 pounds (28,420 kg). The B-1 has the internal weapons bay space to carry more GBU-31 JDAMs and JASSMs, but the B-52 upgraded with the conventional rotary launcher can carry more of other JDAM variants.[^cite_note-133]

The [AGM-183A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-183A) Air-Launched Rapid Response (ARRW) [hypersonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypersonic) missile and the future [Long Range Stand Off](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Range_Stand_Off_Weapon) (LRSO) [nuclear](21785.md)-armed [air-launched cruise missile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air-launched_cruise_missile) will join the B-52 inventory in the future.[^cite_note-134]

### Engines



USAF B-52H Stratofortress TF33 turbofan engines

The eight engines of the B-52 are paired in pods and suspended by four pylons beneath and forward of the wings' [leading edge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leading_edge). The careful arrangement of the pylons also allowed them to work as [wing fences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wing_fence) and delay the onset of [stall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stall_\(fluid_dynamics\)). The first two prototypes, XB-52 and YB-52, were both powered by experimental [Pratt & Whitney YJ57-P-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_&_Whitney_J57) turbojet engines with 8,700 pounds-force (39 kN) of static thrust each.[^cite_note-aer-111]

The B-52A models were equipped with Pratt & Whitney J57-P-1W turbojets, providing a dry thrust of 10,000 pounds-force (44 kN) which could be increased for short periods to 11,000 pounds-force (49 kN) with [water injection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_injection_\(engine\)). The water was carried in a 360-US-gallon (300 imp gal; 1,400 L) tank in the rear fuselage.[^cite_note-135]

B-52B, C, D and E models were equipped with Pratt & Whitney J57-P-29W, J57-P-29WA, or J57-P-19W series engines, all rated at 10,500 lbf (47 kN). The B-52F and G models were powered by Pratt & Whitney J57-P-43WB turbojets, each rated at 13,750 pounds-force (61.2 kN) static thrust with water injection.

On 9 May 1961, the B-52H began to be delivered to the USAF with cleaner-burning and quieter [Pratt & Whitney TF33-P-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_&_Whitney_JT3D) turbofans with a maximum thrust of 17,100 pounds-force (76 kN).[^cite_note-aer-111]

#### Engine retrofit

In a study for the USAF in the mid-1970s, Boeing investigated replacing the engines, changing to a new wing, and other improvements to upgrade B-52G/H aircraft as an alternative to the B-1A, then in development.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJenkins199939-136]

In 1996, Rolls-Royce and Boeing jointly proposed fitting each B-52 with four leased [Rolls-Royce RB211](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolls-Royce_RB211) engines. This would have involved replacing the eight Pratt & Whitney TF33 engines (total thrust 136,000 lbf (600 kN)) with four RB211-535E4 engines (total thrust 172,400 lbf (767 kN)), which would increase range and reduce fuel consumption.[^cite_note-137][^cite_note-FOOTNOTELake2003b115-138] However, a USAF analysis in 1997 concluded that Boeing's estimated savings of US\$4.7 billion would not be realized and that reengining would instead cost US\$1.3 billion more than keeping the existing engines, citing significant up-front procurement and re-tooling expenditure.[^cite_note-NSIAD-97-210BR-139]

The USAF's 1997 rejection of reengining was subsequently disputed in a [Defense Science Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defense_Science_Board) (DSB) report in 2003. The DSB urged the USAF to re-engine the aircraft without delay,[^cite_note-140] saying doing so would not only create significant cost savings but reduce [greenhouse gas emissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_of_aviation) and increase aircraft range and endurance; these conclusions were in line with the conclusions of a separate Congress-funded study conducted in 2003. Criticizing the USAF cost analysis, the DSB found that among other things, the USAF failed to account for the cost of aerial refueling; the DSB estimated that aerial refueling cost \$17.50 per US gallon (\$4.62/L), whereas the USAF had failed to account for the cost of delivering the fuel and so had only priced fuel at \$1.20 per US gallon (\$0.32/L).[^cite_note-141]

On 23 April 2020, the USAF released its request for proposals for 608 commercial engines plus spares and support equipment, with the plan to award the contract in May 2021.[^cite_note-fgRFP-142] This Commercial Engine Reengining Program (CERP) saw General Electric propose its [CF34-10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric_CF34) and [Passport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric_Passport) turbofans, Pratt & Whitney its [PW800](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_&_Whitney_Canada_PW800), and Rolls-Royce its BR725 to be designated [F130](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolls-Royce_BR700#BR725).[^cite_note-fgRFP-142] On 24 September 2021, the USAF selected the Rolls-Royce F130 as the winner and announced plans to purchase 650 engines (608 direct replacements and 42 spares), for US\$2.6 billion.[^cite_note-143][^cite_note-Tirpak_2023-90]

Unlike the previous re-engine proposal, which also involved reducing the number of engines from eight to four, the F130 re-engine program maintains eight engines on the B-52. Although four-engine operation would be more efficient, retrofitting the airframe to operate with only four engines would involve additional changes to the aircraft's systems and control surfaces (particularly the rudder), thereby increasing the time, cost, and complexity of the project.[^cite_note-Tirpak_2019-144] B-52Hs upgraded with Rolls Royce F130 engines will be redesignated as "B-52Js".[^cite_note-Tirpak_2023-90]

## Operational history

### Introduction

Although the B-52A was the first production variant, these aircraft were used only in testing. The first operational version was the B-52B, which had been developed in parallel with the prototypes since 1951. First flying in December 1954, B-52B, AF Serial Number 52-8711, entered operational service with [93rd Heavy Bombardment Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/93rd_Heavy_Bombardment_Wing) (93rd BW) at [Castle Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castle_Air_Force_Base), California, on 29 June 1955. The wing became operational on 12 March 1956. The training for B-52 crews consisted of five weeks of ground school and four weeks of flying, accumulating 35 to 50 hours in the air. The new B-52Bs replaced operational B-36s on a one-to-one basis.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988234–237-146]

Early operations were problematic;[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoyne2001216-147] in addition to supply problems, there were also technical issues.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988237-148] Ramps and taxiways deteriorated under the aircraft's weight, the fuel system was prone to leaks and icing,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoyne2001220-149] and bombing and fire control computers were unreliable.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988237-148] The split-level cockpit presented a temperature control problem – the pilots' cockpit was heated by sunlight while the observer and the navigator on the bottom deck sat on the ice-cold floor. Thus, a comfortable temperature setting for the pilots caused the other crew members to freeze, while a comfortable temperature for the bottom crew caused the pilots to overheat.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988238-150] The J57 engines proved unreliable. [Alternator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternator) failure caused the first fatal B-52 crash in February 1956;[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELake2003a119-151] as a result, the fleet was briefly grounded. In July, fuel and hydraulic issues grounded the B-52s again. In response to maintenance issues, the USAF set up "Sky Speed" teams of 50 contractors at each B-52 base to perform maintenance and routine checkups, taking an average of one week per aircraft.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988240-152]

Black-and-white photo of three B-52s parked close together facing left, as personnel on the ground prepare them for departure

Three B-52Bs of the 93rd Bomb Wing prepare to depart [March Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_Air_Force_Base) for Castle Air Force Base, California, after their record-setting round-the-world flight in 1957

On 21 May 1956, a B-52B (52–13) dropped a [Mk-15 nuclear bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_15_nuclear_bomb) over the [Bikini Atoll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bikini_Atoll) in a test code-named [Cherokee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Redwing). It was the first air-dropped [thermonuclear weapon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermonuclear_weapon).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988243-153] This aircraft now is on display at the [National Museum of Nuclear Science and History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Nuclear_Science_and_History) in [Albuquerque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albuquerque), [New Mexico](21649.md). From 24 to 25 November 1956, four B-52Bs of the 93rd BW and four B-52Cs of the 42nd BW flew nonstop around the perimeter of North America in *[Operation Quick Kick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Quick_Kick_\(1956\)?action=edit\&redlink=1)*, which covered 15,530 miles (13,500 nmi; 24,990 km) in 31 hours, 30 minutes. SAC noted the flight time could have been reduced by 5 to 6 hours had the four inflight refuelings been done by fast jet-powered tanker aircraft rather than propeller-driven [Boeing KC-97 Stratofreighters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_KC-97_Stratofreighter).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988244-154] In a demonstration of the B-52's global reach, from 16 to 18 January 1957, three B-52Bs made a [non-stop flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-stop_flight) around the world during *[Operation Power Flite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Power_Flite)*, during which 24,325 miles (21,138 nmi; 39,147 km) was covered in 45 hours 19 minutes (536.8 mph or 863.9 km/h) with several in-flight refuelings by KC-97s.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECondor199442-155][^cite_note-156]

The B-52 set many records over the next few years. On 26 September 1958, a B-52D set a world speed record of 560.705 miles per hour (487.239 kn; 902.367 km/h) over a 10,000 kilometers (6,200 miles; 5,400 nautical miles) closed circuit without a payload. The same day, another B-52D established a world speed record of 597.675 miles per hour (519.365 kn; 961.865 km/h) over a 5,000 kilometers (3,100 miles; 2,700 nautical miles) closed circuit without a payload.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988259-157] On 14 December 1960, a B-52G set a world distance record by flying unrefueled for 10,078.84 miles (8,758.27 nmi; 16,220.32 km); the flight lasted 19 hours 44 minutes (510.75 mph or 821.97 km/h).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988282-158] From 10 to 11 January 1962, a B-52H (60–40) set a world distance record by flying unrefueled, surpassing the prior B-52 record set two years earlier, from [Kadena Air Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kadena_Air_Base), [Okinawa Prefecture](22477.md), Japan, to [Torrejón Air Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torrej%C3%B3n_Air_Base), Spain, which covered 12,532.28 miles (10,890.25 nmi; 20,168.75 km).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988289-66][^cite_note-159] The flight passed over Seattle, Fort Worth and the Azores.

### Cold War

Main article: [Cold War](325329.md)

Diagram of the route that nuclear bomb-carrying B-52s would take to enemy countries. It follows the Mediterranean Sea, and passes over Italy before turning north over the Adriatic Sea.

Southerly route of the [Operation Chrome Dome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Chrome_Dome) airborne nuclear alert

When the B-52 entered service, the [Strategic Air Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_Air_Command) (SAC) intended to use it to deter and counteract the vast and modernizing [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union)'s military. As the Soviet Union increased its nuclear capabilities, destroying or "countering" the forces that would deliver nuclear strikes (bombers, missiles, etc.) became of great strategic importance.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETillman2007100-160] The [Eisenhower administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eisenhower_administration) endorsed this switch in focus; the President in 1954 expressed a preference for military targets over civilian ones, a principle reinforced in the [Single Integrated Operation Plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_Integrated_Operation_Plan) (SIOP), a plan of action in the case of nuclear war breaking out.[^cite_note-161]

Throughout the [Cold War](325329.md), B-52s and other US strategic bombers performed airborne alert patrols under code names such as [Head Start](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Head_Start), [Chrome Dome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Chrome_Dome), [Hard Head](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thule_Monitor_Mission), [Round Robin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Round_Robin) and [Giant Lance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Giant_Lance). Bombers [loitered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loiter_\(flight\)) at high altitudes near the borders of the Soviet Union to provide rapid first strike or retaliation capability in case of nuclear war.[^cite_note-162] These airborne patrols formed one component of the US's nuclear deterrent, which would act to prevent the breakout of a large-scale war between the US and the Soviet Union under the concept of [Mutually Assured Destruction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutually_Assured_Destruction).[^cite_note-163]

Due to the late 1950s-era threat of [surface-to-air missiles](182664.md) (SAMs) that could threaten high-altitude aircraft,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJenkins199921Spick19864–5-164] seen in practice in the [1960 U-2 incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1960_U-2_incident),[^cite_note-165] the intended use of B-52 was changed to serve as a [low-level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nap-of-the-earth) penetration bomber during a foreseen attack upon the Soviet Union, as [terrain masking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrain_mask#Terrain_masking) provided an effective method of avoiding radar and thus the threat of the SAMs.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESpick19866–8-166] The aircraft was planned to fly towards the target at 400–440 mph (640–710 km/h) and deliver their weapons from 400 ft (120 m) or lower.[^cite_note-167] Although never intended for the low-level role, the B-52's flexibility allowed it to outlast several intended successors as the nature of aerial warfare changed. The B-52's large airframe enabled the addition of multiple design improvements, new equipment, and other adaptations over its service life.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988279–180-168]

1965 United States military nuclear weapons safety handling and aerial delivery training film.

In November 1959, to improve the aircraft's combat capabilities in the changing strategic environment, SAC initiated the *Big Four* modification program (also known as *Modification 1000*) for all operational B-52s except early B models.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200487Spick19866–8-169] The program was completed by 1963.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988252–254-170] The four modifications were the ability to launch AGM-28 Hound Dog standoff nuclear missiles and [ADM-20 Quail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ADM-20_Quail) decoys, an advanced electronic countermeasures (ECM) suite, and upgrades to perform the all-weather, low-altitude (below 500 feet or 150 m) interdiction mission in the face of advancing Soviet missile-based air defenses.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988252–254-170]

In the 1960s, there were concerns over the fleet's capable lifespan. Several projects beyond the B-52, the [Convair B-58 Hustler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convair_B-58_Hustler) and [North American XB-70 Valkyrie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_XB-70_Valkyrie), had either been aborted or proved disappointing in light of changing requirements, which left the older B-52 as the main bomber as opposed to the planned successive aircraft models.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMiller198569–70Greenwood1995289-171] On 19 February 1965, General Curtis E. LeMay testified to Congress that the lack of a follow-up bomber project to the B-52 raised the danger that, "The B-52 is going to fall apart on us before we can get a replacement for it."[^cite_note-172] Other aircraft, such as the [General Dynamics F-111 Aardvark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Dynamics_F-111_Aardvark), later complemented the B-52 in roles the aircraft was not as capable in, such as missions involving high-speed, low-level penetration dashes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchwartz1998119-173]

### Vietnam War

See also: [Vietnam War](32611.md)



Soviet specialists inspect the wreckage of the B-52 Stratofortress shot down near Hanoi on 23 December 1972.

With the escalating situation in Southeast Asia, 28 B-52Fs were fitted with external racks for 24 of the 750 pounds (340 kg) bombs under project *South Bay* in June 1964; an additional 46 aircraft received similar modifications under project *Sun Bath*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELake2003b103-74] In March 1965, the United States commenced [Operation Rolling Thunder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Rolling_Thunder). The first combat mission, [Operation Arc Light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Arc_Light), was flown by B-52Fs on 18 June 1965, when 30 bombers of the 9th and 441st Bombardment Squadrons struck a communist stronghold near the [Bến Cát District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%E1%BA%BFn_C%C3%A1t_District) in South Vietnam. The first wave of bombers arrived too early at a designated rendezvous point, and while maneuvering to maintain station, two B-52s collided, which resulted in the loss of both bombers and eight crewmen. The remaining bombers, minus one more that turned back due to mechanical problems, continued towards the target.[^cite_note-174] Twenty-seven Stratofortresses bombed a one-by-two-mile (1.6 by 3.2 km) target box from between 19,000 and 22,000 feet (5,800 and 6,700 m), with a little more than 50% of the bombs falling within the target zone.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHobson200122–23-175] The force returned to [Andersen Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andersen_Air_Force_Base) except for one bomber with electrical problems that recovered to [Clark Air Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clark_Air_Base), the mission having lasted 13 hours. Post-strike assessment by teams of South Vietnamese troops with American advisors found evidence that the Viet Cong had departed from the area before the raid, and it was suspected that infiltration of the south's forces may have tipped off the north because of the [South Vietnamese Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Army_of_the_Republic_of_Vietnam) troops involved in the post-strike inspection.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchlight198852-176]

Against a blue sky with white clouds, a B-52F releases bombs over Vietnam

B-52F dropping bombs on Vietnam

Beginning in late 1965, a number of B-52Ds underwent *Big Belly* modifications to increase bomb capacity for [carpet bombings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpet_bombing).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELake200430-177] While the external payload remained at 24 of 500 pounds (230 kg) or 750 pounds (340 kg) bombs, the internal capacity increased from 27 to 84 for 500 lb (230 kg) bombs, or from 27 to 42 for 750 lb (340 kg) bombs.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDickPatterson2006161-178] The modification created enough capacity for a total of 60,000 pounds (27,000 kg) using 108 bombs. Thus modified, B-52Ds could carry 22,000 pounds (10,000 kg) more than B-52Fs.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988256-179] Designed to replace B-52Fs, modified B-52Ds entered combat in April 1966 flying from [Andersen Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andersen_Air_Force_Base), [Guam](11974.md). Each bombing mission lasted 10 to 12 hours and included an aerial refueling by [KC-135 Stratotankers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KC-135_Stratotanker).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDonald1997161–162-52] In spring 1967, B-52s began flying from [U-Tapao Airfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U-Tapao_Royal_Thai_Navy_Airfield) in Thailand so that refueling was not required.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDickPatterson2006161-178]

B-52s were employed during the [Battle of Ia Drang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Ia_Drang) in November 1965, notable as the aircraft's first use in a tactical support role.[^cite_note-180]

> "The B-52s were restricted to bombing suspected Communist bases in relatively uninhabited sections because their potency approached that of a tactical nuclear weapon. A formation of six B-52s, dropping their bombs from 30,000 ft (9,100 m), could "take out"... almost everything within a "box" approximately five-eighths mile wide by two miles long (1km × 3.2 km). Whenever [Arc Light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Arc_Light) struck ... in the vicinity of Saigon, the city woke from the tremor..."

— [Neil Sheehan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neil_Sheehan), war correspondent, writing before the mass attacks on heavily populated cities including North Vietnam's capital

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECondor199437-181]

On 22 November 1972, a B-52D (55–110) from U-Tapao was hit by a SAM while on a raid over [Vinh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vinh). The crew was forced to abandon the damaged aircraft over Thailand. This was the first B-52 destroyed by hostile fire.[^cite_note-182][^cite_note-183]

The zenith of B-52 attacks in Vietnam was [Operation Linebacker II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Linebacker_II) (also known as the Christmas bombings), conducted from 18 to 29 December 1972, which consisted of waves of B-52s (mostly D models, but some Gs without jamming equipment and with a smaller bomb load). Over 12 days, B-52s flew 729 [sorties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sortie) and dropped 15,237 tons of bombs on [Hanoi](56667.md), [Haiphong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haiphong), and other targets in North Vietnam.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDickPatterson2006187Condor199438Budiansky2004394-184] Originally 42 B-52s were committed to the war; however, numbers were frequently twice this figure.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELake200432-185] During Operation Linebacker II, fifteen B-52s were shot down, five were heavily damaged (one crashed in Laos), and five suffered medium damage. A total of 25 crewmen were killed in these losses.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDorrPeacock2000-186] During the war, 31 B-52s were lost, including ten shot down over North Vietnam.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcCarthy2009172-187]

#### Air-to-air combat



Tail armament of a B-52D, the model used on both occasions that a B-52 scored an aerial victory. In later models, the tail gunner was moved from the conventional position to the forward crew compartment before being removed altogether.

During the [Vietnam War](32611.md), B-52D [tail gunners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tail_gunner) were credited with shooting down two [MiG-21 "Fishbeds"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikoyan-Gurevich_MiG-21). On 18 December 1972 tail gunner [Staff Sergeant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staff_Sergeant) Samuel O. Turner's B-52 had just completed a bomb run for Operation Linebacker II and was turning away when a [Vietnam People's Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_People's_Air_Force) (VPAF) MiG-21 approached.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcCarthy2009139-188] The MiG and the B-52 locked onto each other. When the fighter drew within range, Turner fired his quad (four guns on one mounting) [.50 (12.7 mm) caliber machine guns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/.50_caliber_machine_gun).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcCarthy200919-189] The MiG exploded aft of the bomber,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcCarthy2009139-188] as confirmed by [Master Sergeant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_Sergeant) Louis E. Le Blanc, the tail gunner in a nearby Stratofortress. Turner received a [Silver Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_Star) for his actions.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFutrellGreenhalghGrubbHasselwander1976-190] His B-52, tail number 56-676, is preserved on display with air-to-air kill markings at [Fairchild Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairchild_Air_Force_Base) in [Spokane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spokane), Washington.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcCarthy2009139-188]

On 24 December 1972, during the same bombing campaign, the B-52 *Diamond Lil* was headed to bomb the [railroad yards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_yard) of [Thái Nguyên](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Th%C3%A1i_Nguy%C3%AAn_province) when tail gunner [Airman First Class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airman_First_Class) Albert E. Moore spotted a fast-approaching MiG-21.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcCarthy2009141-191] Moore opened fire with his quad .50 caliber guns at 4,000 yd (3,700 m), and kept shooting until the fighter disappeared from his scope. [Technical Sergeant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_Sergeant) Clarence W. Chute, a tail gunner aboard another Stratofortress, watched the MiG catch fire and fall away;[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcCarthy200919-189] this was not confirmed by the VPAF.[^cite_note-Toperczer_#29_2001-192] *Diamond Lil* is preserved on display at the [United States Air Force Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Air_Force_Academy) in [Colorado](5399.md).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcCarthy2009141-191] Moore was the last [bomber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bomber) gunner believed to have shot down an enemy aircraft with machine guns in [aerial combat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerial_combat).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcCarthy200919-189]

The two B-52 tail gunner kills were not confirmed by VPAF, and they admitted to the loss of only three MiGs, all by F-4s.[^cite_note-Toperczer_#29_2001-192] Vietnamese sources have attributed a third air-to-air victory to a B-52, a MiG-21 shot down on 16 April 1972.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEToperczer1998-193] These victories make the B-52 the largest aircraft credited with air-to-air kills.[^cite_note-194] The last Arc Light mission without fighter escort took place on 15 August 1973, as US military action in Southeast Asia was wound down.[^cite_note-195]

### Post-Vietnam War service

B-52Bs reached the end of their structural service life by the mid-1960s and all were retired by June 1966, followed by the last of the B-52Cs on 29 September 1971; except for [NASA](18426568.md)'s B-52B "[008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balls_8)" which was eventually retired in 2004 at [Edwards Air Force Base](107530.md), California.[^cite_note-196] Another of the remaining B Models, "[52-005](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wings_Over_the_Rockies_Air_and_Space_Museum#B-52B_Stratofortress_s/n_52-005_\(previously_RB-52B\))" is on display at the [Wings Over the Rockies Air and Space Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wings_Over_the_Rockies_Air_and_Space_Museum) in [Denver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denver), Colorado.[^cite_note-197]

Aerial view of B-52 fly above white clouds and the sea. It carries two triangular-shaped vehicles under the wings between the fuselage and inboard engines.

B-52H modified to carry two [Lockheed D-21](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_D-21)B drones

A few time-expired E models were retired in 1967 and 1968, but the bulk (82) were retired between May 1969 and March 1970. Most F models were also retired between 1967 and 1973, but 23 survived as trainers until late 1978. The fleet of D models served much longer;[^cite_note-pfusflt-198] 80 D models were extensively overhauled under the *Pacer Plank* program during the mid-1970s.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHolder1978-199] Skinning on the lower wing and fuselage was replaced, and various structural components were renewed. The fleet of D models stayed largely intact until late 1978 when 37 not already upgraded Ds were retired.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWillis2005b39-200] The remainder were retired between 1982 and 1983.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWillis2005b41-201]

The remaining G and H models were used for nuclear standby ("alert") duty as part of the United States' [nuclear triad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_triad); the combination of nuclear-armed land-based missiles, submarine-based missiles, and manned bombers. The B-1, intended to supplant the B-52, replaced only the older models and the supersonic FB-111.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAnderton198598–99,_174-202] In 1991, B-52s ceased continuous 24-hour SAC alert duty.[^cite_note-203]

After Vietnam, the experience of operations in a hostile air defense environment was considered. Due to this, B-52s were modernized with new weapons, equipment, and both offensive and defensive avionics. This, and the use of low-level tactics, marked a major shift in the B-52's utility. The upgrades were:

- Supersonic short-range nuclear missiles: G and H models were modified to carry up to 20 SRAM missiles, replacing existing gravity bombs. Eight SRAMs were carried internally on a special rotary launcher and 12 SRAMs were mounted on two wing pylons. With SRAM, the B-52s could strike heavily defended targets without entering the terminal defenses.

- New countermeasures: Phase VI ECM modification, codenamed "Rivet Ace",

  [^cite_note-RivetAce-113]

  &#x20;was the sixth major ECM program for the B-52. It improved the aircraft's self-protection capability in the dense Soviet air defense environment. The new equipment expanded signal coverage, improved threat warnings, provided new countermeasures techniques, and increased the quantity of expendables. The power requirements of Phase VI ECM also consumed most of the excess electrical capacity on the B-52G.

- B-52G and Hs were also modified with an electro-optical viewing system (EVS) that made low-level operations and terrain avoidance much easier and safer. EVS system contained a low light level television (LLTV) camera and a forward-looking infrared (FLIR) camera to display information needed for penetration at lower altitudes.

- Subsonic-cruise unarmed decoy: SCUD resembled the B-52 on the radar. As an active decoy, it carried ECM and other devices, and it had a range of several hundred miles. Although SCUD was never deployed operationally, the concept was developed, becoming known as the air-launched cruise missile (ALCM-A).

These modifications increased weight by nearly 24,000 pounds (11,000 kg) and decreased operational range by 8–11%. This was considered acceptable for the increase in capabilities.[^cite_note-fasb52-204]

After the fall of the Soviet Union, all B-52Gs remaining in service were destroyed in accordance with the terms of the [Strategic Arms Reduction Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/START_I) (START). The [Aerospace Maintenance and Regeneration Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/309th_Aerospace_Maintenance_and_Regeneration_Group) (AMRC) cut the 365 B-52s into pieces. Russia verified the completion destruction task via satellite and first-person inspection at the AMARC facility.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWillis2005b51–52-205]

### Gulf War and later

See also: [Gulf War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_War)

Aerial view of B-52s and other aircraft slowly being scrapped in the desert.

Retired B-52s are stored at the [309th AMARG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/309th_Aerospace_Maintenance_and_Regeneration_Group) (formerly AMARC), a desert storage facility often called the "Boneyard" at [Davis–Monthan Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davis%E2%80%93Monthan_Air_Force_Base), near [Tucson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tucson,_Arizona), Arizona.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEArana-Barradas2001-206]

B-52 strikes were an important part of [Operation Desert Storm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Desert_Storm). Starting on 16 January 1991, a flight of B-52Gs flew from [Barksdale Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barksdale_Air_Force_Base), Louisiana, refueled in the air en route, struck targets in Iraq, and returned home –  a journey of 35 hours and 14,000 miles (23,000 km) round trip. It set a record for the longest-distance combat mission, breaking the record previously held by an RAF [Vulcan bomber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avro_Vulcan) in 1982; however, this was achieved using forward refueling.[^cite_note-Barksdale_Fact_Sheet-11][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWillis2005b50-207] Those seven B-52s flew the first combat sorties of Operation Desert Storm, firing 35 [AGM-86C CALCM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-86) standoff missiles and successfully destroying 85–95 percent of their targets.[^cite_note-208] B-52Gs operating from the [King Abdullah Air Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Abdulaziz_International_Airport) at Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, [RAF Fairford](475013.md) in the United Kingdom, [Morón Air Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mor%C3%B3n_Air_Base), Spain, and the island of [Diego Garcia](8630.md) in the [British Indian Ocean Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Indian_Ocean_Territory) flew bombing missions over Iraq, initially at low altitude. After the first three nights, the B-52s moved to high-altitude missions instead, which reduced their effectiveness and psychological impact compared to the low-altitude role initially played.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECordesmanWagner1996451-209]

The conventional strikes were carried out by three bombers, which dropped up to 153 of the 750 lb (340 kg) [M117 bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M117_bomb) over an area of 1.5 by 1 mi (2.4 by 1.6 km). The bombings demoralized the defending Iraqi troops, many of whom surrendered in the wake of the strikes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDickPatterson2006225-210] In 1999, the science and technology magazine *[Popular Mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_Mechanics)* described the B-52's role in the conflict: "The Buff's value was made clear during the Gulf War and Desert Fox. The B-52 turned out the lights in Baghdad."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGarvey1999-211] During Operation Desert Storm, B-52s flew about 1,620 sorties and delivered 40% of the weapons dropped by coalition forces.[^cite_note-AF_B52-4]

During the conflict, several claims of Iraqi air-to-air successes were made, including an Iraqi pilot, Khudai Hijab, who allegedly fired a [Vympel R-27](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vympel_R-27)R missile from his [MiG-29](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MiG-29) and damaged a B-52G on the opening night of the Gulf War.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELake200448-212] However, the USAF disputes this claim, stating the bomber was actually hit by friendly fire, an [AGM-88](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-88_HARM) High-speed, Anti-Radiation Missile (HARM) that homed on the fire-control radar of the B-52's tail gun; the jet was subsequently nicknamed *In HARM's Way*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELake200447–48-213] Shortly following this incident, General [George Lee Butler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Lee_Butler) announced that the gunner position on B-52 crews would be eliminated, and the gun turrets permanently deactivated, commencing on 1 October 1991.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECondor199444-214]

Since the mid-1990s, the B-52H has been the only variant remaining in military service;[^cite_note-215] it is currently stationed at:

- [Minot Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minot_Air_Force_Base), [North Dakota](21651.md) –  [5th Bomb Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/5th_Bomb_Wing)

- [Barksdale Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barksdale_Air_Force_Base), [Louisiana](18130.md) –  [2nd Bomb Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2nd_Bomb_Wing) (active Air Force) and [307th Bomb Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/307th_Bomb_Wing) ([Air Force Reserve Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Force_Reserve_Command))

- One B-52H is assigned to [Edwards Air Force Base](107530.md) and is used by [Air Force Materiel Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Force_Materiel_Command) at the USAF Flight Test Center.

- One additional B-52H has been used by NASA at Dryden Flight Research Center (now [Armstrong Flight Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armstrong_Flight_Research_Center)), California as part of the Heavy-lift Airborne Launch program.

  [^cite_note-216]

From 2 to 3 September 1996, two B-52Hs conducted a mission as part of [Operation Desert Strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Desert_Strike). The B-52s struck Baghdad power stations and communications facilities with 13 AGM-86C conventional air-launched cruise missiles (CALCM) during a 34-hour, 16,000 mi (26,000 km) round trip mission from Andersen Air Force Base, Guam, the longest distance ever flown for a combat mission.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDickPatterson2006222-217]



A B-52H of the [2nd Bomb Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2nd_Bomb_Wing) takes off from [Andersen Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andersen_Air_Force_Base), [Guam](11974.md)

On 24 March 1999, when [Operation Allied Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Allied_Force) began, B-52 bombers bombarded Serb targets throughout the [Federal Republic of Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Republic_of_Yugoslavia), including during the [Battle of Kosare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Kosare).[^cite_note-218]

The B-52 contributed to [Operation Enduring Freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_in_Afghanistan_\(2001%E2%80%93present\)) in 2001 (Afghanistan/Southwest Asia), providing the ability to loiter high above the battlefield and provide Close Air Support (CAS) through the use of precision-guided munitions, a mission which previously would have been restricted to fighter and ground attack aircraft.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWillis2005b56–57-219] In late 2001, ten B-52s dropped a third of the bomb tonnage in Afghanistan.[^cite_note-220] B-52s also played a role in [Operation Iraqi Freedom](201936.md), which commenced on 20 March 2003 (Iraq/Southwest Asia). On the night of 21 March 2003, B-52Hs launched at least 100 AGM-86C CALCMs at targets within Iraq.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDickPatterson2006242-221]

### B-52 and maritime operations

The B-52 can be employed in ocean surveillance, anti-ship and mine-laying operations. For example, a pair of B-52s, in two hours, can monitor 140,000 square miles (360,000 square kilometers) of the ocean surface. During the 2018 Baltops exercise, B-52s conducted mine-laying missions off the coast of Sweden, simulating a counter-amphibious invasion mission in the Baltic.[^cite_note-fasb52-204][^cite_note-222]

In the 1970s, the US Navy worried that combined attacks from Soviet bombers, submarines, and warships could overwhelm its defenses and sink its aircraft carriers. After the [Falklands War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falklands_War), US planners feared the damage that could be created by 200-mile (170 nmi; 320 km)-range missiles carried by [Tupolev Tu-22M](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tupolev_Tu-22M) "Backfire" bombers and 250-mile (220 nmi; 400 km)-range missiles carried by Soviet surface ships. New US Navy maritime strategy in the early 1980s called for the aggressive use of carriers and surface action groups against the Soviet navy. To help protect the carrier battle groups, some B-52Gs were modified to fire Harpoon anti-ship missiles. These bombers were based in Guam and Maine in the later 1970s to support both the Atlantic and Pacific fleets. In case of war, B-52s would coordinate with tanker support and surveillance aircraft. B-52Gs could strike Soviet Navy targets on the flanks of the US carrier battle groups, leaving them free to concentrate on offensive strikes against Soviet surface combatants. Mines laid by B-52s could establish minefields in significant enemy chokepoints (mainly the [Kuril Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuril_Islands) and the [GIUK gap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GIUK_gap)). These minefields would force the Soviet fleet to disperse, making individual ships more vulnerable to Harpoon attacks.[^cite_note-223][^cite_note-224]

From the 1980s, B-52Hs were modified to use a wide range of cruise missiles, laser- and satellite-guided bombs, and unguided munitions. B-52 bomber crews honed sea-skimming flight profiles that would allow them to penetrate stiff enemy defenses and attack Soviet ships.[^cite_note-225][^cite_note-226][^cite_note-227]

Recent expansion and modernization of the [People's Liberation Army Navy](24918.md) of China has caused the USAF to re-implement strategies for finding and attacking ships. The B-52 fleet has been certified to use the [Quickstrike](22102.md#MK65_Quickstrike) family of naval mines using JDAM-ER guided wing kits. This weapon provides the ability to lay minefields over wide areas, in a single pass, with extreme accuracy, at a range of over 40 miles (35 nmi; 64 km). Besides this, with a view to enhancing B-52 maritime patrol and strike performance, an AN/ASQ-236 Dragon's Eye underwing pod, has also been certified for use by B-52H bombers. Dragon's Eye contains an advanced electronically scanned array radar that will allow B-52s to quickly scan vast Pacific Ocean areas. This radar will complement the Litening infrared targeting pod already used by B-52s for inspecting ships.[^cite_note-228][^cite_note-229] In 2019, Boeing selected the [Raytheon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raytheon) to develop the new AN/APQ-188 AESA radar to replace its mechanically scanning [AN/APQ-166](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AN/APQ-166?action=edit\&redlink=1) attack radar; the APQ-188 is a variant of the [AN/APG-79](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AN/APG-79) and also incorporates technology from the [AN/APG-82](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AN/APG-82)(V)1.[^cite_note-230][^cite_note-231][^cite_note-232]

### 21st century service



B-52 Crew Chief SSgt Figueroa stenciling completed bombing sorties on B-52H (61–0010) during  [Operation Inherent Resolve](44120613.md), in part of the [Military intervention against ISIL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_intervention_against_ISIL) at [Al Udeid Air Base](3830783.md), in Qatar

On 20 August 2003, a B-52 publicly landed in Russia for the first time, at [Zhukovsky International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhukovsky_International_Airport) for the [MAKS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MAKS_\(air_show\)) air show. In the early 1990s a B-52 "flew into Russia" as part of a "military contact visit".[^cite_note-233]

In August 2007, a B-52H ferrying AGM-129 ACM cruise missiles from Minot Air Force Base to Barksdale Air Force Base for dismantling was [mistakenly loaded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_United_States_Air_Force_nuclear_weapons_incident) with six missiles with their nuclear warheads. The weapons did not leave USAF custody and were secured at Barksdale.[^cite_note-234][^cite_note-235]

Four of 18 B-52Hs from [Barksdale Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barksdale_Air_Force_Base) were retired and were in the "boneyard" of 309th AMARG at [Davis-Monthan Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davis-Monthan_Air_Force_Base) as of 8 September 2008.[^cite_note-236]

The exterior of a B-52 cockpit.

B-52H "Ghost Rider" leaving the "bone yard"

In February 2015, (61-0007) *Ghost Rider* became the first stored B-52 to return to service after six years in storage at Davis-Monthan Air Force Base.[^cite_note-237]

In May 2019, a second aircraft (60-0034) was resurrected from long-term storage in Davis-Monthan. The B-52, nicknamed "Wise Guy", had been at AMARG since 2008. It flew to Barksdale Air Force Base on 13 May 2019. It was completed in four months by a team of 13–20 maintainers from the 307th Maintenance Squadron.[^cite_note-238]



B-52 "Wise Guy" leaving the "bone yard"

B-52s are periodically refurbished at USAF maintenance depots such as [Tinker Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tinker_Air_Force_Base), [Oklahoma](22489.md).[^cite_note-NSIAD-97-210BR-139] Even while the USAF works on the new [Long Range Strike Bomber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Range_Strike_Bomber), it intends to keep the B-52H in service until 2050, which is 95 years after the B-52 first entered service (and will be about 88 years after the last B-52H was delivered to the US Air Force), an unprecedented length of service for any aircraft, civilian or military.[^cite_note-AF_B52-4][^cite_note-239][^cite_note-240][^cite_note-243]

The USAF continues to rely on the B-52 because it remains an effective and economical heavy bomber in the absence of sophisticated air defenses, particularly in the type of missions that have been conducted since the end of the Cold War against nations with limited defensive capabilities. The B-52 has also continued in service because there has been no reliable replacement.[^cite_note-244] The B-52 has the capacity to "loiter" for extended periods, and can deliver precision [standoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standoff_missile) and [direct fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_fire) munitions from a distance, in addition to direct bombing. It has been a valuable asset in supporting ground operations during conflicts such as [Operation Iraqi Freedom](5043324.md).[^cite_note-245] The B-52 had the highest [mission capable rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Availability) of the three types of heavy bombers operated by the USAF in the 2000–2001 period. The B-1 averaged a 53.7% ready rate, the B-2 Spirit achieved 30.3%, and the B-52 averaged 80.5%.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEArana-Barradas2001-206] The B-52's US\$72,000 cost per hour of flight is more than the B-1B's US\$63,000 cost per hour, but less than the B-2's US\$135,000 per hour.[^cite_note-axe20120326-246]

The Long Range Strike Bomber program is intended to yield a stealthy successor for the B-52 and B-1 that would begin service in the 2020s; it is intended to produce 80 to 100 aircraft. Two competitors, Northrop Grumman and a joint team of Boeing and Lockheed Martin, submitted proposals in 2014;[^cite_note-Successor,_Fortune-247] Northrop Grumman was awarded a contract in October 2015.[^cite_note-248]

On 12 November 2015, the B-52 began [freedom of navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_navigation) operations in the [South China Sea](74209.md) in response to [Chinese human-made islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_expansionism) in the region. Chinese forces, [claiming jurisdiction](32005536.md#United_States) within a 12-mile exclusion zone of the islands, ordered the bombers to leave the area, but they refused, not recognizing jurisdiction.[^cite_note-249] On 10 January 2016, a B-52 overflew parts of South Korea escorted by South Korean F-15Ks and US F-16s in response to the supposed test of a hydrogen bomb by North Korea.[^cite_note-250]

On 9 April 2016, an undisclosed number of B-52s arrived at [Al Udeid Air Base](3830783.md) in Qatar as part of [Operation Inherent Resolve](44120613.md), part of the [military intervention against ISIL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_intervention_against_ISIL). The B-52s took over heavy bombing after B-1 Lancers that had been conducting airstrikes rotated out of the region in January 2016.[^cite_note-251] In April 2016, B-52s arrived in Afghanistan to take part in the war in Afghanistan and began operations in July, proving its flexibility and precision carrying out close-air support missions.[^cite_note-252]

According to a statement by the US military, an undisclosed number of B-52s participated in the [US strikes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Khasham) on pro-government forces in eastern Syria on 7 February 2018.[^cite_note-harrigianstatement-253] A number of B-52s were deployed in airstrikes against the Taliban during the [2021 Taliban offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Taliban_offensive).[^cite_note-254] In 2022, the US Air Force used a B-52 as a platform to test a [Hypersonic Air-breathing Weapon Concept](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypersonic_Air-breathing_Weapon_Concept) (HAWC) missile.[^cite_note-255] In late October 2022, [ABC News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABC_News_\(Australia\)) reported that the USAF intended to deploy six B-52s at [RAAF Tindal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAAF_Base_Tindal) in Australia in the near future, which would include building facilities to handle the aircraft.[^cite_note-256]



B-52H Stratofortress with [AGM-158 JASSM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-158_JASSM) cruise missiles during the [2026 Iran war](82533582.md), 4 March 2026

On 3 November 2024, CENTCOM confirmed an undisclosed number of B-52s from Minot Air Force Base's 5th Bomb Wing arrived in the Middle East.[^cite_note-257] In December 2024, B-52s, alongside undisclosed numbers of [F-15E](495724.md) fighters and [A-10](12502446.md) attack aircraft, participated in airstrikes against over 75 Islamic State targets within Syria, following the [ousting of the al-Assad government](78548066.md).[^cite_note-258]

On 3 March 2026, CENTCOM announced that a B-52 bomber force was deployed during the [2026 Iran war](82533582.md), and was used to strike ballistic missile sites along with command & control posts within Iran.[^cite_note-259] At the end of the month B-52 bombers began flying over Iranian territory after the level of air superiority achieved was deemed adequate.[^cite_note-260]

The B-52 went through several design changes and variants over its 10 years of production.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988-145]

**XB-52**

Two prototype aircraft with limited operational equipment, used for aerodynamic and handling tests.

**YB-52**

One XB-52 modified with some operational equipment and re-designated.

**B-52A**

Only three of the first production version, the B-52A, were built, all loaned to Boeing for flight testing.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDonald1997-261]

&#x20;The first production B-52A differed from prototypes in having a redesigned forward fuselage. The bubble canopy and tandem seating was replaced by a side-by-side arrangement and a 21-inch (53 cm) nose extension accommodated more [avionics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avionics) and a new sixth crew member.

[\[Note 8\]](#cite_note-263)

&#x20;In the rear fuselage, a tail turret with four 0.50-inch (12.7 mm) machine guns with a fire-control system, and a [water injection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_injection_\(engines\)) system to augment engine power with a 360 US gal (300 imp gal; 1,400 L) water tank were added. The aircraft also carried a 1,000-US-gallon (830 imp gal; 3,800 L) external fuel tank under each wing. The tanks damped wing flutter and kept wingtips close to the ground for ease of maintenance.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDorr199026-262]

**NB-52A**



NB-52A carrying an X-15

The last B-52A (serial 52-003) was modified and redesignated NB-52A in 1959 to carry the [North American X-15](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_X-15). A pylon was fitted under the right wing between the fuselage and the inboard engines with a 6-by-8-foot (1.8 m × 2.4 m) section removed from the right wing [flap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flap_\(aircraft\)) to fit the X-15 tail. Liquid oxygen and hydrogen peroxide tanks were installed in the bomb bays to fuel the X-15 before launch. Its first flight with the X-15 was on 19 March 1959, with the first launch on 8 June 1959. The NB-52A, nicknamed "The High and Mighty One" carried the X-15 on 93 of the program's 199 flights.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWillis2005a70-264]

**B-52B**



NASA's NB-52B *[Balls 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balls_8)* (lower) and its replacement B-52H on the flight line at [Edwards Air Force Base](107530.md) in 2004

The B-52B was the first version to enter service with the USAF on 29 June 1955 with the [93rd Bombardment Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/93rd_Bombardment_Wing) at Castle Air Force Base, California.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDorr199026-262]

&#x20;This version included minor changes to engines and avionics, enabling an extra 12,000 pounds-force (53,000 N) of thrust using water injection.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoyne1982-265]

&#x20;Temporary grounding of the aircraft after a crash in February 1956 and again the following July caused training delays, and at mid-year, there were still no combat-ready B-52 crews.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELake2003a119-151]

&#x20;Seven B-52Bs were brought to B-52C standard under *Project Sunflower*.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWillis2005a59-266]

**XR-16A/RB-52B**

XR-16A was the initial designation allocated to the reconnaissance variant of the B-52B, but not used. The aircraft were designated RB-52Bs instead.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAndrade197955-267]

&#x20;Of the 50 B-52Bs built, 27 were capable of carrying a reconnaissance pod as RB-52Bs (the crew was increased to eight in these aircraft).

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDonald1997-261]

&#x20;The 300-pound (140 kg) pod contained radio receivers, a combination of K-36, K-38, and T-11 cameras, and two operators on downward-firing [ejection seats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ejection_seat). The pod required only four hours to install.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELake2003a119-151]

**NB-52B**

The NB-52B was B-52B number 52-8 converted to an X-15 launch platform. It subsequently flew as "[Balls 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balls_8)" in support of [NASA](18426568.md) research from 1966 to 17 December 2004, making it the oldest flying B-52B.

[^cite_note-Balls8-268]

&#x20;It was replaced by a modified B-52H.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESkipper202453-269]

**B-52C/RB-52C**

The B-52C's fuel capacity (and range) was increased to 41,700 US gallons (34,700 imp gal; 158,000 L) by adding larger 3,000-US-gallon (2,500 imp gal; 11,000 L) underwing fuel tanks. The gross weight was increased by 30,000 pounds (14,000 kg) to 450,000 pounds (200,000 kg). A new fire control system, the MD-9, was introduced on this model.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECondor199437-181]

&#x20;The belly of the aircraft was painted with [anti-flash white](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-flash_white) paint, which was intended to reflect the thermal radiation of a nuclear detonation.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELake2003a121-270]

&#x20;The RB-52C was the designation initially given to B-52Cs fitted for reconnaissance duties in a similar manner to RB-52Bs. As all 35 B-52Cs could be fitted with the reconnaissance pod, the RB-52C designation was little used and was quickly abandoned.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELake2003a121-270]

**B-52D**

B-52D

B-52D dropping 500-lb bombs

The B-52D was a dedicated long-range bomber without a reconnaissance option. The *Big Belly* modifications allowed the B-52D to carry heavy loads of conventional bombs for [carpet bombing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpet_bombing) over Vietnam,

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoyne1982-265]

&#x20;while the *Rivet Rambler* modification added the Phase V [ECM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Counter_Measures) systems, which was better than the systems used on most later B-52s. Because of these upgrades and its long range capabilities, the D model was used more extensively in Vietnam than any other model.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECondor199437-181]

&#x20;Aircraft assigned to Vietnam were painted in a camouflage color scheme with black bellies to defeat searchlights.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELake2003b100–101-72]

**B-52E**

The B-52E received updated avionics and bombing navigational system, which was eventually debugged and included in the following models.

**JB-52E**

One aircraft leased by General Electric to test TF39 and CF6 engines.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoyne1982-265]



Boeing NB-52E during the Air Force Flight Dynamics Laboratory-Boeing Control Configured Vehicles program 061128-F-1234S-001

**NB-52E**

One aircraft (AF Serial No. 56-632) was modified as a testbed for various B-52 systems. Redesignated NB-52E, the aircraft was fitted with horizontal and vertical forward stabilizer fins near the cockpit and a Load Alleviation and Mode Stabilization system which reduced airframe fatigue from wind gusts during low-level flight. In one test, the aircraft flew 10 knots (12 mph; 19 km/h) faster than the [never exceed speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VNE) without damage because the forward stabilizer fins eliminated 30% of vertical and 50% of horizontal vibrations caused by wind gusts.

**B-52F**

This aircraft was given J57-P-43W engines with a larger capacity water injection system to provide greater thrust than previous models.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoyne1982-265]

&#x20;This model had problems with fuel leaks which were eventually solved by several service modifications: *Blue Band*, *Hard Shell*, and *QuickClip*.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKnaack1988266–267-104]

**B-52G**



B-52G on static display at [Langley Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langley_Air_Force_Base) in [Hampton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hampton,_Virginia), Virginia



A static B-52G that resides at [Griffiss International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Griffiss_International_Airport) (formerly Griffiss Air Force Base). Also pictured is an [AGM-86 ALCM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AGM-86_ALCM) cruise missile.

The B-52G was proposed to extend the B-52's service life during delays in the [B-58 Hustler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B-58_Hustler) program. At first, a radical redesign was envisioned with a completely new wing and [Pratt & Whitney J75](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_&_Whitney_J75) engines. This was rejected to avoid slowdowns in production, although a large number of changes were implemented.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoyne1982-265]

&#x20;The most significant of these was a new "wet" wing with integral fuel tanks, increasing gross aircraft weight by 38,000 pounds (17,000 kg). In addition, a pair of 700 US gallon (2,650 L) external fuel tanks were fitted under the wings on wet hardpoints.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200486-273]

&#x20;The traditional [ailerons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aileron) were also eliminated, and the [spoilers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoiler_\(aeronautics\)) now provided all roll control (roll control had always been primarily with spoilers due to the danger of wing twist under aileron deflection, but older models had small "feeler" ailerons fitted to provide feedback to the controls). The tail fin was shortened by 8 feet (2.4 m), water injection system capacity was increased to 1,200 US gallons (4,540 L), and the nose [radome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radome) was enlarged.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoyne2001221-274]

&#x20;The tail gunner was relocated to the forward fuselage, aiming via a radar scope, and was now provided with an ejection seat.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200486-273]

&#x20;Dubbed the "Battle Station" concept, the offensive crew (pilot and copilot on the upper deck and the two bombing navigation system operators on the lower deck) faced forward, while the defensive crew (tail gunner and ECM operator) on the upper deck faced aft.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECondor199437-181]

&#x20;The B-52G entered service on 13 February 1959 (a day earlier, the last B-36 was retired, making SAC an all-jet bomber force). 193 B-52Gs were produced, making this the most produced B-52 variant. Most B-52Gs were destroyed in compliance with the 1992 *[Strategic Arms Reduction Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/START_I)*; the last B-52G, number 58-224, was dismantled under [New START](26709878.md) treaty requirements in December 2013.

[^cite_note-275]

[^cite_note-276]

&#x20;A few examples remain on display for museums.

[^cite_note-277]

**B-52H**



A Boeing B-52H Stratofortress in flight over the Persian Gulf

The B-52H had the same crew and structural changes as the B-52G. The most significant upgrade was the switch to [TF33-P-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_&_Whitney_JT3D) turbofan engines, which, despite the initial reliability problems (corrected by 1964 under the *Hot Fan* program), offered considerably better performance and fuel economy than the J57 [turbojets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbojet).

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECondor199437-181]

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBoyne2001221-274]

&#x20;The ECM and avionics were updated, a new fire control system was fitted, and the rear defensive armament was changed from machine guns to a 20 mm [M61 Vulcan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M61_Vulcan) cannon,

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETagg200486-273]

&#x20;which were then removed between 1991 and 1994. The final 18 aircraft were manufactured with provision for the [ADR-8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ADR-8) countermeasures rocket, which was later retrofitted to the remainder of the B-52G and B-52H fleet.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDorrPeacock200052-278]

&#x20;A provision was made for four [GAM-87 Skybolt ballistic missiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GAM-87_Skybolt). The aircraft's first flight occurred on 10 July 1960, and it entered service on 9 May 1961. This is the only variant currently in use.

[^cite_note-AF_B52-4]

&#x20;A total of 102 B-52Hs were built. The last production aircraft, B-52H AF Serial No. 61-40, left the factory on 26 October 1962.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDorr199027-279]

**B-52J**

Upgrade of B-52H with [Rolls-Royce F130](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolls-Royce_BR700) engines, the AN/APQ-188 AESA radar (a variant of the [AN/APG-79](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AN/APG-79) radar), and new communications and navigation equipment. To be operational before the end of the 2020s.

[^cite_note-Tirpak_2023-90]

&#x20;It will improve fuel efficiency by 30 percent, state-of-the-art radar systems, engine pylons, digital engine controls, and other upgrades. The planned Initial Operational Capability for the new radar is 2027.

List of incidents resulting in loss of life, severe injuries, or loss of aircraft:

- In 1956, there were three crashes in eight months, all at [Castle Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castle_Air_Force_Base).

  [^cite_note-290]

- A fourth crash occurred 42 days later on 10 January 1957 in [New Brunswick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Brunswick), Canada.

  [^cite_note-JB1955-291]

  [^cite_note-292]

- On 29 March 1957, B-52C (54–2676) retained by Boeing and used for tests as JB-52C, crashed during Boeing test flight from Wichita, Kansas. Two of the four crew on board were killed.

  [^cite_note-JB1954-293]

- On 11 February 1958, B-52D (56–0610) crashed short of the runway at [Ellsworth AFB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellsworth_AFB), South Dakota, due to total loss of power during final approach. Two of the eight crewmembers on board were killed in addition to three ground personnel. The crash was determined to be from frozen fuel lines that clogged fuel filters. It was previously unknown that jet fuel absorbs water vapor from the atmosphere. After this accident, over two hundred previous aircraft losses listed as "cause unknown" were attributed to frozen fuel lines.

  [^cite_note-JB1956-294]

  [^cite_note-295]

- On 8 September 1958, two B-52Ds (56‑0661 and 56‑0681) from the 92d Bombardment Wing collided in midair near Fairchild AFB. All thirteen crew members on the two aircraft were killed.

  [^cite_note-JB1956-294]

- On 23 June 1959, B-52D (56‑0591), nicknamed "Tommy's Tigator", operating out of [Larson AFB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larson_AFB), crashed in the [Ochoco National Forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ochoco_National_Forest) near [Burns, Oregon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burns,_Oregon). The aircraft was operated by Boeing personnel during a test flight and crashed after turbulence-induced failure in the horizontal stabilizer at a low elevation. All five Boeing personnel were killed.

  [^cite_note-JB1956-294]

- On 15 October 1959, B-52F (57‑0036) from the [4228th Strategic Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4228th_Strategic_Wing) at [Columbus AFB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbus_AFB), Mississippi, carrying two [nuclear weapons](21785.md) collided in midair with a KC-135 tanker (57–1513) near Hardinsburg, Kentucky during a [mid-air refueling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid-air_refueling). Four of the eight crew members on the bomber and all four crew on the tanker were killed. One of the nuclear bombs was damaged by fire, but both weapons were recovered.

  [^cite_note-JB1957-296]

- On 15 December 1960, B-52D (55‑0098) from the [4170th Strategic Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4170th_Strategic_Wing) collided with a KC-135 during mid-air refueling. The refueling probe from the KC-135 pierced the skin on the wing of the B-52. Upon landing at [Larson AFB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larson_AFB), the starboard wing failed, and the aircraft caught fire during the landing roll. The runway at Larson was damaged. All crew members were evacuated. The KC-135 landed at Fairchild AFB.

  [^cite_note-JB1955-291]

- On 19 January 1961, B-52B (53‑0390), call sign "Felon 22", from the [95th Bombardment Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/95th_Bombardment_Wing) out of [Biggs AFB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biggs_AFB), [El Paso, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Paso,_Texas) crashed just north of [Monticello, Utah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monticello,_Utah), after a turbulence-induced structural failure, the tail snapped off, at altitude. Only the copilot survived after ejecting. The other seven crewmen died.

  [^cite_note-JB1953-297]

  [^cite_note-298]

- On 24 January 1961, B-52G (58‑0187) from the [4241st Strategic Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4241st_Strategic_Wing) [broke up in midair and crashed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1961_Goldsboro_B-52_crash) on approach to [Seymour Johnson AFB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seymour_Johnson_AFB) near [Goldsboro, North Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldsboro,_North_Carolina), dropping two [nuclear bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_bombs) in the process without detonation. The aircraft suffered a fuel leak at altitude due to [fatigue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatigue_\(material\)) failure of the starboard wing. A loss of control resulted when the flaps were applied during the emergency approach to Seymour Johnson AFB. Three of the eight crew members were killed.

  [^cite_note-JB1958-299]

  [^cite_note-300]

- On 14 March 1961, B-52F (57‑0166) of the [4134th Strategic Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4134th_Strategic_Wing) operating out of [Mather AFB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mather_AFB), California, carrying two nuclear weapons experienced an [uncontrolled decompression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncontrolled_decompression), necessitating a descent to 10,000 feet (3,000 m) to lower the [cabin altitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabin_pressurization). Due to increased fuel consumption at the lower altitude and being unable to rendezvous with a tanker in time, the aircraft ran out of fuel. The crew ejected safely, while the [now-unmanned bomber crashed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1961_Yuba_City_B-52_crash) 15 miles (24 km) west of [Yuba City, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuba_City,_California).

  [^cite_note-JB1957-296]

  [^cite_note-JNACC-301]

- On 7 April 1961, B-52B (53‑0380), nicknamed "Ciudad Juarez", from the 95th Bombardment Wing out of Biggs AFB was accidentally shot down by the launch of a [AIM-9 Sidewinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AIM-9_Sidewinder) from a [F-100A Super Sabre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-100A_Super_Sabre) (53–1662) of the [New Mexico Air National Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Mexico_Air_National_Guard) during a practice intercept maneuver. The missile struck the engine pylon on the B-52 resulting in separation of the wing. The aircraft crashed on [Mount Taylor, New Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Taylor_\(New_Mexico\)), with three of the eight crew on board killed. A firing circuit electrical fault caused the inadvertent launch of the missile.

  [^cite_note-JB1953-297]

  [^cite_note-302]

- On 24 January 1963, B-52C (53–0406) with nine crew members on board lost its vertical stabilizer due to buffeting stresses during turbulence at low altitude and crashed on Elephant Mountain in Piscataquis County, Maine, United States, six miles (9.7 km) from Greenville. Of the 9-man crew, only the pilot and the navigator survived the accident.

  [^cite_note-303]

- On 13 January 1964, the vertical stabilizer broke off B-52D (55‑0060), callsign "Buzz 14", causing a [crash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_Savage_Mountain_B-52_crash) on [Savage Mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savage_Mountain) in western Maryland. Excessive turbulence resulted in structural failure in a winter storm. The two MK53 nuclear bombs being ferried were found "relatively intact". Four of the crew of five ejected but two of them died due to exposure from the winter cold.

  [^cite_note-JB1955-291]

  [^cite_note-FOOTNOTESagan1995202-304]

  [^cite_note-305]

- On 18 June 1965, two B-52Fs (57‑0047 and 57‑0179) collided mid-air during a refueling maneuver at 33,000 feet (10,000 m) above the South China Sea. The head-on collision took place just northwest of the Luzon Peninsula, Philippines, in the night sky above [Super Typhoon Dinah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1965_Pacific_typhoon_season#Super_Typhoon_Dinah_\(Huling\)), a category 5 storm with maximum winds of 185 mph (298 km/h) and waves reported as high as 70 feet (21 m). Both aircraft were from the same squadron ([441st Bombardment Squadron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/441st_Bombardment_Squadron)) of the [7th Bombardment Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/7th_Bombardment_Wing), [Carswell AFB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carswell_AFB), Texas and assigned to [3960th Strategic Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3960th_Strategic_Wing) operating out of [Andersen AFB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andersen_AFB), [Guam](11974.md). Eight of twelve total crew members in two planes were killed. The rescue of four crew members who had managed to eject only to parachute into one of the largest typhoons of the 20th century remains one of the most remarkable survival stories in the history of aviation. The crash was the first combat mission ever for the B-52.

  [^cite_note-JB1957-296]

  [^cite_note-306]

  [^cite_note-307]

The two unexploded [B-28 FI 1.45-megaton-range nuclear bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B28_nuclear_bomb) on the B-52 were eventually recovered; the conventional explosives of two more bombs detonated on impact, with serious dispersion of both [plutonium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutonium) and [uranium](31743.md), but without triggering a nuclear explosion. After the crash, 1,400 metric tons (3,100,000 lb) of contaminated soil was sent to the United States.

A B-52 carrying nuclear weapons was a key part of [Stanley Kubrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Kubrick)'s 1964 black comedy film *[Dr. Strangelove or: How I Learned to Stop Worrying and Love the Bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dr._Strangelove)*.[^cite_note-326] A 1960s hairstyle, the [beehive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beehive_\(hairstyle\)), is also called a B-52 for its resemblance to the aircraft's distinctive nose.[^cite_note-Beehive-327] The popular band [the B-52s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_B-52s) was subsequently named after this hairstyle.[^cite_note-Beehive-327][^cite_note-328]It appears in the 2010 [first-person shooter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-person_shooter) game [Call of Duty: Black Ops 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Call_of_Duty:_Black_Ops) in the campaign and in the multiplayer as the "Rolling Thunder" killstreak that performs a [carpet bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpet_bombing).[^cite_note-329] The B-52 was added to [War Thunder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_Thunder) as a reward for completing stages in the 2026 [April fools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/April_Fools'_Day) "Nuclear Thunder" limited time event.[^cite_note-330]
