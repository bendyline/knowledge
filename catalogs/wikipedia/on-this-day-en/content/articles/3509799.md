Malling House, Lewes, a Grade I listed building.

**Sussex Police** are the [territorial police force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territorial_police_force) responsible for policing the [ceremonial counties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceremonial_counties_of_England) of [East Sussex](67923.md) and [West Sussex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Sussex) in [South East England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_East_England). Their headquarters are in [Malling House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malling_House), [Lewes](67930.md), East Sussex.[^cite_note-4]

## History

Policing in Sussex can be traced back to [Brighton Borough Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brighton_Borough_Police), established in 1830.&#x20;

A few years later on 13 March 1844, Chief Constable [Henry Solomon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Solomon) was murdered in his office by a detainee he was interviewing. He is believed to be the only [chief police officer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_police_officer) to have suffered such a fate.&#x20;

Prior to 1830 local watchmen were appointed to provide some degree of law enforcement in the area.&#x20;

In 1812, there were some 12 watchmen who were responsible for the town. By 1814 the number had grown to 28 and at this time the title of [constable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constable) was in use for them.&#x20;

By 1868 the force had grown to 100 officers and helmets replaced top hats.&#x20;

In 1918, the first woman was appointed as a police officer in this force.&#x20;

By 1930, it had grown to 216 officers.&#x20;

On 14 September 1933, Brighton Borough Police were the first force to introduce police radios.

Forces were established at various times for the counties of East Sussex and West Sussex, as well as separate forces in the boroughs of [Brighton](49796.md), Hove, [Eastbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastbourne) and [Hastings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hastings).&#x20;

In April 1943, in order to provide a co-ordinated approach to the wartime emergency, these forces were amalgamated to form the Sussex Combined Police, with headquarters at Haywards Heath.[^cite_note-5]

In April 1947, with the exception of Hove, policing reverted to the old system for another two decades. Hove remained part of East Sussex Constabulary.

On 1 January 1968, Sussex Constabulary was created from the amalgamation of Brighton Borough Police, Eastbourne Borough Police, Hastings Borough Police, [West Sussex Constabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Sussex_Constabulary) and [East Sussex Constabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Sussex_Constabulary). In 1974, the amalgamated forces became Sussex Police.[^cite_note-6]

### Old Police Cells Museum

Opened on 4 May 2005 by Councillor Pat Drake, the then mayor of Brighton & Hove, the [Old Police Cells Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Police_Cells_Museum) is located in the basement of Brighton Town Hall and offers an educational and entertaining insight into the history of policing within Sussex.

It provides an opportunity to visit Brighton Borough main police station for the period 1830 to 1965 and learn about the murder of Chief Constable [Henry Solomon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Solomon) in 1844 by a prisoner. It is possible to view the old cells with their graffiti from the [mods and rockers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mods_and_rockers) era, police memorabilia and artifacts. The museum also houses a unique collection of truncheons and tipstaffs, one of the largest in the country. This collection was made by Alderman Caffyn throughout his lifetime and is on permanent loan to the Museum from the Sussex Police Authority.[^cite_note-sussex.police.uk-12]

## Organisation

Sussex Police is commanded by [Chief Constable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_Constable) Jo Shiner.&#x20;

The remainder of the command team consists of [Deputy Chief Constable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deputy_Chief_Constable) Dave McLaren, [Assistant Chief Constable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assistant_Chief_Constable) Paul Court, Assistant Chief Constable Peter Gardner and Assistant Chief Constable Stephen Rayland.&#x20;

Also forming part of the command team are the assistant chief officer, the director of finance and chief information officer although these roles are filled by civilian members of staff.[^cite_note-13]

The force consists of three divisions, each being led by a [chief superintendent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_superintendent): West Sussex, East Sussex, and Brighton & Hove.&#x20;

Divisions are sub-divided into districts, each led by a [chief inspector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_inspector), providing a local identity for policing. These districts are Chichester, Arun, Horsham, Adur & Worthing, Crawley, Mid Sussex, Brighton & Hove, Wealden, Lewes, Eastbourne, Rother and Hastings.

Sussex Police is also responsible for policing [Gatwick Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gatwick_Airport).

Districts are further divided into Neighbourhood Policing Teams (NPT), each led by an [inspector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inspector).&#x20;

### Neighbourhood Policing Teams

The NPTs are responsible for the bulk of the community work undertaken in an area, and look to deal with long term local issues including anti-social behaviour.&#x20;

Their role stems from the traditional view of 'bobbies on the beat' with [police community support officers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_community_support_officer) (PCSOs) providing a high visibility profile on the street, albeit with limited policing powers.&#x20;

[Special constables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_constable) also serve alongside various teams including NRT, Prevention and on specialist teams such as RPU and Dogs units; they have full police powers when on duty.

### Neighbourhood Response Teams

Police response is covered by Neighbourhood Response Teams (NRT) operating from a number of "hub" stations across the area and providing the initial response to most emergency and prompt attendance calls.&#x20;

These teams are led locally by a [sergeant](219424.md) and overall they are managed by an inspector. These teams work 24 hours a day, seven days a week, 365 days a year.&#x20;

Secondary investigation of crime not dealt with by specialist teams – for example CID – is managed by Response Investigation Teams (RIT) who work closely with the NRT.

The force has a total of six custody suites located around the force area for the detention of arrested persons. They are located in Brighton with 36 cells, Crawley with 27 cells, Eastbourne with 22 cells, Hastings with 10 cells, Worthing with 19 cells, and Chichester with 19 cells.[^cite_note-14]

## Police and Crime Commissioner

Main article: [Sussex Police and Crime Commissioner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sussex_Police_and_Crime_Commissioner)

Oversight of Sussex Police was provided by Sussex Police Authority until November 2012, when this role was taken over by a [police and crime commissioner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_and_Crime_Commissioner) following the [first elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/England_and_Wales_Police_and_Crime_Commissioner_elections,_2012).&#x20;

[Katy Bourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katy_Bourne) was elected police and crime commissioner for Sussex Police on 15 November 2012, with a majority of 24,426.

The police and crime commissioner is scrutinised by the Sussex Police and Crime Panel, made up of elected councillors from the local authorities in the police area.

Sussex [Police Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_Authority) had nine councillors (drawn from the three councils), three [justices of the peace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justices_of_the_peace) and five independent members.[^cite_note-25]

## Specialist units

### Air Support Unit

Main article: [Sussex Police Air Operations Unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sussex_Police_Air_Operations_Unit)

Police aviation is now provided across England and Wales by the [National Police Air Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Police_Air_Service) (NPAS).&#x20;

Prior to the establishment of NPAS in 2012, Sussex Police used to operate an [MD-902](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MD_Helicopters_MD_Explorer) helicopter, callsign Hotel 900 jointly with the [South East Coast Ambulance Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_East_Coast_Ambulance_Service), providing both policing and emergency medical support to Sussex and beyond.&#x20;

The closest NPAS base to Sussex Police is at [Redhill Aerodrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redhill_Aerodrome) in neighbouring Surrey. The helicopter's call-sign is NPAS 15 and it has a registration of G-CPAS. This aircraft was previously used by Cleveland Police until NPAS was formed.

### Counter-terrorism

Sussex Police has a dedicated Counter-Terrorist Intelligence Unit (CTIU) which works alongside the rest of the force in protecting and reassuring the public, and reducing the risk from terrorism.&#x20;

The unit engages with communities, local authorities, higher education and universities and other local bodies, in order to prevent [violent extremism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Violent_extremism). The unit also offers specialist protective security advice to locations deemed to be at a greater risk from terrorism.

The CTIU is involved in the disruption and detection of terrorist activity and has close links between local police and the communities it serves. It also works at ports and airports alongside Border Force[^cite_note-26]

### Dog Support Unit



A Ford Mondeo of Sussex DSU at Chichester

The Sussex police have a Dog Support Unit that tracks, chases and detains suspects, searches for stolen property and missing people, and assists public order units with crowd control.&#x20;

Specialist search dogs are used to recover drugs, casg and weapons; detect explosives; and follow the scent of a specific person. Dog handlers are also trained to deal with dangerous dogs.[^cite_note-sussex.police.uk-12]

### Rural Crime Team

The Rural Crime Team, based out of Heathfield and Midhurst, are primarily responsible for policing the less built up areas. They will handle any rural forms of crime, from hunting and agricultural crimes and thefts of agricultural equipment etc. They work hand in hand with the Environment Agency as well as the National Wildlife Crime Unit. The team is headed up by an inspector who is supported by 3 sergeants. Unlike most other specialised unit, the rural crime team employes Police Community Support Officers (PCSOs) as well as police officers.

### Emergency & Operations Planning

The Emergency and Planning Team provides Sussex Police with the ability to plan for major incidents, natural disasters and large policing operations that occur throughout the communities of Sussex.&#x20;

The team delivers specialist equipment to front line officers, staff for operations and tactical advice to police commanders; this helps to deliver the best service possible to the public during critical times.&#x20;

The team also has responsibilities with regards to business continuity, local resilience partnership working and contingency planning.[^cite_note-sussex.police.uk-12]

### Roads Policing Unit (RPU)



[BMW 3 Series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BMW_3_Series) Road Policing Unit

The Road Policing Unit (RPU) covers the whole force from three bases. Those bases include Sussex Police HQ ([Lewes](67930.md)), [Arundel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arundel) and [Bexhill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bexhill-on-Sea).&#x20;

The primary aims of RPU are to deny criminals the use of the road, tackle anti-social driving such as mobile phone use and drink-driving and to bring down the number of KSI (killed and seriously injured) casualties on the roads of Sussex.&#x20;

Vehicles in use include fully marked and unmarked vehicles of various types fitted with covert warning equipment.

### Specialist Enforcement Unit (SEU)

The SEU was formed on 26 January 2021 with the sole task of denying criminals the use of the roads, particularly car crime such as car theft and ‘county line’ drug smuggling between counties. It differs from the RPU as it targets these specific areas.

### Tactical Enforcement Unit (TEU)

The TEU was formed in 2021, tasked with targeting violent and high level offenders throughout the two counties of West Sussex and East Sussex. The unit specialises in method of entry, as well as proactive policing approaches to capturing these offenders.

### Specialist Search Unit (SSU)

This unit was disbanded in June 2015, due to cuts in the police budget.[^cite_note-sussex.police.uk-12][^cite_note-27]

Prior to disbandment Specialist Search Unit officers searched where other police officers could not go. They were trained to search in demanding environments that needed specialist equipment, such as underwater (mostly inland waters), at height, in flooded areas as in the Lewes floods in 2000, and confined spaces.&#x20;

The team were experts in searching for missing people, stolen property, drugs, weapons and firearms, and they were also licensed to find and identify [improvised explosive devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Improvised_explosive_devices). A police boat was used by the unit to undertake marine patrols along the Sussex coastline and during diving operations.[^cite_note-sussex.police.uk-12]

### Tactical Firearms Unit (TFU)

The Tactical Firearms Unit covers the entire force from two bases.&#x20;

Those bases include Sussex Police HQ (Lewes) and Gatwick. Firearms officers are deployed to incidents involving the use of firearms or other lethal weapons either on a spontaneous or pre-planned basis.&#x20;

They are able to be deployed across all of Sussex, dealing with high priority crime such as drugs, burglary and violent crime. All tactical firearms (TFU) officers are trained in conflict resolution methods, meaning every incident is resolved using the minimum amount of force necessary.&#x20;

Non-lethal methods used by officers include the Baton Gun for firing rubber bullets and the [Taser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taser) stun-gun.&#x20;

Officers are rigorously trained in [threat assessment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threat_assessment) and perception, ready to make split-second decisions to protect the public against threats of violence. Officers are required to complete a two-year probationary period as firearms officers at [Gatwick Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gatwick_Airport) after completing initial training.[^cite_note-sussex.police.uk-12]

### Use of drones

Sussex Police currently operates a number of different drone models in conjunction with Surrey Police to assist with searches for missing persons, road traffic collisions, major crime and industrial accident investigation, event planning and management, and to provide situational awareness to officers and commanders in a variety of policing situations.

## Police cadets

Sussex Police, like many other forces in the UK, has [police cadets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_cadets_in_the_United_Kingdom). There are around 200 operational cadets in Sussex Police. Cadets within the VCC (Volunteer Cadet Corps) often have duties at police stations across Sussex. They have a similar uniform to non-operational police officers and police staff which consists of:

Standard issue white shirt and black clip on tie, black police fleece with hi-vis cadet badges, hi-vis police coat with cadet badges, black trousers. Epaulette saying "cadet" and a visible rank designation (normally chevrons (inversed), although they have now switched to bars), these will be located on the shoulders of the cadet's shirt, fleece and hi-vis. Standard issue police cap with a blue band and Sussex Police crest (without the crown) saying 'Sussex VCC'

Cadets learn many police skills as well as a student officer syllabus in preparation for joining as an officer. They also assist at public events and displays, as well as low risk police activities such as area searches for weapons (Weapon Sweeps), leaflet delivery.

## Future plans

Proposals were made by the [Home Secretary](149104.md) on 20 March 2006 to merge the force with [Surrey Police](3006917.md) forming a single strategic police force for Sussex and Surrey.[^cite_note-28] Opposed by both Sussex Police and Surrey Police, the plans were effectively abandoned by the Home Office in July 2006.[^cite_note-29]

In a report published by [HM Inspectorate of Constabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HM_Inspectorate_of_Constabulary) in July 2011,[^cite_note-30] the impact on the number of police officers and staff partly due to the reduction to Sussex Police's budget following the comprehensive spending review is as follows:

|  | Police officers | Police staff | PCSOs | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 31 March 2010 (actual) | 3,213 | 2,155 | 377 | 5,745 |
| 31 March 2015 (proposed) | 2,713 | 1,605 | 377 | 4,695 |

In common with other UK forces, Sussex is being forced to save 20% from its budget by 2015. Whilst efforts are being made to minimise the impact of these cuts, inevitably there have been, and continue to be reductions in the numbers of warranted officers and police staff. In September 2010, Chief Constable Martin Richards announced plans to cut up to 1,050 police officers and staff over the following five years, saying that job cuts were inevitable as the force faced estimated budget cuts of £52m by 2015. It was estimated that about 500 of the affected jobs would be police officers.[^cite_note-31]

On 10 May 2012, off-duty officers from Sussex police joined an estimated 30,000 others from around the UK to protest at the cutbacks in a march through London.[^cite_note-32]

Reports in 2026 indicate that doorstep boycott campaigning is not currently treated as a policing priority by Sussex Police unless it meets the threshold of a criminal offence. The police force stated it would not investigate [pro-Palestinian](47246228.md) activists conducting "door-to-door" boycott campaigns in Brighton, noting that there has been “no evidence of criminal activity” despite complaints and concerns about intimidation, with no indication of a shift in police approach to such cases.[^cite_note-33][^cite_note-34]

## Online initiatives

In November 2011, Sussex Police became the first force worldwide to live stream unedited footage during a 24-hour period.[^cite_note-35]

Published in March 2012, Sussex Police became the first force in the UK to launch a mobile based app for reporting crime.[^cite_note-36]
