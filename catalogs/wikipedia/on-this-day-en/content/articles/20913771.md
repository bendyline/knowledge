**Theatre** or **theater**[^cite_note-1] is a collaborative form of [performing art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Performing_art) that uses live performers, usually [actors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actor), to present experiences of a real or imagined event before a live audience in a specific place, often a [stage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stage_\(theatre\)). The performers may communicate this experience to the audience through combinations of [gesture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gesture), [speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speech), [song](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Song), [music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music), and [dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dance).&#x20;

It is the oldest form of [drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drama), though live theatre has now been joined by modern recorded forms. Elements of art, such as painted scenery and [stagecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stagecraft) such as [lighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lighting) are used to enhance the physicality, presence and immediacy of the experience.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECarlson198636-2] Places, normally buildings, where performances regularly take place are also called "theatres" (or "theaters"), as derived from the [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) θέατρον (théatron, "a place for viewing"), itself from θεάομαι (theáomai, "to see", "to watch", "to observe").

Modern Western theatre comes, in large measure, from the [theatre of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_ancient_Greece), from which it borrows technical terminology, classification into [genres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genre), and many of its [themes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theme_\(narrative\)), [stock characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock_character), and plot elements. Theatre artist [Patrice Pavis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrice_Pavis) defines theatricality, [theatrical language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_language), stage writing and the [specificity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medium_specificity) of theatre as synonymous expressions that differentiate theatre from the other [performing arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Performing_arts), [literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literature) and the arts in general.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPavis1998345–346-3][^cite_note-4]

A **theatre company** is an organisation that produces theatrical performances,[^cite_note-compdef-5] as distinct from a [theatre troupe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_troupe) (or acting company), which is a group of theatrical performers working together.[^cite_note-troupedef1-6][^cite_note-troupedef2-7]

Modern theatre includes performances of [plays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Play_\(theatre\)) and [musical theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_theatre). The art forms of [ballet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballet) and [opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opera) are also theatre and use many conventions such as [acting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acting), costumes and staging. They were influential in the development of musical theatre.

## History of theatre

Main article: [History of theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_theatre)

### Classical, Hellenistic Greece and Magna Graecia

Main article: [Theatre of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_ancient_Greece)



The best-preserved example of a classical Greek theatre, the [Ancient Theatre of Epidaurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Theatre_of_Epidaurus), has a circular *orchêstra* and probably gives the best idea of the original shape of the Athenian theatre, though it dates from the 4th century BC.

[^cite_note-8]



[Greek Theatre of Taormina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_theatre_of_Taormina), [Sicily](27619.md), [Magna Graecia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magna_Graecia), in present-day [Italy](14532.md)

The [city-state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polis) of [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Athens) is where Western theatre originated.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown1998441-9][^cite_note-FOOTNOTECartledge19973–5-10][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGoldhill199754-11][^cite_note-12] It was part of a broader [culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture) of theatricality and performance in [classical Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Greece) that included [festivals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenian_festivals), [religious rituals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_ancient_Greece), [politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece#Political_structure), [law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_law), athletics and gymnastics, [music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece), [poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_literature#Classical_and_Pre-Classical_Antiquity), weddings, funerals, and *[symposia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symposium_\(ancient_Greece\))*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECartledge19973,_6-13][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGoldhill199754-11][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGoldhill200420–xx-14][^cite_note-FOOTNOTERehm19923-15][^cite_note-17]

Participation in the city-state's many festivals—and mandatory attendance at the [City Dionysia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dionysia#City_Dionysia) as an audience member (or even as a participant in the theatrical productions) in particular—was an important part of [citizenship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizenship).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPelling200583-18] Civic participation also involved the evaluation of the [rhetoric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhetoric) of [orators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orators) evidenced in performances in the [law-court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenian_law_court_\(classical_period\)) or [political assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesia_\(ancient_Athens\)), both of which were understood as analogous to the theatre and increasingly came to absorb its dramatic vocabulary.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGoldhill200425-19][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPelling200583–84-20] The Greeks also developed the concepts of [dramatic criticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dramatic_criticism) and theatre architecture.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDukore197431-21][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJanko1987ix-22][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWard20071-23] Actors were either amateur or at best semi-professional.[^cite_note-24] The [theatre of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_ancient_Greece) consisted of three types of [drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drama): [tragedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragedy), [comedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comedy_\(drama\)), and the [satyr play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satyr_play).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrockettHildy200315–19-25]

The origins of theatre in ancient Greece, according to [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle) (384–322 BCE), the first theoretician of theatre, are to be found in the festivals that honoured [Dionysus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dionysus). The performances were given in semi-circular auditoria cut into hillsides, capable of seating 10,000–20,000 people. The stage consisted of a dancing floor (orchestra), dressing room and scene-building area (skene). Since the words were the most important part, good acoustics and clear delivery were paramount. The actors (always men) wore masks appropriate to the characters they represented, and each might play several parts.[^cite_note-26]

Athenian tragedy—the oldest surviving form of tragedy—is a type of [dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dance)-drama that formed an important part of the theatrical culture of the city-state.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown1998441-9][^cite_note-FOOTNOTECartledge19973–5-10][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGoldhill199754-11][^cite_note-FOOTNOTELey2007206-27][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStyan2000140-28][^cite_note-30] Having emerged sometime during the 6th century BCE, it flowered during the 5th century BCE (from the end of which it began to spread throughout the Greek world), and continued to be popular until the beginning of the [Hellenistic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrockettHildy200332–33-31][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown1998444-32][^cite_note-FOOTNOTECartledge19973–5-10][^cite_note-34]

No tragedies from the 6th century BCE and only 32 of the more than a thousand that were performed in during the 5th century BCE have survived.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrockettHildy20035-35][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKovacs2005379-36][^cite_note-37] We have complete texts [extant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extant_literature) by Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrockettHildy200315-38][^cite_note-39] The origins of tragedy remain obscure, though by the 5th century BCE it was [institutionalised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institution) in competitions (*[agon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agon)*) held as part of festivities celebrating Dionysus (the [god](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_tree_of_the_Greek_gods) of [wine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wine) and [fertility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertility)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrockettHildy200313–15-40][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown1998441–447-41] As contestants in the City Dionysia competition (the most prestigious of the festivals to stage drama), playwrights were required to present a [tetralogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetralogy) of plays (though the individual works were not necessarily connected by story or theme), which usually consisted of three tragedies and one satyr play.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown1998442-42][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrockettHildy200315–17-43][^cite_note-44] The performance of tragedies at the City Dionysia may have begun as early as 534 BCE; official records (*didaskaliai*) begin from 501 BCE, when the satyr play was introduced.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrockettHildy200313,_15-45][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown1998442-42][^cite_note-47]

Most Athenian tragedies dramatise events from [Greek mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mythology), though *[The Persians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Persians)*—which stages the [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire) response to news of their military defeat at the [Battle of Salamis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Salamis) in 480 BCE—is the notable exception in the surviving drama.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown1998442-42][^cite_note-48] When Aeschylus won first prize for it at the City Dionysia in 472 BCE, he had been writing tragedies for more than 25 years, yet its tragic treatment of recent history is the earliest example of [drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drama) to survive.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrown1998442-42][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrockettHildy200315–16-49] More than 130 years later, the philosopher [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle) analysed 5th-century Athenian tragedy in the oldest surviving work of [dramatic theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dramatic_theory)—his *[Poetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetics_\(Aristotle\))* (c. 335 BCE).

[Athenian comedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_comedy) is conventionally divided into three periods, "Old Comedy", "Middle Comedy", and "New Comedy". Old Comedy survives today largely in the form of the eleven surviving plays of [Aristophanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristophanes), while Middle Comedy is largely lost (preserved only in relatively short fragments in authors such as [Athenaeus of Naucratis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenaeus_of_Naucratis)). New Comedy is known primarily from the substantial papyrus fragments of [Menander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menander). Aristotle defined comedy as a representation of laughable people that involves some kind of blunder or ugliness that does not cause pain or disaster.[^cite_note-50]

In addition to the categories of comedy and tragedy at the City Dionysia, the festival also included the [Satyr Play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satyr_Play). Finding its origins in rural, agricultural rituals dedicated to Dionysus, the satyr play eventually found its way to Athens in its most well-known form. Satyr's themselves were tied to the god Dionysus as his loyal woodland companions, often engaging in drunken revelry and mischief at his side. The satyr play itself was classified as tragicomedy, erring on the side of the more modern burlesque traditions of the early twentieth century. The plotlines of the plays were typically concerned with the dealings of the pantheon of Gods and their involvement in human affairs, backed by the chorus of [Satyrs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satyrs). However, according to [Webster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._B._L._Webster), satyr actors did not always perform typical satyr actions and would break from the acting traditions assigned to the character type of a mythical forest creature.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWebster1967-51]

The Greek colonists in [Southern Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Italy), the so-called [Magna Graecia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magna_Graecia), brought theatrical art from their motherland.[^cite_note-melogranoarte-52] The [Greek Theatre of Syracuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Theatre_of_Syracuse), the [Greek Theatre of Segesta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Theatre_of_Segesta?action=edit\&redlink=1), the [Greek Theatre of Tindari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Theatre_of_Tindari?action=edit\&redlink=1), the [Greek Theatre of Hippana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Theatre_of_Hippana?action=edit\&redlink=1), the [Greek Theatre of Akrai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Theatre_of_Akrai?action=edit\&redlink=1), the [Greek Theatre of Monte Jato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Theatre_of_Monte_Jato?action=edit\&redlink=1), the [Greek Theatre of Morgantina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Theatre_of_Morgantina?action=edit\&redlink=1) and the most famous [Greek Theatre of Taormina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_theatre_of_Taormina), amply demonstrate this. Only fragments of original dramaturgical works are left, but the tragedies of the three great giants [Aeschylus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeschylus), [Sophocles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophocles) and [Euripides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euripides) and the comedies of [Aristophanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristophanes) are known.[^cite_note-53] Some famous playwrights in the Greek language came directly from [Magna Graecia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magna_Graecia). Others, such as Aeschylus and [Epicharmus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicharmus_of_Kos), worked for a long time in [Sicily](27619.md). Epicharmus can be considered Syracusan in all respects, having worked all his life with the [tyrants of Syracuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tyrants_of_Syracuse). His comedy preceded that of the more famous Aristophanes by staging the gods for the first time in comedy. While Aeschylus, after a long stay in the Sicilian colonies, died in Sicily in the colony of [Gela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gela) in 456 BC. Epicarmus and [Phormis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phormis), both of 6th century BC, are the basis, for [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle), of the invention of the Greek comedy, as he says in his book on *[Poetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetics_\(Aristotle\))*:[^cite_note-54]

> As for the composition of the stories (Epicharmus and Phormis) it came in the beginning from Sicily

— Aristotle, *Poetics*

Other native dramatic authors of Magna Graecia, in addition to the Syracusan Formides mentioned, are [Achaeus of Syracuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaeus_of_Syracuse), [Apollodorus of Gela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollodorus_of_Gela), [Philemon of Syracuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philemon_\(poet\)) and his son Philemon the younger. From [Calabria](44772.md), precisely from the colony of [Thurii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thurii), came the playwright [Alexis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexis_\(poet\)). While [Rhinthon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhinthon), although Sicilian from Syracuse, worked almost exclusively for the colony of [Taranto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taranto) in [Apulia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apulia).[^cite_note-55]

### Roman theatre

Main article: [Theatre of ancient Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_ancient_Rome)



[Roman theatre of Benevento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Theatre,_Benevento)



Roman mosaic depicting actors and an *[aulos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aulos)* player (House of the Tragic Poet, [Pompeii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompeii))

Western theatre developed and expanded considerably under the [Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome). The Roman historian [Livy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Livy) wrote that the Romans first experienced theatre in the 4th century BC, with a performance by [Etruscan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etruscan_civilization) actors.[^cite_note-56] Beacham argues that Romans had been familiar with "pre-theatrical practices" for some time before that recorded contact.[^cite_note-57]

The [theatre of ancient Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_ancient_Rome) was a thriving and diverse art form, ranging from [festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_festival) performances of [street theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_theatre), nude dancing, and acrobatics, to the staging of [Plautus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plautus)'s broadly appealing situation [comedies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comedy_\(drama\)), to the [high-style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_culture), verbally elaborate [tragedies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragedy) of [Seneca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seneca_the_Younger). Although Rome had a native tradition of performance, the [Hellenisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenization) of [Roman culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_ancient_Rome) in the 3rd century BC had a profound and energising effect on Roman theatre and encouraged the development of [Latin literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_literature) of the highest quality for the stage.

Following the expansion of the [Roman Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republic) (509–27 BC) into several Greek territories between 270 and 240 BC, Rome encountered [Greek drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_ancient_Greece).[^cite_note-h43-58] From the later years of the republic and by means of the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire) (27 BC-476 AD), theatre spread west across Europe, around the Mediterranean and reached England; Roman theatre was more varied, extensive and sophisticated than that of any culture before it.[^cite_note-59] While Greek drama continued to be performed throughout the Roman period, the year 240 BC marks the beginning of regular Roman drama.[^cite_note-h43-58][^cite_note-60] From the beginning of the empire, however, interest in full-length drama declined in favour of a broader variety of theatrical entertainments.[^cite_note-61]

The first important works of [Roman literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_literature) were the tragedies and comedies that [Livius Andronicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Livius_Andronicus) wrote from 240 BC.[^cite_note-h47-62] Five years later, [Gnaeus Naevius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnaeus_Naevius) also began to write drama.[^cite_note-h47-62] No plays from either writer have survived. While both dramatists composed in both [genres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genre), Andronicus was most appreciated for his tragedies and Naevius for his comedies; their successors tended to specialise in one or the other, which led to a separation of the subsequent development of each type of drama.[^cite_note-h47-62] By the beginning of the 2nd century BC, drama was firmly established in Rome and a [guild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guild) of writers (*collegium poetarum*) had been formed.[^cite_note-63]

The Roman comedies that have survived are all *[fabula palliata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fabula_palliata)* (comedies based on Greek subjects) and come from two dramatists: [Titus Maccius Plautus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plautus) (Plautus) and [Publius Terentius Afer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terence) (Terence).[^cite_note-64] In re-working the Greek originals, the Roman comic dramatists abolished the role of the [chorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_chorus) in dividing the drama into [episodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episode) and introduced musical accompaniment to its [dialogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialogue) (between one-third of the dialogue in the comedies of Plautus and two-thirds in those of Terence).[^cite_note-h49-65] The action of all scenes is set in the exterior location of a street and its complications often follow from [eavesdropping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eavesdropping).[^cite_note-h49-65] Plautus, the more popular of the two, wrote between 205 and 184 BC and twenty of his comedies survive, of which his [farces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farce) are best known; he was admired for the [wit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wit) of his dialogue and his use of a variety of [poetic meters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meter_\(poetry\)).[^cite_note-h48-66] All of the six comedies that Terence wrote between 166 and 160 BC have survived; the complexity of his plots, in which he often combined several Greek originals, was sometimes denounced, but his double-plots enabled a sophisticated presentation of contrasting human behaviour.[^cite_note-h48-66]

No early Roman tragedy survives, though it was highly regarded in its day; historians know of three early tragedians—[Quintus Ennius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quintus_Ennius), [Marcus Pacuvius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Pacuvius) and [Lucius Accius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucius_Accius).[^cite_note-h49-65] From the time of the empire, the work of two tragedians survives—one is an unknown author, while the other is the [Stoic philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoicism) [Seneca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seneca_the_Younger).[^cite_note-h50-67] Nine of Seneca's tragedies survive, all of which are *fabula crepidata* (tragedies adapted from Greek originals); his *[Phaedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phaedra_\(Seneca\))*, for example, was based on [Euripides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euripides)' *[Hippolytus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippolytus_\(play\))*.[^cite_note-68] Historians do not know who wrote the only [extant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extant_literature) example of the *fabula praetexta* (tragedies based on Roman subjects), *[Octavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octavia_\(play\))*, but in former times it was mistakenly attributed to Seneca due to his appearance as a [character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Character_\(arts\)) in the tragedy.[^cite_note-h50-67]

In contrast to Ancient Greek theatre, the theatre in Ancient Rome did allow female performers. While the majority were employed for dancing and singing, a minority of actresses are known to have performed speaking roles, and there were actresses who achieved wealth, fame and recognition for their art, such as [Eucharis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eucharis_\(actress\)), [Dionysia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dionysia_\(stage_artist\)), [Galeria Copiola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galeria_Copiola) and [Fabia Arete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fabia_Arete): they also formed their own acting guild, the *[Sociae Mimae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociae_Mimae)*, which was evidently quite wealthy.[^cite_note-69]

### Indian theatre

Main articles: [Theatre of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_India) and [Indian classical drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_classical_drama)

See also: [Koothu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koothu) and [Koodiyattam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koodiyattam)



*[Rakshasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rakshasa)* or the demon as depicted in [Yakshagana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakshagana), a form of musical [dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dance)-[drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drama) from [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_India)

The first form of [Indian theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_theatre) was the [Sanskrit theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_theatre),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERichmondSwannZarrilli199312-70] earliest-surviving fragments of which date from the 1st century CE.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrandon1993xvii-71][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrandon1997516–517-72] It began after the development of [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_ancient_Greece) and [Roman theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_ancient_Rome) and before the development of theatre in other parts of Asia.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERichmondSwannZarrilli199312-70] It emerged sometime between the 2nd century BCE and the 1st century CE and flourished between the 1st century CE and the 10th, which was a period of relative peace in the [history of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_India) during which hundreds of plays were written.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrandon199770-73][^cite_note-FOOTNOTERichmond1998516-74] The wealth of archeological evidence from earlier periods offers no indication of the existence of a tradition of theatre.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERichmond1998516-74]

The ancient *[Vedas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas)* ([hymns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hymn) from between 1500 and 1000 BCE that are among the earliest examples of [literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_literature#India) in the world) contain no hint of it (although a small number are composed in a form of [dialogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialogue)) and the [rituals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ritual) of the [Vedic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic_period) do not appear to have developed into theatre.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERichmond1998516-74] The *[Mahābhāṣya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mah%C4%81bh%C4%81%E1%B9%A3ya)* by [Patañjali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pata%C3%B1jali) contains the earliest reference to what may have been the seeds of Sanskrit drama.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERichmond1998517-75] This treatise on [grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammar) from 140 BCE provides a feasible date for the beginnings of [theatre in India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_in_India).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERichmond1998517-75]

The major source of evidence for Sanskrit theatre is *[A Treatise on Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natyashastra)* (*Nātyaśāstra*), a compendium whose date of composition is uncertain (estimates range from 200 BCE to 200 CE) and whose authorship is attributed to [Bharata Muni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bharata_Muni). The *Treatise* is the most complete work of dramaturgy in the ancient world. It addresses [acting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acting), [dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dance), [music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music), [dramatic construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dramaturgy), [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theater_\(structure\)), [costuming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Costume_design), [make-up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatrical_makeup), [props](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prop), the organisation of companies, the audience, competitions, and offers a [mythological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_mythology) account of the origin of theatre.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERichmond1998517-75] In doing so, it provides indications about the nature of actual theatrical practices. Sanskrit theatre was performed on sacred ground by priests who had been trained in the necessary skills (dance, music, and recitation) in a \[hereditary process]. Its aim was both to educate and to entertain.



Performer playing [Sugriva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugriva) in the [Koodiyattam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koodiyattam) form of [Sanskrit theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_theatre)

Under the patronage of royal courts, performers belonged to professional companies that were directed by a stage manager (*sutradhara*), who may also have acted.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrandon1993xvii-71][^cite_note-FOOTNOTERichmond1998517-75] This task was thought of as being analogous to that of a [puppeteer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puppetry)—the literal meaning of "*sutradhara*" is "holder of the strings or threads".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERichmond1998517-75] The performers were trained rigorously in vocal and physical technique.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERichmond1998518-76] There were no prohibitions against female performers; companies were all-male, all-female, and of mixed gender. Certain sentiments were considered inappropriate for men to enact, however, and were thought better suited to women. Some performers played characters their own age, while others played ages different from their own (whether younger or older). Of all the elements of theatre, the *Treatise* gives most attention to acting (*abhinaya*), which consists of two styles: realistic (*lokadharmi*) and conventional (*natyadharmi*), though the major focus is on the latter.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERichmond1998518-76][^cite_note-77]

Its drama is regarded as the highest achievement of [Sanskrit literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_literature).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrandon1993xvii-71] It utilised [stock characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock_character), such as the hero (*nayaka*), heroine (*nayika*), or clown (*vidusaka*). Actors may have specialised in a particular type. [Kālidāsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C4%81lid%C4%81sa) in the 1st century BCE, is arguably considered to be ancient [India](14533.md)'s greatest Sanskrit dramatist. Three famous romantic plays written by Kālidāsa are the *[Mālavikāgnimitram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81lavik%C4%81gnimitram)* (*Mālavikā and Agnimitra*), *[Vikramuurvashiiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vikramuurvashiiya)* (*Pertaining to Vikrama and Urvashi*), and *[Abhijñānaśākuntala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abhij%C3%B1%C4%81na%C5%9B%C4%81kuntala)* (*The Recognition of Shakuntala*). The last was inspired by a story in the *Mahabharata* and is the most famous. It was the first to be translated into [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language) and [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language). *[Śakuntalā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abhij%C3%B1%C4%81na%C5%9B%C4%81kuntalam)* (in English translation) influenced [Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goethe)'s *[Faust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goethe's_Faust)* (1808–1832).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrandon1993xvii-71]

The next great Indian dramatist was [Bhavabhuti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhavabhuti) (c. 7th century CE). He is said to have written the following three plays: *Malati-Madhava*, *Mahaviracharita* and *Uttar Ramacharita*. Among these three, the last two cover between them the entire epic of *Ramayana*. The powerful Indian emperor [Harsha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harsha) (606–648) is credited with having written three plays: the comedy *[Ratnavali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratnavali)*, *[Priyadarsika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priyadarsika)*, and the [Buddhist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist) drama *[Nagananda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagananda)*.

### East Asian theatre

Main articles: [Theatre of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_China), [Theatre of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_Japan), [Theater in Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theater_in_Korea), and [Theatre of Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_Vietnam)



The July 1858 production of *[Shibaraku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shibaraku)* is portrayed at the [Ichimura-za theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ichimura-za) in [Edo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo_\(Tokyo\)); [Triptych](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triptych) [woodblock print](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodblock_print) by [Utagawa Toyokuni III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kunisada)



Public performance in Jade Dragon Snow Mountain Open Air Theatre

The [Tang dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tang_dynasty) is sometimes known as "The Age of 1000 Entertainments". During this era, Ming Huang formed an acting school known as The [Pear Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pear_Garden) to produce a form of drama that was primarily musical. That is why actors are commonly called "Children of the Pear Garden". During the dynasty of Empress Ling, [shadow puppetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadow_puppetry) first emerged as a recognised form of theatre in China. There were two distinct forms of shadow puppetry, Pekingese (northern) and Cantonese (southern). The two styles were differentiated by the method of making the puppets and the positioning of the rods on the [puppets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puppet), as opposed to the type of [play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Play_\(theatre\)) performed by the puppets. Both styles generally performed plays depicting great adventure and fantasy, rarely was this very stylised form of theatre used for political propaganda.

Japanese forms of [Kabuki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabuki), [Nō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N%C5%8D), and [Kyōgen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ky%C5%8Dgen) developed in the 17th century CE.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDeal2007276-78]

Cantonese shadow puppets were the larger of the two. They were built using thick leather which created more substantial shadows. Symbolic colour was also very prevalent; a black face represented honesty, a red one bravery. The rods used to control Cantonese puppets were attached perpendicular to the puppets' heads. Thus, they were not seen by the audience when the shadow was created. Pekingese puppets were more delicate and smaller. They were created out of thin, translucent leather (usually taken from the belly of a donkey). They were painted with vibrant paints, thus they cast a very colourful shadow. The thin rods which controlled their movements were attached to a leather collar at the neck of the puppet. The rods ran parallel to the bodies of the puppet and then turned at a ninety degree angle to connect to the neck. While these rods were visible when the shadow was cast, they laid outside the shadow of the puppet; thus they did not interfere with the appearance of the figure. The rods are attached at the necks to facilitate the use of multiple heads with one body. When the heads were not being used, they were stored in a muslin book or fabric-lined box. The heads were always removed at night. This was in keeping with the old superstition that if left intact, the puppets would come to life at night. Some puppeteers went so far as to store the heads in one book and the bodies in another, to further reduce the possibility of reanimating puppets. Shadow puppetry is said to have reached its highest point of artistic development in the eleventh century before becoming a tool of the government.

In the [Song dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Song_dynasty), there were many popular plays involving acrobatics and music. These developed in the [Yuan dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuan_dynasty) into a more sophisticated form known as *[zaju](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaju)*, with a four- or five-act structure. Yuan drama spread across China and diversified into numerous regional forms, one of the best known of which is [Peking Opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peking_Opera) which is still popular today.

[Xiangsheng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xiangsheng) is a certain traditional Chinese comedic performance in the forms of monologue or dialogue.

### Indonesian theatre

Main articles: [Theatre of Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_Indonesia) and [Balinese theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balinese_theatre)



Rama and Shinta in [Wayang Wong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayang_Wong) performance near [Prambanan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prambanan) temple complex

In [Indonesia](14579.md), theatre performances have become an important part of local culture, theatre performances in Indonesia have been developed for thousands of years. Most of [Indonesia's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_Indonesia) oldest theatre forms are linked directly to local literary traditions (oral and written). The prominent [puppet theatres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puppet_theatre)—[wayang golek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayang_golek) (wooden rod-puppet play) of the [Sundanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sundanese_people#culture) and [wayang kulit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayang_kulit) (leather shadow-puppet play) of the [Javanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Javanese_culture) and [Balinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balinese_culture)—draw much of their repertoire from indigenised versions of the [Ramayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramayana) and [Mahabharata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahabharata). These tales also provide source material for the wayang wong (human theatre) of [Java](69336.md) and [Bali](4147.md), which uses actors. Some wayang golek performances, however, also present Muslim stories, called *menak*.[^cite_note-Rubin2001p184-79][^cite_note-80]

[Wayang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayang) is an ancient form of storytelling that renowned for its elaborate puppet/human and complex musical styles.[^cite_note-UNESCO-81] The earliest evidence is from the late 1st millennium CE, in medieval-era texts and archeological sites.[^cite_note-brandon143-82] The oldest known record that concerns wayang is from the 9th century. Around 840 AD an Old Javanese (Kawi) inscriptions called Jaha Inscriptions issued by Maharaja Sri Lokapala from [Mataram kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mataram_kingdom) in [Central Java](453584.md) mentions three sorts of performers: atapukan, aringgit, and abanol. Aringgit means Wayang puppet show, Atapukan means Mask dance show, and abanwal means joke art. Ringgit is described in an 11th-century Javanese poem as a leather shadow figure.

### Medieval Islamic traditions

Theatre in the [medieval Islamic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Islamic_world) included [puppet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puppet) theatre (which included hand puppets, [shadow plays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadow_play) and [marionette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marionette) productions) and live passion plays known as *[ta'ziyeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ta'ziyeh)*, where actors re-enact episodes from [Muslim history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_history). In particular, [Shia Islamic](26961.md) plays revolved around the *[istishhād](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shahid)* (martyrdom) of [Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali)'s sons [Hasan ibn Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasan_ibn_Ali) and [Husayn ibn Ali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Husayn_ibn_Ali). Secular plays were known as *akhraja*, recorded in medieval *[adab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adab_\(behavior\))* literature, though they were less common than puppetry and *ta'ziya* theatre.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMoreh1986565–601-83]

### Early modern and modern theatre in the West



*[Commedia dell'arte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commedia_dell'arte)* troupe [I Gelosi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Gelosi) performing, by [Hieronymus Francken I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hieronymus_Francken_I), c. 1590

Theatre took on many alternative forms in the West between the 15th and 19th centuries, including *[commedia dell'arte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commedia_dell'arte)* from [Italian theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_theatre), and [melodrama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melodrama). The general trend was away from the poetic drama of the Greeks and the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance) and toward a more naturalistic prose style of dialogue, especially following the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKuritz1988305-84]

Theatre took a big pause from 1642 to 1660 in England because of the [Puritan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puritan) Interregnum.[^cite_note-OUP-85] The rising anti-theatrical sentiment among Puritans saw [William Prynne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Prynne) write *[Histriomastix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Histriomastix)* (1633), the most notorious attack on theatre prior to the ban.[^cite_note-OUP-85] Viewing theatre as sinful, the Puritans ordered the [closure of London theatres in 1642](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_theatre_closure_1642).[^cite_note-86] On 24 January 1643, the actors protested against the ban by writing a pamphlet titled *The Actors remonstrance or complaint for the silencing of their profession, and banishment from their severall play-houses*.[^cite_note-87] This stagnant period ended once Charles II came back to the throne in 1660 in the [Restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restoration_\(England\)). Theatre (among other arts) exploded, with influence from French culture, since Charles had been exiled in France in the years previous to his reign.



The [Theatre Royal, Drury Lane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_Royal,_Drury_Lane) in the [West End](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_End_theatre) was opened in May 1663, it is the oldest theatre in London.

[^cite_note-Oldest-88]

In 1660, two companies were licensed to perform, the [Duke's Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duke's_Company) and the [King's Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_Company). Performances were held in converted buildings, such as [Lisle's Tennis Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisle's_Tennis_Court). The first [West End theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_End_theatre), known as Theatre Royal in [Covent Garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covent_Garden), London, was designed by [Thomas Killigrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Killigrew) and built on the site of the present [Theatre Royal, Drury Lane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_Royal,_Drury_Lane).[^cite_note-Oldest-88]

One of the big changes was the new theatre house. Instead of the type of the Elizabethan era, such as the [Globe Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globe_Theatre), round with no place for the actors to prepare for the next act and with no "theatre manners", the theatre house became transformed into a place of refinement, with a stage in front and stadium seating facing it. Since seating was no longer all the way around the stage, it became prioritised—some seats were obviously better than others. The king would have the best seat in the house: the very middle of the theatre, which got the widest view of the stage as well as the best way to see the point of view and vanishing point that the stage was constructed around. [Philippe Jacques de Loutherbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippe_Jacques_de_Loutherbourg) was one of the most influential set designers of the time because of his use of floor space and scenery.

Because of the turmoil before this time, there was still some controversy about what should and should not be put on the stage. [Jeremy Collier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Collier), a preacher, was one of the heads in this movement through his piece *A Short View of the Immorality and Profaneness of the English Stage*. The beliefs in this paper were mainly held by non-theatre goers and the remainder of the Puritans and very religious of the time. The main question was if seeing something immoral on stage affects behaviour in the lives of those who watch it, a controversy that is still playing out today.[^cite_note-89]

The seventeenth century also introduced women to the stage, which was considered inappropriate earlier. These women were regarded as celebrities (a new concept stemming from ideas on individualism that arose in the wake of [Renaissance Humanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_Humanism)) yet their presence on stage was still revolutionary. Consequently, critics viewed them as unladylike and some looked down upon them. Charles II did not like young men playing the parts of young women, so he asked that women play their own parts.[^cite_note-90] Because women were allowed on the stage, playwrights had more leeway with plot twists, like women dressing as men, and having narrow escapes from morally sticky situations as forms of comedy.



Billing for a [British theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_theatre) in 1829

Comedies were full of the young and very much in vogue, with the storyline following their love lives: commonly a young roguish hero professing his love to the chaste and free minded heroine near the end of the play, much like [Sheridan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Brinsley_Sheridan)'s *[The School for Scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_School_for_Scandal)*. Many of the comedies were fashioned after the French tradition, mainly Molière, again hailing back to the French influence brought back by the King and the Royals after their exile. [Molière](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moli%C3%A8re) was one of the top comedic playwrights of the time, revolutionising the way comedy was written and performed by combining Italian [commedia dell'arte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commedia_dell'arte) and [neoclassical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoclassicism) French comedy to create some of the longest lasting and most influential satiric comedies.[^cite_note-91] Tragedies were similarly victorious in their sense of righting political power, especially poignant because of the recent Restoration of the Crown.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlack2010533–535-92] They were also imitations of French tragedy, although the French had a larger distinction between comedy and tragedy, whereas the English fudged the lines occasionally and put some comedic parts in their tragedies. Common forms of non-comedic plays were sentimental comedies as well as something that would later be called *tragédie bourgeoise*, or [domestic tragedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestic_tragedy)—that is, the tragedy of common life—were more popular in England because they appealed more to English sensibilities.[^cite_note-93]

While [theatre troupes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_troupe) were formerly often travelling, the idea of the national theatre gained support in the 18th century, inspired by [Ludvig Holberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludvig_Holberg). The major promoter of the idea of the national theatre in Germany, and also of the *[Sturm und Drang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sturm_und_Drang)* poets, was [Abel Seyler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abel_Seyler), the owner of the [Hamburgische Entreprise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamburgische_Entreprise) and the [Seyler Theatre Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seyler_Theatre_Company).[^cite_note-abel-seyler-94]



The "Little House" of the [Vanemuine Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanemuine) from 1918 in [Tartu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tartu), [Estonia](28222445.md)

[^cite_note-95]

Through the [19th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nineteenth-century_theatre), the popular theatrical forms of [Romanticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism), [melodrama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melodrama), [Victorian burlesque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_burlesque) and the [well-made plays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-made_play) of [Scribe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eug%C3%A8ne_Scribe) and [Sardou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorien_Sardou) gave way to the [problem plays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Problem_play) of [Naturalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalism_\(theatre\)) and [Realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realism_\(theatre\)); the [farces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farce) of [Feydeau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feydeau); [Wagner's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wagner's) [operatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opera) *[Gesamtkunstwerk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gesamtkunstwerk)*; [musical theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_theatre) (including [Gilbert and Sullivan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert_and_Sullivan)'s operas); [F. C. Burnand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._C._Burnand)'s, [W. S. Gilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._S._Gilbert)'s and [Oscar Wilde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Wilde)'s drawing-room comedies; [Symbolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolism_\(arts\)); proto-[Expressionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expressionism) in the late works of [August Strindberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Strindberg) and [Henrik Ibsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrik_Ibsen);[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrockettHildy2003293–426-96] and [Edwardian musical comedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwardian_musical_comedy).

These trends continued through the [20th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twentieth-century_theatre) in the [realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realism_\(theatre\)) of [Stanislavski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanislavski) and [Lee Strasberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_Strasberg), the political theatre of [Erwin Piscator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erwin_Piscator) and [Bertolt Brecht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertolt_Brecht), the so-called [Theatre of the Absurd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_the_Absurd) of [Samuel Beckett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Beckett) and [Eugène Ionesco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eug%C3%A8ne_Ionesco), American and British musicals, the collective creations of companies of actors and directors such as [Joan Littlewood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_Littlewood)'s [Theatre Workshop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_Workshop), experimental and [postmodern theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postmodern_theatre) of [Robert Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Wilson_\(director\)) and [Robert Lepage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Lepage), the [postcolonial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postcolonial) theatre of [August Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Wilson) or [Tomson Highway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomson_Highway), and [Augusto Boal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augusto_Boal)'s [Theatre of the Oppressed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_the_Oppressed).

## Types

### Drama

Main article: [Drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drama)



Depiction of a scene from Shakespeare's play *[Richard III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_III_\(play\))*

[Drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drama) is the specific [mode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mode_\(literature\)) of [fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiction) [represented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mimesis) in [performance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Performance).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEElam198098-97] The term comes from a [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) word meaning "[action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_\(philosophy\))", which is derived from the verb δράω, *dráō*, "to do" or "to act". The enactment of drama in theatre, performed by [actors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actor) on a [stage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stage_\(theatre\)) before an [audience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audience), presupposes [collaborative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collaborative) modes of production and a [collective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collective) form of reception. The [structure of dramatic texts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dramatic_structure), unlike other forms of [literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literature), is directly influenced by this collaborative production and collective reception.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPfister200011-98]

The [early modern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Renaissance_theatre) [tragedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragedy) *[Hamlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamlet)* (1601) by [Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare) and the [classical Athenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_ancient_Greece) tragedy *[Oedipus Rex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oedipus_Rex)* (c. 429 BCE) by [Sophocles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophocles) are among the masterpieces of the art of drama.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFergusson19682–3-99] A modern example is *[Long Day's Journey into Night](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Day's_Journey_into_Night)* by [Eugene O'Neill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_O'Neill) (1956).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBurt200830–35-100]

Considered as a genre of [poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetry) in general, the dramatic mode has been contrasted with the [epic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_poetry) and the [lyrical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyric_poetry) modes ever since [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle)'s *[Poetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetics_\(Aristotle\))* (c. 335 BCE); the earliest work of [dramatic theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dramatic_theory).[^cite_note-101] The use of "drama" in the narrow sense to designate a specific *type* of [play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Play_\(theatre\)) dates from the [19th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nineteenth-century_theatre). Drama in this sense refers to a play that is *neither* a comedy nor a tragedy—for example, [Zola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89mile_Zola)'s *[Thérèse Raquin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Th%C3%A9r%C3%A8se_Raquin)* (1873) or [Chekhov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anton_Chekhov)'s *[Ivanov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivanov_\(play\))* (1887). In Ancient Greece however, the word *drama* encompassed all theatrical plays, tragic, comic, or anything in between.

Drama is often combined with [music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_music) and [dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dance): the drama in [opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opera) is generally sung throughout; [musicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_theatre) generally include both spoken [dialogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialogue) and [songs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Song); and some forms of drama have [incidental music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incidental_music) or musical accompaniment underscoring the dialogue ([melodrama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melodrama) and Japanese [Nō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N%C5%8D), for example).[^cite_note-102] In certain periods of history (the ancient [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome) and modern [Romantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism)) some dramas have been written to be [read](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closet_drama) rather than performed.[^cite_note-103] In [improvisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Improvisational_theatre), the drama does not pre-exist the moment of performance; performers devise a dramatic script spontaneously before an audience.[^cite_note-104]

### Musical theatre

Main article: [Musical theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_theatre)



*[Cats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cats_\(musical\))* at the [London Palladium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Palladium)

[Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music) and theatre have had a close relationship since ancient times—[Athenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Athens) [tragedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragedy), for example, was a form of [dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dance)-[drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drama) that employed a [chorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_chorus) whose parts were sung (to the accompaniment of an *[aulos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aulos)*—an instrument comparable to the modern [oboe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oboe)), as were some of the actors' responses and their 'solo songs' ([monodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monodies)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERehm1992150n7-105] Modern [musical theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_theatre) is a form of theatre that also combines music, spoken dialogue, and dance. It emerged from [comic opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comic_opera) (especially [Gilbert and Sullivan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert_and_Sullivan)), [variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variety_show), [vaudeville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaudeville), and [music hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_hall) genres of the late [19th](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nineteenth-century_theatre) and early [20th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twentieth-century_theatre).[^cite_note-106][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJones20034–11-107]

After the [Edwardian musical comedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwardian_musical_comedy) that began in the 1890s, the [Princess Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princess_Theatre,_New_York_City) musicals of the early 20th century, and comedies in the 1920s and 1930s (such as the works of [Rodgers and Hammerstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodgers_and_Hammerstein)), with *[Oklahoma!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma!)* (1943), musicals moved in a more dramatic direction.[^cite_note-108] Famous musicals over the subsequent decades included *[My Fair Lady](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/My_Fair_Lady)* (1956), *[West Side Story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Side_Story)* (1957), *[The Fantasticks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Fantasticks)* (1960), *[Hair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hair_\(musical\))* (1967), *[A Chorus Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Chorus_Line)* (1975), *[Les Misérables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_Mis%C3%A9rables_\(musical\))* (1980), *[Cats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cats_\(musical\))* (1981), *[Into the Woods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Into_the_Woods)* (1986), and *[The Phantom of the Opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Phantom_of_the_Opera_\(1986_musical\))* (1986),[^cite_note-109] as well as more contemporary hits including *[Rent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rent_\(musical\))* (1994), *[The Lion King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lion_King_\(musical\))* (1997), *[Wicked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wicked_\(musical\))* (2003), *[Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton_\(musical\))* (2015) and *[Frozen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frozen_\(musical\))* (2018).

Musical theatre may be produced on an intimate scale [Off-Broadway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Off-Broadway), in [regional theatres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community_theatre), and elsewhere, but it often includes spectacle. For instance, [Broadway](725252.md) and [West End](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_End_theatre) musicals often include lavish costumes and sets supported by multimillion-dollar budgets.

### Comedy

Main article: [Comedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comedy)



Theatrical masks of [Tragedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragedy) and [Comedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comedy), a Roman mosaic, 2nd century AD, at the [Capitoline Museums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitoline_Museums) in [Rome](25458.md)

Theatre productions that use [humour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humour) as a vehicle to tell a story qualify as comedies. This may include a modern [farce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farce) such as *[Boeing Boeing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing-Boeing_\(play\))* or a classical play such as *[As You Like It](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/As_You_Like_It)*. Theatre expressing bleak, controversial or taboo subject matter in a deliberately humorous way is referred to as [black comedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_comedy). Black Comedy can have several genres like slapstick humour, dark and sarcastic comedy.

### Tragedy

Main article: [Tragedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragedy)

> Tragedy, then, is an imitation of an action that is serious, complete, and of a certain magnitude: in language embellished with each kind of artistic ornament, the several kinds being found in separate parts of the play; in the form of action, not of narrative; through pity and fear effecting the proper purgation of these emotions.

— [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle), *[Poetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetics_\(Aristotle\))*[^cite_note-110]

Aristotle's phrase "several kinds being found in separate parts of the play" is a reference to the structural origins of drama. In it the spoken parts were written in the [Attic dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attic_dialect) whereas the choral (recited or sung) ones in the [Doric dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doric_Greek), these discrepancies reflecting the differing religious origins and [poetic metres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre_\(poetry\)) of the parts that were fused into a new entity, the theatrical *drama*.

[Tragedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragedy) refers to a specific [tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetic_tradition) of [drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drama) that has played a unique and important role historically in the self-definition of [Western civilisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_culture).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBanham19981118-111][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilliams196614–16-112] That tradition has been multiple and discontinuous, yet the term has often been used to invoke a powerful effect of [cultural identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_identity) and historical continuity—"the [Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Athens) and the [Elizabethans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabethans), in one cultural form; [Hellenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period) and [Christians](18337522.md), in a common activity", as [Raymond Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_Williams) puts it.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilliams196616-113] From its obscure origins in the [theatres of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_ancient_Greece) 2,500 years ago, from which there survives only a fraction of the work of [Aeschylus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeschylus), [Sophocles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophocles) and [Euripides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euripides), through its singular articulations in the works of [Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare), [Lope de Vega](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lope_de_Vega), [Racine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Racine), and [Schiller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schiller), to the more recent [naturalistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalism_\(theatre\)) tragedy of [Strindberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strindberg), [Beckett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Beckett)'s [modernist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernist) meditations on death, loss and suffering, and [Müller's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heiner_M%C3%BCller) [postmodernist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postmodernist) reworkings of the tragic canon, tragedy has remained an important site of cultural experimentation, negotiation, struggle, and change.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilliams196613–84-114][^cite_note-FOOTNOTETaxidou2004193–209-115] In the wake of Aristotle's *[Poetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetics_\(Aristotle\))* (335 BCE), tragedy has been used to make [genre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genre) distinctions, whether at the scale of [poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetry) in general (where the tragic divides against [epic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_poetry) and [lyric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyric_poetry)) or at the scale of the drama (where tragedy is opposed to [comedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comedy_\(drama\))). In the [modern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernity) era, tragedy has also been defined against drama, [melodrama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melodrama), [the tragicomic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragicomedy), and [epic theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_theatre).[^cite_note-116]

### Improvisation

Main article: [Improvisational theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Improvisational_theatre)



[Dario Fo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dario_Fo), one of the most widely performed playwrights in modern theatre, received international acclaim for his highly [improvisational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Improvisational_theatre) style.

[^cite_note-117]

[^cite_note-118]

&#x20;He was awarded the [Nobel Prize for Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_for_Literature) in 1997.

[^cite_note-119]

Improvisation has been a consistent feature of theatre, with the Commedia dell'arte in the sixteenth century being recognised as the first improvisation form. Popularised by [1997 Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1997_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature) winner [Dario Fo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dario_Fo) and troupes such as the [Upright Citizens Brigade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upright_Citizens_Brigade) improvisational theatre continues to evolve with many different streams and philosophies.

[Keith Johnstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keith_Johnstone) and [Viola Spolin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viola_Spolin) are recognised as the first teachers of improvisation in modern times, with Johnstone exploring improvisation as an alternative to scripted theatre and Spolin and her successors exploring improvisation principally as a tool for developing dramatic work or skills or as a form for situational comedy. Spolin also became interested in how the process of learning improvisation was applicable to the development of human potential.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGordon2006\[httpsbooksgooglecombooksidFmAue-VUMmYClpgPA194pgPA194_194\]-120]

Spolin's son, [Paul Sills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Sills) popularised improvisational theatre as a theatrical art form when he founded, as its first director, [The Second City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Second_City) in Chicago.

## Theories

Main article: [Dramatic theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dramatic_theory)



Village feast with theatre performance c. 1600

Having been an important part of human culture for more than 2,500 years, theatre has evolved a wide range of different [theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dramatic_theory) and practices. Some are related to political or spiritual ideologies, while others are based purely on "artistic" concerns. Some processes focus on a story, some on theatre as event, and some on theatre as catalyst for social change.&#x20;

The [classical Greek philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_philosophy) [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle), in his seminal treatise, *[Poetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetics_\(Aristotle\))* (c. 335 BCE) is the earliest-surviving example and its arguments have influenced theories of theatre ever since.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDukore197431-21][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJanko1987ix-22] In it, he offers an account of what he calls "poetry" (a term which in Greek literally means "making" and in this context includes [drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drama)—[comedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comedy_\(drama\)), [tragedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragedy), and the [satyr play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satyr_play)—as well as [lyric poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyric_poetry), [epic poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_poetry), and the [dithyramb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dithyramb)). He examines its "first principles" and identifies its [genres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genre) and basic elements; his analysis of tragedy constitutes the core of the discussion.[^cite_note-121]

Aristotle argues that tragedy consists of six qualitative parts, which are (in order of importance) *[mythos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mythos_\(Aristotle\))* or "plot", *[ethos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethos)* or "character", *[dianoia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dianoia)* or "thought", *[lexis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexis_\(Aristotle\))* or "diction", *[melos](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/melos)* or "song", and *[opsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opsis)* or "spectacle".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECarlson199319-122][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJanko1987xx,_7–10-123] "Although Aristotle's *Poetics* is universally acknowledged in the [Western](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_culture) critical tradition", [Marvin Carlson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marvin_Carlson) explains, "almost every detail about his seminal work has aroused divergent opinions."[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECarlson199316-124] Important [theatre practitioners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_practitioner) of the [20th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twentieth-century_theatre) include [Konstantin Stanislavski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konstantin_Stanislavski), [Vsevolod Meyerhold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vsevolod_Meyerhold), [Jacques Copeau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Copeau), [Edward Gordon Craig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Gordon_Craig), [Bertolt Brecht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertolt_Brecht), [Antonin Artaud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonin_Artaud), [Joan Littlewood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_Littlewood), [Peter Brook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Brook), [Jerzy Grotowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerzy_Grotowski), [Augusto Boal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augusto_Boal), [Eugenio Barba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugenio_Barba), [Dario Fo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dario_Fo), [Viola Spolin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viola_Spolin), [Keith Johnstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keith_Johnstone) and [Robert Wilson (director)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Wilson_\(director\)).

Stanislavski treated the theatre as an [art-form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_arts) that is [autonomous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medium_specificity) from [literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literature) and one in which the [playwright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playwright)'s contribution should be respected as that of only one of an ensemble of creative artists.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBenedetti1999124,_202-125][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBenedetti20086-126][^cite_note-FOOTNOTECarnicke1998162-127][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGauss19992-128][^cite_note-129] His innovative contribution to modern acting theory has remained at the core of mainstream [western](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_culture) performance training for much of the last century.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBanham19981032-130][^cite_note-FOOTNOTECarnicke19981-131][^cite_note-FOOTNOTECounsell199624–25-132][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGordon200637–40-133][^cite_note-FOOTNOTELeach200429-134] That many of the precepts of his [system of actor training](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanislavski's_system) seem to be common sense and self-evident testifies to its hegemonic success.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECounsell199625-135] Actors frequently employ his basic concepts without knowing they do so.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECounsell199625-135] Thanks to its promotion and elaboration by acting teachers who were former students and the many translations of his theoretical writings, Stanislavski's 'system' acquired an unprecedented ability to cross cultural boundaries and developed an international reach, dominating debates about acting in Europe and the United States.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBanham19981032-130][^cite_note-FOOTNOTECarnicke19981,_167-136][^cite_note-FOOTNOTECounsell199624-137][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMillingLey20011-138] Many actors routinely equate his 'system' with the North American [Method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Method_acting), although the latter's exclusively psychological techniques contrast sharply with Stanislavski's multivariant, holistic and [psychophysical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychophysiology) approach, which explores character and action both from the 'inside out' and the 'outside in' and treats the actor's mind and body as parts of a continuum.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBenedetti2005147–148-139][^cite_note-FOOTNOTECarnicke19981,_8-140]

## Technical aspects



A theatre stage building in the backstage of [Vienna State Opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna_State_Opera)

Main article: [Stagecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stagecraft)

Theatre presupposes [collaborative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collaborative) modes of production and a [collective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collective) form of reception. The [structure of dramatic texts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dramatic_structure), unlike other forms of [literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literature), is directly influenced by this collaborative production and collective reception.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPfister200011-98] The production of [plays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Play_\(theatre\)) usually involves contributions from a [playwright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playwright), [director](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_director), a [cast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cast_member) of [actors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actor), and a technical [production team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Production_team) that includes a [scenic or set designer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_designer), [lighting designer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lighting_designer), [costume designer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Costume_designer), [sound designer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_design), [stage manager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stage_manager), [production manager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Production_management_\(performing_arts\)) and [technical director](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_director#Theatre). Depending on the production, this team may also include a [composer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composer), [dramaturg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dramaturg), [video designer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_design) or [fight director](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fight_director).



The rotating auditorium of the open air [Pyynikki Summer Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyynikki_Summer_Theatre) in [Tampere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tampere), [Finland](10577.md)

Stagecraft is a generic term referring to the technical aspects of theatrical, film, and video production. It includes, but is not limited to, constructing and rigging scenery, hanging and focusing of lighting, design and procurement of costumes, makeup, procurement of props, stage management, and recording and mixing of sound. Stagecraft is distinct from the wider umbrella term of scenography. Considered a technical rather than an artistic field, it relates primarily to the practical implementation of a designer's artistic vision.



Theatre mechanical curtain puller

In its most basic form, stagecraft is managed by a single person (often the stage manager of a smaller production) who arranges all scenery, costumes, lighting, and sound, and organises the cast. At a more professional level, for example in modern Broadway houses, stagecraft is managed by hundreds of skilled carpenters, painters, electricians, stagehands, stitchers, wigmakers, and the like. This modern form of stagecraft is highly technical and specialised: it comprises many subdisciplines and a vast trove of history and tradition. The majority of stagecraft lies between these two extremes. Regional theatres and larger community theatres will generally have a technical director and a complement of designers, each of whom has a direct hand in their respective designs.

### Repertory companies



[Theatre Royal, Drury Lane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_Royal,_Drury_Lane), London, c. 1821

While most modern theatre companies rehearse one piece of theatre at a time, perform that piece for a set "run", retire the piece, and begin rehearsing a new show, [repertory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repertory) companies rehearse multiple shows at one time. These companies are able to perform these various pieces upon request and often perform works for years before retiring them. Most dance companies operate on this repertory system. The [Royal National Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_National_Theatre) in London performs on a repertory system.

Repertory theatre generally involves a group of similarly accomplished actors, and relies more on the reputation of the group than on an individual star actor. It also typically relies less on strict control by a director and less on adherence to theatrical conventions, since actors who have worked together in multiple productions can respond to each other without relying as much on convention or external direction.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPeterson1982-141]

### Other terminology



[La Scala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Scala) in [Milan](36511.md), Italy, is the world's most famous opera house.

[^cite_note-142]

A theatre company is an organisation that produces theatrical performances,[^cite_note-compdef-5] as distinct from a [theatre troupe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_troupe) (or acting company), which is a group of theatrical performers working together.[^cite_note-troupedef1-6] A [touring company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Touring_theatre) is an independent theatre or dance company that travels, often internationally, being presented at a different theatre venue in each city.[^cite_note-143]

In order to put on a piece of theatre, both a theatre company and a [theatre venue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_\(structure\)) are needed. When a theatre company is the sole company in residence at a theatre venue, this theatre (and its corresponding theatre company) are called a resident theatre or a producing theatre, because the venue produces its own work. Other theatre companies, as well as dance companies, who do not have their own theatre venue, perform at rental theatres or at presenting theatres. Both rental and presenting theatres have no full-time resident companies. They do, however, sometimes have one or more part-time resident companies, in addition to other independent partner companies who arrange to use the space when available. A rental theatre allows the independent companies to seek out the space, while a presenting theatre seeks out the independent companies to support their work by presenting them on their stage.

Some performance groups perform in non-theatrical spaces. Such performances can take place outside or inside, in a non-traditional performance space, and include [street theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_theatre), and [site-specific theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Site-specific_theatre). Non-traditional venues can be used to create more immersive or meaningful environments for audiences. They can sometimes be modified more heavily than traditional theatre venues, or can accommodate different kinds of equipment, lighting and sets.[^cite_note-144]
