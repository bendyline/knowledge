France is [divided](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Administrative_divisions_of_France) into eighteen administrative regions ([French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language): *régions administratives*, singular *région administrative* or just *région*, [\[ʁeʒjɔ̃\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/French)). Twelve of which are located in the [Hexagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_France) (in Europe), another one is [Corsica](5714828.md), while the other five are [overseas regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overseas_departments_and_regions_of_France) (not to be confused with the [overseas collectivities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overseas_collectivity), which have a semi-autonomous status).[^cite_note-INSEE-1]

All of the thirteen metropolitan administrative regions (including [Corsica](5714828.md) as of 2019) are further subdivided into two to thirteen administrative [departments](38523.md), with the [prefect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefect_\(France\)) of each region's administrative centre's department also acting as the regional prefect. The overseas regions administratively consist of only one department each and hence also have the status of overseas departments.

Most administrative regions also have the status of regional [territorial collectivities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territorial_collectivity), which comes with a [local government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_government), with departmental and communal collectivities below the regional level. The exceptions are Corsica, [French Guiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Guiana), [Mayotte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayotte) and [Martinique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martinique), where region and department functions are managed by single local governments having consolidated jurisdiction and which are known as [single territorial collectivities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_territorial_collectivity).

## History

Further information: [Territorial evolution of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territorial_evolution_of_France) and [Decentralisation in France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decentralisation_in_France)

### 1982–2015

The term *région* was officially created by the [Law of Decentralisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decentralisation_in_France) (2 March 1982), which also gave regions their legal status. The first direct elections for regional representatives took place on 16 March 1986.[^cite_note-2]

Between 1982 and 2015, there were 22 regions in Metropolitan France. Before 2011, there were four [overseas regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overseas_department_and_region) ([French Guiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Guiana), [Guadeloupe](12343.md), [Martinique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martinique), and [Réunion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%A9union)); in 2011 [Mayotte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayotte) became the fifth.

### Reform and mergers of regions

In 2014, the French parliament passed a law reducing the number of metropolitan regions from 22 to 13 effective 1 January 2016.[^cite_note-5]

The law gave interim names for most of the new regions by combining the names of the former regions, e.g. the region composed of [Aquitaine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquitaine), [Poitou-Charentes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poitou-Charentes) and [Limousin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limousin) was temporarily called *Aquitaine-Limousin-Poitou-Charentes*. However, the combined region of Upper and Lower Normandy was simply called "Normandy" (*Normandie*). Permanent names were proposed by the new regional councils by 1 July 2016 and new names confirmed by the *[Conseil d'État](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conseil_d'%C3%89tat_\(France\))* by 30 September 2016.[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7] The legislation defining the new regions also allowed the Centre region to officially change its name to "[Centre-Val de Loire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre-Val_de_Loire)" with effect from January 2015.[^cite_note-8] Two regions, [Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes](45093325.md) and [Bourgogne-Franche-Comté](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourgogne-Franche-Comt%C3%A9), opted to retain their interim names.[^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10]

## Role

Regions lack separate legislative authority and therefore cannot write their own statutory law. They levy their own taxes and, in return, receive part of their budget from the central government, which gives them a portion of the taxes it levies. They also have considerable budgets managed by a [regional council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_councils_of_France) *(conseil régional)* made up of representatives voted into office in regional elections.

A region's primary responsibility is to build and furnish high schools. In March 2004, the French central government unveiled a controversial plan to transfer regulation of certain categories of non-teaching school staff to the regional authorities. Critics of this plan contended that tax revenue was insufficient to pay for the resulting costs, and that such measures would increase regional inequalities.

In addition, regions have considerable discretionary power over infrastructural spending, e.g., education, public transit, universities and research, and assistance to business owners. This has meant that the heads of wealthy regions such as [Île-de-France](1716427.md) or [Rhône-Alpes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rh%C3%B4ne-Alpes) can be high-profile positions.

Proposals to give regions limited legislative autonomy have met with considerable resistance; others propose transferring certain powers from the departments to their respective regions, leaving the former with limited authority.

## Overseas regions

**Overseas region** ([French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language): *Région d'outre-mer*) is a recent designation, given to the [overseas departments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overseas_department) that have similar powers to those of the regions of [metropolitan France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_France). As integral parts of the [French Republic](5843419.md), they are represented in the [National Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_National_Assembly), [Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Senate) and [Economic and Social Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Economic_and_Social_Council), elect a Member of the [European Parliament](9581.md) (MEP) and use the [euro](9472.md) as their currency.

Although these territories have had these political powers since 1982, when France's [decentralisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decentralisation) policy dictated that they be given elected [regional councils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_councils_of_France) along with other regional powers, the designation *overseas regions* dates only to the 2003 constitutional change; indeed, the new wording of the constitution aims to give no precedence to either appellation *overseas department* or *overseas region*, although the second is still virtually unused by French media.

The following have overseas region status:

- in the [Indian Ocean](14580.md) ([Africa](5334607.md)):&#x20;

  - **[Mayotte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayotte)**

  - **[Réunion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%A9union)**

- in the [Americas](29833.md):&#x20;

  - **[French Guiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Guiana)** in [South America](26769.md)

  - **[Guadeloupe](12343.md)** in the [Antilles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antilles) ([Caribbean](18956035.md))

  - **[Martinique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martinique)** in the [Antilles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antilles) ([Caribbean](18956035.md))

^ [Saint Pierre and Miquelon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Pierre_and_Miquelon) (located just south of Newfoundland, Canada, in North America), once an overseas department, was demoted to a [territorial collectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territorial_collectivity) in 1985.
