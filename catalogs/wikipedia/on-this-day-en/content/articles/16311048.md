The **Djiboutians** ([French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language): *Djiboutiens*, [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language): جيبوتيون) are the native inhabitants of [Djibouti](17207794.md), as well as the global diaspora of Djibouti. The country is mainly composed of two ethnic groups, the [Issa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Issa_\(clan\)) [Somali](1571696.md) and the [Afar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afar_people). It has many languages - though [Somali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_language) and [Afar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afar_language) are the most widely spoken ones, [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic) and [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language) serve as the official languages. There is a small Djiboutian diaspora in [North America](21139.md), [Europe](9239.md), [Middle East](19323.md), [Asia](689.md) and [Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australia_\(continent\)).

## Demographics



A [Somali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_people) boy from Djibouti wearing a traditional [turban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turban)

Djibouti has a population of about 884,017 inhabitants.[^cite_note-UN_WPP-4][^cite_note-UN_WPP_2022-5] It is a [multiethnic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiethnic) country. The local population grew rapidly during the latter half of the 20th century, increasing from about 69,589 in 1955 to around 869,099 by 2015. The two largest ethnic groups are the [Somalis](1571696.md) (60%) and [Afars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afar_people) (35%). The [Somali clan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_clan) component is mainly composed of the [Issa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Issa_\(clan\)), followed by the [Gadabuursi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gadabuursi) and the [Isaaq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaaq).[^cite_note-6] The remaining 5% of Djibouti's population primarily consists of Djiboutian [Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab), Ethiopians and Europeans (French and Italians). Approximately 76% of local residents are urban dwellers; the remainder are [pastoralists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastoralism).[^cite_note-CIA-3]

## Culture

Main article: [Culture of Djibouti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Djibouti)

Djiboutians' culture is primarily rooted in Somali and Afar traditions. They speak different [Cushitic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cushitic_languages), which are part of the larger [Afro-Asiatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afroasiatic_languages) language family. Historically, they have been nomadic [pastoralists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastoralists). However, recently the population has become urbanized - today, more than half live in the [capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Djibouti_\(city\)), along with the nearby towns and villages of the interior. Poetry has been traditionally recited in the villages by special readers called gabaye. This was a way of recording the community's history and customs, as well as current events.

### Cuisine

Main article: [Djiboutian cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Djiboutian_cuisine)

[Djiboutian cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Djiboutian_cuisine) is a mixture of [Somali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_cuisine), Afar, [Yemeni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yemeni_cuisine), and [French cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_cuisine), with some additional [South Asian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Asian_cuisine) (especially [Indian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_cuisine)) culinary influences. Local dishes are commonly prepared using Middle Eastern spices, ranging from [saffron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saffron) to [cinnamon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinnamon). Spicy dishes come in many variations, from the traditional *Fah-fah* or "*Soupe Djiboutienne*" (spicy boiled beef soup), to the *yetakelt wet* (spicy mixed vegetable stew). *Xalwo* (pronounced "halwo") or [halva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halva) is a popular confection eaten during festive occasions, such as [Eid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eid_ul-Fitr) celebrations or wedding receptions. Halva is made from [sugar](27712.md), [corn starch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corn_starch), [cardamom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardamom) powder, [nutmeg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutmeg) powder, and [ghee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghee). [Peanuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peanut) are sometimes added to enhance texture and flavour.[^cite_note-7] After meals, homes are traditionally perfumed using [incense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incense) (*cuunsi*) or [frankincense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankincense) (*lubaan*), which is prepared inside an incense burner referred to as a *[dabqaad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dabqaad)*.

### Music

Main article: [Music of Djibouti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Djibouti)

&#x20;Djiboutian music has Somali and Afar roots. Most Somali songs follow the [pentatonic scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentatonic_scale). That is, they only use five [pitches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_\(music\)) per [octave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octave) in contrast to a [heptatonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heptatonic_scale) (seven note) scale, often found in [Western music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_culture#Music). Somali music could be mistaken, when it is first heard, for the sounds of nearby regions such as Ethiopia, [Sudan](27421.md) or the [Arabian Peninsula](47858.md), but it is ultimately recognisable by its unique tunes and styles. Traditional Afar music resembles the folk music of other parts of the [Horn of Africa](62142.md) such as [Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Ethiopia); it also contains elements of [Arabic music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_music). The history of Djibouti is recorded in the poetry and songs of its nomadic people, and goes back to a time when the peoples of Djibouti traded hides and skins for the perfumes and spices of ancient [Egypt](8087628.md), [India](14533.md), and [China](5405.md). Afar Oral Literature is also quite musical. Popular Djiboutian musicians include [Nima Djama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nima_Djama), [Abdo Xamar Qoodh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdo_Xamar_Qoodh), [Mohamed Ali Fourchette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohamed_Ali_Fourchette), [Abdallah Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdallah_Lee), [Shay Lia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shay_Lia), and [Xabiiba Cabdilaahi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xabiiba_Cabdilaahi).&#x20;

### Cinema

Main article: [Cinema of Djibouti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinema_of_Djibouti)

Storytelling is an ancient custom in Djiboutian culture. This tradition is continued by a love of cinema. The earliest forms of public film display in Djibouti were in [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language). In the 1920s, the first local movie theatres opened, during a time when [Djibouti City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Djibouti_\(city\)) began to grow in size.[^cite_note-8] Film theatres became a place where local residents could watch movies in a relaxed atmosphere. With the development of the local film industry, additional theatres were launched. Among these establishments were the *Eden* in 1934, *Olympia* in 1939, *Le Paris* in 1965, and *Al Hilal* in 1975.

During the 1970s, the capital city had five movie theatres, with one in each district. A few local attempts at shooting films were also concurrently carried out with the participation of local actors. One of these was *Burta Djinka*, a film in [Somali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_language) directed by G. Borg in 1972. Following independence in 1977, a growing number of government-owned production and distribution companies as well as actual projection theatres sprang up.

In the 1990s two of the biggest cinemas, Odeon and Olympia, closed their doors.[^cite_note-9]

## Languages

Main article: [Languages of Djibouti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Djibouti)

The languages of Djibouti include [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic) and [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language) (official), and [Somali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_language) and [Afar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afar_language) (primary), which are the mother tongues of the Somali and Afar ethnic groups, respectively. Both languages belong to the larger [Afroasiatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afroasiatic_languages) family.

## Religion

Religion in Djibouti (2016)

1. [Islam](6037917.md) (94.0%)

2. Other (6.00%)

Main article: [Religion in Djibouti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_Djibouti)

[Islam](6037917.md) entered the region very early on, as a result of the increase in trade with Muslim traders in the [Red Sea](26191.md) region. In 1900, during the early part of the colonial era, there were virtually no [Christians](18337522.md) in the area, with only about 100–300 followers coming from the schools and orphanages of the few Catholic missions in [French Somaliland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Somaliland). Islam is the driving force for unity among the various ethnic groups from different parts of the country, and has significantly shaped the values and traditions of Djibouti.
