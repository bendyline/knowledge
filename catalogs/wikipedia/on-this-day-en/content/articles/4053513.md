**Indigenous peoples in Colombia** ([Spanish](26825.md): *Pueblos indígenas en Colombia*), also known as **Native Colombians** ([Spanish](26825.md): *Colombianos nativos*), are the [Indigenous peoples of the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_the_Americas) whose ancestors lived in [Colombia](5222.md) before the 16th-century [Spanish colonization of Colombia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_conquest_of_New_Granada).

Estimates of the percentage of Colombians who are Indigenous vary, from 3% or 1.5 million to 10% or 5 million. According to the [2018 Colombian census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Colombian_census), they comprise 4.4% of the country's population, belonging to 115 different [tribal nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_South_America), up from 3.4% in the [2005 Colombian census](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censo%20de%20Colombia%20de%202005).[^cite_note-iwgia-1][^cite_note-7] However, a [Latinobarómetro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latinobar%C3%B3metro) survey from the year 2024 found that 75 people or 7.1% of Colombian respondents self-identified as Indigenous.[^cite_note-8] The percentage of Indigenous peoples has been growing since an all-time low of 1965, where it was estimated only 1% of Colombians were Indigenous.[^cite_note-9]

Approximately two-thirds of the registered Indigenous peoples live in [La Guajira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Guajira_Department), [Cauca](549662.md), [Nariño](549702.md), [Córdoba](549670.md) and [Sucre Departments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sucre_Department). More than 70 distinct Indigenous ethnic groups live in the sparsely populated [Amazon basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_basin) of Colombia.[^cite_note-iwgia-1]

Both historically and in recent times, Indigenous peoples have endured have [violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Violence_against_Indigenous_peoples_in_Colombia) and oppression, ranging from land theft to massacres to the targeted killings of Indigenous activists and politicians.[^cite_note-violence-10]

## Population history

In the [pre-Columbian era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Columbian_cultures_of_Colombia), the total population of Colombia had an estimated 6 million people.[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12] However, after [Spanish conquest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_conquest_of_New_Granada), the population of Colombia was lowered to only 750,000 people in which Native people made up 80% of the population, at 600,000.[^cite_note-13] That percentage would lower after [independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colombian_War_of_Independence), when the population grew to 1.327 million, of which Native people made up 53% of the population, at 700,000.[^cite_note-14]

In the 1912 census, the Native population accounted for 6.3% of the people, down from the 17.8% reported in 1852. By the 1993 census, that number had further lowered to 1%. However, increased recognition from the government made the proportion of registered Indigenous people grow to 3.4% in the 2005 census and further to 4.3% in the 2018 census.[^cite_note-Palacios_1-15] The demographic decline can be explained by liberal policies implemented by new republican elites, which tried to abolish Indigenous collective land ownership, which had been recognized by the [Spanish monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_monarchy), and they forced Natives to assimilate in mainstream national culture.[^cite_note-16]

As of 2023, the total population of Colombia has grown significantly to around 52 million people in which full-blooded Native people are estimated to make up around 10% of the population, at 5.2 million. The increase is caused by the raised awareness among Colombians about their Indigenous identity and from the 1991 constitution, which gave more legal rights to Indigenous communities.[^cite_note-17]

Despite the reduction in the percentage of the total population, Native people make up a large part of the genetic ancestry of Colombians. A study determined that the average Colombian (of all races) has a mixture of 47% Amerindian, 42% European, and 11% African,[^cite_note-Rojas2010-18] with Native people having the most significant contribution in the study.

## History

Some theories claim the earliest human habitation of South America to be as early as 43,000 BC, but the current scholarly consensus among archaeologists is that human habitation in South America only dates back to around 15,000 BC at the earliest. Anthropologist [Tom Dillehay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Dillehay) dates the earliest hunter-gatherer cultures on the continent at almost 10,000 BC, during the late [Pleistocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleistocene) and early [Holocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocene) periods.[^cite_note-loc-22] According to his evidence based on rock shelters, Colombia's first human inhabitants were probably concentrated along the Caribbean coast and on the Andean highland slopes.[^cite_note-loc-22] By that time, these regions were forested and had a climate resembling today's.[^cite_note-loc-22] Dillehay has noted that [Tibitó](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibit%C3%B3), located just north of [Bogotá](211271.md), is one of the oldest known and most widely accepted sites of early human occupation in Colombia, dating from about 9,790 BC. There is evidence that the highlands of Colombia were occupied by significant numbers of human foragers by 9,000 BC, with permanent village settlement in northern Colombia by 2,000 BC.[^cite_note-loc-22]

Beginning in the 1st millennium BC, groups of [Amerindians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amerindian) including the [Muisca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_people), [Quimbaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quimbaya_civilization), [Tairona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tairona), [Calima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calima_culture), [Zenú](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zen%C3%BA), [Tierradentro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tierradentro), [San Agustín](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Agust%C3%ADn_Archaeological_Park), [Tolima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panche_people), and Urabá became skilled in farming, mining, and metalcraft; and some developed the political system of *[cacicazgos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cacicazgo)* with a pyramidal structure of power headed by [caciques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cacique_Nutibara_Bloc).

Colombia's Indigenous culture evolved from three main groups—the [Quimbaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quimbaya_culture), who inhabited the western slopes of the [Cordillera Central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cordillera_Central_\(Colombia\)); the Chibchas; and the [Kalina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalina_people) (Caribs).[^cite_note-loc-22] When the Spanish arrived in 1509, they found a flourishing and heterogeneous [Amerindian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amerindian) population that numbered around 6 million,[^cite_note-Banrep.co-23] belonged to several hundred tribes, and largely spoke mutually unintelligible dialects.[^cite_note-loc-22] The two most advanced cultures of Amerindian peoples at the time were the [Muisca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_people) and [Taironas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taironas), who belonged to the Chibcha group and were skilled in farming, mining, and metalcraft.[^cite_note-loc-22] The Muisca lived mainly in the present departments of [Cundinamarca](189405.md) and [Boyacá](185665.md), where they had fled centuries earlier after raids by the warlike Caribs, some of whom eventually migrated to Caribbean islands near the end of the first millennium A.D.[^cite_note-loc-22] The Taironas, who were divided into two subgroups, lived in the Caribbean lowlands and the highlands of the [Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Nevada_de_Santa_Marta).[^cite_note-loc-22] The Muisca civilization was well organized into distinct provinces governed by communal land laws and powerful [caciques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caciques), who reported to one of the two supreme leaders.[^cite_note-loc-22]

### Pre-Columbian history



Chaquira-style beaded ceremonial mask made by a [Kamëntšá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kam%C3%ABnt%C5%A1%C3%A1) artist

The complexity of Indigenous social organization and technology varieed tremendously, from stratified agricultural chiefdoms to tropical farm villages and nomadic hunting and food-gathering groups.[^cite_note-loc-22] At the end of the colonial period, the Native population still constituted about half of the total population.[^cite_note-loc-22] In the agricultural chiefdoms of the highlands, the Spaniards successfully imposed institutions designed to ensure their control of the Amerindians and thereby the use of their labor.[^cite_note-loc-22] The colonists had organized political and religious administration by the end of the 16th century, and they had begun attempts to religiously convert the Amerindians to Christianity, specifically Roman Catholicism.[^cite_note-loc-22]

The most important institution that regulated the lives and welfare of the highland Amerindians was the *[resguardo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resguardo?action=edit\&redlink=1)*, a reservation system of communal landholdings.[^cite_note-loc-22] Under this system, Amerindians were allowed to use the land but could not sell it.[^cite_note-loc-22] Similar in some respects to the [Native American reservation system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_reservation) of the United States, the resguardo has lasted with some changes even to the present and has been an enduring link between the government and the remaining highland tribes.[^cite_note-loc-22] As land pressures increased, however, encroachment of white or [mestizo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mestizo) settlers onto resguardo lands accelerated, often without opposition from the government.[^cite_note-loc-22]

The government generally had not attempted to legislate in the past in matters affecting the forest Amerindians.[^cite_note-loc-22] During the colonial period, [Roman Catholic](606848.md) missions were granted jurisdiction over the lowland tribes.[^cite_note-loc-22] With the financial support of the government, a series of agreements with the [Holy See](13393.md) from 1887 to 1953 entrusted the [evangelization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelization) and education of these Amerindians to the missions, which worked together with government agencies.[^cite_note-loc-22] Division of the resguardos stopped in 1958, and a new program of community development began to try to bring the Amerindians more fully into the national society.[^cite_note-loc-22]

The struggle of the Indigenous people on these lands to protect their holdings from neighboring landlords and to preserve their traditions continued into the late 20th century, when the [1991 constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colombian_Constitution_of_1991) incorporated many of the Amerindian demands.[^cite_note-loc-22] New resguardos have been created, and others have been reconstituted, among forest tribes as well as highland communities.[^cite_note-loc-22] The 1991 constitution opened special political and social arenas for Indigenous and other minority groups.[^cite_note-loc-22] For example, it allowed for creation of a special commission to design a law recognizing the [black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afro-Colombian) communities occupying unsettled lands in the riverine areas of the Pacific Coast.[^cite_note-loc-22] Article 171 provides special [Senate](645242.md) representation for Amerindians and other ethnic groups, while Article 176 provides special representation in the [Chamber of Representatives](645347.md): two seats "for the black communities, one for Indian communities, one for political minorities, and one for Colombians residing abroad".[^cite_note-loc-22] Article 356 guarantees Amerindian territorial and cultural rights, and several laws and decrees have been enacted protecting them.[^cite_note-loc-22] Article 356 refers somewhat vaguely to both "Indigenous territorial entities" and Indigenous *resguardos*.[^cite_note-loc-22]

By 1991, the country's 587 resguardos contained 800,271 people and including 60,503 families.[^cite_note-loc-22] The general regional distribution of these resguardos was as follows: [Amazonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_Region_of_Colombia), 88; [llanos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orinoqu%C3%ADa_Region_of_Colombia), 106; [Caribbean lowlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caribbean_Region_of_Colombia), 31; [Andean highlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andean_Region_of_Colombia), 104; and [Pacific lowlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Region_of_Colombia), 258.[^cite_note-loc-22] They totaled 27,900,000 hectares (108,000 sq mi), or about 24 percent of the national territory.[^cite_note-loc-22] Colombia today may have as many as 710 resguardos in 27 of the 32 departments.[^cite_note-loc-22]

## Indigenous political organization



[Muisca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca) Community in the Colombian locality of [Bosa, Bogotá](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosa,_Bogot%C3%A1).

Individual Indigenous groups have a variety of governance structures. A number of Indigenous groups are represented through the [National Indigenous Organization of Colombia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Indigenous_Organization_of_Colombia) (ONIC - Organización Nacional Indígena de Colombia). Increasing organization and agitation have sharply broadened the Indigenous land base over the past forty years. The government titled more than 200 new reserves from 1960 to 1990, with 334 total operating as autonomous municipalities by 1997.[^cite_note-26]

## Territories



Distribution of Native Colombians according to the 2018 census



[Arhuaco people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arhuaco) in the [Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Nevada_de_Santa_Marta) Mountains.

Indigenous peoples hold title to substantial portions of Colombia, primarily in the form of Indigenous Reserves ([Spanish](26825.md): *resguardos*), which encompass one-third of the country's land.[^cite_note-iwgia-1] The Indigenous Affairs division of the Ministry of Interior has 567 reserves on record, covering approximately 365,004 km<sup>2</sup> which are home to 800,272 persons in 67,503 families.[^cite_note-27]

The 1991 National Constitution of Colombia defined Territorial Entities (*Entidades Territoriales*) as departments, districts, municipalities and Indigenous territories. Within an [Indigenous Territory Entity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_territory_\(Colombia\)) (ETI) the people have autonomy in managing their interests, and within the limits of the constitution have the right to manage resources and define taxes required to perform their duties. ETIs are to be defined by the government in conformance with the Organic Law on Land Management. However, this law has yet to be sanctioned so in practice the territories are unregulated.[^cite_note-28]

## Major ethnic groups

According to the [National Indigenous Organization of Colombia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Indigenous_Organization_of_Colombia) (ONIC), there are 102 Indigenous groups in Colombia.[^cite_note-31] The ethnic groups with the greatest number of members are the [Wayuu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayuu_people) (380,460), [Zenú](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zen%C3%BA), (307,091), [Nasa](2478404.md) (243,176) and Pastos (163,873). These peoples account for 58.1% of Colombia's Indigenous population.[^cite_note-32]

Highland peoples refer to the cultures of the [Andes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andes) and the [Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Nevada_de_Santa_Marta) of Colombia, while lowland peoples refer to the inhabitants of [Chocó](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tumbes-Choc%C3%B3-Magdalena), [Amazonía](9299263.md), [Guajira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guajira_Peninsula) and the [Caribbean Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caribbean_Region_of_Colombia), the [Urabá Region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urab%C3%A1_Antioquia) and other non-mountain cultures.

## Struggle for rights

Indigenous people are 4.4–10% of the population of Colombia, and their level of income and the indicators of human development as education and health conditions are lower than those of the rest of Colombians.[^cite_note-34] During the last 20 years, there has been a remarkable increase of the interest dedicated to the concerns of Indigenous communities all over the world. Therefore, the United Nations proclaimed the disclosure of the International Decade of the World's Indigenous People and in Latin America on 10 December 1994 and in Latin America. More than in any other region, the period was characterized by a wave of Indigenous movements that practised a growing political power from the Chiapas resistance of 1994 until the fall of the governments of Ecuador and Bolivia.

The rise of Indigenous mobilization in Colombia is explained as a reaction of crisis at various levels: a crisis of representation caused by the shortcomings of political parties with sufficient representation to shoulder all collectives' interests. a crisis of participation from the lack of citizens' participation in public affairs and a legitimation crisis from discrimination against some social groups.[^cite_note-35]

During their struggle for rights, Indigenous people abandoned the armed struggle of the 1980s, and their new strategy included forms of legal liberalism, a politics of identity, and the use of transnational networks putting pressure on the state to achieve recognition and respect. That ha no't always led to success and often turned into victims of the cultural project of neoliberalism. Indigenous people's cultural accomplishments were accompanied by an escalation of the acts of persecution and the number of violations committed against them.

According to the Indigenous National Organization of Colombia (ONIC), there are 102 Indigenous peoples in Colombia, and only 82 of them are recognized by the Colombian government. One of the main problems that the Colombian Indigenous communities currently face is the lack of recognition of their right to be consulted. Poverty is another aspect that is central to understand the contemporary situation of the Indigenes of Colombia, which has been measured by using of the Unsatisfied Basic Needs (UBN), whoch conders poverty to be insufficiencies in living, services, and education.

There are differences among zones: those of greater influence of poverty measured with the UBN standard are Chocó, Sucre, Boyacá, Nariño, and Córdoba, which exceed the 50% of the population and those of less influence are found in Bogotá and the departments of El Valle, Atlántico and the coffee core: Caldas, Quindío, and Risaralda.

In 1986, the concept of *pobreza absoluta* was introduced in the nation during a crisis of governability and an escalation of problems concerning the armed conflict. With the politics of struggle against poverty, the state tried to consolidate its presenece in zones thatwere considered "marginal," especially those areas including Indigenous people.

Policies between 1986 and 1990 tried to rehabilitate the marginal zones and their integration to achieve development. Specific institutions were set up to work with Indigenous communities, which were seen as farmer communities whose habits and forms of production had to be modernized. As a consequence, the Indigenous minorities revolted and argued that it was not up to them to reintegrate but for the state to reform its ideas and recognize the people as the original Colombians.

The goal was to solve the crisis of governability by eliminating poverty without excluding local necessities and to encourage development from the perspective of diversity. The Indigenous communities were considered to be marginal sectors in disadvantage and highly retarded populations, which had to be incorporated and integrated in greater society. The Indigenous people were not seen as a part of the diversity of the nation whose participation was needed for its construction. That conception has survived since the colonization of the continent until now; Indigenous and black people are generally still seen as negative elements, whose diversity needs to be reduced or wiped out to guarantee Latin American societies' development and modernization.

Despite the Constitution of 1991 introducing the multiethnic and multicultural character of the Colombian nation, the contemporary relation between the state and the Indigenous communities seems to be contradictory, particularly since the communities demand autonomy. The Colombian government has always recognized the Indigenous groups only as communities since they are considered to be culturally diverse and to require therefore different political strategies to be integrated in the national society.

Different forms of participation have been assigned to the communities but always in [conformity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformity) with the state's legal and constitutional regulations, as has defined and established throughout history. The 1990s were a decade of mobilization and, in some way, a victory in terms of neoliberal multiculturalism, but 20 years of the Constitution of 1991 has made people realize the need to turn to other forms of mobilization, more than [legal mobilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_mobilization). They have seen that the recognition of equality is not enough, and Indigenous peoples have also demanded their right to difference: access to particular rights as Indigenous communities.

Many people in Colombia choose not to identify as Indigenous because the history of discrimination against them and the immense [wealth gap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wealth_gap) between those who identify as Indigenous and others. That led to only 1%, or under a million people, of Colombians in the 1993 census identifying as Indigenous. During the most recent censuses, however, more people have begun to identify as Indigenous, from 3.4% in the 2005 census to 4.31% in the 2018 census. Many scholars estimate the true Indigenous population of Colombia to be around 10% of the country's population, or 5 million people.

The same can be said for [Afro-Colombians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afro-Colombians) who are estimated to be around 20% of the total population, or 10 million people, despite only 6% of Colombians identifying as Afro-Colombian in the 2018 census.[^cite_note-col_indigenous-36][^cite_note-undercount-37][^cite_note-indigenous_discrimination-38]

Indigenous political participation, both in national and local elections, has remained low for various reasons: the fragmentation of the movement from the several groups within the Colombian Indigenous communities; the loss of the vote from non-Indigenous leaders; and the low number of voters since many who identify as Indigenous comprise a small part of the national population, with most of them living in the countryside without the possibility to vote.

## Notable Indigenous Colombians



Daguerreotype of José María Melo

- [Hunzahúa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunzah%C3%BAa) (c. 1470), [zaque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaque) of [Hunza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunja) within the [Muisca Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_Confederation).

- [India Catalina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India_Catalina) (c. 1495–1538), interpreter and translator, of [Mokaná](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mokan%C3%A1) descent.

- [Aquiminzaque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquiminzaque) (unknown–1540), last [zaque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaque) of [Hunza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunja) within the [Muisca Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muisca_Confederation).

- [Agustín Agualongo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agust%C3%ADn_Agualongo) (1780–1824), military commander of the [Royalist forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royalist_\(Spanish_American_independence\)) during the [Colombian War of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colombian_War_of_Independence), of [Pasto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasto_language) descent.

- [José María Melo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Mar%C3%ADa_Melo) (1800–1860), 7th [President](251302.md) of the [Republic of New Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_New_Granada), of [Pijao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pijao_people) descent.

- [Quintín Lame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quint%C3%ADn_Lame) (1880–1967), Indigenous rebel of [Nasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasa_people) origin.

- [Feliciano Valencia](https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feliciano%20Valencia) (1955–), [Senator of Colombia](645242.md) for [Cauca](549662.md), of [Nasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasa_people) origin.

- [Lorenzo Muelas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorenzo_Muelas) (1938–), Indigenous activist of [Misak](37689084.md) origin.

- [María Clemencia Herrera Nemerayema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mar%C3%ADa_Clemencia_Herrera_Nemerayema) (1968–), Indigenous [Amazonian](48139.md) activist, of [Witoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witoto) origin.

- [Aida Quilcué](70974895.md) (1973–), [Senator of Colombia](645242.md) for [Cauca](549662.md), of [Nasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasa_people) origin.

- [Lido Pimienta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lido_Pimienta) (1986–), [Colombian-Canadian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colombian_Canadian) songwriter and musician, of [Wayuu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayuu_people) descent.

- [Martha Peralta Epieyú](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martha_Peralta_Epiey%C3%BA) (1988–), [Senator of Colombia](645242.md) for [La Guajira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Guajira_Department), of [Wayuu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayuu_people) descent.

- [Luis Díaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luis_D%C3%ADaz_\(footballer,_born_1997\)) (1997–), Colombian footballer, of [Wayuu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayuu_people) descent.
