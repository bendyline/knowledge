**Maura Tracy Healey** (born February 8, 1971)[^cite_note-1] is an American lawyer and politician serving as the 73rd [governor of Massachusetts](252510.md) since 2023. A member of the [Democratic Party](5043544.md), she served as [Massachusetts Attorney General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Attorney_General) from 2015 to 2023 and was [elected governor in 2022](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Massachusetts_gubernatorial_election).

Hired by Massachusetts Attorney General [Martha Coakley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martha_Coakley) in 2007, Healey served as chief of the Civil Rights Division, where she led the state's challenge to the federal [Defense of Marriage Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defense_of_Marriage_Act). She was then appointed chief of the Public Protection and Advocacy Bureau and then chief of the Business and Labor Bureau, before resigning in 2013 to run for attorney general in 2014. She defeated former State Senator [Warren Tolman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warren_Tolman) in the Democratic primary and Republican attorney John Miller in the general election. Healey was reelected in 2018.[^cite_note-NYTresult-2] She was [elected governor of Massachusetts in 2022](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Massachusetts_gubernatorial_election).[^cite_note-3]

In 2014, Healey became the [first openly lesbian woman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_first_openly_LGBTQ_politicians_in_the_United_States) elected [attorney general of a U.S. state](6065438.md) and the [first openly LGBTQ person](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_first_openly_LGBTQ_politicians_in_the_United_States) elected to statewide office in [Massachusetts](1645518.md).[^cite_note-4] In 2022, she became one of the [first two openly lesbian women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_first_openly_LGBTQ_politicians_in_the_United_States) and the [joint-third openly LGBT person](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_first_openly_LGBTQ_politicians_in_the_United_States) elected governor of a [U.S. state](18618239.md), as well as the first woman elected [governor of Massachusetts](252510.md).[^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6]

## Early life and education

Healey was born on February 8, 1971, at [Walter Reed Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Reed_National_Military_Medical_Center), in [Bethesda, Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bethesda,_Maryland).[^cite_note-backstory-7] When she was nine months old, her family moved to [Hampton Falls, New Hampshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hampton_Falls,_New_Hampshire), where she was raised. Healey has four younger siblings.[^cite_note-8] Her mother was a nurse at [Lincoln Akerman School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln_Akerman_School) in [Hampton Falls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hampton_Falls); her father was a captain in the [United States Public Health Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Public_Health_Service) and an engineer. After divorcing, her mother sold her wedding ring to pay for a backyard basketball court.[^cite_note-backstory-7] Healey's stepfather, Edward Beattie, taught history and coached girls' sports at [Winnacunnet High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winnacunnet_High_School). Several of her grandparents and great-grandparents were born in [Ireland](147575.md).[^cite_note-9]

Healey attended Winnacunnet High School,[^cite_note-10] and majored in government at [Harvard College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_College), graduating *cum laude* in 1992. She was co-captain of the [Harvard Crimson women's basketball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_Crimson_women's_basketball) team.[^cite_note-11] After graduation, Healey spent two years playing as a starting point guard for a professional basketball team in [Austria](26964606.md), UBBC Wüstenrot Salzburg, now called BBU Salzburg.[^cite_note-12][^cite_note-probasketball2014-13] Upon returning to the United States, she earned a [Juris Doctor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juris_Doctor) from [Northeastern University School of Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeastern_University_School_of_Law) in 1998.[^cite_note-mass-14]

## Career

Healey began her legal career by clerking for Judge [A. David Mazzone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._David_Mazzone) of the [United States District Court for the District of Massachusetts](915466.md), where she prepared monthly compliance reports on the cleanup of the [Boston Harbor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston_Harbor) and assisted the judge with trials, hearings, and case conferences. Healey subsequently spent more than seven years at the law firm [Wilmer Cutler Pickering Hale and Dorr LLP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilmer_Cutler_Pickering_Hale_and_Dorr_LLP), where she worked as an associate and then junior partner and focused on commercial and securities litigation.[^cite_note-globe-15]

She also served as a special assistant district attorney in [Middlesex County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middlesex_County,_Massachusetts), where she tried drug, assault, domestic violence, and motor vehicle cases in bench and jury sessions and argued bail hearings, motions to suppress, and probation violations and surrenders.[^cite_note-globe-15]



Healey (right) and Massachusetts Attorney General [Martha Coakley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martha_Coakley) at a July 8, 2010, [press conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Press_conference) on the *[Massachusetts v. U.S. Dep't of Health & Human Servs.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_v._U.S._Dep't_of_Health_&_Human_Servs.)* lawsuit challenging the [Defense of Marriage Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defense_of_Marriage_Act)

Hired by Massachusetts Attorney General [Martha Coakley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martha_Coakley) in 2007, Healey served as chief of the Civil Rights Division, where she spearheaded the state's challenge to the federal [Defense of Marriage Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defense_of_Marriage_Act). She led the winning arguments for Massachusetts in the country's first lawsuit striking down the law.[^cite_note-16]

In 2012, Healey was promoted to chief of the Public Protection and Advocacy Bureau.[^cite_note-17] She was then appointed chief of the Business and Labor Bureau.[^cite_note-wbur-18]

As a division chief and bureau head in the Attorney General's Office, Healey oversaw 250 lawyers and staff members and supervised the areas of [consumer protection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumer_protection), fair labor, ratepayer advocacy, environmental protection, health care, insurance and financial services, civil rights, antitrust, Medicaid fraud, nonprofit organizations and charities, and business, technology, and economic development.[^cite_note-globe-15][^cite_note-wbur-18]

## Attorney General of Massachusetts (2015–2023)



Healey's official attorney general photo, 2015

### Elections

#### 2014

Main article: [2014 Massachusetts elections § Attorney general](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Massachusetts_elections#Attorney_general)

In October 2013, Healey announced her candidacy for attorney general. Coakley was retiring from the office to [run for governor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_gubernatorial_election,_2014). On September 9, 2014, Healey won the Democratic primary by 126,420 votes, defeating former State Senator [Warren Tolman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warren_Tolman), 62.4% to 37.6%.[^cite_note-19]

Healey's campaign was endorsed by State Senators [Stan Rosenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stan_Rosenberg), [Dan Wolf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Wolf), and [Jamie Eldridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamie_Eldridge).[^cite_note-20] It was also endorsed by Northwestern District Attorney David Sullivan, [Holyoke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holyoke,_Massachusetts) Mayor Alex Morse, [Fitchburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitchburg,_Massachusetts) Mayor Lisa Wong, and [Northampton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northampton,_Massachusetts) Mayor David Narkewicz.[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-22] Organizations that endorsed the campaign include the Planned Parenthood Advocacy Fund of Massachusetts, [MassEquality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MassEquality), the [Victory Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gay_&_Lesbian_Victory_Fund), and [EMILY's List](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EMILY's_List).[^cite_note-23][^cite_note-24][^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26] Healey wrote an op-ed in the *Worcester Telegram and Gazette* on upholding the Massachusetts buffer zone law, which she worked on at the Attorney General's Office.[^cite_note-mass-14] She also authored an op-ed in *[The Boston Globe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Boston_Globe)* outlining her plan to combat student loan predators.[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28][^cite_note-29]

Healey defeated Republican nominee John Miller, an attorney, in the general election, 62.5% to 37.5%. Upon taking office, she became the United States' first openly lesbian state attorney general.[^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31]

#### 2018

Main article: [2018 Massachusetts elections § Attorney general](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Massachusetts_elections#Attorney_general)

On November 6, 2018, Healey was reelected Massachusetts Attorney General, defeating Republican nominee James McMahon with 69.9% of the vote.[^cite_note-NYTresult-2]

### Tenure



Healey (far right) in 2016 with (left to right): State Senator [Karen Spilka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karen_Spilka), State Rep. [Ken Gordon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Gordon_\(Massachusetts_politician\)), U.S. Secretary of Labor [Tom Perez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Perez), and State Rep. [John Scibak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Scibak)

Healey's plan to reduce gun violence addresses what she perceives as its root causes. The program includes enhancing the background check system to include information regarding recent restraining orders, pending indictments, and any relations to domestic violence, parole, and probation information. The plan also seeks to track better stolen and missing guns. Healey advocates fingerprint trigger locks and firearm micro-stamping on all guns sold in Massachusetts.[^cite_note-MassLive-32][^cite_note-Nasho-33]

Healey's plan for criminal justice reform includes ending mandatory sentences for nonviolent drug offenders and focusing on treatment rather than incarceration.[^cite_note-34]

Healey plans to combat prescription drug abuse and Massachusetts's heroin epidemic by implementing a "lock-in" program. The program will be carried out in pharmacies to identify and track prescription drug abusers and distributors. Her plan includes deployment of new resources to drug trafficking hotspots, improvement of treatment accessibility, and expanding access to Narcan.[^cite_note-35]

#### Abortion

Healey's women's rights platform focuses on sex education, expanding access to abortion services in Massachusetts, and ensuring that every woman in Massachusetts has access to abortion, regardless of where she lives, her occupation, or her income.[^cite_note-36] In 2026, Healey signed into law a bill removing all restrictions on abortion.[^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38]

#### Gun control

On July 20, 2016, Healey announced her intention to ban the manufacturing of most assault rifles in Massachusetts.[^cite_note-39] In 2021, as a result of this and other "arbitrary and damaging legislation",[^cite_note-40] [Smith & Wesson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smith_&_Wesson) announced plans to relocate its headquarters and much of its manufacturing from Massachusetts to Tennessee. The new factory opened in [Maryville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryville,_Tennessee), in 2023.[^cite_note-41]

#### Trump administration

On January 31, 2017, Healey announced that her office was joining a lawsuit challenging President [Donald Trump](4848272.md)'s [Executive Order 13769](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_13769),[^cite_note-\:0-42][^cite_note-43] commonly known as a "Muslim ban."[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-45] Healey condemned the order as "motivated by anti-Muslim sentiment and Islamophobia, not by a desire to further national security."[^cite_note-\:0-42] A federal court eventually struck the order down on similar grounds.[^cite_note-46]

On March 9, 2017, Healey announced that her office was joining a lawsuit challenging Trump's [Executive Order 13780](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_13780).[^cite_note-\:1-47][^cite_note-\:2-48] She said the new order, a revised version of the one that had been struck down, "remains a discriminatory and unconstitutional attempt to make good on \[Trump's] campaign promise to implement a Muslim ban."[^cite_note-\:1-47] The order has been blocked in various federal courts on similar grounds.[^cite_note-\:2-48][^cite_note-49]

On May 11, 2017, after Trump fired FBI Director [James Comey](2156690.md), Healey led efforts calling for a special counsel to investigate Russia's meddling in the 2016 U.S. presidential election. Her office sent a letter to that effect, signed by 20 Attorneys General across the nation, to Deputy U.S. Attorney General [Rod Rosenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rod_Rosenstein).[^cite_note-50] On May 17, Rosenstein appointed a special counsel, former FBI director [Robert Mueller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Mueller).[^cite_note-51]

#### Purdue Pharma

In 2021, Healey announced a resolution against the Sackler family and [Purdue Pharma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purdue_Pharma). The resolution requires a payment of more than \$4.3 billion for prevention, treatment, and recovery efforts in communities across the country. It will also require Purdue Pharma to be wound down or sold by 2024 and ensure that the Sacklers are banned from the opioid business and are required to turn over control of family foundations to an independent trustee to be used to address the [opioid epidemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opioid_epidemic_in_the_United_States).[^cite_note-52]

On January 20, 2022, Healey announced her candidacy in the 2022 Massachusetts gubernatorial election.[^cite_note-53] Her announcement came after the incumbent governor, [Charlie Baker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlie_Baker), a Republican, announced he would not seek reelection.[^cite_note-54] On September 6, Healey won the Democratic primary election. She defeated [Sonia Chang-Díaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonia_Chang-D%C3%ADaz), who withdrew from the primary.[^cite_note-55] Healey was endorsed by Vice President [Kamala Harris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamala_Harris)[^cite_note-56] and U.S. Senators [Elizabeth Warren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Warren) and [Ed Markey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ed_Markey).[^cite_note-57]

On November 8, 2022, Healey defeated Republican nominee [Geoff Diehl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoff_Diehl), receiving 64% of the vote in the general election to Diehl's 35%. This made her the first woman elected governor of Massachusetts and one of the first two openly lesbian governors in the U.S., along with [Tina Kotek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tina_Kotek) of Oregon, who was also elected in 2022.[^cite_note-58][^cite_note-59][^cite_note-\:3-60] She was inaugurated on January 5, 2023.[^cite_note-61]

#### 2026

Main article: [2026 Massachusetts gubernatorial election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Massachusetts_gubernatorial_election)

In February 2025, Healey announced her intention to run for reelection in 2026.[^cite_note-62][^cite_note-\:3-60] On September 1, 2026, she won the Democratic nomination in an uncontested primary.[^cite_note-63] In September 2026, Healey received various law enforcement union and organization endorsements, including the four largest police unions in Massachusetts. The Boston Police Patrolmen's Association, Massachusetts Coalition of Police (MassCOP), the State Police Association of Massachusetts (SPAM), and the Massachusetts State Police Commissioned Officers Association all endorsed Healey. None of them did so in the previous gubernatorial election.[^cite_note-64]

Healey secured the assistance of various prominent national Democrats during the general election season, including fundraisers headlined by Michigan Governor [Gretchen Whitmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gretchen_Whitmer), former US Transportation Secretary [Pete Buttigieg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pete_Buttigieg), and Arizona Senator [Mark Kelly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Kelly). Maryland Governor [Wes Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wes_Moore) urged voters to donate to her reelection bid in an email circulated by the Healey campaign.[^cite_note-65] On October 1, 2026, Boston Mayor [Michelle Wu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelle_Wu) endorsed Healey for reelection. Healey had declined to publicly support Wu in the 2025 mayoral primary against [Josh Kraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josh_Kraft). Wu said, "I'm grateful for our work together, from a historic summer that delivered economic impact throughout our neighborhoods, to lowering costs, building more housing, keeping residents safe, and expanding opportunity throughout the Commonwealth".[^cite_note-66]

### Tenure



Healey [taking oath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oath_of_office) as governor in 2023



Healey speaking with former governor [Michael Dukakis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Dukakis) at her 2024 State of the Commonwealth address

The day after being sworn in, Healey signed an Executive Order establishing the Office of Climate Innovation and Resilience and creating a cabinet-level position of Climate Chief to head the office. According to Healey, the office will be tasked with working with state and local leaders to help the Commonwealth reach its climate goals and help coordinate the efforts. The Climate Chief will also be the governor's primary advisor on climate issues. Healey appointed [Melissa Hoffer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melissa_Hoffer) to the role.[^cite_note-67][^cite_note-68]

In February 2023, the Healey administration announced a \$742 million [tax cut](455990.md) package to be filed, as an addition to its proposed [fiscal year](5752099.md) 2024 budget. Among the proposals included was an increase in the [child and family tax credit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_tax_credit_\(United_States\)) from \$240 to \$600 per child or dependent. The plans would also increase the rental deduction cap from 50% of rent up to \$3,000 to 50% of \$4,000. Under the proposal, the state's short-term [capital gains tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_gains_tax_in_the_United_States) would be reduced from 12% to 5% and the estate tax threshold would be raised from \$1 million to \$3 million.[^cite_note-69][^cite_note-70] The state legislature passed a scaled-back version of this proposal that increased the child and dependent tax credit to \$310 for the 2023 tax year and \$440 for the following years. The short-term capital gains tax was reduced to 8.5%, and the [estate tax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estate_tax) was eliminated for all estates under \$2 million. Healey signed these changes into law on October 4, 2023.[^cite_note-71]

At a [news conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Press_conference) held at [Bunker Hill Community College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunker_Hill_Community_College) in March 2023, Healey announced a \$20 million appropriation to her 2024 fiscal year state budget proposal to create a [free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_education) [community college](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community_colleges_in_the_United_States) program, "MassReconnect", for Massachusetts residents 25 or older with a [secondary school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_education_in_the_United_States) [degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_school_diploma) or [post-secondary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_education_in_the_United_States) [course credits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Course_credit), to address the [skills gap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_unemployment) in the state workforce.[^cite_note-72][^cite_note-73] The state legislature approved the plan, as part of the 2024 fiscal year state budget, which Healey signed into law in August.[^cite_note-74][^cite_note-75] In May 2023, Healey's administration announced \$24.4 million in job creation [tax incentives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_incentive) for 43 [life sciences companies in the state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biotechnology_industry_in_Boston) to create 1,600 jobs.[^cite_note-76]

In August 2023, Healey declared a state of emergency due to an increase in migrants seeking shelter in the state. Massachusetts is the only U.S. state that must provide emergency housing to families who qualify. At the time of the emergency declaration, the shelter system was housing over 20,000 people.[^cite_note-77][^cite_note-78] Healey set a limit of 7,500 on the number of families that could be housed in the state's emergency shelter system. The state exceeded this limit in November 2023. On November 9, Healey announced that families would be placed on a waiting list and would enter the shelter system as housing units became available.[^cite_note-79] In December, she signed a \$3.1 billion supplemental budget bill that added another \$250 million in funding for the state's shelter system and created an overflow location for migrants who were unable to enter the state's shelter system. Later that month, the administration designated five locations as overflow sites.[^cite_note-80][^cite_note-81] On April 30, 2024, Healey signed a bill that directed another \$251 million into the shelter system for the rest of fiscal year 2024 and limited how long families can stay in the shelter system to nine months.[^cite_note-82]

In February 2024, Healey nominated her former romantic partner, appellate court judge [Gabrielle Wolohojian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabrielle_Wolohojian), to the [Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Supreme_Judicial_Court). Facing criticism for this decision, especially from [Massachusetts Republican Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Republican_Party) chair Amy Carnevale, who called on her to withdraw the nomination, Healey defended the choice. "I don't want the fact that she had a personal relationship with me to deprive the commonwealth of a person who's most qualified for the position", she told reporters.[^cite_note-83][^cite_note-84] The [Governor's Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Governor's_Council) approved the nomination on February 28 and Wolohojian was sworn in on April 22.[^cite_note-85][^cite_note-86]

On October 29, 2025, Healey fired her Western Massachusetts Deputy Director, LaMar Cook, after he was charged with both cocaine trafficking and gun-related charges.[^cite_note-87]

## Political views

Healey is regarded as a [liberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberalism_in_the_United_States).[^cite_note-88][^cite_note-NameRecognitionAndLiberalTrackRecord-89] In her early career, she was called a "[progressive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressivism_in_the_United_States) prosecutor",[^cite_note-KashinskySolidarity-90] but some progressives criticized her as insufficiently supportive of law enforcement reform.[^cite_note-91] During her 2022 campaign for governor, Healey was characterized as staking out somewhat [moderate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_moderate) positions on several issues.[^cite_note-KashinskySolidarity-90] As governor, she has taken several moderate and [centrist](39619035.md) positions on policy.[^cite_note-92][^cite_note-PoliticalWuComplicatedThanItSeems-93] She is regarded as more moderate (less progressive and less liberal) than some other leading Massachusetts Democratic politicians, such as [Elizabeth Warren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Warren)[^cite_note-=-94] and [Michelle Wu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelle_Wu).[^cite_note-KashinskySolidarity-90][^cite_note-PoliticalWuComplicatedThanItSeems-93] In a 2024 interview, Healey called herself a "pro-growth Democrat" on economic matters, citing her support of tax cuts for the [middle class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_class).[^cite_note-95]

## Personal life



Healey with her partner, Joanna Lydgate (left), in 2023

In July 2022, Healey moved from Boston to [Cambridge, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge,_Massachusetts).[^cite_note-96] She plays basketball recreationally.[^cite_note-97][^cite_note-98] On January 9, 2023, shortly after being inaugurated as governor, Healey announced that she was in a relationship with attorney Joanna Lydgate, her former chief deputy. She clarified that their relationship did not begin until Lydgate had departed the role to co-found the States United Democracy Center, a voting rights advocacy organization.[^cite_note-99] Lydgate has taken on the role of "First Partner of Massachusetts".[^cite_note-100][^cite_note-101]

Healey was baptized in the [Catholic Church](606848.md).[^cite_note-102][^cite_note-103]
