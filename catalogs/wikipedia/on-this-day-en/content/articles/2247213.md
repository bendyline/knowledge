&#x20;Elements in a [Hindu temple architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_temple_architecture)

A **Hindu temple**, also known as a **Mandir**, **Mandil**, **Devasthanam**, **Pura**, **Kshetram**, **Ambalam** or **[Kovil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kovil)**, is a sacred place where [Hindus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindus) worship and show their devotion to [deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_deities) through worship, sacrifice, and prayers.[^cite_note-\:1-1] It is considered the house of the god to whom it is dedicated.[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-Michell1977p61-3] [Hindu temple architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_temple_architecture), which makes extensive use of squares and circles, has its roots in later [Vedic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic) traditions, which also influence the temples' construction and symbolism. Through astronomical numbers and particular alignments connected to the temple's location and the relationship between the deity and the worshipper, the temple's design also illustrates the idea of recursion and the [equivalency of the macrocosm and the microcosm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microcosm%E2%80%93macrocosm_analogy).[^cite_note-stellakvol1-4][^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6] A temple incorporates all elements of the Hindu cosmos—presenting the good, the evil and the human, as well as the elements of the Hindu sense of cyclic time and the essence of life—symbolically presenting [dharma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharma), [artha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artha), [kama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kama), [moksha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moksha), and [karma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karma).[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9]

The spiritual principles symbolically represented in Hindu temples are detailed in the ancient [Vedic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedic) texts, while their structural rules are described in various ancient Sanskrit treatises on architecture, such as [Bṛhat Saṃhitā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Var%C4%81hamihira), and [Vāstu Śāstras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vastu_shastra).[^cite_note-susanlchap4-10][^cite_note-11] The layout, motifs, plan, and the building process express ancient rituals and geometric symbolism, reflecting beliefs and values innate to various schools of Hinduism.[^cite_note-stellakvol1-4] A Hindu temple serves as a spiritual destination for many Hindus, as well as a landmark around which ancient arts, community celebrations, and local economies have historically flourished.[^cite_note-bstein-12][^cite_note-13]

[Hindu temple architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_temple_architecture) are presented in many styles, are situated in diverse locations, deploy different construction methods, are adapted to different deities and regional beliefs, and share certain core ideas, symbolism and themes.[^cite_note-14] They are found in South Asia, particularly [India](14533.md) and [Nepal](171166.md), [Bangladesh](3454.md), [Pakistan](23235.md), [Sri Lanka](26750.md), in Southeast Asian countries such as [Philippines](23440.md), [Cambodia](334751.md), [Vietnam](202354.md), [Malaysia](3607937.md), and [Indonesia](14579.md),[^cite_note-15][^cite_note-16] and countries such as [Canada](5042916.md), [Fiji](10707.md), [France](5843419.md), [Guyana](17238691.md), [Kenya](188171.md), [Mauritius](19201.md), the [Netherlands](21148.md), [South Africa](17416221.md), [Suriname](26828.md), [Tanzania](30118.md), [Trinidad and Tobago](3565457.md), [Uganda](31816.md), the [United Kingdom](31717.md), the [United States](3434750.md), [Australia](4689264.md), [New Zealand](4913064.md), and other countries with a significant Hindu population.[^cite_note-17] The current state and outer appearance of Hindu temples reflect arts, materials and designs as they evolved over two millennia; they also reflect the effect of conflicts between [Hinduism and Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism_and_Islam) since the 12th century.[^cite_note-18] The [Swaminarayanan Akshardham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swaminarayan_Akshardham_\(North_America\)) in [Robbinsville, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robbinsville,_New_Jersey), between the [New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_metropolitan_area) and [Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia_metropolitan_area) metropolitan areas, was inaugurated in 2014 as one of the world's largest Hindu temples.[^cite_note-LargestHinduTempleNJ-19]

## Significance and meaning of a temple

A Hindu temple reflects a synthesis of arts, the ideals of [dharma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharma), beliefs, values and the way of life cherished under Hinduism. It is a link between man, deities, and the Universal *Puruṣa* in a sacred space.[^cite_note-gmichell88-20] It represents the triple-knowledge (trayi-[vidya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vidya_\(philosophy\))) of the Vedic vision by mapping the relationships between the cosmos ([brahmaṇḍa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_egg)) and the cell (pinda) by a unique plan based on astronomical numbers.[^cite_note-21] Subhash Kak sees the temple form and its iconography to be a natural expansion of Vedic ideology related to recursion, change and equivalence.[^cite_note-22]



The 9x9 (81) grid "Parama Sayika" layout plan (above) found in large ceremonial Hindu Temples. It is one of many grids used to build Hindu temples. In this structure of symmetry, each concentric layer has significance. The outermost layer, *Paisachika padas*, signifies aspects of Asuras and evil; while the inner *Devika padas* layer signifies aspects of Devas and good. In between the good and evil is the concentric layer of *Manusha padas*, signifying human life. All these layers surround *Brahma padas*, which signifies creative energy and the site for a temple's primary *murti* for darsana. Finally at the very center of the *Brahma padas* is the *Garbhagriha* (Purusa Space), signifying the Universal Principle present in everything and everyone.

[^cite_note-stellakvol1-4]

&#x20;In ancient Indian texts, a temple is a place of pilgrimage, known in India as a *[Tirtha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirtha_and_Kshetra)*.[^cite_note-stellakvol1-4] It is a sacred site whose ambience and design attempts to symbolically condense the ideal tenets of the Hindu way of life.[^cite_note-gmichell88-20] In a Hindu temple, all the cosmic components that produce and maintain life are there, from fire to water, from depictions of the natural world to gods, from genders that are feminine or masculine to those that are everlasting and universal.

Susan Lewandowski states[^cite_note-susanlchap4-10] that the underlying principle in a Hindu temple is the belief that all things are one, that everything is connected. The pilgrim is welcomed through 64-grid or 81-grid mathematically structured spaces, a network of art, pillars with carvings and statues that display and celebrate the four important and necessary principles of human life—the pursuit of *[artha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artha)* (prosperity, wealth), of *[kama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kama)* (pleasure, sex), of *[dharma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharma)* (virtues, ethical life) and of *[moksha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moksha)* (release, self-knowledge).[^cite_note-23][^cite_note-24] At the centre of the temple, typically below and sometimes above or next to the [deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deity), is mere hollow space with no decoration, symbolically representing *Purusa*, the Supreme Principle, the sacred Universal, one without form, which is omnipresent, connects everything, and is the essence of everyone. A Hindu temple is meant to encourage reflection, facilitate purification of one's mind, and trigger the process of inner realization within the devotee.[^cite_note-stellakvol1-4] The specific process is left to the devotee's school of belief. The primary deity of different Hindu temples varies to reflect this spiritual spectrum.[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26]

In Hindu tradition, there is no dividing line between the [secular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secular) and the lonely sacred.[^cite_note-susanlchap4-10] In the same spirit, Hindu temples are not just sacred spaces; they are also secular spaces. Their meaning and purpose have extended beyond spiritual life to social rituals and daily life, offering thus a social meaning. Some temples have served as a venue to mark festivals, to celebrate arts through dance and music, to get married or commemorate marriages,[^cite_note-27] the birth of a child, other significant life events or the death of a loved one. In political and economic life, Hindu temples have served as a venue for succession within dynasties and landmarks around which economic activity thrived.[^cite_note-Susan_Lewandowski_pp_71-73-28]

## Forms and designs of Hindu temples

Main article: [Hindu temple architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_temple_architecture)

Almost all Hindu temples take two forms: a house or a palace. A house-themed temple is a simple shelter that serves as a deity's home. The temple is a place where the devotee visits, just like he or she would visit a friend or relative. The use of moveable and immoveable [images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murti) is mentioned by [Pāṇini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C4%81%E1%B9%87ini). In the [Bhakti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhakti) school of Hinduism, temples are venues for [puja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puja_\(Hinduism\)), which is a hospitality ritual, where the deity is honored, and where devotee calls upon, attends to and connects with the deity. In other schools of Hinduism, the person may simply perform *[japa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japa)*, or meditation, or [yoga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoga), or introspection in his or her temple. Palace-themed temples often incorporate more elaborate and monumental architecture.[^cite_note-29]

### Site

The appropriate site for a temple, suggests ancient Sanskrit texts, is near water and gardens, where lotus and flowers bloom, where swans, ducks and other birds are heard, and where animals rest without fear of injury or harm.[^cite_note-stellakvol1-4] These harmonious places were recommended in these texts with the explanation that such are the places where gods play, and thus the best site for Hindu temples.[^cite_note-stellakvol1-4][^cite_note-susanlchap4-10]



Hindu temple sites cover a wide range. The most common sites are those near water bodies, embedded in nature, such as the [Bhutanatha temple complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhutanatha_group_of_temples,_Badami) at [Badami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Badami), which is next to a [waterfall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterfall).

> The gods always play where lakes are,\
> &#x20;where the sun's rays are warded off by umbrellas of lotus leaf clusters,\
> &#x20;and where clear waterpaths are made by swans\
> &#x20;whose breasts toss the white lotus hither and thither,\
> &#x20;where swans, ducks, curleys and paddy birds are heard,\
> &#x20;and animals rest nearby in the shade of Nicula trees on the river banks.\
> &#x20;\
> &#x20;The gods always play where rivers have for their braclets\
> &#x20;the sound of curleys and the voice of swans for their speech,\
> &#x20;water as their garment, carps for their zone,\
> &#x20;the flowering trees on their banks as earrings,\
> &#x20;the confluence of rivers as their hips,\
> &#x20;raised sand banks as breasts and plumage of swans their mantle.\
> &#x20;\
> &#x20;The gods always play where groves are near, rivers, mountains and springs, and in towns with pleasure gardens.

— [Varāhamihira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Var%C4%81hamihira), *Brhat Samhita* 1.60.4-8, 6th century CE[^cite_note-30]

While major Hindu temples are recommended at sangams (confluence of rivers), river banks, lakes and seashore, *Brhat Samhita* and *Puranas* suggest temples may also be built where a natural source of water is not present. Here too, they recommend that a pond be built preferably in front or to the left of the temple with water gardens. If water is neither present naturally nor by design, water is symbolically present at the consecration of the temple or the deity. Temples may also be built, suggests *Visnudharmottara* in Part III of Chapter 93,[^cite_note-31] inside caves and carved stones, on hill tops affording peaceful views, on mountain slopes overlooking beautiful valleys, inside forests and hermitages, next to gardens, or at the head of a town street.

### Manuals

Ancient builders of Hindu temples created manuals of architecture, called the *[Vastu-Sastra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vastu_shastra)* (literally, the "science" of dwelling; *vas-tu* is a composite Sanskrit word; *vas* means "reside", *tu* means "you"). These manuals contain Vastu-Vidya (literally, knowledge of dwelling)[^cite_note-bbdutt-32], while *Sastra* refers to a system or body of knowledge in Sanskrit. There are many Vastu-Sastras on the art of building temples, such as the treatise by [Thakkura Pheru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thakkura_Pheru), which describes where and how temples should be built.[^cite_note-stellakramrisch76-33][^cite_note-34] Sanskrit manuals have been found in India since the 6th century CE.[^cite_note-35] Vastu-Sastra manuals included chapters on home construction, town planning,[^cite_note-bbdutt-32] and how villages, towns and kingdoms could efficiently integrate temples, water bodies, and gardens within them to achieve harmony with nature.[^cite_note-Sherri_Silverman_2007-36][^cite_note-37] While it is unclear, according to Barnett,[^cite_note-38] whether these temple and town planning texts were theoretical studies and if or when they were properly implemented in practice, the manuals suggest that town planning and [Hindu temples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Hindu_temples_in_India) were conceived as ideals of art and as an integral part of Hindu social and spiritual life.[^cite_note-bbdutt-32]



Ancient India produced many Sanskrit manuals for Hindu temple design and construction, covering arrangement of spaces (above) to every aspect of its completion. Yet, the Silpins were given wide latitude to experiment and express their creativity.

[^cite_note-mmgeometry-39]

The *Silpa Prakasa* of Odisha, authored by Ramacandra Bhattaraka Kaulacara in the 9th or 10th centuries CE, is another Sanskrit treatise on Temple Architecture.[^cite_note-aliceboner66-40] Silpa Prakasa describes the geometric principles underlying every aspect of the temple and symbolism such as the 16 emotions of human beings carved as 16 types of female figures. These styles were perfected in Hindu temples prevalent in the eastern states of India. Other ancient texts have been found that expand these architectural principles, suggesting that different parts of India developed, invented and added their own interpretations. For example, in the *Saurastra* tradition of temple building found in western states of India, the feminine form, expressions and emotions are depicted in 32 types of *Nataka-stri* compared to 16 types described in *Silpa Prakasa*.[^cite_note-aliceboner66-40] Silpa Prakasa provides a brief introduction to 12 types of Hindu temples. Other texts, such as *Pancaratra Prasada Prasadhana* compiled by Daniel Smith[^cite_note-41] and Silpa Ratnakara compiled by Narmada Sankara[^cite_note-42] provide a more extensive list of Hindu temple types.

Ancient Sanskrit manuals for temple construction discovered in Rajasthan, in the northwestern region of India, include Sutradhara Mandana's *Prasadamandana* (literally, manual for planning and building a temple).[^cite_note-43] *Manasara*, a text of South Indian origin, estimated to have been in circulation by the 7th century CE, is a guidebook on South Indian temple design and construction.[^cite_note-susanlchap4-10][^cite_note-44] *Isanasivagurudeva paddhati* is another Sanskrit text from the 9th century describing the art of temple building in India in south and central India.[^cite_note-skramrisch1958-45][^cite_note-46] In north India, *Brihat-samhita* by [Varāhamihira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Var%C4%81hamihira) is the widely cited ancient Sanskrit manual from 6th century describing the design and construction of *Nagara* style of Hindu temples.[^cite_note-mmgeometry-39][^cite_note-hgood-47][^cite_note-48]

A Hindu temple design follows a geometrical design called *vastu-purusha-mandala*. The name is a composite Sanskrit word with three of the most important components of the plan. *Mandala* means circle, *Purusha* is universal essence at the core of Hindu tradition, while *Vastu* means the dwelling structure.[^cite_note-sl6869-49] The Vastu-purusha-mandala is a [yantra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yantra),[^cite_note-stellakramrisch76-33] a design laying out a Hindu temple in a symmetrical, self-repeating structure derived from central beliefs, myths, cardinality and mathematical principles.

The four cardinal directions help create the axis of a Hindu temple, around which is formed a perfect square in the space available. The circle of the mandala circumscribes the square. The square is considered divine for its perfection and as a symbolic product of knowledge and human thought, while the circle is considered earthly, human and observed in everyday life (moon, sun, horizon, water drop, rainbow). Each supports the other.[^cite_note-stellakvol1-4] The square is divided into perfect 64 (or in some cases 81) sub-squares called padas.[^cite_note-mmgeometry-39][^cite_note-50] Each pada is conceptually assigned to a symbolic element, sometimes in the form of a deity. The central square(s) of the 64- or 81-grid is dedicated to [Brahman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahman) (not to be confused with Brahmin, the scholarly and priestly class in India), and are called *Brahma padas*.

The 49-grid design is called *Sthandila* and is of great importance in creative expressions of Hindu temples in South India, particularly in *Prakaras*.[^cite_note-51] The symmetric Vastu-purusa-mandala grids are sometimes combined to form a temple superstructure with two or more attached squares.[^cite_note-52] The temples face sunrise, and the entrance for the devotee is typically this east side. The mandala pada facing sunrise is dedicated to [Surya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surya), the sun-god. The *Surya pada* is flanked by the padas of Satya, the deity of Truth, on one side and [Indra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indra), the king of the demigods, on other. The east and north faces of most temples feature a mix of gods and demigods; while the west and south feature demons and demigods related to the underworld.[^cite_note-53] This *vastu-purusha-mandala* plan and symbolism is systematically seen in ancient Hindu temples on the Indian subcontinent as well as those in southeast Asia, with regional creativity and variations.[^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55]

Beneath the mandala's central square(s) is the space for the all-pervasive, all-connecting Universal Spirit, the [highest reality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahman), the *purusha*.[^cite_note-56] This space is sometimes known as the *garbha-griya* (literally, "womb house")—a small, perfect square, windowless, enclosed space without ornamentation that represents universal essence.[^cite_note-sl6869-49] In or near this space is typically a cult image—which, though many Indians may refer to casually as an idol, is more formally known as a *murti,* or the main worshippable deity, who varies with each temple. Often this *murti* gives the temple a local name, such as a [Vishnu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishnu) temple, [Krishna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krishna) temple, [Rama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rama) temple, [Narayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narayana) temple, [Shiva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shiva) temple, [Lakshmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakshmi) temple, [Ganesha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ganesha) temple, [Durga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durga) temple, [Hanuman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanuman) temple, Surya temple, etc.[^cite_note-gmichell88-20] It is this [garbha-griya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garbhagriha) which devotees seek for *[darsana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darshana)* (literally, a sight of knowledge,[^cite_note-57] or vision[^cite_note-sl6869-49]).

Above the *vastu-purusha-mandala* is a superstructure with a dome called *[Shikhara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shikhara)* in north India, and *[Vimana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vimana_\(tower\))* in south India, that stretches towards the sky.[^cite_note-sl6869-49] Sometimes, in makeshift temples, the dome may be replaced with symbolic bamboo with few leaves at the top. The vertical dimension's cupola or dome is designed as a pyramid, a cone or other mountain-like shape, once again using the principle of concentric circles and squares.[^cite_note-stellakvol1-4] Scholars suggest that this shape is inspired by the cosmic mountain of Meru or Himalayan Kailasa, the abode of the gods, according to Vedic mythology.[^cite_note-sl6869-49]





A Hindu temple has a Shikhara (Vimana or Spire) that rises symmetrically above the central core of the temple. These spires come in many designs and shapes, but they all have mathematical precision and geometric symbolism. One of the common principles found in Hindu temple spires is circles and turning-squares theme (left), and a concentric layering design (right) that flows from one to the other as it rises towards the sky.

[^cite_note-stellakvol1-4]

[^cite_note-mwmmountain-58]

In larger temples, the central space typically is surrounded by an ambulatory for the devotee to walk around and ritually circumambulate the Purusa, the universal essence.[^cite_note-stellakvol1-4] Often this space is visually decorated with carvings, paintings or images meant to inspire the devotee. In some temples, these images may be stories from Hindu Epics; in others, they may be Vedic tales about right and wrong or virtues and vice; in yet others, they may be *murtis* of locally worshipped deities. The pillars, walls and ceilings typically also have highly ornate carvings or images of the four just and necessary pursuits of life—kama, artha, dharma and moksa. This walk around is called *pradakshina*.[^cite_note-sl6869-49]

Large temples also have pillared halls, called *mandapa*—one of which, on the east side, serves as the waiting room for pilgrims and devotees. The *mandapa* may be a separate structure in older temples, but in newer temples this space is integrated into the temple superstructure. Mega-temple sites have a main temple surrounded by smaller temples and shrines, but these are still arranged by principles of symmetry, grids and mathematical precision. An important principle found in the layout of Hindu temples is mirroring and repeating fractal-like design structure,[^cite_note-Trivedi,_K._1989-59] each unique yet also repeating the central common principle, one which Susan Lewandowski refers to as "an organism of repeating cells".[^cite_note-Susan_Lewandowski_pp_71-73-28]





An illustration of Hindu temple Spires (*Shikhara, Vimana*) built using concentric circles and the rotating-squares principle. The left one is from Vijayanagar in [Karnataka](16880.md), and the right one is from Pushkar in [Rajasthan](26291.md).

The ancient texts on Hindu temple design, the *Vāstu-puruṣa-mandala* and *Vastu Śāstras*, do not limit themselves to the design of a Hindu temple.[^cite_note-60] They describe the temple as a holistic part of its community, and lay out various principles and a diversity of alternate designs for home, village and city layout along with the temple, gardens, water bodies and nature.[^cite_note-stellakvol1-4][^cite_note-Sherri_Silverman_2007-36]

Exceptions to the square grid principle

A predominant number of Hindu temples exhibit the perfect-square grid principle.[^cite_note-61] However, there are some exceptions. For example, the [Telika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teli_ka_Mandir) Mandir in [Gwalior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwalior), built in the 8th century CE, is not a square but a rectangle in 2\:3 proportion. Further, the temple explores a number of structures and shrines in 1\:1, 1\:2, 1\:3, 2\:5, 3\:5 and 4\:5 ratios. These ratios are exact, suggesting that the architect intended to use these harmonic ratios, and the rectangle pattern was not a mistake, nor an arbitrary approximation. Other examples of non-square harmonic ratios are found at the Naresar temple site of Madhya Pradesh and at the Nakti-Mata temple near [Jaipur](166537.md), Rajasthan. [Michael Meister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_W._Meister) suggests that these exceptions mean that the ancient Sanskrit manuals for temple building were guidelines, and Hinduism permitted its artisans flexibility in expression and aesthetic independence.[^cite_note-mmgeometry-39]

### Symbolism



[Chaturbhuj Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaturbhuj_Temple_\(Orchha\)) at [Orchha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orchha), is noted for having one of the [tallest Vimana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_Gopurams#Tallest_Vimana) among Hindu temples standing at 344 feet.

A Hindu temple is a symbolic reconstruction of the universe and the universal principles that enable everything in it to function.[^cite_note-stellakvol1011-62][^cite_note-63] The temples reflect Hindu philosophy and its diverse views on the cosmos and on truth.[^cite_note-Trivedi,_K._1989-59][^cite_note-64]

Hinduism has no traditional ecclesiastical order, no centralized religious authorities, no governing body, no prophet nor any binding holy book save the Vedas; Hindus can choose to be [polytheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytheism), [pantheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantheism), [monistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monism), or [atheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheism).[^cite_note-65] Within this diffuse and open structure, spirituality in Hindu philosophy is an individual experience, and referred to as *kṣaitrajña* ([Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_language): क्षैत्रज्ञ)[^cite_note-66]). It defines spiritual practice as one's journey towards *[moksha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moksha)*, awareness of self, the discovery of higher truths, true nature of reality, and a consciousness that is liberated and content.[^cite_note-67][^cite_note-gf-68] A Hindu temple reflects these core beliefs. The central core of almost all Hindu temples is not a large communal space; the temple is designed for the individual, a couple or a family—a small, private space to allow visitors to experience *darsana*.

*Darsana* is itself a symbolic word. In ancient Hindu scripts, *darsana* is the name of six methods or alternate viewpoints of understanding truth.[^cite_note-skv189-69] These are *Nyaya, Vaisesika, Sankhya, Yoga, Mimamsa* and *Vedanta*—which flowered into individual schools of Hinduism, each of which is considered a valid, alternate path to understanding truth and achieving self-realization in the Hindu way of life.

From names to forms, from images to stories carved into the walls of a temple, symbolism is everywhere in a Hindu temple. Life principles such as the pursuit of joy, connection and emotional pleasure (kama) are fused into mystical, erotic and architectural forms in Hindu temples. These motifs and principles of human life are part of the sacred texts of the Hindus, such as its Upanishads; the temples express these same principles in a different form, through art and spaces. For example, *Brihadaranyaka Upanisad* (4.3.21) recites:

> In the embrace of the beloved, one forgets the whole world, everything both within and without;\
> &#x20;in the same way, one who embraces the Self knows neither within nor without.

— [Brihadaranyaka Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brihadaranyaka_Upanishad), 7th century BCE[^cite_note-70]

The architecture of Hindu temples is also symbolic. The whole structure fuses the daily life and its surroundings with the divine concepts, through a structure that is open yet raised on a terrace, transitioning from the secular towards the sacred,[^cite_note-eleach1983-71] inviting the visitor inwards and upwards towards the *Brahma pada,* the temple's central core, a symbolic space marked by its spire (*shikhara, vimana*). The ancient temples had grand, intricately carved entrances but no doors, and they lacked a boundary wall. In most cultures, suggests [Edmund Leach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Leach),[^cite_note-eleach1983-71] a boundary and gateway separates the secular and the sacred, and this gateway door is grand. In Hindu tradition, this is discarded in favor of an open and diffusive architecture, where the secular world was not separated from the sacred, but transitioned and flowed into the sacred.[^cite_note-72] The Hindu temple has structural walls, which were patterned usually within the 64-grid, or other geometric layouts. Yet the layout was open on all sides, except for the core space with a single opening for darsana. The temple space is laid out in a series of courts (*mandapas*). The outermost regions may incorporate the negative and suffering side of life with the symbolism of evil, *asuras* and *rakshashas*; but in small temples this layer is dispensed with. When present, this outer region diffuse into the next inner layer that bridges as human space, followed by another inner *Devika padas* space and symbolic arts incorporating the positive and joyful side of life about the good and the gods. This divine space then concentrically diffuses inwards and lifts the guest to the core of the temple, where resides the main *murti*, as well as the space for the *Purusa,* and ideas held to be most sacred principles in Hindu tradition. The symbolism in the arts and temples of Hinduism, suggests Edmund Leach, is similar to those in Christianity and other major religions of the world.[^cite_note-73]

### Building teams







The 6th-century *Brihat samhita* is a Sanskrit encyclopedia. Its chapters 57–60 discuss different styles and design of Hindu temples. Above: the text and commentary in Nepalaksara, Devanagari and Tamil Grantha scripts.

Indian texts call the craftsmen and builders of temples "Silpin" ([Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_language): शिल्पिन्[^cite_note-74]), derived from "Silpa".[^cite_note-sparker1987-75] One of the earliest mentions of the Sanskrit word "Silpa" is in [Atharvaveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atharvaveda), from about 1000 BCE; according to scholars, the word was used to denote any work of art.[^cite_note-76] Some scholars suggest that the word "Silpa" has no direct or one-word translation in English, nor does the word "Silpin". "Silpa", explains Stella Kramrisch,[^cite_note-skramrisch1958-45] is a multicolored word and incorporates art, skill, craft, ingenuity, imagination, form, expression and inventiveness of any art or craft. Similarly, "Shilpin", notes Kramrisch, is a complex Sanskrit word, describing any person who embodies art, science, culture, skill, and rhythm and employs creative principles to produce any divine form of expression. Silpins who built Hindu temples, as well as the artworks and sculptures within them, were considered by the ancient Sanskrit texts to deploy arts whose number are unlimited, Kala (techniques) that were 64 in number,[^cite_note-77] and Vidya (science) that were of 32 types.[^cite_note-skramrisch1958-45]

The Hindu manuals of temple construction describe the education, characteristics of good artists and architects. The general education of a Hindu Shilpin in ancient India included Lekha or Lipi (alphabet, reading and writing), Rupa (drawing and geometry), Ganana (arithmetic). These were imparted from age 5 to 12. The advanced students would continue in higher stages of Shilpa Sastra studies till the age of 25.[^cite_note-78][^cite_note-79] Apart from specialist technical competence, the manuals suggest that best Silpins for building a Hindu temple are those who know the essence of Vedas and Agamas, consider themselves as students, keep well verse with principles of traditional sciences and mathematics, painting and geography.[^cite_note-stellakramrisch76-33] Further they are kind, free from jealousy, righteous, have their sense under control, of happy disposition, and ardent in everything they do.[^cite_note-skramrisch1958-45]

According to Silparatna, a Hindu temple project would start with a Yajamana (patron), and include a Sthapaka (guru, spiritual guide and architect-priest), a Sthapati (architect) who would design the building, a Sutragrahin (surveyor), and many Vardhakins (workers, masons, painters, plasterers, overseers) and Taksakas (sculptors).[^cite_note-stellakramrisch76-33][^cite_note-hgood-47] While the temple is under construction, all those working on the temple were revered and considered sacerdotal by the patron as well as others witnessing the construction.[^cite_note-sparker1987-75] Further, it was a tradition that all tools and materials used in temple building and all creative work had the sanction of a sacrament.[^cite_note-stellakramrisch76-33] For example, if a carpenter or sculptor needed to fell a tree or cut a rock from a hill, he would propitiate the tree or rock with prayers, seeking forgiveness for cutting it from its surroundings, and explaining his intent and purpose. The axe used to cut the tree would be anointed with butter to minimize the hurt to the tree.[^cite_note-skramrisch1958-45] Even in modern times, in some parts of India such as [Odisha](250724.md), Visvakarma Puja is a ritual festival every year where the craftsmen and artists worship their arts, tools and materials.[^cite_note-80]

## Social functions of Hindu temples



Hindu Shiva temple depicted in a coin from the [1st century BCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1st_century_BC).



Example of a Hindu temple constructed in the same manner as the one depicted in the coin.

Hindu temples served as nuclei of important social, economic, artistic and intellectual functions in ancient and medieval India.[^cite_note-michelltempleschool-81][^cite_note-82] [Burton Stein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burton_Stein) states that South Indian temples managed regional development function, such as irrigation projects, land reclamation, post-disaster relief and recovery. These activities were paid for by the donations (melvarum) they collected from devotees.[^cite_note-bstein-12] According to James Heitzman, these donations came from a wide spectrum of the Indian society, ranging from kings, queens, officials in the kingdom to merchants, priests and shepherds.[^cite_note-83] Temples also managed lands endowed to it by its devotees upon their death. They would provide employment to the poorest.[^cite_note-84] Some temples had large treasury, with gold and silver coins, and these temples served as banks.[^cite_note-85]



[The Golden Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Temple,_Sripuram) at [Vellore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vellore) is gilded with 1500 kg of pure gold.

Hindu temples over time became wealthy from grants and donations from royal patrons as well as private individuals. Major temples became employers and patrons of economic activity. In [Dharmanomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharmanomics), economist Balasubramanian notes that many major temples were strategically located closer to coastlines including [Somnath temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somnath_temple) in Gujarat and [Padmanabhaswamy temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Padmanabhaswamy_Temple) in [Thiruvananthapuram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thiruvananthapuram).[^cite_note-86] They served as crucial financial hubs for maritime trade and sponsored land reclamation and infrastructure improvements, states Michell. These built facilities such as water tanks, irrigation canals and new roads.[^cite_note-michell58-87] A very detailed early record from 1101 lists over 600 employees (excluding the priests) of the [Brihadisvara Temple, Thanjavur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brihadisvara_Temple,_Thanjavur), still one of the largest temples in [Tamil Nadu](29918.md). Most worked part-time and received the use of temple farmland as reward.[^cite_note-michell58-87] For those thus employed by the temple, according to Michell, "some gratuitous services were usually considered obligatory, such as dragging the temple chariots on festival occasions and helping when a large building project was undertaken".[^cite_note-michell58-87] Temples also acted as refuge during times of political unrest and danger.[^cite_note-michell58-87]

Historically, the [Scheduled Castes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheduled_Castes) or [Dalits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalits) were prohibited from the entry into temples.[^cite_note-88][^cite_note-89][^cite_note-90]

In contemporary times, the process of building a Hindu temple by emigrants and diasporas from South Asia has also served as a process of building a community, a social venue to network, reduce prejudice and seek civil rights together.[^cite_note-91]

### Library of manuscripts

John Guy and Jorrit Britschgi state Hindu temples served as centers where ancient manuscripts were routinely used for learning and where the texts were copied when they wore out.[^cite_note-92] In South India, temples and associated *mathas* served custodial functions, and a large number of manuscripts on [Hindu philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_philosophy), poetry, grammar and other subjects were written, multiplied and preserved inside the temples.[^cite_note-93] Archaeological and epigraphical evidence indicates existence of libraries called *Sarasvati-bhandara*, dated possibly to early 12th-century and employing librarians, attached to Hindu temples.[^cite_note-94]

Palm-leaf manuscripts called [lontar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palm-leaf_manuscript#Javanese_and_Balinese) in dedicated stone libraries have been discovered by archaeologists at Hindu temples in [Bali](4147.md) Indonesia and in 10th century Cambodian temples such as Angkor Wat and [Banteay Srei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banteay_Srei).[^cite_note-95]

### Temple schools

Inscriptions from the 4th century CE suggest the existence of schools around Hindu temples, called *Ghatikas* or *Mathas*, where the [Vedas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas) were studied.[^cite_note-96] In south India, 9th century Vedic schools attached to Hindu temples were called *Calai* or *Salai*, and these provided free boarding and lodging to students and scholars.[^cite_note-97][^cite_note-Dayalan1992p202-98] The temples linked to [Bhakti movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhakti_movement) in the early 2nd millennium, were dominated by non-Brahmins.[^cite_note-scharfetempleschools-99] These assumed many educational functions, including the exposition, recitation and public discourses of Sanskrit and Vedic texts.[^cite_note-scharfetempleschools-99] Some temple schools offered wide range of studies, ranging from Hindu scriptures to Buddhist texts, grammar, philosophy, martial arts, music and painting.[^cite_note-michelltempleschool-81][^cite_note-scharfetempleschools2-100] By the 8th century, Hindu temples also served as the social venue for tests, debates, team competition and Vedic recitals called *Anyonyam*.[^cite_note-michelltempleschool-81][^cite_note-scharfetempleschools2-100]

### Hospitals, community kitchen, monasteries

According to Kenneth G. Zysk—a professor specializing in Indology and ancient medicine, Hindu *mathas* and temples had by the 10th-century attached medical care along with their religious and educational roles.[^cite_note-Zysk1998p45-101] This is evidenced by various inscriptions found in Bengal, Andhra Pradesh and elsewhere. An inscription dated to about 930 CE states the provision of a physician to two *matha* to care for the sick and destitute. Another inscription dated to 1069 at a Vishnu temple in Tamil Nadu describes a hospital attached to the temple, listing the nurses, physicians, medicines and beds for patients. Similarly, a stone inscription in Andhra Pradesh dated to about 1262 mentions the provision of a *prasutishala* (maternity house), *vaidya* (physician), an *arogyashala* (health house) and a *viprasattra* (hospice, kitchen) with the religious center where people from all social backgrounds could be fed and cared for.[^cite_note-Zysk1998p45-101][^cite_note-102] According to Zysk, both Buddhist monasteries and Hindu religious centers provided facilities to care for the sick and needy in the 1st millennium, but with the destruction of Buddhist centers after the 12th century, the Hindu religious institutions assumed these social responsibilities.[^cite_note-Zysk1998p45-101] According to George Michell, Hindu temples in South India were active charity centers and they provided free meal for wayfarers, pilgrims and devotees, as well as boarding facilities for students and hospitals for the sick.[^cite_note-103]

The 15th and 16th century Hindu temples at [Hampi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hampi) featured storage spaces (temple granary, *kottara*), water tanks and kitchens.[^cite_note-104][^cite_note-105][^cite_note-106] Many major pilgrimage sites have featured *dharmashalas* since early times. These were attached to Hindu temples, particularly in South India, providing a bed and meal to pilgrims. They relied on any voluntary donation the visitor may leave and to land grants from local rulers.[^cite_note-sen126-107] Some temples have operated their kitchens on a daily basis to serve the visitor and the needy, while others during major community gatherings or festivals. Examples include the major kitchens run by Hindu temples in [Udupi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Udupi) (Karnataka), [Puri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puri_\(city\)) (Odisha) and [Tirupati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tirupati) (Andhra Pradesh). The tradition of sharing food in smaller temple is typically called *prasada*.[^cite_note-sen126-107][^cite_note-108]

Step well temples

In arid western parts of India, such as Rajasthan and Gujarat, Hindu communities built large walk-in wells that served as the only source of water in dry months but also served as social meeting places and carried religious significance. These monuments went down into the earth towards subterranean water, up to seven storeys, and were part of a temple complex.[^cite_note-jneubauer-113] These vav (literally, stepwells) had intricate art reliefs on the walls, with numerous *murtis* and images of Hindu deities, water spirits and erotic symbolism. The step wells were named after Hindu deities; for example, [Mata Bhavani's Stepwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mata_Bhavani's_Stepwell), Ankol Mata Vav, Sikotari Vav and others.[^cite_note-jneubauer-113] The temple ranged from being small single pada (cell) structure to large nearby complexes. These stepwells and their temple compounds have been variously dated from late 1st millennium BCE through 11th century CE. Of these, [Rani ki vav](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rani_ki_vav), with hundreds of art reliefs including many of [Vishnu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishnu) deity [avatars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avatar), has been declared a UNESCO World Heritage site.[^cite_note-114]

Cave temples

The [Indian rock-cut architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_rock-cut_architecture) evolved in Maharashtran temple style in the 1st millennium CE. The temples are carved from a single piece of rock as a complete temple or carved in a cave to look like the interior of a temple. [Ellora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellora) Temple is an example of the former, while The [Elephanta Caves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elephanta_Caves) are representative of the latter style.[^cite_note-\:0-115] The [Elephanta Caves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elephanta_Caves) consist of two groups of caves—the first is a large group of five Hindu caves and the second is a smaller group of two Buddhist caves. The Hindu caves contain rock-cut stone sculptures, representing the Shaiva Hindu sect, dedicated to the god Shiva.[^cite_note-\:0-115]

## Arts inside Hindu temples



Dancing [Ganesha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ganesha) sculpture at [Hoysaleswara Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoysaleswara_Temple), intricate designs of [Hoysala architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoysala_architecture) are seen.

A typical, ancient Hindu temple has a profusion of arts—from paintings to sculpture, from symbolic icons to engravings, from thoughtful layout of space to fusion of mathematical principles with Hindu sense of time and cardinality.

Ancient Sanskrit texts classify *murtis* and images in a number of ways. For example, one method of classification is the dimensionality of completion:[^cite_note-grao1914-116]

- **Chitra**: images that are three-dimensional and completely formed

- **Chitrardha**: images that are engraved in half relief

- **Chitrabhasa**: images that are two-dimensional, such as paintings on walls and cloth





Images and *murtis* inside Hindu temples vary widely in their expression. *Raudra* or *ugra* images express destruction, fear and violence, such as the Kali image at left. *Shanta* or *saumya* images express joy, knowledge and harmony, such as the Saraswati image at right.

Another way of classification is by the expressive state of the image:

- **Raudra** or **Dugra**: are images that were meant to terrify, induce fear. These typically have wide, circular eyes, carry weapons, have skulls and bones as adornment. These *murtis* were worshiped by soldiers before going to war, or by people in times of distress or terrors. Raudra deity temples were not set up inside villages or towns, but invariably outside and in remote areas of a kingdom.

  [^cite_note-grao1914-116]

- **Shanta** and **saumya**: are images that were pacific, peaceful and expressive of love, compassion, kindness and other virtues in Hindu pantheon. These images would carry symbolic icons of peace, knowledge, music, wealth, flowers, sensuality among other things. In ancient India, these temples were predominant inside villages and towns.

  [^cite_note-grao1914-116]

A Hindu temple may or may not include a *murti* or images, but larger temples usually do. Personal Hindu temples at home or a hermitage may have a pada for yoga or meditation, but be devoid of anthropomorphic representations of god. Nature or others arts may surround him or her. To a Hindu yogin, states Gopinath Rao,[^cite_note-grao1914-116] one who has realised the Self and the Universal Principle within himself, there is no need for any temple or divine image for worship. However, for those who have yet to reach this height of realization, various symbolic manifestations through images, *murtis* and icons as well as mental modes of worship are offered as one of the spiritual paths in the Hindu way of life. Some ancient Hindu scriptures like the Jabaladarshana Upanishad appear to endorse this idea[^cite_note-grao1914-116]

> शिवमात्मनि पश्यन्ति प्रतिमासु न योगिनः ।\
> &#x20;अज्ञानं भावनार्थाय प्रतिमाः परिकल्पिताः ॥५९॥\
> &#x20;\- जाबालदर्शनोपनिषत्
>
> A yogin perceives god (Siva) within himself; images are for those who have not reached this knowledge.

— *Jabaladarsana Upanishad*, verse 59[^cite_note-117]

However, devotees aspiring to a personal relationship with the Supreme Lord, whom they worship variously as Krishna or Shiva, for example, tend to reverse such hierarchical views of self-realization, holding that the personal form of the deity, as the source of the Brahma-jyoti, or the light into which impersonalists, according to their ideals, propose to merge themselves and their individual identities, will benevolently accept worship through an *arca vigraha,* an authorized form constructed not according to imagination but in pursuit of scriptural directives.

## Historical development and destruction

Main article: [Hindu temple architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_temple_architecture)

A number of ancient Indian texts suggest the prevalence of *murtis*, temples and shrines in Indian subcontinent for thousands of years. For example, the temples of the Koshala kingdom are mentioned in the [Valmiki Ramayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valmiki_Ramayana)[^cite_note-118] (various recent scholars' estimates for the earliest stage of the text range from the 7th to 4th centuries BCE, with later stages extending up to the 3rd century CE)[^cite_note-Brockington1998-119] The 5th century BCE text, *[Astadhyayi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astadhyayi)*, mentions male deity *arcas* or *murtis* of Agni, Indra, Varuna, Rudra, Mrda, Pusa, Surya, and Soma being worshipped, as well as the worship of arcas of female goddesses such as Indrani, Varunani, Usa, Bhavani, Prthivi and Vrsakapayi.[^cite_note-mm1988e-120] The 2nd century BCE "[Mahabhasya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahabhasya)" of [Patanjali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patanjali) extensively describes temples of *Dhanapati* (deity of wealth and finance, Kubera), as well as temples of Rama and Kesava, wherein the worship included dance, music and extensive rituals. The *Mahabhasya* also describes the rituals for Krsna, Visnu and Siva. An image recovered from Mathura in north India has been dated to the 2nd century BCE.[^cite_note-mm1988e-120] Kautilya's [Arthashastra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthashastra) from 4th century BCE describes a city of temples, each enshrining various Vedic and Puranic deities. All three of these sources have common names, describe common rituals, symbolism and significance possibly suggesting that the idea of *murtis*, temples and shrines passed from one generation to next, in ancient India, at least from the 4th century BCE.[^cite_note-mm1988e-120] The oldest temples, suggest scholars, were built of [brick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brick) and [wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood). [Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_stone) became the preferred material of construction later.[^cite_note-121][^cite_note-The_Hindu_Temple_1946-122]

Early Jain and Buddhist literature, along with Kautilya's Arthashastra, describe structures, embellishments and designs of these temples—all with motifs and deities currently prevalent in Hinduism. Bas-reliefs and *murtis* have been found from 2nd to 3rd century, but none of the temple structures have survived. Scholars[^cite_note-mm1988e-120] theorize that those ancient temples of India, later referred to as Hindu temples, were modeled after domestic structure—a house or a palace. Beyond shrines, nature was revered, in forms such as trees, rivers, and stupas, before the time of Buddha and Vardhamana Mahavira. As Jainism and Buddhism branched off from the religious tradition later to be called Hinduism, the ideas, designs and plans of ancient Vedic and Upanishad era shrines were adopted and evolved, likely from the competitive development of temples and arts in Jainism and Buddhism. Ancient reliefs found so far, states Michael Meister,[^cite_note-mm1988e-120] suggest five basic shrine designs and combinations thereof in 1st millennium BCE:

1. A raised platform with or without a symbol

2. A raised platform under an umbrella

3. A raised platform under a tree

4. A raised platform enclosed with a railing

5. A raised platform inside a pillared pavilion

Many of these ancient shrines were roofless, some had *[toranas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torana)* and roof.

From the 1st century BCE through 3rd century CE, the evidence and details about ancient temples increases. The ancient literature refers to these temples as *Pasada* (or Prasada), *stana*, *mahasthana*, *devalaya*, *devagrha*, *devakula*, *devakulika*, *ayatana* and *harmya*.[^cite_note-mm1988e-120] The entrance of the temple is referred to as *dvarakosthaka* in these ancient texts notes Meister,[^cite_note-mm1988e-120] the temple hall is described as *sabha* or *ayagasabha*, pillars were called *kumbhaka*, while *vedika* referred to the structures at the boundary of a temple.

With the start of [Gupta dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gupta_dynasty) in the 4th century, Hindu temples flourished in innovation, design, scope, form, use of stone and new materials as well as symbolic synthesis of culture and [dharmic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharma) principles with artistic expression.[^cite_note-125][^cite_note-126] It is this period that is credited with the ideas of garbhagrha for *Purusa*, mandapa for sheltering the devotees and rituals in progress, as well as symbolic motifs relating to dharma, karma, kama, artha and moksha. Temple superstructures were built from stone, brick and wide range of materials. Entrance ways, walls and pillars were intricately carved, while parts of temple were decorated with gold, silver and jewels. Visnu, Siva and other deities were placed in Hindu temples, while Buddhists and Jains built their own temples, often side by side with Hindus.[^cite_note-127]

The 4th through 6th century marked the flowering of *Vidharbha* style, whose accomplishments survive in central India as [Ajanta Caves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ajanta_Caves), [Pavnar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pavnar?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Mandhal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandhal) and [Mahesvar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahesvar?action=edit\&redlink=1). In the [Malaprabha river](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaprabha_river) basin, South India, this period is credited with some of the earliest stone temples of the region: the [Badami Chalukya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Badami_Chalukya_architecture) temples are dated to the 5th century by some scholars,[^cite_note-128] and the 6th by some others.[^cite_note-129]

Over 6th and 7th centuries, temple designs were further refined during [Maurya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurya) dynasty, evidence of which survives today at [Ellora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellora) and in the [Elephanta cave temples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elephanta_Caves).

It is the 5th through 7th century CE when outer design and appearances of Hindu temples in north India and south India began to widely diverge.[^cite_note-130] Nevertheless, the forms, theme, symbolism and central ideas in the grid design remained same, before and after, pan-India as innovations were adopted to give distinctly different visual expressions.

The [Western Chalukya architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Chalukya_architecture) of the 11th- and 12th-century [Tungabhadra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tungabhadra) region of modern central [Karnataka](16880.md) includes many temples. [Step-wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Step-well) are consist of a shaft dug to the [water table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_table), with steps descending to the water; while they were built for secular purposes, some are also decorated as temples, or serve as a [temple tank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_tank).

During the 5th to 11th century, Hindu temples flourished outside Indian subcontinent, such as in [Cambodia](334751.md), [Vietnam](202354.md), [Malaysia](3607937.md) and [Indonesia](14579.md). In Cambodia, [Khmer architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khmer_architecture) favoured the [Temple mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khmer_architecture#Temple_mountain) style famously used in Angkor Wat, with a [prang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prang_\(architecture\)) spire over the sanctum cell. Indonesian [candi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candi_of_Indonesia) developed regional forms. In what is modern south and central Vietnam, [Champa architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_of_Champa#Temples_of_brick) built brick temples.

Destruction, conversion, and rebuilding Many Hindu temples have been destroyed, some, after rebuilding, several times. Deliberate temple destruction usually had religious motives. Richard Eaton has listed 80 campaigns of Hindu temple site destruction stretching over centuries, particularly from the 12th through the 18th century.[^cite_note-131] Others temples have served as non-Hindu places of worship, either after conversion or simultaneously with Hindu use.

In the 12th–16th century, during [Muslim conquests in the Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_conquests_in_the_Indian_subcontinent) and South Asia, Hindu temples, along with the temples of Buddhists and Jains, intermittently became targets of armies from Persian, Central Asian, and Indian sultanates. Imagined by these foreign zealots to be mere idols, sacred [Forms of various deities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iconoclasm) were broken, spires and pillars were torn down, and temples were looted of their treasury. Some temples were [converted into mosques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversion_of_non-Islamic_places_of_worship_into_mosques), or parts used to build mosques.[^cite_note-132] There exist both [Indian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_religion_in_India#Tradition_of_religious_freedom) and [Muslim traditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhimmi) of religious toleration. Muslim rulers led campaigns of temple destruction and forbade repairs to damaged temples, following the Muslim traditions. The [Delhi Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delhi_Sultanate) destroyed a large number of temples; [Sikandar the Iconoclast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikandar_Shah_Miri), [Sultan of Kashmir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Kashmir#Prelude_and_Kashmir_Sultanate_\(1346%E2%80%931580s\)), was also known for his intolerance.[^cite_note-133]

The 16th- and 19th-century [Goa Inquisition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goa_Inquisition) destroyed hundreds of Hindu temples. All Hindu temples in Portuguese colonies in India were destroyed, according to a 1569 letter in the Portuguese royal archives.[^cite_note-134] Temples were converted into churches.[^cite_note-135][^cite_note-136][^cite_note-137] Religious conflict and desecrations of places of worship continued during the [British colonial era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Raj).[^cite_note-138] Historian Sita Ram Goel's book "What happened to Hindu Temples" lists over 2000 sites where temples have been destroyed and mosques have been built over them. Some historians suggest that around 30,000 temples were destroyed by Islamic rulers between 1200 and 1800 CE. Destruction of Hindu temple sites was comparatively less in the southern parts of India, such as in [Tamil Nadu](29918.md). Cave-style Hindu temples that were carved inside a rock, hidden and rediscovered centuries later, such as the [Kailasa Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kailasa_Temple,_Ellora), have also preferentially survived. Many are now UNESCO world heritage sites.[^cite_note-139]

In India, the Place of Worship (Special Provisions) Act was enacted in 1991 which prohibited the conversion of any religious site from the religion to which it was dedicated on 15 August 1947.[^cite_note-140][^cite_note-141][^cite_note-142]

## Customs and etiquette



[Jagannath Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jagannath_Temple,_Puri) at [Puri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puri_\(city\)), one of the [Char Dham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Char_Dham): the four main spiritual centers of Hinduism

In Hinduism, temples are considered sacred spaces where the divine is believed to manifest, and devotees visit these places to experience the presence and blessings of the deity. The customs and etiquette for visiting temples varies across India. Devotees in major temples may bring in symbolic offerings for the [puja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puja_\(Hinduism\)). This includes fruits, flowers, sweets and other symbols of the bounty of the natural world. Temples in India are usually surrounded with small shops selling these offerings.[^cite_note-147]

When inside the temple, devotees keep both hands folded ([namaste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Namaste) [mudra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mudra)). The inner sanctuary, where the [murtis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murtis) reside, is known as the *[garbhagriha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garbhagriha)*. It symbolizes the birthplace of the universe, the meeting place of the gods and humankind, and the threshold between the transcendental and the phenomenal worlds.[^cite_note-148] It is in this inner shrine that devotees seek *[darśana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darshan_\(Indian_religions\))* (seeing and being seen by the auspicious sight of the divine)[^cite_note-149] and offer prayers. Devotees may or may not be able to personally present their offerings at the feet of the deity. In most large Indian temples, only the [pujaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pujari) (priest) are allowed to enter the main sanctum.[^cite_note-Narayanan-150]

Temple management staff typically announce the hours of operation, including timings for special [pujas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puja_\(Hinduism\)). These timings and nature of special puja vary from temple to temple. Additionally, there may be specially allotted times for devotees to perform [pradakshina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pradakshina) (circumambulations) around the temple.[^cite_note-Narayanan-150]

Visitors and worshipers to large Hindu temples may be required to deposit their shoes and other footwear before entering. Where this is expected, the temples provide an area and help staff to store footwear. Dress codes vary. It is customary in temples in [Kerala](4349459.md), for men to remove shirts and to cover pants and shorts with a traditional cloth known as a *[Mundu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mundu)*.[^cite_note-151] In Java and Bali (Indonesia), before one enters the most sacred parts of a Hindu temple, shirts are required as well as a [sarong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarong) around one's waist.[^cite_note-152] At many other locations, this formality is unnecessary.

## Regional variations in Hindu temples

### Nagara Architecture of North Indian temples



[Kedarnath Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kedarnath_Temple), [Uttarakhand](1429154.md)



[Sivasagar Sivadol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sivasagar_Sivadol), [Assam](186162.md)

North Indian temples are referred to as Nagara style of temple architecture.[^cite_note-adamhardy-153] They have *sanctum sanctorum* where the deity is present, open on one side from where the devotee obtains *[darśana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dar%C5%9Bana)*. There may or may not be many more surrounding corridors, halls, etc. However, there will be space for devotees to go around the temple in clockwise fashion [circumambulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumambulation). In North Indian temples, the tallest towers are built over the *sanctum sanctorum* in which the deity is installed.[^cite_note-154]

The north India Nagara style of temple designs often deploy [fractal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractal)-theme, where smaller parts of the temple are themselves images or geometric re-arrangement of the large temple, a concept that later inspired French and Russian architecture such as the [matryoshka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matryoshka_doll) principle. One difference is the scope and cardinality, where Hindu temple structures deploy this principle in every dimension with garbhgriya as the primary locus, and each pada as well as zones serving as additional centers of [loci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Focus_\(geometry\)). This makes a Nagara Hindu temple architecture symbolically a perennial expression of movement and time, of centrifugal growth fused with the idea of unity in everything.[^cite_note-adamhardy-153]

### Temples in West Bengal



[Dakshineswar Kali Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dakshineswar_Kali_Temple), [Kolkata](47905.md)

In [West Bengal](34040.md), the [Bengali terra cotta temple architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengal_temple_architecture#West_Bengal_terra_cotta_temple_architecture) is found. Due to lack of suitable stone in the alluvial soil locally available, the temple makers had to resort to other materials instead of stone. This gave rise to using [terracotta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terracotta) as a medium for temple construction. Terracotta exteriors with rich carvings are a unique feature of Bengali temples. The town of [Bishnupur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishnupur,_Bankura) in West Bengal is renowned for this type of architecture. There is also a popular style of building known as Naba-ratna (nine-towered) or Pancha-ratna (five-towered). An example of Navaratna style is the [Dakshineswar Kali Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dakshineswar_Kali_Temple).[^cite_note-155]

### Temples in Odisha

[Odisha](250724.md) temple architecture is known as [Kalinga architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalinga_architecture),[^cite_note-156] classifies the spire into three parts, the *Bāḍa* (lower limb), the *Ganḍi* (body) and the *Cuḷa/Mastaka* (head). Each part is decorated in a different manner. Kalinga architecture is a style which flourished in Kalinga, the name for kingdom that included ancient Odisha. It includes three styles: *Rekha Deula*, *Pidha Deula* and *Khakhara Deula*.[^cite_note-157] The former two are associated with [Vishnu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishnu), [Surya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surya) and [Shiva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shiva) temples while the third is mainly associated with [Chamunda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamunda) and [Durga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durga) temples. The *Rekha Deula* and *Khakhara Deula* houses the *[sanctum sanctorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanctum_sanctorum)* while the *Pidha Deula* style includes space for outer dancing and offering halls.

### Temples of Goa and Konkani



Saptakoteshwar Temple, Goa

The temple architecture of Goa is quite unique. As Portuguese colonial hegemony increased, Goan Hindu temples became the rallying point to local resistance.[^cite_note-158] Many these temples are not more than 500 years old, and are a unique blend of original Goan temple architecture, Dravidian, Nagar and [Hemadpanthi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemadpanthi) temple styles with some [British](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_architecture) and [Portuguese architectural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_architecture) influences. Goan temples were built using sedimentary rocks, wood, limestone and clay tiles, and copper sheets were used for the roofs. These temples were decorated with mural art called as *[Kavi kala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaavi_art)* or *ocher art*. The interiors have murals and wood carvings depicting scenes from the [Hindu mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_mythology).

  A row of gopurams (towers) in the [Srirangam Ranganathaswmy temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranganathaswamy_Temple,_Srirangam), a typical South Indian [Vaishnavate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaishnavism) temple complex in [Srirangam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Srirangam), [Tamil Nadu](29918.md)

- Koneswaram Temple, a Tamil Saivate temple in Tirukonamalai, Sri Lanka

  [Koneswaram Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koneswaram_Temple), a Tamil [Saivate temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saivaite) in [Tirukonamalai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trincomalee), [Sri Lanka](26750.md)

South Indian temples have a large [gopuram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gopuram), a monumental tower, usually ornate, at the entrance of the temple. This forms a prominent feature of [Koils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koil), [Hindu temples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_temple_architecture) of the [Dravidian style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dravidian_architecture).[^cite_note-ching2-159] They are topped by the *kalasam*, a bulbous stone [finial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finial). They function as gateways through the walls that surround the temple complex.[^cite_note-ching-160] The gopuram's origins can be traced back to early structures of the [Tamil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamil_people) kings [Pallavas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pallavas); and by the twelfth century, under the [Pandya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pandya) rulers, these gateways became a dominant feature of a temple's outer appearance, eventually overshadowing the inner sanctuary which became obscured from view by the gopuram's colossal size.[^cite_note-mitchell-161] It also dominated the inner sanctum in amount of ornamentation. Often a shrine has more than one gopuram.[^cite_note-162] They also appear in architecture outside India, especially [Khmer architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khmer_architecture), as at [Angkor Wat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angkor_Wat). A koil may have multiple gopurams, typically constructed into multiple walls in tiers around the main shrine. The temple's walls are typically square with the outer most wall having gopuras. The [sanctum sanctorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanctum_sanctorum) and its towering roof (the central deity's shrine) are also called the *[vimanam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vimana_\(shrine\))*.[^cite_note-163] The inner sanctum has restricted access with only priests allowed beyond a certain point.

#### Temples in Kerala



[Padmanabhaswamy Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Padmanabhaswamy_Temple) in [Thiruvananthapuram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thiruvananthapuram), [Kerala](4349459.md)

Temples in Kerala have a different architectural style (keeping the same essence of Vastu), especially due to climatic differences Kerala have with other parts of India with larger rainfall. The temple roof is mostly tiled and is sloped and the walls are often square, the innermost shrine being entirely enclosed in another four walls to which only the pujari (priest) enters. The walls are decorated with either mural paintings or rock sculptures which many times are emphasised on Dwarapalakas.

#### Temples in Tamil Nadu



An aerial view of the [Ranganathaswamy Temple, Srirangam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranganathaswamy_Temple,_Srirangam) in [Srirangam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Srirangam), often known as *Bhuloka Vaikuntham* and First among the 108 [Vaishnavate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaishnavism) [Divya Desams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divya_Desam)

The [Srirangam Ranganathaswamy temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranganathaswamy_Temple,_Srirangam) is the worlds largest functioning Hindu temple.[^cite_note-164] The temple is present in [Tamil Nadu](29918.md), the temple was first built by the Chola ruler, Dharmavarma. The Kaveri river flood destroyed the temple and *vimanam* submerged in the island, and later, the [early Cholas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Cholas) King [Killivalavan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killivalavan) rebuilt the temple complex as is present today. Beyond the ancient textual history, archaeological evidence such as inscriptions refer to this temple, and these stone inscriptions are from late 100 BCE to 100 CE.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESircar197964-165] Hence, making it one of the Oldest surviving active temple complexes in South India. Later, the temple constructions reached its peak during the [Pallavas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pallavas) era. They built various temples around [Kancheepuram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kancheepuram), and [Narasimhavarman II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narasimhavarman_II) built the [Thirukadalmallai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirukadalmallai) and [Shore Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shore_Temple) in Mamallapuram, a UNESCO [World Heritage Site](44940.md). The [Pandyas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pandyas) rule created temples such as the [Koodal Azhagar temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koodal_Azhagar_temple) and [Meenakshi Amman Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meenakshi_Amman_Temple) at Madurai and [Srivilliputhur Andal Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Srivilliputhur_Andal_Temple) at Srivilliputhur.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESastri197018–182-166] The [Cholas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chola_dynasty) were prolific temple builders right from the times of the first medieval king [Vijayalaya Chola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vijayalaya_Chola). The Chola temples include [Sri Ranganathaswamy Temple at Srirangam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranganathaswamy_Temple,_Srirangam), the [Brihadeeshwarar temple at Tanjore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brihadisvara_Temple,_Thanjavur), [Brihadeeshwarar temple at Gangaikonda Cholapuram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brihadisvara_Temple,_Gangaikonda_Cholapuram) and the [Airavatesvarar Temple of Darasuram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airavatesvara_Temple) which are among the [UNESCO](21786641.md) World Heritage Sites. The [Nayaks of Madurai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madurai_Nayak_dynasty) reconstructed some of the well-known temples in Tamil Nadu such as the [Meenakshi Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meenakshi_Temple).[^cite_note-susanlchap4-10] One of the southernmost famous temples in South India, the Ramanathaswamy Temple was built in the 17th century on the island of Rameshwaram in Tamil Nadu.[^cite_note-167]

### Khmer Temples



Art relief at the Hindu temple [Banteay Srei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banteay_Srei) in Cambodia

[Angkor Wat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angkor_Wat) was built as a Hindu temple by King Suryavarman II in the early 12th century in Yasodharapura (Khmer, present-day [Angkor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angkor)), the capital of the Khmer Empire, as his state temple and eventual mausoleum. Breaking from the Shaiva tradition of previous kings, Angkor Wat was instead dedicated to Vishnu. The Spire in Khmer Hindu temple is called Giri (mountain) and symbolizes the residence of gods just like Meru does in Bali Hindu mythology and Ku (Guha) does in Burmese Hindu mythology.[^cite_note-169]

Angkor Wat is just one of numerous Hindu temples in [Cambodia](334751.md), most of them in ruins. Hundreds of Hindu temples are scattered from Siem Reap to Sambor Prei Kuk in central Cambodian region.[^cite_note-170]

### Temples in Indonesia

Main articles: [Candi of Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candi_of_Indonesia), [List of Hindu temples in Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Hindu_temples_in_Indonesia), and [Balinese temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balinese_temple)



[Besakih Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Besakih_Temple) in [Bali](4147.md), [Indonesia](14579.md)

Ancient Hindu temples in [Indonesia](14579.md) are called *[Candi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candi_of_Indonesia)* (read: *chandi*). Prior to the rise of [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_Indonesia), between the 5th to 15th century Dharmic faiths (Hinduism and Buddhism) were the majority in Indonesian archipelago, especially in [Java](69336.md) and [Sumatra](28679.md). As the result numerous Hindu temples, locally known as *[candi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candi_of_Indonesia)*, constructed and dominated the landscape of Java. According to local beliefs, Java valley had thousands of Hindu temples which co-existed with Buddhist temples, most of which were buried in [massive eruption of Mount Merapi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Merapi#Geological_history) in 1006 CE.[^cite_note-171][^cite_note-172]



[Pura Ulun Danu Bratan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pura_Ulun_Danu_Bratan), [Bali](4147.md), [Indonesia](14579.md)

Between 1,100 and 1,500 additional Hindu temples were built, but abandoned by Hindus and Buddhists as Islam spread in Java circa 15th to 16th century.

In last 200 years, some of these have been rediscovered mostly by farmers while preparing their lands for crops. Most of these ancient temples were rediscovered and reconstructed between 19th to 20th century, and treated as the important [archaeological findings](18951655.md) and also as [tourist attraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourist_attraction),[^cite_note-Hindu_Temple_Tour-173] but not as the house of worship. Hindu temples of ancient [Java](69336.md) bear resemblances with temples of [South Indian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Indian) style. The largest of these is the 9th century Javanese Hindu temple, [Prambanan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prambanan) in [Yogyakarta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogyakarta), now a [UNESCO](21786641.md) world heritage site. It was designed as three concentric squares and has 224 temples. The inner square contains 16 temples dedicated to major Hindu deities, of which [Shiva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shiva) temple is the largest.[^cite_note-174] The temple has extensive wall reliefs and carvings illustrating the stories from the Hindu epic [Ramayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramayana).[^cite_note-175]



[Prambanan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prambanan) temple complex in [Yogyakarta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogyakarta), Indonesia

In [Bali](4147.md), the [Hindu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balinese_Hinduism) temple is known as "[Pura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pura_\(Balinese_temple\))", which is designed as an open-air worship place in a walled compound. The compound walls have a series of intricately decorated gates without doors for the devotee to enter. The design, plan and layout of the holy pura follows a square layout.[^cite_note-176][^cite_note-177]

The majority of Hindu temples in Java were dedicated to Shiva, who Javanese Hindus considered as the God who commands the energy to destroy, recombine and recreate the cycle of life. Small temples were often dedicated to Shiva and his family (wife Durga, son Ganesha). Larger temple complexes include temples for Vishnu and Brahma, but the most majestic, sophisticated and central temple was dedicated to Shiva. The 732 CE Canggal inscription found in Southern Central Java, written in Indonesian Sanskrit script, eulogizes Shiva, calling him God par-excellence.

Other sites in Vietnam with Hindu temples include Phan Rang with the Cham temple [Po Klong Garai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Po_Klong_Garai).[^cite_note-179]

Although most indigenous Hindu temples are ruins, a few such as [Devasathan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devasathan) in Bangkok are actively used.

### Temples outside Asia



Hindu Mandir in Helenelund, [Sollentuna Municipality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sollentuna_Municipality), Sweden

Many members of the diaspora from the Indian subcontinent have established Hindu temples outside India as a means of preserving and celebrating cultural and spiritual heritage abroad. Describing the hundreds of temples that can be found throughout the United States, scholar Gail M. Harley observes, "The temples serve as central locations where Hindus can come together to worship during holy festivals and socialize with other Hindus. Temples in America reflect the colorful kaleidoscopic aspects contained in Hinduism while unifying people who are disbursed throughout the American landscape."[^cite_note-180] Numerous temples in North America and Europe have gained particular prominence and acclaim, many of which were built by the [Bochasanwasi Akshar Purushottam Swaminarayan Sanstha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bochasanwasi_Akshar_Purushottam_Swaminarayan_Sanstha). [New Jersey](21648.md) and [New York City](645042.md) have developed an exceptional variety of Hindu temples. The Ganesh temple of [Hindu Temple Society of North America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_Temple_Society_of_North_America), in [Flushing](267693.md), [Queens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queens), [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indians_in_the_New_York_City_metropolitan_region), is the oldest Hindu temple in the [Western Hemisphere](73162.md), and its [canteen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cafeteria) feeds 4,000 people a week, with as many as 10,000 during the [Diwali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diwali) (Deepavali) festival.[^cite_note-FlushingHinduTempleCanteen-181] Meanwhile, the Om Sri Sai Balaji Temple in [Monroe Township](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monroe_Township,_Middlesex_County,_New_Jersey), [Middlesex County, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middlesex_County,_New_Jersey), hosts the largest indoor [Hanuman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanuman) statue in the Western Hemisphere, embellished by a [laser lighting display](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_lighting_display) portraying the [Ramayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramayana) and Hanuman's heroic accomplishments in this epic Hindu story.[^cite_note-LargestIndoorHanumanStatue-182]

## Temple management

The [Archaeological Survey of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeological_Survey_of_India) has control of most ancient temples of archaeological importance in India. In India, day-to-day activities of a temple are managed by a temple board [committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee) that administers its [finances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finance), management, and events. Since independence, the autonomy of individual Hindu religious denominations to manage their own affairs with respect to temples of their own denomination has been severely eroded and the state governments have taken control of major Hindu temples in some countries; however, in others, such as the United States, private temple management autonomy has been preserved.

[^cite_note-50]: &#x20;The square is symbolic and has Vedic origins from the fire altar to [Agni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agni). The alignment along cardinal directions, similarly, is an extension of Vedic rituals of three fires. This symbolism is also found among Greek and other ancient civilizations, through the [gnomon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnomon). In Hindu temple manuals, design plans are described with 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81 up to 1024 squares; 1 pada is considered the simplest plan, as a seat for a hermit or devotee to sit and meditate on, or make offerings with the Vedic fire in front. The second design of 4 padas lacks the central core, and is also a meditative constructive. The 9-pada design has a sacred surrounded center, and is the template for the smallest temple. Older Hindu temple vastu-mandalas may use the 9- through 49-pada series, but 64 is considered the most sacred geometric grid in Hindu temples. It is also called *Manduka*, *Bhekapada* or *Ajira* in various ancient Sanskrit texts.
