**Baghdad**[^cite_note-4] is the capital and largest city in [Iraq](7515928.md). It is located on the banks of the [Tigris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigris) in central Iraq. The [city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City) has an estimated population of 8 million. It ranks among the [most populous and largest cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_cities_in_the_Arab_world) in the [Middle East](19323.md) and the [Arab world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_world) and constitutes 22% of [Iraq's population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographics_of_Iraq). Baghdad is a primary financial and commercial center in the region.

Founded in 762 AD by [al-Mansur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mansur) on a site with settlement dating back to at least the [Neo-Babylonian period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Babylonian_Empire), Baghdad became the capital of the [Abbasid Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Caliphate) and its most notable development project. The city evolved into an intellectual and cultural center. This, in addition to housing several key academic institutions, including the [House of Wisdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Wisdom), as well as a multi-ethnic and multi-religious environment, garnered it a worldwide reputation as the "Center of Learning". For much of the Abbasid era, during the [Islamic Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age), Baghdad was one of the largest cities in the world and rivaled [Chang'an](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang'an), as the population peaked at more than one million. It was largely [destroyed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Baghdad_\(1258\)) at the hands of the [Mongol Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_Empire) in 1258, resulting in a decline that would linger for many centuries due to frequent plagues and shifts in power between multiple successive empires. Later, Baghdad served as the [administrative center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad_Eyalet) of [Ottoman Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Iraq),[^cite_note-Ceylan_2009_61-5] exercising authority over the provinces of [Basra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basra_Eyalet), [Mosul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosul_Eyalet), and [Shahrizor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shahrizor_Eyalet).[^cite_note-Ceylan_2011_121-6]

During the [First World War](4764461.md), Baghdad was made the capital of Mandatory Iraq. With the recognition of Iraq as an [independent monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Iraq) in 1932, it gradually regained some of its former prominence as a significant center of [Arab culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_culture). During the Ba'ath Party rule, the city experienced a period of relative prosperity and growth. However, it faced [severe infrastructural damage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damage_to_Baghdad_during_the_Iraq_War) due to the [Iraq War](5043324.md), which began with the [invasion of Iraq](201936.md) in 2003, resulting in a [substantial loss of cultural heritage and historical artifacts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeological_looting_in_Iraq). During the [insurgency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_insurgency_\(2011%E2%80%932013\)) and renewed [war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_in_Iraq_\(2013%E2%80%932017\)) from 2013 to 2017, it had one of the highest rates of [terrorist attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_massacres_in_Iraq) in the world. However, these attacks have gradually declined since the territorial defeat of the [Islamic State](9087364.md) militant group in Iraq in 2017, and are now [rare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_State_insurgency_in_Iraq_\(2017%E2%80%93present\)). Since the end of the war, numerous reconstruction projects have been underway to induce stability.

Iraq's largest city, Baghdad is the seat of government. It generates 40% of the [economy of Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Iraq). A major center of [Islamic history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_history), Baghdad is home to [numerous historic mosques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_mosques_in_Baghdad), as well as churches, [mandis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandi_\(Mandaeism\)) and [synagogues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Jewish_sites_in_Iraq), highlighting the city's historical diversity. Religious sites such as [Masjid al-Kadhimayn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Kazimiyya_Mosque), [Buratha Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buratha_Mosque), the [Shrine of Abdul-Qadir Gilani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mausoleum_of_Abdul-Qadir_Gilani) and [Abu Hanifa Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Hanifa_Mosque) are visited by millions of people annually. It was once home to a large [Jewish](25955086.md) community and was regularly visited by [Sikh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikh) pilgrims from India.[^cite_note-Baghdad2-7] Baghdad is a regional cultural hub. The city is well known for its coffeehouses.

## Name

The name *Baghdad* is [pre-Islamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Islamic_Arabia), and its origin is disputed.[^cite_note-EI2-8] The site where the city of Baghdad developed has been populated for millennia. Archaeological evidence shows that the site of Baghdad was occupied long before the [Arab conquest of Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_conquest_of_Mesopotamia) in 637 CE, and that several ancient empires had their capitals located in the surrounding area.[^cite_note-9]

Arab authors, realizing the pre-Islamic origins of Baghdad's name, generally looked for its roots in [Middle Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Persian).[^cite_note-EI2-8] They suggested various meanings, the most common of which was "bestowed by God".[^cite_note-EI2-8][^cite_note-10] Modern scholars generally tend to favor this etymology,[^cite_note-EI2-8] which views the word as a Persian compound of *bagh* () "god" and *dād* () "given".[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12] In [Old Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Persian) the first element can be traced to *boghu* and is related to [Indo-Iranian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Aryan_languages) *bhag* and [Slavic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavic_languages) *bog* "god."[^cite_note-EI2-8][^cite_note-FOOTNOTELe_Strange201110-13] A similar term in Middle Persian is the name *Mithradāt* (*[Mehrdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehrdad)* in [New Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Persian)), known in English by its borrowed Hellenistic form *Mithridates*, meaning "Given by [Mithra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mithra)" (*dāt* is the more archaic form of *dād*, related to Sanskrit *dāt*, Latin *dat* and English *donor*).[^cite_note-EI2-8] There are a number of other locations whose names are compounds of the Middle Persian word *bagh*, including [Baghlan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghlan) and [Bagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bagram) in Afghanistan, [Baghshan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghshan) in Iran,[^cite_note-14] and [Baghdati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdati) in [Georgia](48768.md), which likely share the same etymological Iranic origins.[^cite_note-15][^cite_note-books.google.nl-16][^cite_note-BRILL1987-17]

Other authors have suggested older origins for the name, in particular the name *Bagdadu* or *Hudadu* that existed in [Old Babylonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkadian_language) (spelled with a sign that can represent both *bag* and *hu*), and the [Jewish Babylonian Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_Babylonian_Aramaic) name of a place called *Baghdatha* (בגדתא).[^cite_note-EI2-8][^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19] A later attestation of the name is found in a boundary stone from the reign of [Merodach-baladan I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marduk-apla-iddina_I) (12th century BC), where it appears in cuneiform as Bag-da-du.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELevy20115-20] Some scholars suggested Aramaic derivations.[^cite_note-EI2-8]

Another highly recommended view is that Baghdad is a reference to Bagh and Dad as in Dadan, Dedan, and Dad as in Hadad, Adad. Another view suggested by Christophe Wall-Romana, is that name of "Baghdad" is derived from "[Akkad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkad_\(city\))", as the cuneiform logogram for Akkad (𒀀𒂵𒉈𒆠) is pronounced "*a-ga-dè*<sup>KI</sup>" ("Agade") and its resemblance to "Baghdad" is compelling.[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-Sallaberger-1999-22] It is argued that, throughout all the various spellings of the city's name, whether *Baghdad* \[بغداد], *Baghdadh* \[بغداذ], *Baghdan* \[بغدان], *Maghdad* \[مغداد], *Maghdadh* \[مغداذ], or *Maghdan* \[مغدان], the only phonetically definite segment of the name appears to be *Aghda* \[ىَغْدَا], which could be equated with the pronunciation of the name Agade.[^cite_note-Sallaberger-1999-22][^cite_note-FOOTNOTELe_Strange201110–11-23]

When the Abbasid caliph [al-Mansur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mansur) founded a completely new city for his capital, he chose the name "City of peace" (Arabic: مدینة السلام, romanized: *Madīnat as-Salām*), which now refers to the [Round City of Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Round_City_of_Baghdad) proper. By the 11th century, *Baghdad* became almost the exclusive name for the world-renowned metropolis.

Christophe Wall-Romana has suggested that al-Mansur's choice to found his "new city" at Baghdad because of its strategic location was the same criteria which influenced [Sargon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sargon_of_Akkad)'s choice to found the original city of [Akkad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akkad_\(city\)) in the exact same location.[^cite_note-24][^cite_note-25]

### Foundation

Archaeological evidence indicates that the site of Baghdad had been occupied prior to its Abbasid foundation. An extensive facing of [Neo-Babylonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Babylonian_Empire) brickwork has been identified along the western bank of the Tigris at Baghdad, with bricks stamped with the name and titles of [Nebuchadnezzar II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebuchadnezzar_II). Assyrian geographical catalogues during the reign of [Sardanapalus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sardanapalus) from the [Neo-Assyrian period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Assyrian_Empire) record a place whose name was very similar to “Baghdad”, which likely refers to the town standing on the site where the Abbasids would later build their capital.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELe_Strange20117–10-26]



The [Round City of Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Round_City_of_Baghdad) between 767 and 912 AD

After the fall of the [Umayyads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umayyad_Caliphate), the victorious Abbasids sought a new capital.[^cite_note-Corzine-2005-27][^cite_note-Times_Books-2000-28] On 30 July 762, the Caliph [Al-Mansur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mansur) commissioned Baghdad's construction, guided by the [Iranian Barmakids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barmakids). He believed Baghdad was ideal for ruling the Islamic Empire. Historian [al-Tabari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Tabari) recorded a prophecy from [Christian monks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_monk) about a leader named [Miklas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miklas) building a great city in the area, and Al-Mansur, who was once called Miklas, saw this as a good omen. He expressed deep affection for the site, declaring it would be the home of his dynasty.[^cite_note-Corzine-2005-27][^cite_note-Times_Books-2000-28][^cite_note-29][^cite_note-30] The two designers who were hired by [Al-Mansur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mansur) to plan the city's design were [Naubakht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naubakht), a Zoroastrian who also determined that the date of the foundation of the city would be astrologically auspicious, and [Mashallah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mashallah_ibn_Athari), a Jew from [Khorasan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khorasan_province), [Iran](14653.md).[^cite_note-ref2-31][^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33] They determined the city's auspicious founding date under the sign of [Leo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_\(astrology\)) the [lion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asiatic_lion), symbolizing strength and expansion.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWiet197112-34]



[Statue of Abu Ja'far al-Mansur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statue_of_Abu_Ja'far_al-Mansur), the founder of Baghdad

The city's layout consisted of two large semicircles, with a 2 km-wide circular core known as the "Round City." It had parks, gardens, villas, and promenades. Unlike European cities of the time, Baghdad had a [sanitation system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanitation), [fountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fountains), and [public baths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_bathing), with thousands of [*hammams*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammam) enhancing hygiene. The mosque and guard headquarters stood at the center, though some central space's function remains unknown. Baghdad's circular design reflected ancient Near Eastern urban planning, similar to the Sasanian city of [Gur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firuzabad,_Fars) and older Mesopotamian cities like Mari.[^cite_note-ox-35][^cite_note-36][^cite_note-Bobrick_2012_67-37] While [Tell Chuera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tell_Chuera) and [Tell al-Rawda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Rawda_\(tell\)) also provide examples of this type of urban planning existing in Bronze Age [Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria_\(region\)).[^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39] This style of urban planning contrasted with Ancient [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece) and [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome) urban planning, in which cities are designed as squares or rectangles with streets intersecting each other at right angles.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWiet197113-40][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWiet197112-34][^cite_note-ox-35]

Baghdad was designed to symbolize [paradise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise) as described in the [Qur'an](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qur'an).[^cite_note-41] It took four years (764–768) to build, with over 100,000 workers involved.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWiet197113-40][^cite_note-Na-42] Al-Mansur recruited engineers and artisans worldwide. Astrologers Naubakht Ahvazi and Mashallah advised starting construction under Leo, associated with fire, productivity, and expansion. Bricks for the city were 18 inches square, and [Abu Hanifah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Hanifah) supervised their production. A canal supplied water for drinking and construction.[^cite_note-43] Marble was used extensively, including steps leading to the river.[^cite_note-44] The Golden Gate Palace, home of the caliph, stood at Baghdad's center with a grand 48-meter green dome. Only the caliph could approach its esplanade on horseback. Nearby were officer residences and a guardhouse. After Caliph [Al-Amin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Amin)'s death in 813, the palace ceased to be the caliph's residence. It was divided into three judicial districts: Round City (*Madinat al-Mansur*), al-Karkh (*al-Sharqiyya*), and Askar al-Mahdi. The city had four walls named after major destinations—[Kufa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kufa), [Basra](57580.md), [Khurasan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khurasan), and [Syria](7515849.md); [their gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gates_of_Baghdad) pointed in on these destinations.[^cite_note-45] The gates were 2.4 km apart, with massive iron doors requiring several men to operate and walls, up to 44m thick and 30m high, were reinforced with a second wall, towers, and a moat for added defense.[^cite_note-46]

### Center of learning (8th–9th centuries)

Further information: [Islamic Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age)



Courtyard of the [Abbasid palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Palace), built by caliph [al-Nasir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Nasir)

[^cite_note-Ceylan_2011_121-6]

Baghdad's strategic location along the Tigris and its abundant water supply contributed to its rapid growth. To prevent disturbances, Al-Mansur moved markets to al-Karkh. Over time, Baghdad became a hub for merchants and craftsmen. Officials called "Muhtasib" monitored trade to prevent fraud.[^cite_note-47][^cite_note-48] Baghdad surpassed [Ctesiphon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ctesiphon), the former Sassanid capital, located 30 km southeast. The ruins of Ctesiphon remain in [Salman Pak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salman_Pak), where [Salman the Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salman_the_Persian) is believed to be buried.[^cite_note-aljayyash1-49] Ctesiphon itself had replaced [Seleucia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucia), which had earlier succeeded [Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylon).[^cite_note-50][^cite_note-OP2011-51] According to the traveler [Ibn Battuta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Battuta), Baghdad was one of the largest cities,[^cite_note-52] not including the damage it has received. The residents are mostly [Hanbalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanbali).[^cite_note-KassamBlomfield-53] Most residents were Hanbali Muslims. The city housed [Abu Hanifa's grave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Hanifa_Mosque), marked by a mosque and cell.[^cite_note-Elders_of_Time_and_Neighbors_of_Nu'man-54] Its ruler, [Abu Sa'id Bahadur Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Sa'id_Bahadur_Khan), was a [Tatar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tatars) who had embraced Islam.[^cite_note-55]



Courtyard of [Mustansiriyya Madrasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mustansiriyya_Madrasa), established in 1227. Its building survived the [Mongol invasion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Baghdad_\(1258\)) of 1258. The modern [Mustansiriyah University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mustansiriya_University) was established in 1963.

Within a generation of its founding, Baghdad became a hub of [learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Learning) and [commerce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commerce). The city flourished into an unrivaled intellectual center of [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science), [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine), [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy), and [education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education), especially with the [Abbasid translation movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_translation_movement) began under the second caliph [Al-Mansur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mansur) and thrived under the seventh caliph [Al-Ma'mun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ma'mun).[^cite_note-Meri-56] *[Baytul-Hikmah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Wisdom)* or the "House of Wisdom" was among the most well known academies,[^cite_note-Kh-57] and had the largest selection of books in the world by the middle of the 9th century. Notable scholars based in Baghdad during this time include translator [Hunayn ibn Ishaq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunayn_ibn_Ishaq), mathematician [al-Khwarizmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_ibn_Musa_al-Khwarizmi), and philosopher [Al-Kindi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Kindi).[^cite_note-Kh-57] Baghdad was lively, with attractions like cabarets, chess halls, live plays, concerts, and acrobatics.[^cite_note-iranica-baghdad-iranian-58] Storytelling flourished, with professional storytellers (*[al-Qaskhun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Qaskhun)*) captivating crowds, inspiring the tales of *Arabian Nights*.[^cite_note-59]

Although Arabic was used as the language of science, the scholarship involved not only Arabs, but also [Persians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persians), [Syriacs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Christianity),[^cite_note-60] [Nestorians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_the_East), [Jews](25955086.md), [Arab Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibad),[^cite_note-61][^cite_note-62] and people from other ethnic and religious groups native to the region.[^cite_note-63][^cite_note-64][^cite_note-65][^cite_note-66] These are considered among the fundamental elements that contributed to the flourishing of scholarship in the Medieval Islamic world.[^cite_note-67][^cite_note-68][^cite_note-69] Baghdad was also a significant center of Islamic religious learning, with [Al-Jahiz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Jahiz) contributing to the formation of [Mu'tazili](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu%CA%BFtazila) theology, as well as [Al-Tabari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Tabari) culminating in the scholarship on the [Quranic exegesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tafsir).[^cite_note-Meri-56] Baghdad is likely to have been the [largest city in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_urban_community_sizes) from shortly after its foundation until the 930s, when it tied with [Córdoba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C%C3%B3rdoba,_Andalusia).[^cite_note-70] Several estimates suggest that the city contained over a million inhabitants at its peak.[^cite_note-71]

Many of the *[One Thousand and One Nights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Thousand_and_One_Nights)* tales, widely known as the *Arabian Nights*, are set in Baghdad during this period. It would surpass even Constantinople in prosperity and size.[^cite_note-72] Among the notable features of Baghdad during this period were its exceptional libraries. Many of the Abbasid caliphs were patrons of learning and enjoyed collecting both ancient and contemporary literature. Although some of the princes of the previous Umayyad dynasty had begun to gather and translate Greek scientific literature, the Abbasids were the first to foster Greek learning on a large scale. Many of these libraries were private collections intended only for the use of the owners and their immediate friends, but the libraries of the caliphs and other officials soon took on a public or a semi-public character.[^cite_note-mackensen-73]

Four great libraries were established in Baghdad during this period. The earliest was that of the famous [al-Ma'mun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ma'mun), who was caliph from 813 to 833. Another was established by [Sabur ibn Ardashir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabur_ibn_Ardashir) in 991 or 993 for the literary men and scholars who frequented his academy.[^cite_note-mackensen-73] This second library was plundered and burned by the Seljuks only seventy years after it was established. This was a good example of the sort of library built up out of the needs and interests of a literary society.[^cite_note-mackensen-73] The last two were examples of *madrasa* or theological college libraries. The [Nizamiyya of Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nizamiyya_of_Baghdad) was founded by the Persian [Nizam al-Mulk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nizam_al-Mulk), who was vizier of two early Seljuk sultans.[^cite_note-mackensen-73] It continued to operate even after the coming of the Mongols in 1258. The [Mustansiriyya Madrasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mustansiriyya_Madrasa), which owned an exceedingly rich library, was founded by [al-Mustansir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mustansir_\(Baghdad\)), the second last Abbasid caliph, who died in 1242.[^cite_note-mackensen-73] This would prove to be the last great library built by the caliphs of Baghdad.

### Stagnation and invasions (10th–16th centuries)



[Bab al-Wastani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_al-Wastani) (built in 1121 AD) is the only surviving historic [gate of Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gates_of_Baghdad).

By the 10th century, the city's population was between 1.2 million[^cite_note-Modelski-74] and 2 million.[^cite_note-75] Baghdad's early meteoric growth eventually slowed due to troubles within the [Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliphate), including relocations of the capital to [Samarra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Samarra) (during 808–819 and 836–892), the loss of the western and easternmost provinces, and periods of political domination by the [Iranian](14653.md) [Buwayhids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buwayhid) (945–1055) and [Seljuk Turks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seljuk_Turks) (1055–1135). The [Seljuks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seljuks) were a clan of the [Oghuz Turks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oghuz_Turks) from Central Asia that converted to the [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni) branch of Islam. In 1040, they destroyed the [Ghaznavids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghaznavids), taking over their land and in 1055, [Tughril Beg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tughril_Beg), the leader of the Seljuks, took over Baghdad. The Seljuks expelled the [Buyid dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buyid_dynasty) of Shiites that had ruled for some time and took over power and control of Baghdad. They ruled as [Sultans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultans) in the name of the Abbasid caliphs (they saw themselves as being part of the Abbasid regime). Tughril Beg saw himself as the protector of the Abbasid Caliphs.[^cite_note-76]



[Zumurrud Khatun Tomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zumurrud_Khatun_Mosque) (built in 1202 AD)

Baghdad was captured in [1394](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capture_of_Baghdad_\(1394\)), [1534](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capture_of_Baghdad_\(1534\)), [1623](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capture_of_Baghdad_\(1624\)) and [1638](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capture_of_Baghdad_\(1638\)). The city has been sieged in [812](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Baghdad_\(812%E2%80%93813\)), [865](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_civil_war_\(865%E2%80%93866\)), [946](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Baghdad_\(946\)), [1157](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Baghdad_\(1157\)), [1258](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Baghdad_\(1258\)) and in 1393 and 1401, by [Tamerlane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamerlane). In 1058, Baghdad was captured by the [Fatimids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatimids) under the Turkish general Abu'l-Ḥārith Arslān al-Basasiri, an adherent of the [Ismailis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ismailis) along with the 'Uqaylid Quraysh.[^cite_note-77] Not long before the arrival of the Saljuqs in Baghdad, al-Basasiri petitioned to the Fatimid Imam-Caliph [al-Mustansir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mustansir_Billah) to support him in conquering Baghdad on the Ismaili Imam's behalf. It has recently come to light that the famed Fatimid *[da'i](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Da'i)*, al-Mu'ayyad al-Shirazi, had a direct role in supporting al-Basasiri and helped the general to succeed in taking [Mawṣil](200475.md), [Wāsit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wasit,_Iraq) and [Kufa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kufa). Soon after,[^cite_note-Daftary,_Farhad_1990-78] by December 1058, a Shi'i *[adhān](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adh%C4%81n)* (call to prayer) was implemented in Baghdad and a *[khutbah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khutbah)* (sermon) was delivered in the name of the Fatimid Imam-Caliph.[^cite_note-Daftary,_Farhad_1990-78] Despite his Shi'i inclinations, Al-Basasiri received support from Sunnis and Shi'is alike, for whom opposition to the Saljuq power was a common factor.[^cite_note-79]



[Conquest of Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Baghdad_\(1258\)) by the Mongols in 1258 CE

On 10 February 1258, Baghdad was captured by the [Mongols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongols) led by [Hulegu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hulegu), a grandson of [Genghis Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genghis_Khan) (*Chingiz Khan*), during the [siege of Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Baghdad_\(1258\)).[^cite_note-80] Many quarters were ruined by fire, siege, or looting. The Mongols massacred most of the city's inhabitants, including the caliph [Al-Musta'sim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Musta'sim), and destroyed large sections of the city. The [canals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canal) and [dykes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levee) forming the city's [irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrigation) system were also destroyed. During this time, in Baghdad, Christians and Shia were tolerated, while Sunnis were treated as enemies.[^cite_note-81] The sack of Baghdad put an end to the Abbasid Caliphate.[^cite_note-82] It has been argued that this marked an end to the Islamic Golden Age and served a blow from which Islamic civilization never fully recovered.[^cite_note-83]

At this point, Baghdad was ruled by the [Ilkhanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilkhanate), a breakaway state of the Mongol Empire, ruling from Iran. In August 1393, Baghdad was occupied by the Central Asian Turkic conqueror [Timur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timur) ("Tamerlane"),[^cite_note-84] by marching there in only eight days from [Shiraz](39622.md). Sultan [Ahmad Jalayir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmad_Jalayir) fled to Syria, where the Mamluk Sultan [Barquq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barquq) protected him and killed Timur's envoys. Timur left the [Sarbadar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarbadar) prince Khwaja Mas'ud to govern Baghdad, but he [was driven out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capture_of_Baghdad_\(1394\)) when [Ahmad Jalayir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmad_Jalayir) returned.



[Khan Murjan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khan_Murjan), built in the 14th century as a [caravanserai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caravanserai)

In 1401, Baghdad was again sacked, by [Timur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timur), a [Central Asian](6742.md) Turko-Mongol figure.[^cite_note-85] When his forces took Baghdad, he spared almost no one, and ordered that each of his soldiers bring back two severed human heads.[^cite_note-86] Baghdad became a provincial capital controlled by the Mongol [Jalayirid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jalayirid) (1400–1411), Turkic [Kara Koyunlu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kara_Koyunlu) (1411–1469), Turkic [Ak Koyunlu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Sheep_Turkmen) (1469–1508), and the Iranian [Safavid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid) (1508–1534) dynasties.

### Ottoman and Mamluks (16th–19th centuries)

See also: [Baghdad Eyalet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad_Eyalet), [Baghdad Vilayet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad_Vilayet), and [History of Baghdad (1831–1917)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Baghdad_\(1831%E2%80%931917\))

The Safavids took control of the city in 1509 under the leadership of Shah Ismail I. It remained under Safavid rule until the Ottomans seized it in 1535, but the Safavids regained control in 1624. A massacre occurred when the Shah's army entered the city. It remained under Safavid rule until 1639 when Sultan Murad IV recaptured it in 1638.

In 1534, Baghdad was [captured](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman%E2%80%93Safavid_War_\(1532%E2%80%9355\)) by the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md),[^cite_note-87] becoming the administrative capital of [Ottoman Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Iraq).[^cite_note-Ceylan_2009_61-5] Under the [Ottomans](22278.md), Baghdad continued into a [period of decline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Baghdad_\(1831%E2%80%931917\)), partially as a result of the enmity between its rulers and [Iranian](14653.md) [Safavids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavids), which did not accept the [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni) control of the city. [Between 1623 and 1638](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman%E2%80%93Safavid_War_\(1623%E2%80%931639\)), it returned to Iranian rule before falling back into Ottoman hands.[^cite_note-88] Baghdad has suffered severely from visitations of the [plague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plague_\(disease\)) and [cholera](7591.md),[^cite_note-89] and sometimes two-thirds of its population has been wiped out.[^cite_note-90] The city became part of an [eyalet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad_Eyalet) and then a [vilayet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad_vilayet).[^cite_note-91]

For a time, Baghdad had been the largest city in the Middle East.[^cite_note-Cetinsaya-1890-92] The city saw relative revival in the latter part of the 18th century, under [Mamluk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamluk_dynasty_of_Iraq) government.[^cite_note-Cetinsaya-1890-92] Direct Ottoman rule was reimposed by [Ali Rıza Pasha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_R%C4%B1za_Pasha_\(governor_of_Baghdad\)) in 1831.[^cite_note-Cetinsaya-1890-92] From 1851 to 1852 and from 1861 to 1867, Baghdad was governed, under the Ottoman Empire by [Mehmed Namık Pasha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehmed_Nam%C4%B1k_Pasha).[^cite_note-Cetinsaya-1890-92] The [Nuttall Encyclopedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuttall_Encyclopedia) reports the 1907 population of Baghdad as 185,000.[^cite_note-Cetinsaya-1890-92]

The city's municipality was established in 1868, and Ibrahim al-Daftari was appointed its first mayor.[^cite_note-neelwafurat-2025-93] The year 1869 is of great importance in the history of Baghdad in the Ottoman era, as it was the beginning of what can be considered a distinct era of the Ottoman eras, the foundations of which were laid by Governor [Midhat Pasha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midhat_Pasha), who implemented a number of reform systems and laws that the state legislated during the era of reforms and reconstruction, which was called the Tanzimat era.[^cite_note-neelwafurat-2025-93] The overall importance of Baghdad to the Ottomans was that they made the headquarters of the Sixth Corps of the Ottoman Army in the city.[^cite_note-neelwafurat-2025-93]

By the 19th century, Baghdad emerged as a leading center for Jewish learning.[^cite_note-issuu-1846-94] The city had a Jewish population of over 6,000 and had numerous yeshivas.[^cite_note-issuu-1846-94] The Jewish population has grown so rapidly that by 1884, there were 30,000 Jews in Baghdad and by 1900, about 50,000, comprising over a quarter of the city's total population.[^cite_note-issuu-1846-94] Large-scale Jewish immigration from Kurdistan to Baghdad continued throughout this period.[^cite_note-issuu-1846-94] By the mid-19th century, the religious infrastructure of Baghdad grew to include a large yeshiva which trained up to sixty rabbis at time.[^cite_note-issuu-1846-94] Religious scholarship flourished in Baghdad, which produced great rabbis, such as Joseph Hayyim ben Eliahu Mazal-Tov, known as the [Ben Ish Chai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Ish_Chai) (1834–1909) or Rabbi [Abdallah Somekh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdallah_Somekh) (1813–1889). During this time, Baghdadi Jews established a successful trade diaspora in China, India and Singapore.

### Modern era

See also: [Mandate for Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandate_for_Mesopotamia), [Kingdom of Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Iraq), [Mandatory Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandatory_Iraq), [Iraqi Republic (1958-1968)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_Republic_\(1958-1968\)), and [Ba'athist Iraq](26215470.md)



[Al-Rashid Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Rashid_Street_\(Iraq\)) in 1932

Baghdad and southern Iraq remained under Ottoman rule until 1918, when they were captured by the British during World War I.[^cite_note-Jackson-2016-95] In 1920, a revolt erupted in Baghdad against new British policies.[^cite_note-atiyyah-96] It began in summer with mass demonstrations by Iraqis, including protests by embittered officers from the old [Ottoman Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Army_\(1861%E2%80%931922\)).[^cite_note-97] The revolt gained momentum and spread to the middle and lower Euphrates.[^cite_note-98] The British authorities retaliated by air bombing across Baghdad, which killed thousands of residents.[^cite_note-BBC_News-2014-99] In 1921, under the Mandate of Mesopotamia, Baghdad became the capital of the British-protected monarchy. Baghdad was made capital of the independent kingdom of Iraq in 1932.

Several architectural and planning projects were commissioned to reinforce this administration.[^cite_note-100] During this period, the substantial Jewish community (probably exceeding 100,000 people) comprised between a quarter and a third of the city's population. The [National Museum of Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Iraq) and the [University of Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Baghdad) were built by King Faisal, who laid foundation for the modern Iraqi state. The city's population grew from an estimated 145,000 in 1900 to 580,000 in 1950.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-101] A development plan came in 1957, visioned by [Frank Lloyd Wright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Lloyd_Wright).[^cite_note-ReferenceA-101] The plan proposed to build a cultural hub on an island on the river, with an opera house, museums, a university, shopping malls, and a 300-foot statue of the fifth Abbasid caliph Harun al-Rashid.

On 1 April 1941, members of the "[Golden Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Square_\(Iraq\))" led by former minister [Rashid Ali al-Gaylani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rashid_Ali_al-Gaylani) staged [a coup in Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1941_Iraqi_coup_d'%C3%A9tat) and installed a pro-[German](21212.md) and pro-[Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Italy) government to replace the pro-British government of [Regent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regent) [Abd al-Ilah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_al-Ilah).[^cite_note-102][^cite_note-Warfare_History_Network-2024-103] The British forces intervened in the resulting [Anglo-Iraqi War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Iraqi_War).[^cite_note-Churchill,_p._234-104] Fearing the advancement, Gaylani and his government had fled, and the mayor of Baghdad surrendered to the British and Commonwealth forces.[^cite_note-Warfare_History_Network-2024-103][^cite_note-Lyman,_p._862-105]

On 1–2 June 1941, during the ensuing [power vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_vacuum), Jewish residents of Baghdad were attacked in the [Farhud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farhud), a pogrom that followed the collapse of Rashid Ali al-Gaylani's pro-Axis government.[^cite_note-106][^cite_note-107][^cite_note-108] Iraqi soldiers and policemen, including supporters of Rashid Ali's coup, participated in the violence, as did civilians and members of nationalist youth movements.[^cite_note-109][^cite_note-110] Historians have also placed the violence in the context of the spread of antisemitism and Nazi propaganda in Iraq during the late 1930s and early 1940s.[^cite_note-111][^cite_note-112] Estimates of the casualties vary. Scholarly accounts commonly place the number of Jews killed at around 180.[^cite_note-113][^cite_note-114] The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum gives an estimate of 150–180 Jews killed, approximately 600 injured, an undetermined number of women raped, and approximately 1,500 Jewish-owned homes and stores looted; it notes that the Iraqi government's investigating commission reported 128 Jews killed and 210 injured.[^cite_note-115][^cite_note-116]

The violence ended on 2 June after Iraqi troops entered Baghdad and killed several hundred rioters while restoring order.[^cite_note-117] Between 1950 and 1951, Jewish targets in Baghdad were attacked in a [series of bombings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1950%E2%80%931951_Baghdad_bombings) whose responsibility remains disputed. [Avi Shlaim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avi_Shlaim) has argued that members of the Zionist underground in Baghdad were responsible for three of the five bombings.[^cite_note-118] Historian Moshe Gat, however, concluded from archival evidence that Israeli emissaries did not carry out the bombings attributed to them by the Iraqi authorities, while [Esther Meir-Glitzenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esther_Meir-Glitzenstein) has argued that archival scholarship does not support the claim that the attacks were an Israeli operation intended to produce mass emigration.[^cite_note-119][^cite_note-120]

On 14 July 1958, a significant portion of the [Iraqi Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_Army) under [Abdul-Karim Qasim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdul-Karim_Qasim), [staged a coup to topple the Kingdom of Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/14_July_Revolution).[^cite_note-bataween-2022-121] The army seized control over Baghdad and stormed the radio station and the [Al-Rehab Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Rehab_Palace).[^cite_note-122][^cite_note-\:3-123] Many people were brutally killed during the coup, including King Faisal II, former Regent [Abd al-Ilah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_al-Ilah) and former Prime Minister [Nuri al-Said](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuri_al-Said) and members of the royal family.[^cite_note-\:3-123] Many of the royal figures' bodies were dragged through the streets and mutilated. Mob violence emerged and several foreign nationals staying at the [Baghdad Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad_Hotel), including [Americans](19792942.md) and [Jordanians](15717.md) were killed.[^cite_note-\:3-123] New principles were adopted for the city's development. New Baghdad and Sadr City were developed during the reign of Qasim. In 1960, Baghdad hosted an international conference with dignitaries from [Iran](14653.md), [Venezuela](32374.md) and [Saudi Arabia](349303.md), that founded [Organization of Petroleum Exporting Countries](166346.md) (OPEC).[^cite_note-124]



Baghdad in 1982

During the 1970s, Baghdad experienced a period of prosperity and growth because of a sharp increase in the [price of petroleum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price_of_petroleum), Iraq's main export.[^cite_note-Stanek-2020-125] New [infrastructure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrastructure) including oil pipelines, modern sewerage, and highways were built.[^cite_note-\:4-126] Master plans of the city (1967 and 1973) were delivered by the Polish planning office Miastoprojekt-Kraków, mediated by Polservice.[^cite_note-\:4-126] [Saddam Hussein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saddam_Hussein) sponsored architectural and artwork events, that attracted world's popular architects.[^cite_note-127] The city had a vibrant lifestyle.[^cite_note-Iddon-128] Baghdad was called as "[Nuremberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuremberg) of 1930s" and "[Las Vegas](47737.md) of the 1980s".[^cite_note-Iddon-128]

However, the [Iran–Iraq War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Iraq_War) of the 1980s was a difficult time for the city, as money was diverted to the military and thousands of residents endured devastations.[^cite_note-wilsoncenter-2024-129] Iran launched a number of missiles and rockets on Baghdad, some of them hitting dangerously close to [Al-Rashid Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Rashid_Street_\(Iraq\)) and the [Jewish Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bataween).[^cite_note-\:5-130][^cite_note-131][^cite_note-132] Power plants and oil refineries were damaged.[^cite_note-\:5-130] A nuclear reactor near Baghdad was destroyed in an airstrike by Israel.[^cite_note-133] Despite the war, preparations were underway for Baghdad to host a Non-Alignment Movement summit.[^cite_note-\:4-126] Conference centers and hotels such as [Palestine Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestine_Hotel), [Al-Mansour Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mansour_Hotel) and [Ishtar Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ishtar_Hotel) were built.[^cite_note-\:4-126] However, the summit was later shifted to New Delhi, due to deteriorating security.[^cite_note-UPI-2025-134][^cite_note-chaos1-135]

During the [Gulf War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_War), Baghdad was the most heavily defended area of Iraq.[^cite_note-136][^cite_note-137] Initially, U.S. airstrikes on Baghdad failed and resulted a tactical victory for the Iraqi Air Force.[^cite_note-138][^cite_note-139] Later, the [Coalition forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coalition_of_the_Gulf_War) proceeded with aerial bombings.[^cite_note-140] Air defense, communication systems, bridges, chemical weapon facilities, and artilleries were damaged.[^cite_note-\:6-141] Oil refineries and airport were targeted.[^cite_note-\:6-141] On 13 February 1991, an [aerial bombing attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amiriyah_shelter_bombing) in [Amiriya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amiriya) killed at least 408 civilians.[^cite_note-142][^cite_note-143] Shortly after the end of the war, ethnic Kurds and Shi'a Muslims led uprisings against the government.[^cite_note-chaos1-135] Clashes took place Shi'a rebels and the Republican Guard led by Qusay Hussein.[^cite_note-bar1-144] Sadr City was besieged until the order restored.[^cite_note-bar1-144] Another [uprising occurred in 1999](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1999_Shia_uprising_in_Iraq), after Ayatollah [Muhammad Sadiq al-Sadr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_al-Sadr_\(jurist\)) was assassinated in [Najaf](200512.md).[^cite_note-DavidBlair-145] Unrest began as large scale protests took place in Shia neighborhoods of Baghdad, specially [Saddam City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saddam_City).[^cite_note-DavidBlair-145] The Republican Guard deployed in the district suppressed the demonstration, leaving between 27 and 100 dead.[^cite_note-DavidBlair-145]

Baghdad was targeted in frequent U.S. airstrikes.[^cite_note-weiner-146] On 26 June 1993, cruise missiles were launched into downtown Baghdad, targeting the intelligence headquarters in the [Mansour district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mansour_district).[^cite_note-\:02-147] The attack killed nine civilians nearby, including actress and painter [Layla Al-Attar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Layla_Al-Attar).[^cite_note-148] During the 1998 bombing of Iraq, missiles struck multiple locations across Baghdad, including presidential palaces, several Republican Guard barrakcs, and offices of the Ministry of Defense and the Military industry. On February 16, 2001, the U.S. launched air strikes on five military targets at Taji.[^cite_note-149][^cite_note-150]

### 21st century

With decades of economic sanctions, the city experienced stangnation.[^cite_note-151] By the end of the 1990s, the government's efforts resulted in the reconstruction of Baghdad.[^cite_note-152] Damaged government offices, presidential palaces, bridges and road were restored.[^cite_note-\:7-153] The airport was reopened with flights from Lebanon, Syria and Jordan.[^cite_note-\:7-153] Numerous mosques and Islamic institutions were built under the Faith Campaign. In 2001, a broad initiative was launched to revive Baghdad's cultural heritage.[^cite_note-154] Older mosques, churches, synagogues, mandis were rebuilt. However, these efforts were interrupted by the war.[^cite_note-Al-Jazeera-155][^cite_note-156][^cite_note-157]



[Central Bank of Iraq Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Bank_of_Iraq_Tower) (under construction), designed by the Iraqi-British architect [Zaha Hadid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaha_Hadid)

Between 2003 and 2020, the city was disturbed by series of violence and terrorist activities.[^cite_note-Tunzelmann-2021-158] In 2003, the United States-led coalition invaded Iraq.[^cite_note-Tunzelmann-2021-158] On 9 April, the U.S forces captured Baghdad.[^cite_note-Tunzelmann-2021-158] A statue of Saddam Hussein was toppled at the Firdous Square, symbolizing the end of his rule.[^cite_note-159] Shortly after the overthrow of the regime, an insurgency began against the U.S-led rule in Iraq.[^cite_note-Karon-2005-160] Also two minor riots took place, causing some distrubance in the population.[^cite_note-161][^cite_note-Vick-2023-162] Religious and ethnic minorities such as Kurds, Assyrians, Mandaeans and Jews were subjected to kidnappings, death threats and violence.[^cite_note-ISW-2025-163] On the eve of Ashura on 2 March 2004, one of the deadliest bombings took place in Baghdad, where at least 80–100 people were killed and 200 were injured.[^cite_note-164][^cite_note-165] Over 965 people were killed in a bridge disaster on [Al-Aimmah Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Aimmah_Bridge). Violence escalated into a civil war consisting of Shia and Sunni insurgents and coalition forces. Shi'a militias were [Muqtada as-Sadr](351058.md)'s [Jaysh al-Mahdi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahdi_Army) and the [Iranian-backed Special Groups](36658251.md) and among Sunni insurgents, the largest was [Al-Qaeda in Iraq (AQI)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Qaeda_in_Iraq).[^cite_note-ISW-2025-163]

Mansour district, before 2003, was home to wealthy Sunnis. It quickly became stronghold for the Sunni insurgency. While Shia militias were based in [Sadr City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sadr_City), [Kadhimiyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kadhimiya), and West Rasheed, with [Bab Al-Sharqi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_Al-Sharqi) becoming stronghold for the Mahdi Army.[^cite_note-ISW-2025-163] Later, they also expanded into the surrounding districts of eastern Baghdad. 9 Nissan, Karadah, and [Rusafa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Rusafa,_Iraq) were dominated by Shias.[^cite_note-ISW-2025-163] In 2007, coalition forces and Iraqi Army successfully defeated the insurgents.[^cite_note-ISW-2025-163] By 2009, the level of violence decreased.[^cite_note-ISW-2025-163] The period surrounding Provincial Elections was remarkably peaceful.[^cite_note-ISW-2025-163] But Baghdad witnessed an uptick in attacks in early April 2009, when a series of suicide bomb and vehicle-borne improvised explosive device attacks were perpetrated. [^cite_note-ISW-2025-163] The war caused [huge damage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damage_to_Baghdad_during_the_Iraq_War) to Baghdad's transportation, power, and sanitary infrastructure.[^cite_note-Tunzelmann-2021-158] Though the war ended in 2011, instability continued. Baghdad was rocked by the Arab Spring protests. An insurgency lasted till 2013. It was followed by another war from 2013 to 2017, which included [suicide bombings](18835454.md) in [January 2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Baghdad_bombings) and [January 2021](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Baghdad_bombings).[^cite_note-166] The city attracted global media attention on 3 January 2020, when Iranian general [Qasem Soleimani](16805217.md) was [assassinated in a U.S. drone strike](62726668.md) near Baghdad Airport.[^cite_note-167]

Most [reconstruction efforts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconstruction_of_Iraq) have been devoted to the restoration and repair of badly damaged urban infrastructure. More visible efforts at reconstruction through private development have also been made. In late 2009, a construction plan was proposed to rebuild the heart of Baghdad, but the plan was never realized because corruption was involved in it. Various projects have commenced in Baghdad to induce stability. A large number of historical sites has been restored. Modern-scale development projects such as shopping malls, hotels and office spaces are now in Baghdad. The area surrounding the airport and presidential complex has emerged as new investment hospot and expansion of Baghdad. In December 2015, Baghdad was selected by [UNESCO](21786641.md) as the first Arab city of the center of literary creativity.[^cite_note-168]

## Geography



Al-Zawraa Park in Baghdad



Baghdad as seen from the [International Space Station](15043.md)

The city is located on a vast plain bisected by the [Tigris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigris) river. The Tigris splits Baghdad in half, with the eastern half being called "[Risafa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Rusafa,_Iraq)" and the Western half known as "[Karkh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karkh)". The land on which the city is built is almost entirely flat and low-lying, being of [quaternary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternary) [alluvial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alluvium) origin due to periodic large flooding of the Tigris river. The [Diyala river](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diyala_river) is a tributary of the Tigris, flowing southeast of the city and bordering its eastern suburbs.

Baghdad is 529.8 kilometres (329.2 mi) northwest of [Basra](57580.md), 402.9 kilometres (250.4 mi) south of [Mosul](200475.md), 366.8 kilometres (227.9 mi) south of [Erbil](24534207.md) and 103.8 kilometres (64.5 mi) northeast of [Karbala](210957.md).[^cite_note-169] Located to the south is [Mahmoudiyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahmoudiyah,_Iraq), which serves as the gateway to Baghdad.

### Climate

Baghdad has a [hot desert climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hot_desert_climate) ([Köppen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification) *BWh*), featuring extremely hot, prolonged, dry summers and mild to cool, slightly wet, short winters. In the summer, from June through August, the average maximum temperature is as high as 44 °C (111 °F) and accompanied by sunshine. Rainfall has been recorded on fewer than half a dozen occasions at this time of year and has never exceeded 1 mm (0.04 in).[^cite_note-Geert-170] Even at night, temperatures in summer are seldom below 24 °C (75 °F). Baghdad's record highest temperature of 51.8 °C (125.2 °F) was reached on 28 July 2020.[^cite_note-171][^cite_note-record-172] [Humidity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humidity) is under 50% in summer, due to Baghdad's distance from both the marshes in southern Iraq and the coasts of the [Persian Gulf](24761.md). [Dust storms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_storm) from the deserts to the west are a normal occurrence during the summer.

Its winter temperatures are those of a [hot desert climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hot_desert_climate). From December through February, Baghdad has maximum temperatures averaging 16 to 19 °C (61 to 66 °F), with highs possible above 21 °C (70 °F). Lows below freezing occur [statistically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical) a couple of times per year.[^cite_note-173]

Annual rainfall, almost entirely confined to the period from November through March, averages approximately 150 mm (5.91 in), but has been as high as 338 mm (13.31 in) and as low as 37 mm (1.46 in).[^cite_note-Geert-170] On 11 January 2008, light snow fell across Baghdad for the first time in 100 years.[^cite_note-174] Snowfall was again reported on 11 February 2020, with accumulations across the city.[^cite_note-175]

## Governance

See also: [List of neighborhoods and districts in Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_neighborhoods_and_districts_in_Baghdad)



Government office in the [Green Zone](856542.md), government precinct&#x20;

Administratively, [Baghdad Governorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad_Governorate) is divided into [districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaza) which are further divided into [sub-districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nahiyah).[^cite_note-USA_Today-2007-180] Municipally, the governorate is divided into 9 municipalities, which have responsibility for local issues.[^cite_note-USA_Today-2007-180] Regional services, however, are coordinated and carried out by a [mayor](101146.md) who oversees the municipalities.[^cite_note-USA_Today-2007-180] The governorate council is responsible for the governorate-wide policy.[^cite_note-USA_Today-2007-180] These official subdivisions of the city served as administrative centers for the delivery of municipal services but until 2003 had no political function.[^cite_note-USA_Today-2007-180] Beginning in April 2003, the U.S—controlled [Coalition Provisional Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coalition_Provisional_Authority) (CPA) began the process of creating new functions for these.[^cite_note-USA_Today-2007-180] The process initially focused on the election of neighborhood councils in official neighborhoods, elected by neighborhood caucuses.[^cite_note-USA_Today-2007-180] The CPA convened a series of meetings in each neighborhood to explain local government, to describe the caucus election process and to encourage participants to spread the word and bring friends, relatives and neighbors to subsequent meetings.[^cite_note-USA_Today-2007-180]

Each neighborhood process ultimately ended with a final meeting where candidates for the new neighborhood councils identified themselves and asked their neighbors to vote for them.[^cite_note-USA_Today-2007-180] Once all 88 neighborhood councils were in place, each neighborhood council elected representatives from among their members to serve on one of the city's nine district councils.[^cite_note-USA_Today-2007-180] The number of neighborhood representatives on a district council is based upon the neighborhood's population.[^cite_note-USA_Today-2007-180] The next step was to have each of the nine district councils elect representatives from their membership to serve on the 37 member Baghdad City Council.[^cite_note-USA_Today-2007-180] Later, the number of official neighborhoods were increased to 89.[^cite_note-USA_Today-2007-180] This three tier system of local government connected the people of Baghdad to the central government through their representatives from the neighborhood, through the district, and up to the city council.[^cite_note-USA_Today-2007-180] The same process was used to provide representative councils for the other communities in Baghdad Province outside of the city itself.[^cite_note-USA_Today-2007-180] There, local councils were elected from 20 neighborhoods (*Nahia*) and these councils elected representatives from their members to serve on six district councils (*Qada*).[^cite_note-USA_Today-2007-180]

As within the city, the district councils then elected representatives from among their members to serve on the 35 member Baghdad Regional Council. The first step in the establishment of the system of local government for Baghdad Province was the election of the Baghdad Provincial Council. As before, the representatives to the Provincial Council were elected by their peers from the lower councils in numbers proportional to the population of the districts they represent. The 41 member Provincial Council took office in February 2004 and served until national elections held in January 2005, when a new Provincial Council was elected. This system of 127 separate councils may seem overly cumbersome; however, Baghdad Province is home to approximately seven million people. ## Demographics

Baghdad's population was estimated at 7.22 million in 2015. The surrounding metropolitan region's population is estimated to be 10,500,000.[^cite_note-Baghdad2-7] It is second largest city in the Arab world after [Cairo](6293.md) and [fourth largest metropolitan area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_metropolitan_areas_in_the_Middle_East) in the Middle East after [Istanbul](3391396.md).[^cite_note-Baghdad2-7] At the beginning of the 21st century, some 1.5 million people migrated to Baghdad.[^cite_note-Baghdad2-7] The [2013–2017 civil war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_in_Iraq_\(2013%E2%80%932017\)) following the [Islamic State's invasion in 2014](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Iraq_offensive_\(June_2014\)) caused hundreds of thousands of Iraqi internally displaced people to flee to the city.[^cite_note-Baghdad2-7]

### Ethnicity



[Assyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrians) praying at [Sayidat al-Nejat Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayidat_al-Nejat_Cathedral) in Baghdad

The vast majority of Baghdad's ethnic population are Iraqi [Arabs](2185.md), while minority groups include Kurds, [Feyli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feyli_\(tribe\)), Kurdish, [Turkmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_Turkmen), [Assyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_people), [Kawliya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kawliya), [Circassians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circassians), [Mandaeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandaeans), and [Armenians](387816.md).[^cite_note-Baghdad2-7][^cite_note-189][^cite_note-190] Post-2003 have left an impact of Baghdad's ethnic composition.\[^cite_note-\:0-191] In 2003, approximately 500,000 Kurds lived in Baghdad.\[^cite_note-\:0-191] As of 2016, about 300,000 remained in Baghdad.\[^cite_note-\:0-191] Among them, about 150,000 are Shi'a mostly of [Luri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luri_language) origin.[^cite_note-192] The main Kurdish neighborhood is situated in central Baghdad, known as the Quarter of Kurds (*Akd al–Akrad*).[^cite_note-193] It is home to more than 200 Kurdish families that have lived there for generations.\[^cite_note-[www.rudaw.net-2025-194](http://www.rudaw.net-2025-194/)][^cite_note-195]

Christians in Baghdad are predominantly ethnic Assyrians and Armenians.[^cite_note-\:1-196] Assyrians began moving to Baghdad by the mid 20th century from northern Iraq and Iran.[^cite_note-\:1-196][^cite_note-197][^cite_note-\:2-198] The historic "Assyrian Quarter" of the city – [Dora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dora,_Baghdad), which boasted a population of 150,000 [Assyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_people) in 2003, made up over 3% of the capital's population then.[^cite_note-Spencer-2014-199] The community has been subject to [kidnappings](169376.md), [death threats](13262036.md), vandalism, and [house burnings](184695.md) by [al-Qaeda](1921.md) and other [insurgent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insurgent) groups.[^cite_note-Spencer-2014-199] As of the end of 2014, only 1,500 Assyrians remained in Dora and others in Karrada district.[^cite_note-Spencer-2014-199] Before the war, 25,000 Armenians lived in Iraq, with majority them concentrated in Baghdad.[^cite_note-\:2-198] Although the Armenian population has reduced, Baghdad is still home to the largest community of [Armenians in Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenians_in_Iraq), primarily concentrated in the Armenian quarter of [Bab al-Sharqi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bab_Al-Sharqi) area.[^cite_note-200]

An estimated 60,000 [Iraqi Turkmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_Turkmen) live in Baghdad, with significant population in the neighborhoods of [Adhamiyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adhamiyah) and [Ragheba Khatun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raghiba_Khatoun).[^cite_note-201][^cite_note-202][^cite_note-203] There is a Circassian neighborhood in the city, which is home to the largest [Circassian community of Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circassians_in_Iraq).[^cite_note-ORSAM-204][^cite_note-205] The metropolitan area and the adjoining governorate is also home to [Kawliya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kawliya), [African Iraqis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afro-Iraqis), [Chechens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chechens) and other groups.[^cite_note-206][^cite_note-207][^cite_note-208]

### Religion



[Al-Khulafa Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Khulafa_Mosque), Baghdad's oldest surviving mosque by foundation date (est. 908), next to the [Latin Cathedral of St. Joseph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Cathedral_of_St._Joseph) (est. 1871) (pictured in 1964)

The majority of the citizens are [Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_Iraq) with minorities of [Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_Iraq), [Yezidis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yazidism), [Jews](25955086.md) and [Mandeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandaeism) also present.[^cite_note-Al-Jawadain20092-209] There are many religious centers distributed around the city including mosques, churches, synagogues and Mashkhannas cultic huts.[^cite_note-Al-Jawadain20092-209] The city historically had a predominantly [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni) population, but by the early 21st century about 52% of the city's population were [Shi'ites](22350732.md).[^cite_note-Baghdad2-7] Sunni Muslims make up 29–34% of Iraq's population and are still a majority in west and north Iraq.[^cite_note-Kamal-2016-210] As early as 2003, about 20% of the population of the city was the result of mixed marriages between Shi'ites and Sunnis.[^cite_note-Kamal-2016-210][^cite_note-Baghdad2-7] Following the [civil war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_civil_war_\(2006%E2%80%932008\)) between Sunni and Shia militia groups during the [occupation of Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupation_of_Iraq_\(2003%E2%80%932011\)), the population of Sunnis significantly decreased as they were pushed out of many neighborhoods.[^cite_note-Baghdad2-7] A majority of the neighborhoods have become either entirely Sunni or Shi'ite, while a few localities are mixed, such as [Yarmouk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yarmouk,_Baghdad).

The [Christian community in Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_Iraq) is divided among mainly the [Chaldean Catholic Church](698821.md) and the [Syriac Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Catholic_Church).[^cite_note-Blackwell_Publishing_Ltd-2017-211] There is also a significant presence of the [Assyrian Church of the East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrian_Church_of_the_East) and the [Syriac Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Orthodox_Church), along with the largest [Armenian Apostolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_Apostolic_Church) and Protestant communities in Iraq, which is also located in Baghdad.[^cite_note-212] The city is the headquarters of the [Chaldean Catholic Church](698821.md), with its see located in the [Cathedral of Our Lady of Sorrows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_of_Our_Lady_of_Sorrows),[^cite_note-213] while the [Ancient Church of the East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Church_of_the_East) has its see in the Cathedral of the Virgin.[^cite_note-Blackwell_Publishing_Ltd-2017-211] Before the [Iraq War](5043324.md) in 2003, Baghdad was home to 300,000–800,000 Christians,[^cite_note-214][^cite_note-Blackwell_Publishing_Ltd-2017-211] primarily concentrated in several neighborhoods with a Christian majority or significant minority, such as [Karrada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karrada) and [al–Dora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dora,_Baghdad), which had about 150,000 Christians.[^cite_note-215] After 2003, a large number of Christians were displaced, and many of them fled to Baghdad after ISIS' takeover of Mosul. About 100,000 Christians remain in Baghdad, primarily in Karrada and [Mansour district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mansour_district).[^cite_note-216][^cite_note-217]

Baghdad was historically home to one of the world's most significant Jewish communities. By the 1920s, close to 140,000 Jews lived in Iraq, most of them in Baghdad, where they made up about one-third of the city's population.[^cite_note-218] The community declined sharply in the mid-20th century. Historian Moshe Gat identifies anti-Jewish policies and discrimination by the Iraqi government, along with Zionist activity and the establishment of Israel, among the factors contributing to the mass departure; more than 120,000 Iraqi Jews left for Israel in 1950 and 1951.[^cite_note-219]

The remaining Jewish community continued to face restrictions and persecution. A 1969 U.S. State Department assessment reported restrictions on Jews' property rights and freedom of movement and increased harassment; nine Iraqi Jews were among fourteen people executed as alleged spies in January 1969.[^cite_note-220] By April 2003, only 33 Jews remained in the Baghdad area, declining to 22 by the end of 2004.[^cite_note-221] In its 2023 reporting, the U.S. State Department stated that only a very small number of Jewish citizens lived in Baghdad and that media organizations reported only a handful of Jews remaining in federal Iraq.[^cite_note-222] The city was historically home to over [60 synagogues, cemeteries, and shrines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Jewish_sites_in_Iraq), many of which were preserved before 2003.[^cite_note-Amwaj-2024-223] However, their condition deteriorated after the war, and only a few sites, such as the [Meir Taweig Synagogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meir_Taweig_Synagogue) and [Al-Habibiyah Jewish Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Habibiyah_Jewish_Cemetery), remain.[^cite_note-Amwaj-2024-223][^cite_note-224]

Beyond their traditional homelands, around [Amarah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amarah) and [Basra](57580.md), Mandaeans are also found in Baghdad.[^cite_note-Baghdad_Governorate-225] By the late 20th century, Mandaeans began settling in Baghdad for better opportunities.[^cite_note-Baghdad_Governorate-225] Most of them live primarily around al-Qadisiyah and Dora, which is near to their places of worship and cultural centers.[^cite_note-Baghdad_Governorate-225] However, persecution of Mandaeans greatly decreased since 2003.[^cite_note-Baghdad_Governorate-225] There is also a small of community of [Baháʼís](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bah%C3%A1%CA%BC%C3%AD_Faith) and [Sikhs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhism_in_Iraq), who live in Baghdad.[^cite_note-BWNS-2013-226] The Sikhs are mostly Indians.[^cite_note-Baghdad2-7] Before 2003, Baghdad was regularly visited by Sikh pilgrims from India.[^cite_note-Baghdad2-7]

## Economy



View of Central Business district

Baghdad serves as the commercial and financial hub, home to 22% of the population, and generates 40% of Iraq's GDP.[^cite_note-sjsu-2024-227] It connects trade routes between Turkey, Syria, India, and Southeast Asia.[^cite_note-rawabet-2025a-228] As the capital, it hosts government institutions and state enterprises, key sources of employment.[^cite_note-rawabet-2025a-228] The public education system follows [Ba'athist socialist ideologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saddamism), for employment in the public sector.[^cite_note-Equal_Times-2023-229] Since 2003, the public sector has struggled to provide jobs, and the private sector hasn't grown sufficiently, leading companies to hire mainly foreigners.[^cite_note-Equal_Times-2023-229] To address this, NGOs are establishing incubation centers in the city.[^cite_note-equaltimes.org-230]

Baghdad serves as headquarters for important companies of Iraq, such as [Iraq National Oil Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq_National_Oil_Company), [State Organization for Marketing of Oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_Organization_for_Marketing_of_Oil) and [Iraqi Airways](179397.md).[^cite_note-rawabet-2025a-228] Baghdad is home to large insurance companies and banks — [Central Bank of Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Bank_of_Iraq), [Rafidain Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rafidain_Bank), and [Rashid Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rasheed_Bank) and regional headquarters for [First Abu Dhabi Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Abu_Dhabi_Bank), [Fransabank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fransabank) and [Saudi National Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saudi_National_Bank).[^cite_note-rawabet-2025b-231] Multinational companies such as [Honeywell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honeywell), [Shell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shell_plc), [General Electric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric), [SalamAir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SalamAir) and [Robert Bosch GmbH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Bosch_GmbH) have established their regional base.[^cite_note-rawabet-2025b-231] Baghdad is also home to Iraq Stock Exchange, that was established in 1992. Most of these establishments are located in [Al-Rasheed Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Rashid_Street_\(Iraq\)), [Karrada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karrada) and [Mansour district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mansour_district).[^cite_note-rawabet-2025a-228]

It was once one of the main destinations in the region with a wealth of cultural attractions.[^cite_note-Lebanon-2022-232] Tourism has diminished due to wars, but in recent years the city has a revival in tourism although still facing challenges.[^cite_note-France_24-2022-233] There are numerous historic, scientific and artistic museums in Baghdad.[^cite_note-Shane-2023-234][^cite_note-Travers-2024-235] Religious tourism in Baghdad has grown since 2003, with sites like [Al-Kadhimiya Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Kadhimiya_Mosque), [Abu Hanifa Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Hanifa_Mosque), [Mausoleum of Abdul-Qadir Gilani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mausoleum_of_Abdul-Qadir_Gilani), and [Buratha Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buratha_Mosque) attracting visitors from [Iran](14653.md), [Pakistan](23235.md), and [India](14533.md), while non-religious tourists mainly come from [Turkey](11125639.md), [France](5843419.md), and the United States.[^cite_note-Al_Arabiya_-2013-236] About 1 million people visit the city annually for religious purposes.[^cite_note-Al_Arabiya_-2013-236] The pilgrims are both [Shia](26961.md) and [Sunni Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam).[^cite_note-237]

The city contains the factories of [carpets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpets), [leather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leather) and [textiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textiles), workshops, [cement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cement) and [tobacco](30942.md) factories.[^cite_note-EB-2024-238] Industrial areas extend from the city center to outside and suburbs in the metropolitan area, such as [Taji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taji) and northern Baghdad.[^cite_note-EB-2024-238] Subsequently, it has produced a wide variety of consumer and industrial goods, including processed foods and beverages, clothes, footwear, wood products, furniture, paper and printed material, bricks, chemicals, plastics, electrical equipment, and metal and nonmetallic products.[^cite_note-EB-2024-238] [Bismayah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bismayah_New_City), southeast of Baghdad, is home to world's largest precast factory.[^cite_note-rawabet-2025b-231] In agricultural aspect, palm groves are spread in the city, and many of its people depends on the cultivation of many yields.[^cite_note-rawabet-2025b-231]

Baghdad, like other provinces such as [Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylon), [Karbala](210957.md) and [Qadissiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Q%C4%81disiyyah_Governorate), contains metals such as [aluminum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminum), [ceramics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceramics), [nickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel), [manganese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manganese) and [chromium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromium), whose size is not yet known, being recently discovered by local Iraqi cadres lacking experience and mechanisms to determine the size of these explorations.[^cite_note-rawabet-2025b-231] An [oilfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oilfield) is located in eastern Baghdad.[^cite_note-孙汝-2025-239] It was believed that the quantities of oil is modest, but the drilling disclosed that its size exceeds the initial estimates, and has northern extensions in the province of [Salah al-Din](200537.md), and southern province of [Wasit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wasit_Governorate).[^cite_note-孙汝-2025-239] The city is also home to Dora Refinery, a large oil refinery in [Dora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dora,_Baghdad), which is the 3rd largest in Iraq in terms of production.[^cite_note-rawabet-2025b-231] The production of it exceeds 200,000 barrels (32,000 m<sup>3</sup>) per day, while its total production estimated if it was developed up to 120,000 barrels (19,000 m<sup>3</sup>) per day.[^cite_note-rawabet-2025b-231]

Most [reconstruction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconstruction_of_Iraq) efforts have been devoted to the restoration and repair of badly damaged urban infrastructure.[^cite_note-ARCADD-2008-240] Some of the private projects includes [Baghdad Renaissance Plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconstruction_of_Iraq#Proposed_Baghdad_Renaissance_Plan), [Sindbad Hotel Complex and Conference Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sindbad_Hotel_Complex_and_Conference_Center), and [Central Bank of Iraq Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Bank_of_Iraq_Tower). Other proposed projects include Romantic Island and Baghdad Gate.[^cite_note-MSNBC/id/264259112-241][^cite_note-Baghdad_Governorate_Website-2010-242] Numerous projects have been also impacted due to corruption.[^cite_note-nytimes22-243] According to a report published by [CNBC](223441.md), there are around 150 entertainment projects planned for the city.[^cite_note-موقع_نبض-2025-244] Many of them were delayed due to government policies.[^cite_note-موقع_نبض-2025-244] Also Baghdad has witnessed the opening of dozens of tourist complexes annually with areas reaching 20,000 square metres (4.9 acres) in addition to some major tourism projects with areas exceeding 50,000 square metres (12 acres) with the aim of investment combining trade and tourism as a distinctive economic model.[^cite_note-موقع_نبض-2025-244] In recent years, Baghdad has also adopted modern economic trends like, establishment of startup hubs, office space and incubation centers, as well as development of shopping malls such as [Baghdad Mall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad_Mall) and Dijlah Village.[^cite_note-equaltimes.org-230]

### Planned Baghdad Metro

Main article: [Baghdad Metro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad_Metro)

The [Baghdad Metro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad_Metro) project was first proposed during the 1970s but did not come to fruition due to wars and sanctions. After the Iraq war, Iraqi authorities intended to revive the project, but it was again delayed due to domestic instability.[^cite_note-254] In 2019, it was reported that Korean [Hyundai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyundai_Rotem) and French [Alstom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alstom) would be building the metro.[^cite_note-255] However, the planned construction did not happen.

As of February 2024, the current plan consisted of fully electric and [automated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automatic_train_operation) (*driverless*) trains running on an extensive railway network including an underground railway portion as well as an elevated railway. The proposed Baghdad Metro system includes seven main lines with a total length of more than 148 kilometres, 64 metro stations, four workshops and depots for trains, several operations control centers (OCC) and seven main power stations (MPS) with a capacity of 250 mega-watts, and several Global System for Mobile Communication (GSM) towers. The metro will be equipped with CCTV and internet as well as USB ports for charging. Special compartments will be allocated for women and children as well as seats for people with special needs, pregnant women, and the elderly. The metro stations will be connected to other public transport networks such as buses and taxis, and 10 parking spaces will be available for commuters. The planned operating speed will be 80–140 km/hour with an estimated 3.25 million riders per day.[^cite_note-256]

In July 2024, it was announced that an international consortium of German, French, Spanish, and Turkish companies was awarded \$17.5 billion contract to construct Baghdad's metro.[^cite_note-257] The consortium includes Alstom, [Systra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systra), [SNCF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SNCF), [Talgo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talgo), [Deutsche Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsche_Bank) and [SENER](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SENER). The consortium was then to negotiate the technical, financial and operational details of the project which is now estimated to be completed in May 2029.[^cite_note-258]

## Cityscape



Restored historical quarter of Baghdad

The Round City was the core of the city, during the establishment of Baghdad. It ceased to exist, as a result of the Mongolian siege. Urban features such as streets, avenues, alleyways and squares clusters a large number of landmarks, which itself creates an identity of cultural or intellectual hubs and define the beauty of Baghdad.

[Al-Rasheed Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Rashid_Street_\(Iraq\)) is one of the most significant landmarks in Baghdad. Located in al-Rusafa area, the street was an artistic, intellectual and cultural center for many Baghdadis. It also included many prominent theaters and nightclubs such as the Crescent Theatre where Egyptian Singer [Umm Kulthum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umm_Kulthum) sang during her visit in 1932 as well as the Chakmakji Company that recorded the music of various Arab singers.[^cite_note-259] The street also contains famous landmarks including the ancient [Haydar-Khana Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haydar-Khana_Mosque) as well as numerous well-known cafés such as [al-Zahawi Café](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Zahawi_Caf%C3%A9) and the [Brazilian Café](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazilian_Caf%C3%A9).[^cite_note-260][^cite_note-261]

[Mutanabbi Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutanabbi_Street) is located near the old quarter of Baghdad; at Al-Rasheed Street. It is the historic center of Baghdadi book-selling, a street filled with bookstores and outdoor book stalls. It was named after the 10th-century classical Iraqi poet [Al-Mutanabbi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mutanabbi).[^cite_note-NYTimes2008-262] This street is well established for bookselling and has often been referred to as the heart and soul of the Baghdad literacy and intellectual community.[^cite_note-NYTimes2008-262] [Firdos Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firdos_Square) is a public open space in Baghdad and the location of two of the best-known hotels, the Palestine Hotel and the Sheraton Ishtar, which are both also the tallest buildings in Baghdad.[^cite_note-263] The square was the site of the statue of Saddam Hussein that was pulled down by the coalition forces in a widely televised event during the 2003 invasion of Iraq.

[Qushla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qushla) or Qishla is a public square and the historical complex located in [al-Rusafa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Rusafa,_Iraq) neighborhood at the riverbank of [Tigris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigris).[^cite_note-Arraf-2018-264] The place and its surroundings is where the historical features and cultural capitals of Baghdad are concentrated, from the [Mutanabbi Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutanabbi_Street), Abbasid-era palace and bridges, Ottoman-era mosques to the [Mustansiriyya Madrasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mustansiriyya_Madrasa).[^cite_note-Arraf-2018-264] The square developed during the Ottoman era as a military barracks.[^cite_note-Arraf-2018-264] It became a place where the citizens of Baghdad find leisure such as reading poetry in gazebos.[^cite_note-Arraf-2018-264] It is characterized by the iconic clock tower which was donated by [George V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_V).[^cite_note-Arraf-2018-264] The entire area is submitted to the [UNESCO](21786641.md) [World Heritage Site](44940.md) [Tentative list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_World_Heritage_Sites_in_Iraq).[^cite_note-265]

### Architecture

During the 1970s and 1980s, [Saddam Hussein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saddam_Hussein)'s government built new monuments, mosques, palaces and hotels.[^cite_note-266] The Street's architecture and aesthetic was inspired by [Renaissance architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_architecture) and also includes the famous Iraqi [shanasheel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mashrabiya).[^cite_note-267]

#### Modern landmarks



[Al-Faw palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Faw_Palace) serves as the [American University of Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_University_of_Iraq_-_Baghdad).

The [National Museum of Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Iraq) whose collection of artifacts was looted during the invasion, and the iconic [Hands of Victory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hands_of_Victory) arches.[^cite_note-Burkeman-2003-268] Multiple political parties are in discussions as to whether the arches should remain as historical monuments or be dismantled.[^cite_note-Burkeman-2003-268] Thousands of ancient manuscripts in the [National Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq_National_Library_and_Archive) were destroyed under [Saddam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saddam)'s command.[^cite_note-Burkeman-2003-268]

[Grand Festivities Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Festivities_Square) is the main square where public celebrations are held and is also the home to three important monuments commemorating Iraqi's fallen soldiers and victories in war; namely Al-Shaheed Monument, the [Victory Arch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victory_Arch) and the [Unknown Soldier's Monument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Monument_to_the_Unknown_Soldier).[^cite_note-Makiya-269] [Al-Shaheed Monument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Shaheed_Monument), also known as the Martyr's Memorial, is dedicated to the Iraqi soldiers who died in the [Iran–Iraq War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Iraq_War).[^cite_note-Makiya-269] However, it is since generally considered by Iraqis to be for all of the martyrs of Iraq, especially those allied with Iran and Syria fighting ISIS, not just of the Iran–Iraq War.[^cite_note-Makiya-269] The monument was opened in 1983, and was designed by the Iraqi architect Saman Kamal and the Iraqi sculptor and artist [Ismail Fatah Al Turk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ismail_Fatah_Al_Turk).[^cite_note-Makiya-269] Though these works symbolize the ruling entity.[^cite_note-Stanek-2020-125] Neverthelsess, they have remained part of architectural legacy, which beautified Baghdad.[^cite_note-Stanek-2020-125]

[Masjid Al-Kadhimain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Kazimiyya_Mosque) is a shrine that is located in the [Kādhimayn suburb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kadhimiyyah) of Baghdad.[^cite_note-Al-Jawadain20092-209][^cite_note-dome-270] It contains the tombs of the seventh and ninth [Twelver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelver) [Shi'ite Imams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imamah_\(Shia_doctrine\)), [Musa al-Kadhim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musa_al-Kadhim) and [Muhammad at-Taqi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_at-Taqi) respectively, upon whom the title of *Kādhimayn* ("Two who swallow their anger") was bestowed.[^cite_note-KazemOmmolKitab-271][^cite_note-dome-270][^cite_note-Al-Jawadain20092-209] Many Shi'ites travel to the mosque from far away places to commemorate those imams.[^cite_note-Al-Jawadain20092-209][^cite_note-dome-270] [A'dhamiyyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A'dhamiyyah) is a predominantly Sunni area with a [Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Hanifa_Mosque) that is associated with the Sunni Imam [Abu Hanifa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Hanifa). The name of *Al-Aʿẓamiyyah* is derived from Abu Hanifa's title, *al-Imām al-Aʿẓam* (the Great Imam).[^cite_note-al-Aadhamy_–_p.29-272][^cite_note-History_of_the_Mosque_of_Abu_Hanifa_and_its_school-273]

The historic Jewish quarters of [Bataween](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bataween) and [Shorja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shorja) is home to numerous sites associated with Jews.[^cite_note-274] These sites were preserved during the Ba'athist regime.[^cite_note-Al-Ahmed-2021-275] However, after 2003, many of them are in poor condition.[^cite_note-Al-Ahmed-2021-275] [Meir Taweig Synagogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meir_Taweig_Synagogue) is the only active synagogue of Iraq, having a large compound that consists of a community center, Jewish school and library.[^cite_note-Al-Ahmed-2021-275] [Daniel Market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shorja) (*Souq Danial*), which was named after [Menahem Saleh Daniel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menahem_Saleh_Daniel), still bears the same name. It is popular for fabrics and shoes.[^cite_note-Al-Ahmed-2021-275] The [Great Synagogue of Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Synagogue_of_Baghdad), the oldest synagogue of Iraq, is restored as a museum.[^cite_note-Al-Ahmed-2021-275] [Al-Habibiyah Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Habibiyah_Jewish_Cemetery) is the largest Jewish cemetery in Baghdad, home to about 1,000 graves.[^cite_note-Al-Ahmed-2021-275] The Tomb of Joshua became a Muslim shrine, but is believed to be the burial site of [Joshua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joshua).[^cite_note-Al-Ahmed-2021-275] Shaykh Yitzhak Tomb and Synagogue was preserved until 2003. but has since become neglected. Other sites include House of [Sassoon Eskell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sassoon_Eskell) and library of [Mir Basri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mir_Basri).[^cite_note-Al-Ahmed-2021-275]

The [Sabian–Mandaean Mandi of Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabian%E2%80%93Mandaean_Mandi_of_Baghdad) is a Mandaen temple in al-Qadisiyyah.[^cite_note-Cese-2024-276] It is the main community center for Mandaeans in Iraq.[^cite_note-Cese-2024-276] Plans are underway to demolish and build a larger one to accommodate more worshippers.[^cite_note-Cese-2024-276] A cultural institute for Mandeans is also in Baghdad.[^cite_note-277] The city is home to [Baba Nanak Shrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baba_Nanak_Shrine), a sacred site in Sikhism.[^cite_note-Baghdad2-7] It was destroyed during the Iraq War in 2003.[^cite_note-Baghdad2-7] In the [Kadhimiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kadhimiya) district of Baghdad, was the house of [Baháʼu'lláh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bah%C3%A1%CA%BCu'll%C3%A1h), (Prophet Founder of the [Baháʼí Faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bah%C3%A1%CA%BC%C3%AD_Faith)) also known as the "Most Great House" (Bayt-i-Aʻzam) and the "House of God", where Baháʼu'lláh mostly resided from 1853 to 1863. It is considered a holy place and a [place of pilgrimage by Baháʼís](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bah%C3%A1%CA%BC%C3%AD_pilgrimage) according to their "[Most Holy Book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kit%C3%A1b-i-Aqdas)".[^cite_note-278] On 23 June 2013, the house was destroyed under unclear circumstances.[^cite_note-BWNS-2013-226] [Baghdad Zoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad_Zoo) was once the largest [zoological park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoological_park) in the [Middle East](19323.md). Within eight days following the 2003 invasion, however, only 35 of the 650 animals in the facility survived.[^cite_note-BBC_radio_4,_zoo-279] This was a result of theft of some animals for human food, and starvation of caged animals that had no food.[^cite_note-BBC_radio_4,_zoo-279] Conservationist [Lawrence Anthony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Anthony) and some of the zoo keepers cared for the animals and fed the carnivores with donkeys they had bought locally.[^cite_note-BBC_radio_4,_zoo-279][^cite_note-280] Eventually [Paul Bremer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Bremer), Director of the [Coalition Provisional Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coalition_Provisional_Authority) in Iraq after the invasion, ordered protection for the zoo and enlisted U.S. engineers to help reopen the facility.[^cite_note-BBC_radio_4,_zoo-279] Al-Zawraa Park is also part of the zoo, which is a main urban park of the city.[^cite_note-BBC_radio_4,_zoo-279]

## Culture

See also: [Culture of Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Iraq), [Baghdad Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad_Arabic), and [Café culture of Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caf%C3%A9_culture_of_Baghdad)



The [Iraqi National Symphony Orchestra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_National_Symphony_Orchestra) performing in July 2007



The National Ballet performing in 2007



The Baghdad Convention Center



Al-Ma'mun's Telecommunication Center in downtown Baghdad

Baghdad has always played a significant role in the broader [Arab cultural sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_culture), contributing several significant writers, musicians and visual artists. Historically, the city had a vibrant modern culture and lifestyle.[^cite_note-Iddon-128] Famous [Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab) poets and singers such as [Nizar Qabbani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nizar_Qabbani), [Umm Kulthum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umm_Kulthum), [Fairuz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairuz), [Salah Al-Hamdani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salah_Al-Hamdani), [Ilham al-Madfai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilham_al-Madfai) and others have performed for the city. The dialect of [Arabic spoken in Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad_Arabic) differs from that of other large urban centers in Iraq, having features more characteristic of nomadic Arabic dialects (Versteegh, *The Arabic Language*). It is possible that this was caused by the repopulating of the city with rural residents after the multiple sackings of the late [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages). For poetry written about Baghdad, see [Reuven Snir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reuven_Snir) (ed.), *Baghdad: The City in Verse* (Harvard, 2013).[^cite_note-284] Baghdad joined the [UNESCO Creative Cities Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creative_Cities_Network) as a City of Literature in December 2015.[^cite_note-285]

Some of the important cultural institutions in the city include the [National Theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Theater_\(Iraq\)), which was looted during the [2003 invasion of Iraq](201936.md), but efforts are underway to restore the theater.[^cite_note-286] The live theater industry received a boost during the 1990s, when UN [sanctions](411315.md) limited the import of foreign films. As many as 30 movie theaters were reported to have been converted to live stages, producing a wide range of [comedies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comedy) and dramatic productions.[^cite_note-287] Institutions offering cultural education in Baghdad include [The Music and Ballet School of Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Music_and_Ballet_School_of_Baghdad) and the Institute of Fine Arts Baghdad. The [Iraqi National Symphony Orchestra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_National_Symphony_Orchestra) is a government funded symphony orchestra in Baghdad. The INSO plays primarily classical European music, as well as original compositions based on Iraqi and Arab instruments and music. Mandaeans had cultural club in Al-Zawraa, where poetry evenings and cultural seminars were held, attended by poets, writers, artists, officials, and dignitaries of the communities.[^cite_note-algardenia.com-2024-288] There is also a social cultural center of Mandaeans at al-Qadisiyyah.[^cite_note-algardenia.com-2024-288] [Baghdad Jewish Community Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad_Jewish_Community_Center) is located in [Al-Rashid Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Rashid_Street_\(Iraq\)).[^cite_note-289]

Baghdad is also home to a number of museums which housed [artifacts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artifact_\(archaeology\)) and relics of [ancient civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_civilization); many of these were stolen, and the museums looted, during the widespread chaos immediately after United States forces entered the city.

During [occupation of Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupation_of_Iraq_\(2003%E2%80%932011\)), [AFN Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AFN_Iraq) ("Freedom Radio") broadcast news and entertainment within Baghdad, among other locations. There is also a private radio station called "Dijlah" (named after the Arabic word for the Tigris River) that was created in 2004 as Iraq's first independent talk radio station. Radio Dijlah offices, in the [Jamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamia) neighborhood of Baghdad, have been attacked on several occasions.[^cite_note-290]

## Sport

See also: [Sport in Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sport_in_Iraq)

Baghdad is home to some of the most successful [football (soccer)](10568.md) teams in Iraq, the biggest being [Al-Shorta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Shorta_SC) (Police), [Al-Quwa Al-Jawiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Quwa_Al-Jawiya) (Air Force), [Al-Zawraa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Zawraa_SC), and [Al-Talaba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Talaba_SC) (Students). The largest stadium in Baghdad is [Al-Shaab Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Shaab_Stadium), which was opened in 1966. In recent years, the capital has seen the building of several football stadiums which are meant be opened in the near future. The city has also had a strong tradition of [horse racing](74711.md) ever since [World War I](4764461.md), known to Baghdadis simply as 'Races'. There are reports of pressures by the Islamists to stop this tradition due to the associated gambling.[^cite_note-291]
