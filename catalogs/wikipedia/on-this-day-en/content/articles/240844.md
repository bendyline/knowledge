# Reef

For other uses, see [Reef (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reef_\(disambiguation\)).

A **reef** is a ridge or [shoal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoal) of rock, [coral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coral), or similar relatively stable material lying beneath the surface of a natural body of water.[^cite_note-NatGeo-1] Many reefs result from natural, [abiotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abiotic_component) (non-living) processes such as [deposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deposition_\(geology\)) of sand or [wave erosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_erosion) planing down rock outcrops. However, reefs such as the [coral reefs](87410.md) of tropical waters are formed by [biotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biotic_component) (living) processes, dominated by corals and [coralline algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coralline_algae). [Artificial reefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_reef), such as shipwrecks and other man-made underwater structures, may occur intentionally or as the result of an accident. These are sometimes designed to increase the physical complexity of featureless sand bottoms to attract a more diverse range of [organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organism). They provide shelter to various aquatic animals which help prevent extinction.[^cite_note-2] Another reason reefs are put in place is for aquaculture, and fish farmers who are looking to improve their businesses sometimes invest in them.[^cite_note-3] Reefs are often quite near to the surface, but not all definitions require this.[^cite_note-NatGeo-1]

Earth's largest coral reef system is the [Great Barrier Reef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Barrier_Reef) in Australia, at a length of over 2,300 kilometres (1,400 miles).[^cite_note-4]

## Etymology

The word "reef" traces its origins back to the [Old Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse) word *rif,* meaning "rib" or "reef". *Rif* comes from the Proto-Germanic term *ribją* meaning "rib".[^cite_note-5]

## Classification

Reefs may be classified in terms of their origin, geographical location, depth, and [topography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topography). For example a tropical coral fringing reef, or a temperate rocky intertidal reef.

### Biotic

See also: [Coral reef § Formation](87410.md#Formation), [Sponge reef § Structure of sponge reefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sponge_reef#Structure_of_sponge_reefs), and [Bivalve reef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bivalve_reef)



Part of [Great Barrier Reef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Barrier_Reef)

A variety of biotic reef types exists, including [oyster reefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oyster_reef) and [sponge reefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sponge_reef), but the most massive and widely distributed are tropical [coral reefs](87410.md).[^cite_note-NatGeo-1] Although corals are major contributors to the framework and bulk material comprising a coral reef, the organisms most responsible for reef growth against the constant assault from ocean waves are [calcareous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcareous) algae, especially, although not entirely, [coralline algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coralline_algae).

Oyster larvae prefer to settle on adult oysters and thereby develop layers building upwards. These eventually form a fairly massive hard stony calcium carbonate structure on which other reef organisms like [sponges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sponges) and [seaweeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seaweeds) can grow and provide a habitat for mobile benthic organisms.[^cite_note-NatGeo-1]

These biotic reef types take on additional names depending upon how the reef lies in relation to the land, if any. Reef types include [fringing reefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fringing_reef), [barrier reefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barrier_reef), and [atolls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atoll). A fringing reef is a reef that is attached to a landmass. Whereas, a barrier reef forms a calcareous barrier around a landmass, resulting in a [lagoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagoon) between the shore and the reef. Conversely, an atoll is a ring reef with no land present.

The reef front, facing the ocean, is a high energy locale. Whereas, the internal lagoon will be at a lower energy with fine grained sediments.

#### Mounds

Both mounds and reefs are considered to be varieties of organosedimentary buildups, which are sedimentary features, built by the interaction of organisms and their environment. These interactions have a synoptic relief and whose biotic composition differs from that found on and beneath the surrounding [sea floor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_floor). However, reefs are held up by a macroscopic skeletal framework, as what is seen on coral reefs. [Corals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corals) and calcareous algae grow on top of one another, forming a three-dimensional framework that is modified in various ways by other organisms and inorganic processes.

Conversely, mounds lack a macroscopic skeletal framework. Instead, they are built by microorganisms or by organisms that also lack a skeletal framework. A microbial mound might be built exclusively or primarily by [cyanobacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanobacteria). Examples of [biostromes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biostrome) formed by cyanobacteria occur in the [Great Salt Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Salt_Lake) in [Utah](31716.md), United States, and in [Shark Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shark_Bay) on the coast of [Western Australia](33613.md).[^cite_note-6]

Cyanobacteria do not have skeletons, and individual organisms are microscopic. However, they can encourage the precipitation or accumulation of calcium carbonate to produce distinct sediment bodies in composition that have relief on the seafloor. Cyanobacterial mounds were most abundant before the evolution of shelly macroscopic organisms, but they still exist today. [Stromatolites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stromatolite), for instance, are microbial mounds with a laminated internal structure. Whereas, [bryozoans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryozoan) and [crinoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crinoid), common contributors to marine sediments during the [Mississippian period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippian_\(geology\)), produce a different kind of mound. Although bryozoans are small and crinoid skeletons disintegrate, bryozoan and crinoid meadows can persist over time and produce compositionally distinct bodies of sediment with depositional relief. [^cite_note-7]

The [Proterozoic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proterozoic) [Belt Supergroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belt_Supergroup) contains evidence of possible [microbial mat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microbial_mat) and dome structures similar to stromatolite reef complexes.[^cite_note-8]

Rocky reefs are underwater outcrops of rock projecting above the adjacent unconsolidated surface with varying relief. They can be found in depth ranges from [intertidal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intertidal) to deep water and provide a substrate for a large range of sessile benthic organisms, and shelter for a large range of mobile organisms.[^cite_note-NOAA_fisheries-9] They are often located in sub-tropical, temperate, and sub-polar latitudes.

#### Structures



Fossil [brain coral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brain_coral) (*Diploria*) at the [Windley Key Fossil Reef Geological State Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windley_Key_Fossil_Reef_Geological_State_Park). [US Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarter_\(United_States_coin\)) near top for scale.

Ancient reefs buried within [stratigraphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratigraphy) sections are of considerable interest to [geologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geologist) because they provide paleo-environmental information about the location in [Earth's history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Earth). In addition, reef structures within a sequence of [sedimentary rocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedimentary_rock) provide a discontinuity which may serve as a trap or conduit for [fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel) or mineralizing fluids to form [petroleum](23195.md) or [ore deposits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ore_deposit).[^cite_note-10]

Corals, including some major extinct groups [Rugosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugosa) and [Tabulata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tabulata), have been important reef builders through much of the [Phanerozoic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phanerozoic) since the [Ordovician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordovician) Period. However, other organism groups, such as calcifying algae, especially members of the red algae ([Rhodophyta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodophyta)), and molluscs (especially the [rudist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudists) bivalves during the [Cretaceous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cretaceous) Period) have created massive structures at various times.

During the [Cambrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambrian) Period, the conical or tubular skeletons of [Archaeocyatha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeocyatha), an extinct group of uncertain affinities (possibly sponges), built reefs.[^cite_note-11] Other groups, such as the Bryozoa, have been important interstitial organisms, living between the framework builders. The corals which build reefs today, the [Scleractinia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scleractinia), arose after the [Permian–Triassic extinction event](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permian%E2%80%93Triassic_extinction_event) that wiped out the earlier rugose corals (as well as many other groups). They became increasingly important reef builders throughout the [Mesozoic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesozoic) Era.[^cite_note-12] They may have arisen from a rugose coral ancestor.

Rugose corals built their skeletons of [calcite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcite) and have a different symmetry from that of the scleractinian corals, whose skeletons are [aragonite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aragonite).[^cite_note-13] However, there are some unusual examples of well-preserved aragonitic rugose corals in the [Late Permian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lopingian). In addition, calcite has been reported in the initial post-larval calcification in a few scleractinian corals. Nevertheless, scleractinian corals (which arose in the middle Triassic) may have arisen from a non-calcifying ancestor independent of the rugosan corals (which disappeared in the late Permian).

### Artificial

Main article: [Artificial reef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_reef)

An artificial reef is a human-created underwater structure, typically built to promote [marine life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_biology#Reefs) in areas with a generally featureless bottom, to control erosion, block ship passage, block the use of [trawling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trawling) nets,[^cite_note-Gray_2018-14] or improve [surfing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surfing).[^cite_note-Bournemouth_Echo-15]

Many reefs are built using objects that were built for other purposes, for example by sinking oil rigs (through the [Rigs-to-Reefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigs-to-Reefs) program), [scuttling ships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinking_ships_for_wreck_diving_sites), or by deploying [rubble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubble) or [construction debris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Construction_debris). Other artificial reefs are purpose built (e.g. the [reef balls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Reef_Ball_Foundation) and [Electrified Reefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrified_reef)). Shipwrecks become artificial reefs on the seafloor. Regardless of construction method, artificial reefs generally provide stable hard surfaces where [algae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algae) and invertebrates such as [barnacles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnacle), corals, and [oysters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oyster) attach; the accumulation of attached marine life in turn provides intricate structure and food for [assemblages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biocoenosis) of fish.
