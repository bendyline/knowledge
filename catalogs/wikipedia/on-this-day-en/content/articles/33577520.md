# Gaijin

This article is about a Japanese word for "foreigner". For the James Clavell novel, see [Gai-Jin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gai-Jin). For the video game developer, see [Gaijin Entertainment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaijin_Entertainment).

***Gaijin*** ([外人](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%A4%96%E4%BA%BA); [\[ɡai(d)ʑiɴ\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Japanese); 'outsider, [alien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alien_\(law\))') is a [Japanese](15606.md) word for foreigners and non-Japanese citizens in Japan, specifically being applied to foreigners of non-Japanese ethnicity and those from the [Japanese diaspora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_diaspora) who are not Japanese citizens.[^cite_note-TROE104-1] The word is composed of two [kanji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanji): *gai* (外, 'outside') and *jin* (人, 'person'). Similarly composed words that refer to foreign things include *gaikoku* (外国, 'foreign country') and *gaisha* (外車, 'foreign car'). Though the term can be applied to all foreigners of non-Japanese citizenship and ethnicity, some non-Japanese East Asians may have specific terminology used instead.[^cite_note-7]

Some feel the word has come to have a negative or [pejorative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pejorative) connotation,[^cite_note-14] while other observers maintain it is neutral.[^cite_note-Koshiro_1999_114-15] ***Gaikokujin*** ([外国人](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%A4%96%E5%9B%BD%E4%BA%BA); [\[ɡaikokɯꜜ(d)ʑiɴ\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Japanese); 'foreign-country person') is a more neutral and somewhat more formal term widely used in the Japanese government and in media. *Gaijin* does not specifically mean a foreigner that is also a white person; instead, the term ***hakujin*** (白人 'white person') can be considered as a type of foreigner, and ***kokujin*** (黒人 'black person') would be the black equivalent.

## Etymology and history

The word *gaijin* can be traced in writing to the 13th-century *[Heike Monogatari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heike_Monogatari)*:

> **外人**もなき所に兵具をとゝのへ[^cite_note-takagi-16]\
> Assembling arms where there are no **gaijin**[^cite_note-17]

Here, *gaijin* refers to outsiders[^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19] and potential enemies.[^cite_note-kojien-20] Another early reference is in *[Renri Hishō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renri_Hish%C5%8D)* (c. 1349) by [Nijō Yoshimoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nij%C5%8D_Yoshimoto), where it is used to refer to a Japanese person who is a stranger, not a friend.[^cite_note-kojien-20] The [Noh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noh) play, *[Kurama tengu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Noh_plays:_A-M)*[^cite_note-21] has a scene where a servant objects to the appearance of a traveling monk:

> 源平両家の童形たちのおのおのござ候ふに、かやうの**外人**は然るべからず候\
> A **gaijin** doesn't belong here, where children from the Genji and Heike families are playing.

Here, *gaijin* also means an outsider or unfamiliar person.[^cite_note-22]

The [Portuguese](23033.md) in the 16th century were the first Europeans to visit Japan; they were called *[nanbanjin](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%8D%97%E8%9B%AE%E4%BA%BA)* ('southern barbarians'),[^cite_note-23] and trade with them was known as the [*Nanban* trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanban_trade). When British and Dutch adventurers such as [William Adams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Adams_\(pilot\)) arrived in the early 17th century, they were usually known as *[kōmōjin](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E7%B4%85%E6%AF%9B%E4%BA%BA)* ('red-haired people'),[^cite_note-24] a term cognate to [one used](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ang_moh) in modern [Hokkien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hokkien) Chinese.

When the [Tokugawa shogunate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokugawa_shogunate) was made to open Japan to foreign contact after [two centuries of self-isolation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sakoku), Westerners were commonly called as *[ijin](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E7%95%B0%E4%BA%BA)* ('different people'), a shortened form of *[ikokujin](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E7%95%B0%E5%9B%BD%E4%BA%BA)* ('different country person') or *[ihōjin](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E7%95%B0%E9%82%A6%E4%BA%BA)* ('different motherland people').[^cite_note-25]

The word *gaikokujin* (外国人) is composed of *[gaikoku](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%A4%96%E5%9B%BD)* ('foreign country') and *[jin](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E4%BA%BA)* ('person'). Early citations exist from c. 1235,[^cite_note-26] but it was largely non-extant until reappearing in 1838.[^cite_note-27] The [Meiji government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_government) (1868–1912) further popularized the term, which came to replace *ijin*, *ikokujin* and *ihōjin*. As the [Empire of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_of_Japan) extended [to Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korea_under_Japanese_rule) and [to Taiwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan_under_Japanese_rule), the term *[naikokujin](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E5%86%85%E5%9B%BD%E4%BA%BA)* ('within-country people') came to refer to nationals of other imperial territories. While other terms fell out of use after [World War II](32927.md), *gaikokujin* remained the official term for non-Japanese people. Some hold that the modern *gaijin* is a contraction of *gaikokujin*.[^cite_note-Gottlieb-28]

## Usage



Foreigners in Japan in 2000 by citizenship

[^cite_note-29]

While all forms of the word mean 'foreigner' or 'outsider', in practice *gaijin* and *gaikokujin* are commonly used to refer to foreigners of non-East Asian ethnicities.[^cite_note-30] For example, other East Asians such as [ethnic Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_people_in_Japan) and [Koreans residing in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koreans_in_Japan) are not referred to as *gaijin*, but by their nationality directly. [Special permanent residents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_permanent_resident_\(Japan\)) with ancestry from Japan's wartime colonies, mostly Koreans, are known as *zainichi* (在日), while for ethnic Chinese specifically *kakyō* (華僑) is also used.[^cite_note-31][^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33]

The term may also sometimes be applied to [Wajin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamato_people) born and raised in other countries.[^cite_note-Koshiro_1999_254-34][^cite_note-Tsuda-35] *Gaijin* is also commonly used within Japanese events such as baseball (there is a limit to non-Japanese players in [NPB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nippon_Pro_Baseball)) and [professional wrestling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puroresu) to collectively refer to the visiting performers from the West who will frequently tour the country.

Japanese speakers commonly refer to non-Japanese people as *gaijin* even while they are overseas. Also, people of Japanese descent native to other countries (especially those countries with large Japanese communities) might also call non-descendants *gaijin*, as a counterpart to [*nikkei*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_diaspora).[^cite_note-Tsuda-35][^cite_note-lie-36][^cite_note-37] This interpretation of the term as neutral in tone continues for some.[^cite_note-Itoh-10][^cite_note-Koshiro_1999_114-15][^cite_note-dillon-38][^cite_note-Wada-39] However, though the term may be used without negative intent by many Japanese speakers,[^cite_note-kodansha-8] it is seen as derogatory by some[^cite_note-books.google-11][^cite_note-books.google_a-12][^cite_note-books.google_b-13] and reflective of exclusionary attitudes.[^cite_note-42]

> While the term itself has no derogatory meaning, it emphasizes the exclusiveness of Japanese attitude and has therefore picked up pejorative connotations that many Westerners resent.

— Mayumi Itoh (1995)[^cite_note-Itoh-10]

In light of these connotations, the more neutral and formal *gaikokujin* is often used as an alternative term to refer to non-Japanese people.[^cite_note-Multiethnic-40][^cite_note-ichiro-43] Nanette Gottlieb, Professor of Japanese Studies at the School of Languages and Comparative Cultural Studies at the University of Queensland, suggests that the term has become controversial and is avoided now by most Japanese television broadcasters.[^cite_note-Gottlieb-28]

*Gaijin* appears frequently in Western literature and pop culture. It forms the title of such novels as Marc Olden's *Gaijin* (New York: Arbor House, 1986), James Melville's *Go gently, gaijin* (New York : St. Martin's Press, 1986), James Kirkup's *Gaijin on the Ginza* (London: Chester Springs, 1991) and James Clavell's [*Gai-Jin* (New York: Delacorte Press, 1993)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gai-Jin_\(novel\)), as well as a song by [Nick Lowe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_Lowe). It is the title of feature films such as Tizuka Yamazaki's *[Gaijin – Os Caminhos da Liberdade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaijin_%E2%80%93_Os_Caminhos_da_Liberdade)* (1980) and *[Gaijin – Ama-me Como Sou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaijin_%E2%80%93_Ama-me_Como_Sou)* (2005), as well as animation shorts such as Fumi Inoue's *Gaijin* (2003).
