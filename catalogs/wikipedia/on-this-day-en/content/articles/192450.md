The **Metropolitan Police Service** (**MPS**), commonly known as the **Metropolitan Police**, **Met Police**, or the "Met", is the [territorial police force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territorial_police_force#United_Kingdom) responsible for law enforcement and crime prevention within [Greater London](45367389.md). In addition, it is responsible for specialised tasks throughout the United Kingdom, such as [dealing with counter-terrorism throughout the UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counter-terrorism_in_the_United_Kingdom), and the protection of certain individuals, including the [monarch](47063.md), [royal family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_royal_family), [governmental officials](25318118.md),[^cite_note-10] and other designated figures. It is also referred to as an [eponym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eponym) as "**Scotland Yard**" or "the Yard", after the location of its original headquarters in [Great Scotland Yard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Scotland_Yard), Whitehall, in the 19th century.[^cite_note-11] The Met is presently headquartered at [New Scotland Yard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Scotland_Yard), in [a building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Scotland_Yard_\(building\)) situated on the [Victoria Embankment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_Embankment).[^cite_note-12]

The main geographical area covered by the Met, the [Metropolitan Police District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Police_District), consists of the 32 [London boroughs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_boroughs),[^cite_note-13] and excludes the square mile of the [City of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_London) – a largely non-residential and financial district, overseen by the [City of London Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_London_Police). As the force responsible for the majority of the UK's capital, the Met has significant responsibilities and unique challenges, such as protecting 164 foreign embassies and [High Commissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_commissioner_\(Commonwealth\)),[^cite_note-\:1-14] policing [London City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_City_Airport) and [Heathrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heathrow_Airport) airports, protecting the [Palace of Westminster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_Westminster), and managing a higher volume of protests and events than any other [British police force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Police_Forces), with 3,500 such events in 2016.[^cite_note-\:1-14]

The force, by officer numbers, ranks as the largest police force within the United Kingdom.[^cite_note-15] Excluding its national roles, the Met oversees the eighth-smallest primary geographic area ([police area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_area)) compared to other territorial police forces in the UK.

The force operates under the leadership of the [Commissioner of Police of the Metropolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commissioner_of_Police_of_the_Metropolis), directly accountable to the [mayor of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor_of_London), through the [Mayor's Office for Policing and Crime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor's_Office_for_Policing_and_Crime) and the [Home Office](225695.md). The post of commissioner was first held jointly by [Sir Charles Rowan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Charles_Rowan) and [Sir Richard Mayne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Richard_Mayne), with [Sir Mark Rowley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Mark_Rowley) currently holding the position since July 2022.[^cite_note-16]

## History

Main article: [History of the Metropolitan Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Metropolitan_Police)

The Metropolitan Police Service was founded in 1829 by Home Secretary Sir [Robert Peel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Peel) under the [Metropolitan Police Act 1829](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Police_Act_1829) and on 29 September of that year, the first constables of the service appeared on the streets of London.[^cite_note-17] Ten years later, [Metropolitan Police Act 1839](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Police_Act_1839) consolidated policing within London by expanding the Metropolitan Police District and either abolishing or amalgamating the various other law enforcement entities within London into the Metropolitan Police such as the [Thames River Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thames_River_Police) and the [Bow Street Runners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bow_Street_Runners).[^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19]

## Governance

Since January 2012, the [Mayor of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor_of_London) is responsible for the governance of the Metropolitan Police through the [Mayor's Office for Policing and Crime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayor's_Office_for_Policing_and_Crime) (MOPAC).[^cite_note-20] The mayor is able to appoint someone to act on his behalf. As of November 2024, the office-holder is the deputy mayor for policing and crime, [Kaya Comer-Schwartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaya_Comer-Schwartz).[^cite_note-21] The work of MOPAC is scrutinised by the Police and Crime Committee (also known as a [police and crime panel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_and_crime_panel)) of the [London Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Assembly). These structures were created by the [Police Reform and Social Responsibility Act 2011](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_Reform_and_Social_Responsibility_Act_2011) and replaced the [Metropolitan Police Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Police_Authority)-appointed board created in 2000 by [Greater London Authority Act 1999](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_London_Authority_Act_1999).

Before 2000, the Metropolitan Police was under the authority of the [Home Secretary](149104.md), the only [British territorial police force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territorial_police_force) to be administered by [central government](25318118.md). The Metropolitan Police Office (MPO), although based at Scotland Yard, was a department of the [Home Office](225695.md) created in 1829 and was responsible for the force's day-to-day administration. Under the authority of the [receiver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Receiver_of_the_Metropolitan_Police), a civilian official who was equivalent in rank to the deputy commissioner and served as the force's chief financial officer, it was headed by a civilian secretary, who was equivalent in rank to the assistant commissioners.[^cite_note-22]

## Police area and other forces



[New Scotland Yard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotland_Yard) is the headquarters of the Metropolitan Police.

The [area policed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_area) by the Metropolitan Police Service is known as the [Metropolitan Police District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Police_District) (MPD). Since 2000, the MPD's boundaries have been identical to those of [Greater London](45367389.md), covering the 32 [London boroughs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_borough); the [City of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_London) (which is not a London borough) is a separate police area and is the responsibility of the [City of London Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_London_Police). Until 2017, the Met was organized into 32 Borough Operational Command Units directly aligned with the 32 boroughs; the MPD is now divided into 12 Basic Command Units (BCUs) made up of two, three or four boroughs.[^cite_note-23]

The [Ministry of Defence Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Defence_Police) is responsible for policing of [Ministry of Defence](203793.md) property throughout the United Kingdom, including [its headquarters](203793.md#Property_portfolio) in Whitehall and other MoD establishments across the MPD.[^cite_note-24]

The [British Transport Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Transport_Police) (BTP) is responsible for policing of the [rail network in Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_transport_in_Great_Britain), including London. Within London, it is also responsible for the policing of the [London Underground](17839.md), [London Trams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tramlink), the [London Cable Car](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Cable_Car) and the [Docklands Light Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Docklands_Light_Railway).[^cite_note-25]

The English part of the [Royal Parks Constabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Parks_Constabulary), which patrolled a number of Greater London's major parks, was merged with the Metropolitan Police in 2004, and those parks are now policed by the [Royal Parks Operational Command Unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Parks_Operational_Command_Unit).[^cite_note-26] There is also a small park police force, the [Kew Constabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kew_Constabulary), responsible for the [Royal Botanic Gardens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Botanic_Gardens,_Kew), whose officers have full police powers within the park. A few local authorities maintain their own borough park constabularies, including [Wandsworth Parks and Events Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wandsworth_Parks_and_Events_Police), [Kensington and Chelsea Parks Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Borough_of_Kensington_and_Chelsea_Parks_Police), [Havering Parks Constabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Havering_Parks_Constabulary) and the [Hampstead Heath Constabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hampstead_Heath_Constabulary). All of these enjoy powers of arrest without warrant as constables;[^cite_note-27] however, the officers of the last mentioned have full police powers, much like officers of the Metropolitan Police, on the heath, whereas the other parks' police primarily focus on [by-law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/By-law) enforcement.

Metropolitan Police officers have legal jurisdiction throughout all of [England and Wales](275187.md), including areas that have their own special police forces, such as the Ministry of Defence, as do all police officers of [territorial police forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territorial_police_force).[^cite_note-28] Officers also have limited powers in Scotland and Northern Ireland.[^cite_note-29] Within the MPD, the Met will take over the investigation of any serious crime from the [Ministry of Defence Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Defence_Police) and to a lesser degree BTP, if it is deemed appropriate. Terrorist incidents and complex murder enquiries will almost always be investigated by the Met,[^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31] with the assistance of any relevant specialist force, even if they are committed on Ministry of Defence or railway property. A minor incursion into the normal jurisdiction of territorial police officers in England and Wales is that Met officers involved in the protection duties of the Royal Family and other VIPs have full police powers in Scotland and Northern Ireland in connection with those duties.[^cite_note-32]

## Organisation and structure

Main article: [Organisation and structure of the Metropolitan Police Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organisation_and_structure_of_the_Metropolitan_Police_Service)

The Metropolitan Police Service is organised into the following directorates:[^cite_note-Home_-_The_Met-33]

- [Frontline Policing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frontline_Policing)

- [Met Operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Met_Operations)

- [Specialist Operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specialist_Operations)

- Professionalism

- Shared Support Services (part of Met headquarters)

Each is overseen by an assistant commissioner or, in the case of administrative departments, a director of [police staff](#Staff_and_PCSOS), which is the equivalent civilian staff grade. The management board is made up of the commissioner, deputy commissioner, assistant commissioners and directors.

## Ranks

See also: [Police ranks of the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_ranks_of_the_United_Kingdom)



Met Police officers on the streets of [Soho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soho). Since 1863, the [custodian helmet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Custodian_helmet) (middle) has been worn by male police [constables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constable#United_Kingdom) and [sergeants](219424.md#United_Kingdom) while on foot patrol.

The Metropolitan Police Service uses the standard British police ranks, indicated by epaulettes, up to [chief superintendent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_superintendent), but uniquely has five ranks above that level instead of the standard three: commander, deputy assistant commissioner, assistant commissioner, deputy commissioner and commissioner.[^cite_note-34] All senior officers of the rank of Commander and above are [chief police officers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_police_officer) of [NPCC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Police_Chiefs'_Council) (previously [ACPO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_of_Chief_Police_Officers)) rank.

The Met approved the use of name badges in October 2003, with new recruits wearing the Velcro badges from September 2004. The badge consists of the wearer's rank, followed by their surname.[^cite_note-35] All officers are assigned a unique identification number, which includes a two-letter BCU (Basic Command Unit) code.&#x20;

Following controversy over assaults by uniformed officers with concealed shoulder identification numbers during the [G20 summit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_G-20_London_summit_protests),[^cite_note-36] Commissioner Sir Paul Stephenson said, "the public has a right to be able to identify any uniformed officer whilst performing their duty" by their shoulder identification numbers.[^cite_note-37]

The Metropolitan Police Service includes full-time, paid officers known as 'regulars', and part-time, voluntary officers from the [Metropolitan Special Constabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Special_Constabulary). Both regulars and specials enjoy full police powers, wear the same uniform, and carry the same kit. As elsewhere in the UK, "regulars" are not employees, but rather [Crown servants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_servant), and holders of the office of constable.&#x20;

## Staff and PCSOs

The Met's police staff are non-warranted civilians. When the Met was formed in 1829, there were only six of them (the [Receiver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Receiver_of_the_Metropolitan_Police), his two clerks and the three Commissioners' clerks, although the Commissioners were also non-warranted right up until 2011),[^cite_note-64] but they now include [police community support officers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_community_support_officer) (PCSOs[^cite_note-66]), designated detention officers (DDOs), and many other non-officer roles.[^cite_note-67] Their numbers are currently:

## Resources

### Fleet



Various Metropolitan Police vehicles attending a protest in 2021



A [Toyota Corolla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toyota_Corolla_\(E210\)) used by the Metropolitan Police

As of May 2026, the Met operates and maintains a fleet of around 5,500 vehicles, with almost a third (30%) fully electric or hybrid.[^cite_note-68] In 2018, the fleet covered 46,777,720 miles (75,281,440 km).[^cite_note-69] The fleet comprises numerous vehicles, including:[^cite_note-fleet-70]

- Incident response vehicles (IRV): attached to the various Basic Command Units (BCU) of the Metropolitan Police area, used for frontline policing duties such as patrol and emergency response.

- Q-cars: covert unmarked vehicles, belonging to a variety of departments.

- Armed response vehicle (ARV): Transports [authorised firearms officers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authorised_firearms_officer) trained to use firearms to deal with incidents involving deadly weapons.

- Traffic units: respond to traffic accidents on major roads, enforce traffic laws and encourage road safety.

- Motorcycles: utilised by the [Roads and Transport Policing Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roads_and_Transport_Policing_Command) and [Parliamentary and Diplomatic Protection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_and_Diplomatic_Protection) for more agile patrol and response.

- Scrambler bikes: used by Operation Venice officers to combat moped gangs.

  [^cite_note-71]

- Collision investigation units (CIU): respond to and appropriately investigate all major road traffic collisions.

- Protected carriers: used for public order duties.

- Personnel carriers: used to transport numerous officers on patrol and to incidents, as well as non-violent public order situations.

- Station vans: used to transport both officers and suspects in a cage in the rear of the van.

- Commercial vehicle units: used to respond to incidents involving commercial vehicles.

- CBRN units: used to mitigate chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear incidents. These are identified by a large number of equipment lockers on newer vans and a large array of detecting equipment on the top of older vans.

- Control units: used for incident command and control purposes.

- Armoured multi-role vehicles: used for public order duties, airport and counter-terrorism duties, or as required.

- General purpose vehicles: used for general support and transportation duties of officers or equipment.

- Training vehicles: used to train police drivers.

- Miscellaneous vehicles: such as horseboxes and trailers.

- The [Metropolitan Police Historic Vehicle Collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Police_Historic_Vehicle_Collection)

&#x20;The majority of vehicles have a service life of three to five years; the Met replaces or upgrades between 800 and 1,000 vehicles each year. Vehicles were initially maintained and repaired on contract by [Babcock International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babcock_International); from November 2023, the contract for 3,700 of the Met's 5,200 vehicles was undertaken by Rivus Fleet Solutions for a ten-year period.[^cite_note-rivuswin-72] Rivus fell into [administration](8824684.md) on 21 June 2024, with a deal being agreed by the Metropolitan Police to buy back its maintenance operations, saving 165 jobs.[^cite_note-73] In 2026 the Met selected BetterFleet to provide charge management software for its EV fleet.[^cite_note-74]



A restored [Rover SD1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rover_SD1) traffic car in the Metropolitan Police's '[jam sandwich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jam_sandwich_\(police_car\))' livery first introduced in 1978

By 2012, the Met was marking all new marked vehicles with [Battenburg markings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battenburg_markings), a highly reflective material on the side of the vehicles, chequered blue and yellow green for the police, and in other colours for other services.[^cite_note-75] The old livery was an orange stripe through the vehicle, with the force's logo, known colloquially as the "[jam sandwich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jam_sandwich_\(police_car\))", which was first introduced in 1978 with the delivery of high-performance [Rover SD1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rover_SD1) traffic cars.[^cite_note-76] Originally, marked vehicles were finished in base white paint; this was changed to silver from 2002 to help improve a vehicle's resale value when it was retired from police use.[^cite_note-77]

The [National Police Air Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Police_Air_Service) provides helicopter support to the Met.

A [marine policing unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Police_Marine_Policing_Unit) operates 22 vessels from its base in [Wapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wapping).

### Stations

In addition to the headquarters at New Scotland Yard, there are many [police stations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_station) in London.[^cite_note-99] These range from large borough headquarters staffed around the clock every day to smaller stations, which may be open to the public only during normal business hours, or on certain days of the week. In 2017, there were 73 working front counters open to the public in London.[^cite_note-100] Most police stations can easily be identified from one or more blue lamps located outside the entrance, which were introduced in 1861.

The oldest Metropolitan police station, which opened in [Bow Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bow_Street) in 1881, closed in 1992 and the adjoining [Bow Street Magistrates' Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bow_Street_Magistrates'_Court) heard its last case on 14 July 2006.[^cite_note-101] One of the oldest operational police stations in London is in [Wapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wapping), which opened in 1908. It is the headquarters of the [marine policing unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Police_Marine_Policing_Unit) (formerly known as Thames Division), which is responsible for policing the [River Thames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Thames). It also houses a mortuary and the River Police Museum. [Paddington Green Police Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paddington_Green_Police_Station), which is no longer operational, received much publicity for its housing of terrorism suspects in an underground complex prior to its closure in 2017.

In 2004, there was a call from the [Institute for Public Policy Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Public_Policy_Research) for more imaginative planning of police stations to aid in improving relations between police forces and the wider community.[^cite_note-102]

## Statistics

### Outcome and detection rates

See also: [Crime statistics in the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime_statistics_in_the_United_Kingdom)

From 2015 the MPS has published monthly 'positive outcome' rates,[^cite_note-103] a somewhat wider definition from the previous 'sanction detection' rates for offenders receiving some formal sanction such as being charged or summonsed.[^cite_note-104] These include charges, but also outcomes such as Penalty Notices for Disorder (PNDs), warnings for cannabis possession, offences that are asked to be taken into consideration by a court (TICs), cautions, warnings, and reprimands.[^cite_note-105][^cite_note-106]

Nationally, detection rates have been falling as police-reported crime has been rising.[^cite_note-\:02-107] The following table shows the percentage 'sanction detection' rates for the Metropolitan Police by offence group for 2022/2023:[^cite_note-w3842-108]

| Total | Violence against the person | Sexual offences | Robbery | Burglary | Offences against vehicles | Other theft offences | Fraud and forgery | Criminal damage | Drug offences | Other offences |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 7.3 | 8.1 | 9 | 7.3 | 6.3 | 0.9 | 1.9 | N/A | 5.7 | 38 | 23.9 |

The corresponding figures for 2010–2011 were far higher, with an average total detection rate of 24%.[^cite_note-109] However, largely owing to the [history of changes in the way the UK police count offenses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime_statistics_in_the_United_Kingdom), crime numbers have seen significant increases since 1998, and particularly from 2014 onward. The same volume of detections is therefore divided by a much larger number of total recorded offences. While this does not fully explain the drop in detection rates, it is thought to have contributed to it. Other reasons include lack of police resources[^cite_note-110] and that the crimes the police record disproportionately affect those offences, which (in law, if not by the public) are treated as the least serious.[^cite_note-\:02-107]

In 2017, the MPS "screened out" 34,164 crimes on the day they were reported, and did not investigate them further. This compares to 13,019 the previous year. 18,093 crimes were closed in 24 hours during the first 5 months of 2018 making it likely that the 2017 total would be exceeded. Crimes not being investigated include sexual assaults and arson, burglaries, thefts and assaults. Some critics believe this shows the effect of [austerity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_government_austerity_programme#Effects) on the force's ability to carry out its responsibilities.[^cite_note-111]

In 2024, it was reported that there was a zero-detection rate for crimes within 166 neighborhood areas under the responsibility of the Metropolitan Police Service.[^cite_note-a4792-112][^cite_note-p2902-113] A report by [His Majesty's Inspectorate of Constabulary and Fire & Rescue Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/His_Majesty's_Inspectorate_of_Constabulary_and_Fire_&_Rescue_Services) said that the Met was rated inadequate or failing regarding crime investigations and managing offenders. It was rated as requiring improvement in five other areas, and as adequate in one.[^cite_note-114]

## Controversies

See also: [History of the Metropolitan Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Metropolitan_Police), [Institutional racism in the Metropolitan Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutional_racism_in_the_Metropolitan_Police), and [Institutional sexism in the Metropolitan Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutional_sexism_in_the_Metropolitan_Police)

The Met was controversial even before its formation in 1829. Since the 1970s, such controversies have often centred on [institutional racism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutional_racism_in_the_Metropolitan_Police) and [institutional sexism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutional_sexism_in_the_Metropolitan_Police) within the organisation, along with the right to protest,[^cite_note-116] failures in investigations,[^cite_note-118] and officers belonging to proscribed organisations.[^cite_note-120]

In 1993, the investigation into the racially motivated [murder of Stephen Lawrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murder_of_Stephen_Lawrence) was heavily criticised. The subsequent Macpherson Inquiry (1999) found the Metropolitan Police to be "institutionally racist," a landmark finding that mandated significant police reform efforts across the UK.

The Met faced widespread criticism over the actions of officers during the [2009 G20 London summit protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_G20_London_summit_protests), including the death of demonstrator [Ian Tomlinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_of_Ian_Tomlinson) after being struck by an officer. This led to public criticism over excessive force and officers concealing their identification numbers.

In 2011, it was disclosed that a number of [undercover officers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_undercover_policing_relationships_scandal) had entered into intimate relationships with members of targeted groups, and even proposed marriage or fathered childred with protesters who were unaware their partner was a police officer in a role as part of their official duties.[^cite_note-guardian-20120120-121][^cite_note-guardian-20201109-122] The groups affected were predominantly left-wing and progressive.[^cite_note-123]

In 2020, two officers assigned to guard the scene of the murders of [Bibaa Henry and Nicole Smallman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murders_of_Bibaa_Henry_and_Nicole_Smallman) were jailed for taking and sharing photographs of the deceased women on [WhatsApp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WhatsApp), referring to them as "dead birds."[^cite_note-124]

In 2021 a review into the handling of the investigation of the [murder of Daniel Morgan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murder_of_Daniel_Morgan) found that the Met Police was "institutionally corrupt".&#x20;

In 2023, a report found racism, misogyny, homophobia and corruption within the organisation after the [abduction](169376.md) of Sarah Everard by Wayne Couzens, a police constable. 12% of female Met employees had been harassed or attacked, with 33% experiencing sexism. Other incidents included a Muslim officer who had bacon stuffed into his boots and a Sikh officer whose beard was cut. The report also found that officers of minority ethnic backgrounds were more likely to be disciplined and leave the force.[^cite_note-125] The report was criticised by the charity Galop for not investigating transphobia.[^cite_note-126] Five former officers admitted in court in 2023 to sending racist messages, the targets of which included the [Duchess of Sussex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meghan,_Duchess_of_Sussex), and a sixth was convicted after a trial. All six were given [suspended jail sentences](711640.md).[^cite_note-127][^cite_note-128]

The Metropolitan Police was criticised in 2024 by former Prime Minister [Boris Johnson](19065069.md) for political bias in its efforts to support an investigation into alleged [Israeli war crimes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israeli_war_crimes) following Israel's response to terror attacks committed in 2023 by [Hamas on 7 October](74998317.md). The Met responded by saying, under the terms of the [1998 Rome Statute](68762.md), it was obliged to support any investigations opened by the [International Criminal Court](14880.md) (ICC) that could involve British subjects.[^cite_note-129] In 2024, [His Majesty's Inspectorate of Constabulary and Fire & Rescue Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/His_Majesty's_Inspectorate_of_Constabulary_and_Fire_&_Rescue_Services) issued a report regarding the Met's handling of pro-Palestinian protests, saying the Met had been largely impartial. The report criticised the then Prime Minister [Rishi Sunak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rishi_Sunak) and the then Home Secretary [Suella Braverman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suella_Braverman) for suggesting otherwise.[^cite_note-130]

In April 2024, the Met settled a claim for [misfeasance in a public office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misfeasance_in_public_office) and [false imprisonment](627536.md) by agreeing to pay a five-figure sum as damages to a French publisher who had been arrested and detained under anti-terrorism laws while he was on his way to a book fair in London.[^cite_note-131]

In November 2024, it was alleged that the Metropolitan Police was told about [allegations of sexual assault against Mohamed Al-Fayed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohamed_Al-Fayed#Sexual_misconduct_allegations), the late businessman and owner of Harrods, ten years earlier than it had acknowledged.[^cite_note-auto1-132] The Met claimed that it first received such allegations in 2005, but the BBC was told that it had been informed by victims in 1995, but had not acted on the information.[^cite_note-133] On 8 November, the [Independent Office for Police Conduct](56063076.md) announced that it would be investigating the Met over its handling of allegations of sexual misconduct related to the case.[^cite_note-134]

In December 2025, the Metropolitan Police and [Greater Manchester Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Manchester_Police) announced tougher enforcement measures following reports that [antisemitic incidents in Britain](39358903.md) had increased more than fourfold since October 2023.[^cite_note-BBCQuadruple-135][^cite_note-StandardHateCrime-136] The announcement followed heightened security concerns after fatal attacks on Jewish communities in Britain and abroad, including a [Synagogue](48559.md) attack in [Manchester](20206.md) and a mass shooting at a [Hanukkah](7388.md) event in [Bondi Beach](368567.md), [Sydney](27862.md), [Australia](4689264.md).[^cite_note-ReutersPolice-137] In a joint statement, police warned that pro-Palestinian chants such as "[Intifada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intifada)" and "globalise the intifada" carried "real-world consequences", and confirmed they would "act decisively and make arrests" where offences were identified.[^cite_note-GuardianIntifada-138]

### Freemasonry

There have long been rumours of undue influence of [Freemasonry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freemasonry) within the Metropolitan Police. In 2025, Freemasons' organizations took legal action to secure an injunction against a policy that would force Freemasons within the Metropolitan Police to reveal their affiliation.[^cite_note-139][^cite_note-140] As of January 2026, although the injunction has yet to be ruled on, more than 300 officers had already declared themselves to be Freemasons.[^cite_note-141]
