**Lead** ([/lɛd/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English) [//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/f/f6/LL-Q1860\_%28eng%29-Naomi_Persephone_Amethyst\_%28NaomiAmethyst%29-lead.wav/LL-Q1860\_%28eng%29-Naomi_Persephone_Amethyst\_%28NaomiAmethyst%29-lead.wav.mp3](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/f/f6/LL-Q1860_%28eng%29-Naomi_Persephone_Amethyst_%28NaomiAmethyst%29-lead.wav/LL-Q1860_%28eng%29-Naomi_Persephone_Amethyst_%28NaomiAmethyst%29-lead.wav.mp3)<sup>ⓘ</sup>) is a [chemical element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_element) with the [symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_symbol) **Pb** (from [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) *plumbum*) and [atomic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_number) 82. It is a [heavy metal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_metal_\(elements\)), [denser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density) than most common materials. Lead is [soft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohs_scale), [malleable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ductility), and has a relatively low [melting point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melting_point). When freshly cut or melted, it appears shiny silvery with a bluish tint, but [tarnishes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarnish) to dull gray on exposure to air. Lead has the highest atomic number of any [stable element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stable_nuclide), and three of its [isotopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotope) are endpoints of major nuclear [decay chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decay_chain) of heavier elements.

Lead is a relatively un-reactive [post-transition metal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-transition_metal). Its weak metallic character is shown by its [amphoteric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphoterism) behavior: lead and [lead oxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_oxide) react with both [acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid) and [bases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_\(chemistry\)), and it tends to form [covalent bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covalent_bond). [Lead compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_compounds) usually occur in the +2 [oxidation state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidation_state) rather than the +4 state common in lighter members of the [carbon group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_group), with exceptions mostly limited to [organolead compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organolead_chemistry). Like the lighter members of the group, lead can [bond with itself](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catenation), forming chains and polyhedral structures.

Since lead is easily extracted from its [ores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ore), prehistoric people in the Near East [were aware of it](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metals_of_antiquity). [Galena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galena) is a principal ore of lead which often bears [silver](27119.md). Interest in silver helped initiate widespread extraction and use of lead in [ancient Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome). Lead production declined after the [fall of Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_Western_Roman_Empire) and did not reach comparable levels until the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution). Lead played a crucial role in the development of the [printing press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing_press), as [movable type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Movable_type) could be relatively easily cast from lead alloys.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETheodore_Low_De_Vinne18999–36-13] In 2022, the annual global production of lead was about twelve million tonnes, about two thirds of which was from recycling. Lead's high density, low melting point, [ductility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ductility) and relative inertness to [oxidation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox) make it useful. These properties, combined with its relative abundance and low cost, resulted in its extensive use in [construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Construction), [plumbing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plumbing), [batteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead%E2%80%93acid_battery), [bullets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bullet), shots ([pellets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shot_\(pellet\))), [weights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass), [solders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solder), [pewter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pewter), [fusible alloys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fusible_alloy), [lead paints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_paint), [leaded gasoline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetraethyllead), and [radiation shielding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_shielding).

Lead is a [neurotoxin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurotoxin) that accumulates in soft tissues and bones. It damages the [nervous system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nervous_system), interferes with biological [enzymes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzyme), and can cause [neurological disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurological_disorder) ranging from behavioral problems to brain damage. It also affects [cardiovascular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circulatory_system) and [renal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renal_System) systems. [Lead's toxicity was noted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_lead_poisoning_theory) by Ancient Greek and Roman writers, but became widely recognized in Europe in the late 19th century.

## Physical properties

### Atomic

A lead [atom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom) has 82 [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron), with the [electron configuration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_configuration) \[[Xe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenon)]4f<sup>14</sup>5d<sup>10</sup>6s<sup>2</sup>6p<sup>2</sup>. The combined first and second [ionization energies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionization_energy)—the total energy required to remove the two 6p electrons—are similar to those of [tin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tin), lead's immediate neighbor above in the [carbon group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_group). This is unusual, as ionization energies typically decrease down a group due to the outer electrons being farther from the [nucleus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus) and more [shielded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shielding_effect) by inner orbitals. However, the sum of the first four ionization energies of lead is higher than that of tin,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELide200510–179-14] contrary to [periodic trends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_trends). This anomaly is explained by [relativistic effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_quantum_chemistry), which become significant in heavier atoms.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPyykkö1988563–594-15] These effects contract the s and p orbitals, giving lead's 6s electrons greater binding energies than its 5s electrons.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEClaudioGodwinMagyar20021–144-16] This leads to the [inert-pair effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inert-pair_effect), where the 6s electrons are less likely to participate in bonding. The result is stabilization of the +2 [oxidation state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidation_state) and unusually long distances between nearest atoms in [crystalline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal) lead.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENorman199636-17]

Lighter carbon-group [congeners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congener_\(chemistry\)) of lead form stable or metastable [allotropes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allotropy) with the tetrahedrally coordinated, [covalently bonded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covalent_bond) [diamond cubic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_cubic) structure. In these elements, the [s-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_orbital) and p-orbital energy levels are close enough to allow mixing into four [hybrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_hybridisation) sp<sup>3</sup> orbitals. In lead, however, the inert pair effect increases the separation between s- and p-orbitals so much that the energy gain from hybridization is insufficient to overcome this gap.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998226–227,_374-18] Instead of a diamond cubic arrangement, lead forms [metallic bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metallic_bonding) in which only the p-electrons are delocalized and shared among Pb<sup>2+</sup> ions. Consequently, lead adopts a [face-centered cubic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_crystal_system) structure,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEChristensen2002867-19] similar to the[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESlater1964-20] [divalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_\(chemistry\)) metals [calcium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium) and [strontium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strontium).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEConsidineConsidine2013501,_2970-21][^cite_note-23][^cite_note-26][^cite_note-28]

### Bulk

Pure lead has a bright, shiny gray appearance with a faint blue tint.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998372-29] It tarnishes when exposed to moist air, developing a dull surface whose color depends on environmental conditions. Lead is characterized by high [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density), malleability, ductility, and resistance to [corrosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corrosion) due to [passivation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passivation_\(chemistry\)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998372–373-30]



Lead fishing weights

Its close-packed face-centered cubic structure and high atomic mass give lead a density[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEThorntonRautiuBrush20016-31] of 11.34 g/cm<sup>3</sup>, greater than that of common metals such as [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron) (7.87 g/cm<sup>3</sup>), [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper) (8.93 g/cm<sup>3</sup>), and [zinc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinc) (7.14 g/cm<sup>3</sup>).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELide200512–35,_12–40-32] This high density is the origin of the idiom *to go over like a lead balloon*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrenner2003396-33][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJones201442-34][^cite_note-35] Some rarer metals are denser: [tungsten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tungsten) and [gold](12240.md) are both 19.3 g/cm<sup>3</sup>, while [osmium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osmium)—the densest known metal—has a density of 22.59 g/cm<sup>3</sup>, nearly twice that of lead.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELide20054–13,_4–21,_4–33-36]

Lead is soft, with a [Mohs hardness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohs_scale) of 1.5, and can be scratched with a fingernail.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEVogelAchilles20138-37] It is very malleable and moderately ductile.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAnderson1869341–343-38][^cite_note-39] Its [bulk modulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulk_modulus)—a measure of resistance to compression—is 45.8 [GPa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal_\(unit\)), compared with 75.2 GPa for [aluminium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium), 137.8 GPa for copper, and 160–169 GPa for [mild steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_steel).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGaleTotemeier200315–2–15–3-40] Lead's [tensile strength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultimate_tensile_strength) is low, at 12–17 MPa (around six times lower than aluminium, ten times lower than copper, and fifteen times lower than mild steel). Its strength can be increased by alloying with small amounts of copper or [antimony](898.md).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEThorntonRautiuBrush20018-41]

A disk of metal

A sample of lead solidified from the molten state

Lead melts at 327.5 °C (621.5 °F),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELide200512-219-42] a relatively low melting point compared to most metals,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEThorntonRautiuBrush20016-31][^cite_note-44] and has a [boiling point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boiling_point) of 1749 °C (3180 °F),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELide200512-219-42] the lowest among the carbon-group elements. Its [electrical resistivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_resistivity_and_conductivity) at 20 °C is 192 [nanoohm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohm)-meters, almost an [order of magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_magnitude) higher than that of good conductors (copper: 15.43 nΩ·m; gold: 20.51 nΩ·m; aluminium: 24.15 nΩ·m).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELide200512-45-45] Lead becomes a [superconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconductivity) below 7.19 [K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelvin),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBlakemore1985272-46] which is the highest [critical temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconductivity#phase_transition) among [type-I superconductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type-I_superconductor) and the third highest among the elemental superconductors.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWebbMarsiglioHirsch2015-47]

### Isotopes

Main article: [Isotopes of lead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotopes_of_lead)

Natural lead consists of four stable [isotopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotope) with mass numbers 204, 206, 207, and 208,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEIAEA_-_Nuclear_Data_Section2017-48] along with traces of six short-lived radioisotopes with mass numbers 209–214. The relatively high number of isotopes is consistent with lead's even [atomic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_number).[^cite_note-49] Lead has a [magic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_number_\(physics\)) of protons (82), making its nucleus especially stable according to the [nuclear shell model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_shell_model).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStone1997-50] Lead-208 also has 126 neutrons, another magic number, which may account for its exceptional stability.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStone1997-50]

With its high atomic number, lead is the heaviest element whose natural isotopes are considered stable; lead-208 is the heaviest stable nucleus known. This distinction previously belonged to [bismuth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bismuth) (atomic number 83) until its sole [primordial isotope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primordial_nuclide), bismuth-209, was found in 2003 to decay extremely slowly.[^cite_note-53] Although the four stable isotopes of lead could theoretically undergo [alpha decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_decay) to [mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_\(element\)) isotopes with an energy release, no such decay has been observed; their predicted half-lives range from 10<sup>35</sup> to 10<sup>189</sup> years,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBeemanBelliniCardaniCasali2013-54] at least 10<sup>25</sup> times the current age of the universe.

A piece of a gray meteorite on a pedestal

The Holsinger meteorite, the largest piece of the [Canyon Diablo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canyon_Diablo_\(meteorite\)) meteorite. [Uranium–lead dating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium%E2%80%93lead_dating) and [lead–lead dating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead%E2%80%93lead_dating) on this meteorite allowed refinement of the [age of the Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Earth) to 4.55 billion ± 70 million years.

Three of lead's stable isotopes—lead-206, lead-207, and lead-208—are the end products of the three major natural [decay chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decay_chain): the [uranium chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_chain) (from [uranium-238](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-238)), the [actinium chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actinium_chain) [(from uranium-235](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-235)), and the [thorium chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorium_chain) [(from thorium-232)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorium-232), respectively.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERadioactive_Decay_Series2012-55][^cite_note-FOOTNOTECommittee_on_Evaluation_of_EPA_Guidelines_for_Exposure_to_Naturally_Occurring_Radioactive_MaterialsCommission_on_Life_SciencesDivision_on_Earth_and_Life_StudiesNational_Research_Council1999-56] The isotopic composition of a rock sample depends on the presence of these parent isotopes; for example, lead-208 abundance can vary from about 52% in ordinary samples to as much as 90% in thorium ores.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESmirnovBorisevichSulaberidze2012-57] For this reason, the standard atomic weight of lead is reported to only one decimal place.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998368-58] Over time, the ratios of these isotopes to lead-204 increase as they are produced by radioactive decay. These variations allow for [lead–lead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead%E2%80%93lead_dating) and [uranium–lead dating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium%E2%80%93lead_dating).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELevin200940–41-59] Lead-207 exhibits [nuclear magnetic resonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_magnetic_resonance), a property used to study its compounds in both solution and solid states,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWebb2000115-60][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWrackmeyerHorchler1990-61] including in biological systems such as the human body.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECangelosiPecoraro2015-62]

## Chemistry

A flame with a small metal rod penetrating it; the flame near the rod is pale blue.

[Flame test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flame_test): lead colors flame pale blue

When exposed to moist air, bulk lead develops a protective surface layer of variable composition. [Lead(II) carbonate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_carbonate) is a common constituent,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEThürmerWilliamsReutt-Robey20022033–2035-63][^cite_note-FOOTNOTETétreaultSiroisStamatopoulou199817–32-64][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEThorntonRautiuBrush200110–11-65] and in urban or maritime environments, lead(II) [sulfate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead\(II\)_sulfate) or lead(II) [chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead\(II\)_chloride) may also be present.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998373-66] This layer renders bulk lead effectively inert under atmospheric conditions.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998373-66] In contrast, finely powdered lead, like many metals, is [pyrophoric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrophoricity)[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBretherick20161442-67] and burns with a bluish-white flame.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarbisonBourgeoisJohnson2015132-68]

Lead reacts with [fluorine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorine) at room temperature to form [lead(II) fluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead\(II\)_fluoride). Its reaction with [chlorine](5667.md) is similar but requires heating, as the resulting chloride layer reduces further reactivity.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998373-66] Molten lead combines with the [chalcogens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcogen) to produce lead(II) chalcogenides.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998374-69]

The metal resists attack by [sulfuric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfuric_acid) and [phosphoric acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphoric_acid) but not by [hydrochloric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrochloric_acid) or [nitric acids](21655.md); the difference arises from the insolubility and subsequent passivation of certain lead salts.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEThorntonRautiuBrush200111–12-70] Organic acids, such as [acetic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetic_acid), dissolve lead in the presence of oxygen.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998373-66] Concentrated [alkalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkali) can also dissolve lead, producing [plumbites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plumbite).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPolyanskiy198620-71]

### Inorganic compounds

See also: [Lead compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_compounds)

Lead exhibits two principal oxidation states: +4 and +2. While the [tetravalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_\(chemistry\)) state is characteristic of the carbon group, the divalent state is rare for [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon) and [silicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon), less common for germanium, significant but not dominant for tin, and the most prevalent for lead.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998373-66] This predominance is linked to [relativistic effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_quantum_chemistry)—specifically the [inert pair effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inert-pair_effect)—which occurs when there is a large [electronegativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronegativity) difference between lead and anions such as [oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxide), [halide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halide), or [nitride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitride). In such cases, lead develops a pronounced partial positive charge, causing a stronger contraction of the 6s orbital compared to the 6p orbital and rendering it relatively unreactive in ionic compounds. The inert pair effect is less pronounced in compounds where lead forms covalent bonds with elements of similar electronegativity, such as carbon in organolead compounds. In these, the 6s and 6p orbitals remain comparable in size, and sp<sup>3</sup> hybridization remains energetically favorable, making lead predominantly tetravalent in such cases.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKaupp20149–10-72]

The electronegativity values further reflect this behavior: lead(II) has a value of 1.87, and lead(IV) has 2.33. This represents a reversal in the general trend of increasing stability of the +4 oxidation state down the carbon group; by comparison, tin has electronegativities of 1.80 (+2 state) and 1.96 (+4 state).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDieterWatson2009509-73]

#### Lead(II)

Cream powder

[Lead(II) oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead\(II\)_oxide)

Lead(II) compounds are characteristic of the inorganic chemistry of lead. Even strong [oxidizing agents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidizing_agent) like fluorine and chlorine react with lead to give only [PbF<sub>2</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead\(II\)_fluoride) and [PbCl<sub>2</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead\(II\)_chloride).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998373-66] Lead(II) ions are usually colorless in solution,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHunt2014215-74] and partially hydrolyze to form Pb(OH)<sup>+</sup> and finally \[Pb<sub>4</sub>(OH)<sub>4</sub>]<sup>4+</sup> (in which the [hydroxyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroxy_group) ions act as [bridging ligands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridging_ligand)),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKing199543–63-75][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBunkerCasey201689-76] but are not [reducing agents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reducing_agent) as tin(II) ions are. [Techniques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qualitative_inorganic_analysis) for identifying the presence of the Pb<sup>2+</sup> ion in water generally rely on the precipitation of lead(II) chloride using dilute hydrochloric acid. As the chloride salt is sparingly soluble in water, in very dilute solutions the precipitation of [lead(II) sulfide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead\(II\)_sulfide) is instead achieved by bubbling [hydrogen sulfide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_sulfide) through the solution.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWhittenGaileyDavid1996904–905-77]

[Lead monoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead\(II\)_oxide) exists in two [polymorphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_polymorphism), [litharge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Litharge) α-PbO (red) and [massicot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massicot) β-PbO (yellow), the latter being stable only above around 488 °C. Litharge is the most commonly used inorganic compound of lead.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998384-78] There is no lead(II) hydroxide; increasing the pH of solutions of lead(II) salts leads to hydrolysis and condensation.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998387-79] Lead commonly reacts with heavier chalcogens. [Lead sulfide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_sulfide) is a [semiconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor), a [photoconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photoconductivity), and an extremely sensitive [infrared radiation detector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_detector). The other two chalcogenides, [lead selenide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_selenide) and [lead telluride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_telluride), are likewise photoconducting. They are unusual in that their color becomes lighter going down the group.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998389-80]

Alternating dark gray and red balls connected by dark gray-red cylinders

&#x20;Lead and [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen) in a tetragonal [unit cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_structure#Unit_cell) of [lead(II,IV) oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead\(II,IV\)_oxide)

Lead dihalides are well-characterized; this includes the diastatide[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEZuckermanHagen1989426-81] and mixed halides, such as PbFCl. The relative insolubility of the latter forms a useful basis for the [gravimetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravimetric_analysis) determination of fluorine. The difluoride was the first solid [ionically conducting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionic_conductivity_\(solid_state\)) compound to be discovered (in 1834, by [Michael Faraday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Faraday)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFunke2013-82] The other dihalides decompose on exposure to ultraviolet or visible light, especially [the diiodide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead\(II\)_iodide).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998382-83] Many lead(II) [pseudohalides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudohalogen) are known, such as the cyanide, cyanate, and [thiocyanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead\(II\)_thiocyanate).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998389-80][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBhararaAtwood20064-84] Lead(II) forms an extensive variety of halide [coordination complexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordination_complex), such as \[PbCl<sub>4</sub>]<sup>2−</sup>, \[PbCl<sub>6</sub>]<sup>4−</sup>, and the \[Pb<sub>2</sub>Cl<sub>9</sub>]<sub>*n*</sub><sup>5*n*−</sup> chain anion.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998382-83]

[Lead(II) sulfate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead\(II\)_sulfate) is insoluble in water, like the sulfates of other heavy divalent [cations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion). [Lead(II) nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead\(II\)_nitrate) and [lead(II) acetate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead\(II\)_acetate) are very soluble, and this is exploited in the synthesis of other lead compounds.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998388-85]

#### Lead(IV)

Few inorganic lead(IV) compounds are known. They are only formed in highly oxidizing solutions and do not normally exist under standard conditions.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEToxicological_Profile_for_Lead2007277-86] Lead(II) oxide gives a mixed oxide on further oxidation, Pb<sub>3</sub>O<sub>4</sub>. It is described as [lead(II,IV) oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead\(II,IV\)_oxide), or structurally 2PbO·PbO<sub>2</sub>, and is the best-known mixed valence lead compound. [Lead dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_dioxide) is a strong oxidizing agent, capable of oxidizing hydrochloric acid to chlorine gas.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDownsAdams20171128-87] This is because the expected PbCl<sub>4</sub> that would be produced is unstable and spontaneously decomposes to PbCl<sub>2</sub> and Cl<sub>2</sub>.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBrescia2012234-88] Analogously to [lead monoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead\(II\)_oxide), lead dioxide is capable of forming [plumbate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plumbate) anions. [Lead disulfide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead\(IV\)_sulfide)[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMacintyre19923775-89] and lead diselenide[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESilverman19662067–2069-90] are only stable at high pressures. [Lead tetrafluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_tetrafluoride), a yellow crystalline powder, is stable, but less so than the [difluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead\(II\)_fluoride). [Lead tetrachloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead\(IV\)_chloride) (a yellow oil) decomposes at room temperature, lead tetrabromide is less stable still, and the existence of lead tetraiodide is questionable.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998381-91]

Some lead compounds exist in formal oxidation states other than +4 or +2. Lead(III) may be obtained, as an intermediate between lead(II) and lead(IV), in larger organolead complexes; this oxidation state is not stable, as both the lead(III) ion and the larger complexes containing it are [radicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_\(chemistry\)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBeckerFörsterFranzenHartrath20089965–9978-93][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMosseriHengleinJanata19902722–2726-94][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKonuChivers2011391–392-95] The same applies for lead(I), which can be found in such radical species.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHadlington201759-96]

Numerous mixed lead(II,IV) oxides are known. When PbO<sub>2</sub> is heated in air, it becomes Pb<sub>12</sub>O<sub>19</sub> at 293 °C, Pb<sub>12</sub>O<sub>17</sub> at 351 °C, Pb<sub>3</sub>O<sub>4</sub> at 374 °C, and finally PbO at 605 °C. A further [sesquioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sesquioxide), Pb<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>, can be obtained at high pressure, along with several non-stoichiometric phases. Many of them show defective [fluorite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorite) structures in which some oxygen atoms are replaced by vacancies: PbO can be considered as having such a structure, with every alternate layer of oxygen atoms absent.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998384–386-97]

Negative oxidation states can occur as [Zintl phases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zintl_phase), as either free lead anions, as in Ba<sub>2</sub>Pb, with lead formally being lead(−IV),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERöhr2017-98] or in oxygen-sensitive ring-shaped or polyhedral cluster ions such as the [trigonal bipyramidal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonal_bipyramidal_molecular_geometry) Pb<sub>5</sub><sup>2−</sup> ion, where two lead atoms are lead(−I) and three are lead(0).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAlsfasser2007261–263-99] In such anions, each atom is at a polyhedral vertex and contributes two electrons to each covalent bond along an edge from their sp<sup>3</sup> hybrid orbitals, the other two being an external [lone pair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lone_pair).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKing199543–63-75] They may be made in [liquid ammonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonia) via the reduction of lead by [sodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998393-100]

### Organolead

Main article: [Organolead compound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organolead_compound)

Lead can form [multiply-bonded chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catenation), a property it shares with its lighter [homologs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homologous_series) in the carbon group. Its capacity to do so is much less because the Pb–Pb [bond energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bond_energy) is over three and a half times lower than that of the [C–C bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C%E2%80%93C_bond).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998374-69] With itself, lead can build metal–metal bonds of an order up to three.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStabenowSaakWeidenbruch2003-101] With carbon, lead forms organolead compounds similar to, but generally less stable than, typical organic compounds[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPolyanskiy198643-102] (due to the Pb–C bond being rather weak).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKing199543–63-75] This makes the [organometallic chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organometallic_chemistry) of lead far less wide-ranging than that of tin.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998404-103] Lead predominantly forms organolead(IV) compounds, even when starting with inorganic lead(II) reactants; very few organolead(II) compounds are known. The most well-characterized exceptions are Pb\[CH(SiMe<sub>3</sub>)<sub>2</sub>]<sub>2</sub> and [plumbocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plumbocene).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998404-103]

The lead analog of the simplest [organic compound](22203.md), [methane](18582230.md), is [plumbane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plumbane). Plumbane may be obtained in a reaction between metallic lead and atomic hydrogen.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWibergWibergHolleman2001918-104] Two simple derivatives, [tetramethyllead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetramethyllead) and [tetraethyllead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetraethyllead), are the best-known [organolead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organolead_chemistry) compounds. These compounds are relatively stable: tetraethyllead only starts to decompose if heated[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEToxicological_Profile_for_Lead2007287-105] or if exposed to sunlight or ultraviolet light.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPolyanskiy198644-106][^cite_note-107] With sodium metal, lead readily forms an equimolar alloy that reacts with [alkyl halides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haloalkane) to form [organometallic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organometallic_chemistry) compounds such as tetraethyllead.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWindholz1976-108] The oxidizing nature of many organolead compounds is usefully exploited: [lead tetraacetate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead\(IV\)_acetate) is an important laboratory reagent for oxidation in organic synthesis.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEZýka1966569-109] Tetraethyllead, once added to automotive gasoline, was produced in larger quantities than any other organometallic compound,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGreenwoodEarnshaw1998404-103] and is still widely used in [fuel for small aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avgas).[^cite_note-110] Other organolead compounds are less chemically stable.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPolyanskiy198643-102] For many organic compounds, a lead analog does not exist.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWibergWibergHolleman2001918-104]

### In space

Lead's per-particle abundance in the [Solar System](26903.md) is 0.121 [ppb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parts-per_notation) (parts per billion).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELodders20031222–1223-111][^cite_note-112] This figure is two and a half times higher than that of [platinum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platinum), eight times more than [mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_\(element\)), and seventeen times more than [gold](12240.md).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELodders20031222–1223-111] The amount of lead in the [universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe) is slowly increasing[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERoedererKratzFrebelChristlieb20091963–1980-113] as most heavier atoms (all of which are unstable) gradually decay to lead.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELochnerRohrbachCochrane200512-114] The abundance of lead in the Solar System since its formation 4.5 billion years ago has increased by about 0.75%.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELodders20031224-115] The Solar System abundances table shows that lead, despite its relatively high atomic number, is more prevalent than most other elements with atomic numbers greater than 40.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELodders20031222–1223-111]

Primordial lead—which comprises the isotopes lead-204, lead-206, lead-207, and lead-208—was mostly created as a result of repetitive neutron capture processes occurring in stars. The two main modes of capture are the [s-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-process) and [r-processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R-process).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBurbidgeBurbidgeFowlerHoyle1957608–615-116]

In the s-process (s is for "slow"), captures are separated by years or decades, allowing less stable nuclei to undergo [beta decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_decay).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBurbidgeBurbidgeFowlerHoyle1957551-117] A stable thallium-203 nucleus can capture a neutron and become thallium-204; this undergoes beta decay to give stable lead-204; on capturing another neutron, it becomes lead-205, which has a half-life of around 17 million years. Further captures result in lead-206, lead-207, and lead-208. On capturing another neutron, lead-208 becomes lead-209, which quickly decays into bismuth-209. On capturing another neutron, bismuth-209 becomes bismuth-210, and this beta decays to polonium-210, which alpha decays to lead-206. The cycle hence ends at lead-206, lead-207, lead-208, and bismuth-209.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBurbidgeBurbidgeFowlerHoyle1957608–609-118]

Uppermost part of the nuclide chart, with only practically stable isotopes and lead-205 shown, and the path of the s-process overlaid on it as well that of the cycle on lead, bismuth, and polonium

Chart of the final part of the [s-process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-process), from [mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_\(element\)) to [polonium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polonium). Red lines and circles represent [neutron captures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_capture); blue arrows represent [beta decays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_decay); the green arrow represents an [alpha decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_decay); cyan arrows represent [electron captures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_capture).

In the r-process (r is for "rapid"), captures happen faster than nuclei can decay.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBurbidgeBurbidgeFowlerHoyle1957553-119] This occurs in environments with a high neutron density, such as a [supernova](27680.md) or the merger of two [neutron stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_star). The neutron flux involved may be on the order of 10<sup>22</sup> neutrons per square centimeter per second.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFrebel2015114–115-120] The r-process does not form as much lead as the s-process.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBurbidgeBurbidgeFowlerHoyle1957608–610-121] It tends to stop once neutron-rich nuclei reach 126 neutrons.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBurbidgeBurbidgeFowlerHoyle1957595-122] At this point, the neutrons are arranged in complete shells in the atomic nucleus, and it becomes harder to energetically accommodate more of them.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBurbidgeBurbidgeFowlerHoyle1957596-123] When the neutron flux subsides, these nuclei beta decay into stable isotopes of [osmium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osmium), [iridium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iridium), and [platinum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platinum).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBurbidgeBurbidgeFowlerHoyle1957582,_609–615-124]

### On Earth



Lead ingot from Roman times, Cartagena, Spain

Lead is classified as a [chalcophile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldschmidt_classification#Chalcophile_elements) under the [Goldschmidt classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldschmidt_classification), meaning it is generally found combined with sulfur.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELangmuirBroecker2012183–184-125] It rarely occurs in its [native](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_metal), metallic form.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDavidsonRymanSutherlandMilner20144–5-126] Many lead minerals are relatively light and, over the course of the Earth's history, have remained in the [crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_crust) instead of sinking deeper into the Earth's interior. This accounts for lead's relatively high [crustal abundance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abundance_of_elements_in_Earth's_crust) of 14 ppm; it is the 36th most [abundant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abundances_of_the_elements_\(data_page\)) element in the crust.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2011286,_passim-127][^cite_note-129]

The main lead-bearing mineral is [galena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galena) (PbS), which is mostly found with zinc ores.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDavidsonRymanSutherlandMilner20144-130] Most other lead minerals are related to galena in some way; [boulangerite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boulangerite), Pb<sub>5</sub>Sb<sub>4</sub>S<sub>11</sub>, is a mixed sulfide derived from galena; [anglesite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglesite), PbSO<sub>4</sub>, is a product of galena oxidation; and [cerussite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerussite) or white lead ore, PbCO<sub>3</sub>, is a [decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_decomposition) product of galena. [Arsenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arsenic), [tin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tin), [antimony](898.md), [silver](27119.md), [gold](12240.md), [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper), and [bismuth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bismuth) are common impurities in lead minerals.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDavidsonRymanSutherlandMilner20144-130]

A line chart generally declining towards its right

Lead is a fairly common element in the [Earth's crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_crust) for its high atomic number (82). Most elements of atomic number greater than 40 are less abundant.

World lead resources exceed two billion tons. Significant deposits are located in Australia, China, Ireland, Mexico, Peru, Portugal, Russia, United States. Global reserves—resources that are economically feasible to extract—totaled 88 million tons in 2016, of which [Australia](4689264.md) had 35 million, China 17 million, Russia 6.4 million.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEUnited_States_Geological_Survey201797-131]

Typical background concentrations of lead do not exceed 0.1 μg/m<sup>3</sup> in the atmosphere; 100 mg/kg in soil; 4 mg/kg in vegetation, 5 μg/L in fresh water and seawater.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERieuwerts2015225-132]

## Etymology

The modern English word *lead* is of Germanic origin; it comes from the [Middle English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_English) *leed* and [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English) *lēad* (with the [macron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macron_\(diacritic\)) above the "e" signifying that the vowel sound of that letter is long).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMerriam-Webster-133] The Old English word is derived from the hypothetical reconstructed [Proto-Germanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Germanic_language) \**lauda-* ('lead').[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKroonen2013*lauda--134] According to linguistic theory, this word bore descendants in multiple Germanic languages of exactly the same meaning.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKroonen2013*lauda--134]

There is no consensus on the origin of the Proto-Germanic \**lauda-*. One hypothesis suggests it is derived from [Proto-Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-European_language) \**lAudh-* ('lead'; capitalization of the vowel is equivalent to the macron).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENikolayev2012-135] Another hypothesis suggests it is borrowed from [Proto-Celtic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Celtic_language) \**ɸloud-io-* ('lead'). This word is related to the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) *plumbum*, which gave the element its [chemical symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_symbol) *Pb*. The word \**ɸloud-io-* is thought to be the origin of Proto-Germanic \**bliwa-* (which also means 'lead'), from which stemmed the German *Blei*.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKroonen2013*bliwa-_2-136]

The name of the chemical element is not related to the verb of the same spelling, which is derived from Proto-Germanic \**laidijan-* ('to lead').[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKroonen2013*laidijan--137]

## History

Main article: [History of lead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_lead)

Lead was first smelted in the 7th millennium BC, when lead was widely adopted because of its ease of working and its association with silver ores. It became especially important in the ancient Mediterranean, where Roman production reached unprecedented levels. After the decline and [fall of the Western Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_Western_Roman_Empire), lead use expanded in Asia and later revived in Europe during the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) and the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution). Growing awareness of lead's toxicity in the modern era led to regulatory restrictions, although the metal remains significant in certain industrial applications.

## Production

A line chart of many lines, some longer than other, most generally growing towards its right

Primary production of lead since 1840

As of 2014, production of lead is increasing worldwide due to its use in lead–acid batteries.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETolliday2014-138] There are two major categories of production: primary from mined ores, and secondary from scrap. In 2014, 4.58 million metric tons came from primary production and 5.64 million from secondary production. The top three producers of mined lead concentrate in that year were China, Australia, and United States.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEUnited_States_Geological_Survey201797-131] The top three producers of refined lead were China, United States, and India.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGuberman201642.14–15-139] According to the [Metal Stocks in Society report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal_Stocks_in_Society_report) of 2010, the total amount of lead in use, stockpiled, discarded, or dissipated into the environment, on a global basis, is 8 kg per capita. Much of this is in more developed countries (20–150 kg per capita) rather than less developed ones (1–4 kg per capita).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGraedel2010-140] In 2022, the annual global production of lead was about twelve million tonnes, about two thirds of which was from recycling.[^cite_note-141]

The primary and secondary lead production processes are similar. Some primary production plants now supplement their operations with scrap lead, and this trend is likely to increase in the future. Given adequate techniques, lead obtained via secondary processes is indistinguishable from lead obtained via primary processes. Scrap lead from the building trade is usually fairly clean and is re-melted without the need for smelting, though refining is sometimes needed. Secondary lead production is therefore cheaper, in terms of energy requirements, than is primary production, often by 50% or more.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEThorntonRautiuBrush200156-142]

### Primary

Most lead ores contain a low percentage of lead (rich ores have a typical content of 3–8%) which must be concentrated for extraction.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDavidsonRymanSutherlandMilner20146-143] During initial processing, ores typically undergo crushing, dense-medium separation, [grinding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grinding_\(abrasive_cutting\)), [froth flotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Froth_flotation), [drying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drying). The resulting concentrate, which has a lead content of 30–80% by mass (regularly 50–60%),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDavidsonRymanSutherlandMilner20146-143] is then turned into (impure) lead metal.

There are two main ways of doing this: a two-stage process involving roasting followed by blast furnace extraction, carried out in separate vessels; or a direct process in which the extraction of the concentrate occurs in a single vessel. The latter has become the most common route, though the former is still significant.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDavidsonRymanSutherlandMilner201417-144]

#### Two-stage process

First, the sulfide concentrate is [roasted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roasting_\(metallurgy\)) in air to oxidize the lead sulfide:[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEThorntonRautiuBrush200151-145]

2 PbS(s) + 3 O

2

(g) → 2 PbO(s) + 2 SO

2

(g)↑

As the original concentrate was not pure lead sulfide, roasting yields not only the desired lead(II) oxide, but a mixture of oxides, sulfates, and silicates of lead and of the other metals contained in the ore.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDavidsonRymanSutherlandMilner201411–12-146] This impure lead oxide is reduced in a [coke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coke_\(fuel\))-fired blast furnace to the (again, impure) metal:[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEThorntonRautiuBrush200151–52-147]

2 PbO(s) + C(s) → 2 Pb(s) + CO

2

(g)↑

Impurities are mostly arsenic, antimony, bismuth, zinc, copper, silver, and gold. Typically they are removed in a series of [pyrometallurgical processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrometallurgy). The melt is treated in a [reverberatory furnace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reverberatory_furnace) with air, steam, sulfur, which oxidizes the impurities except for silver, gold, bismuth. Oxidized contaminants float to the [top of the melt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dross) and are skimmed off.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDavidsonRymanSutherlandMilner201425-148][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPrimary_Lead_Refining-149] Metallic silver and gold are removed and recovered economically by means of the [Parkes process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parkes_process), in which zinc is added to lead. Zinc, which is immiscible in lead, dissolves the silver and gold. The zinc solution can be separated from the lead, and the silver and gold retrieved.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPrimary_Lead_Refining-149][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPauling1947-150] De-silvered lead is freed of bismuth by the [Betterton–Kroll process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betterton%E2%80%93Kroll_process), treating it with metallic [calcium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium) and [magnesium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium). The resulting bismuth dross can be skimmed off.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPrimary_Lead_Refining-149]

Alternatively to the pyrometallurgical processes, very pure lead can be obtained by processing smelted lead electrolytically using the [Betts process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betts_electrolytic_process). Anodes of impure lead and cathodes of pure lead are placed in an electrolyte of lead [fluorosilicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexafluorosilicic_acid) (PbSiF<sub>6</sub>). Once electrical potential is applied, impure lead at the anode dissolves and plates onto the cathode, leaving the majority of the impurities in solution.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPrimary_Lead_Refining-149][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDavidsonRymanSutherlandMilner201434-151] This is a high-cost process and thus mostly reserved for refining bullion containing high percentages of impurities.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDavidsonRymanSutherlandMilner201423-152]

#### Direct process

In this process, lead bullion and [slag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slag) is obtained directly from lead concentrates. The lead sulfide concentrate is melted in a furnace and oxidized, forming lead monoxide. Carbon (as coke or [coal gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_gas)[^cite_note-153]) is added to the molten charge along with [fluxing agents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flux_\(metallurgy\)). The lead monoxide is thereby reduced to metallic lead, in the midst of a slag rich in lead monoxide.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDavidsonRymanSutherlandMilner201417-144]

If the input is rich in lead, as much as 80% of the original lead can be obtained as bullion; the remaining 20% forms a slag rich in lead monoxide. For a low-grade feed, all of the lead can be oxidized to a high-lead slag.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDavidsonRymanSutherlandMilner201417-144] Metallic lead is further obtained from the high-lead (25–40%) slags via submerged fuel combustion or injection, reduction assisted by an electric furnace, or a combination of both.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDavidsonRymanSutherlandMilner201417-144]

#### Alternatives

Research on a cleaner, less energy-intensive lead extraction process continues; a major drawback is that either too much lead is lost as waste, or the alternatives result in a high sulfur content in the resulting lead metal. [Hydrometallurgical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrometallurgy) extraction, in which [anodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anode) of impure lead are immersed into an [electrolyte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrolyte) and pure lead is deposited ([electrowound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrowinning)) onto [cathodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathode), is a technique that may have potential, but is not currently economical except in cases where electricity is very cheap.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEThorntonRautiuBrush200152–53-154]

### Secondary

Further information: [Battery recycling § Lead–acid batteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battery_recycling#Lead%E2%80%93acid_batteries)

Smelting, which is an essential part of the primary production, is often skipped during secondary production. It is only performed when metallic lead has undergone significant oxidation.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEThorntonRautiuBrush200156-142] The process is similar to that of primary production in either a [blast furnace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blast_furnace) or a [rotary furnace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotary_kiln), with the essential difference being the greater variability of yields: blast furnaces produce hard lead (10% antimony) while reverberatory and rotary kiln furnaces produce semisoft lead (3–4% antimony).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEUnited_States_Environmental_Protection_Agency20101-155]

The [ISASMELT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISASMELT) process is a more recent smelting method that may act as an extension to primary production; battery paste from spent lead–acid batteries (containing lead sulfate and lead oxides) has its sulfate removed by treating it with alkali, and is then treated in a coal-fueled furnace in the presence of oxygen, which yields impure lead, with antimony the most common impurity.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEThorntonRautiuBrush200157-156] Refining of secondary lead is similar to that of primary lead; some refining processes may be skipped depending on the material recycled and its potential contamination.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEThorntonRautiuBrush200157-156]

Of the sources of lead for recycling, lead–acid batteries are the most important; lead pipe, sheet, and cable sheathing are also significant.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEThorntonRautiuBrush200156-142]

## Applications

Bricks of lead (alloyed with 4% antimony) are used as radiation shielding.

[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStreetAlexander1998181-157]

Contrary to popular belief, pencil leads in wooden pencils have never been made from lead. When the pencil originated as a wrapped graphite writing tool, the particular type of [graphite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphite) used was named *plumbago* (literally, *lead mockup*).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEvans1908133–179-158]

### Elemental form

Lead metal has several useful mechanical properties, including high density, low melting point, ductility, and relative inertness. Many metals are superior to lead in some of these aspects but are generally less common and more difficult to extract from parent ores. Lead's toxicity has led to its phasing out for some uses.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBairdCann2012537–538,_543–547-159]

Lead was used to cover the [ramparts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rampart_\(fortification\)) protecting the ascent to the [Alamut Castle](205007.md) in Persia,[^cite_note-160] which could absorb attacks by [siege engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_engines).

Lead has been used for bullets since their invention in the Middle Ages. It is inexpensive; its low melting point means small arms ammunition and shotgun pellets can be cast with minimal technical equipment; and it is denser than other common metals, which allows for better retention of velocity. It remains the main material for bullets, alloyed with other metals as hardeners.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERamage19808-161] Concerns have been raised that lead bullets used for hunting can damage the environment.[^cite_note-163] [Shotgun](26838.md) [cartridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shotgun_cartridge) used for [waterfowl hunting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterfowl_hunting) must today be lead-free in the [United States](3434750.md),[^cite_note-164] [Canada](5042916.md),[^cite_note-165] and in [Europe](9239.md).[^cite_note-166]

Lead's high density and resistance to corrosion have been exploited in a number of related applications. It is used as [ballast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballast) in sailboat keels; its density allows it to take up a small volume and minimize water resistance, thus counterbalancing the heeling effect of wind on the sails.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEParker2005194–195-167] It is used in [scuba diving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scuba_diving) [weight belts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diving_weighting_system) to counteract the diver's buoyancy.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKrestovnikoffHalls200670-168] In 1993, the base of the [Leaning Tower of Pisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaning_Tower_of_Pisa) was stabilized with 600 tonnes of lead.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEStreetAlexander1998182-169] Because of its corrosion resistance, lead is used as a protective sheath for underwater cables.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJensen2013136-170]

Yellow sculpture

A 17th-century gold-coated lead sculpture

Lead has many uses in the construction industry; lead sheets are used as [architectural metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architectural_metals) in [roofing material](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestic_roof_construction), [cladding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cladding_\(construction\)), [flashing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flashing_\(weatherproofing\)), [gutters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rain_gutter) and gutter joints, roof parapets.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEThink_Lead_research-171][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWeatherings_to_Parapets-172] Lead is still used in statues and sculptures,[^cite_note-174] including for [armatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armature_\(sculpture\)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPutnam2003216-175] In the past it was often used to [balance the wheels of cars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tire_balance); for environmental reasons this use is being phased out in favor of other materials.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEUnited_States_Geological_Survey201797-131]

Lead is added to copper alloys, such as [brass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brass) and [bronze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze), to improve machinability and for its lubricating qualities. Being practically insoluble in copper, the lead forms solid globules in imperfections throughout the alloy, such as [grain boundaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grain_boundary). In low concentrations, as well as acting as a lubricant, the globules hinder the formation of [swarf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swarf) as the alloy is worked, thereby improving machinability. Copper alloys with larger concentrations of lead are used in [bearings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bearing_\(mechanical\)). The lead provides lubrication, and the copper provides the load-bearing support.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECopper_Development_Association-176]

Lead's high density, atomic number, and formability form the basis for use of lead as a barrier that absorbs sound, vibration, and radiation.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERich1994101-177] Lead has no natural resonance frequencies;[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERich1994101-177] as a result, sheet-lead is used as a sound deadening layer in the walls, floors, and ceilings of sound studios.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGuruswamy200031-178] [Organ pipes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organ_pipe) are often made from a lead alloy, mixed with various amounts of tin to control the tone of each pipe.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAudsley1965250–251-179][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPalmieri2006412–413-180] Lead is an established [shielding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_shielding) material from [radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionizing_radiation) in nuclear science and in X-ray rooms[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENational_Council_on_Radiation_Protection_and_Measurements200416-181] due to its density (which gives it a high [attenuation coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attenuation_coefficient)) as well as its remarkably high [mass attenuation coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_attenuation_coefficient) (it attenuates per unit mass better than many other dense materials).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEThorntonRautiuBrush20017-182] Molten lead has been used as a coolant for [lead-cooled fast reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead-cooled_fast_reactor).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETučekCarlssonWider20061590-183]

### Batteries

The largest use of lead in the early 21st century is in [lead–acid batteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead%E2%80%93acid_battery). The lead in batteries undergoes no direct contact with humans, so there are fewer toxicity concerns.[^cite_note-185] People who work in lead battery production or recycling plants may be exposed to lead dust and inhale it.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEToxicological_Profile_for_Lead20075–6-186] The reactions in the battery between lead, lead dioxide, and sulfuric acid provide a reliable source of [voltage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltage).[^cite_note-188] [Supercapacitors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercapacitor) incorporating lead–acid batteries have been installed in kilowatt and megawatt scale applications in Australia, Japan, and the United States in frequency regulation, solar smoothing and shifting, wind smoothing, and other applications.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEOlinsky-Paul2013-189] These batteries have lower energy density and charge-discharge efficiency than [lithium-ion batteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium-ion_battery), but are significantly cheaper.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGulbinska2014-190]

### Coating for cables

Lead is used in high voltage power cables as shell material to prevent water diffusion into insulation; this use is decreasing as lead is being phased out.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERich1994133–134-191] Its use in [solder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solder) for electronics is also being phased out by some countries to reduce the amount of [environmentally hazardous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_hazard) waste.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEZhao2008440-192]

### Historical Preservation

Museums have an interest in preventing corrosion. Lead is contained in many historical artefacts, and lead corroding agents can be present in the air: [Aldehydes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldehydes), [Organic Acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_Acids) such as [Acetic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetic_acid) or [Formic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formic_acid), and [Acid gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_gases). The [Oddy test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oddy_test) is an environment of accelerated corrosion, using lead and other metals to determine the presence of corroding agents.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBeinerLaviSeriRossin2015-204][^cite_note-FOOTNOTESzczepanowska201384–85-205]

Lead has no confirmed biological role, and there is no confirmed safe level of lead exposure.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWorld_Health_Organization2018-207] A 2009 Canadian–American study concluded that even at levels that are considered to pose little to no risk, lead may cause "adverse mental health outcomes".[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBouchardBellingerWeuveMatthews-Bellinger2009-208] Its prevalence in the human body—at an adult average of 120 mg[^cite_note-210]—is nevertheless exceeded only by zinc (2500 mg) and iron (4000 mg) among the heavy metals.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEmsley2011280,_621,_255-211] Lead [salts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_\(chemistry\)) are very efficiently absorbed by the body.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELuckeyVenugopal1979177–178-212] A small amount of lead (1%) is stored in bones; the rest is excreted in urine and feces within a few weeks of exposure. Only about a third of lead is excreted by a child. Continual exposure may result in the [bioaccumulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioaccumulation) of lead.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEToxic_Substances_Portal-213]

### Toxicity

Lead is a highly poisonous metal (whether inhaled or swallowed), affecting almost every organ and system in the human body.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEUnited_States_Food_and_Drug_Administration201542-214]  At airborne levels of 100 mg/m<sup>3</sup>, it is [immediately dangerous to life and health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immediately_dangerous_to_life_or_health).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENational_Institute_for_Occupational_Safety_and_Health-215] Most ingested lead is absorbed into the bloodstream.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEOccupational_Safety_and_Health_Administration-216] The primary cause of its toxicity is its predilection for interfering with the proper functioning of enzymes. It does so by binding to the [sulfhydryl groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thiol) found on many enzymes,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERudolphRudolphHostetterLister2003369-217] or mimicking and displacing other metals that act as [cofactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cofactor_\(biochemistry\)) in many enzymatic reactions.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDartHurlbutBoyer-Hassen20041426-218] The essential metals that lead interacts with include calcium, iron, and zinc.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKosnett2006238-219] High levels of calcium and iron tend to provide some protection from lead poisoning; low levels cause increased susceptibility.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELuckeyVenugopal1979177–178-212]

### Effects

Lead can cause severe damage to the brain and kidneys and, ultimately, death. By mimicking calcium, lead can cross the [blood–brain barrier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood%E2%80%93brain_barrier). It degrades the [myelin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myelin) sheaths of [neurons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuron), reduces their numbers, interferes with [neurotransmission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurotransmitter) routes, and decreases neuronal growth.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERudolphRudolphHostetterLister2003369-217] In the human body, lead inhibits [porphobilinogen synthase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta-aminolevulinic_acid_dehydratase) and [ferrochelatase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferrochelatase), preventing both [porphobilinogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porphobilinogen) formation and the incorporation of iron into [protoporphyrin IX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protoporphyrin_IX), the final step in [heme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heme) synthesis. This causes ineffective heme synthesis and [microcytic anemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microcytic_anemia).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECohenTrotzkyPincus1981904–906-220]

A chart of a human body with arrows pointing pieces of text to different parts of the body

Symptoms of lead poisoning

Symptoms of lead poisoning include [nephropathy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kidney_disease), [colic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colic)-like abdominal pains, and possibly weakness in the fingers, wrists, or ankles. Small blood pressure increases, particularly in middle-aged and older people, may be apparent and can cause [anemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anemia).[^cite_note-221] Several studies, mostly cross-sectional, found an association between increased lead exposure and decreased heart rate variability.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTENavas-Acien2007-222] In pregnant women, high levels of exposure to lead may cause miscarriage. Chronic, high-level exposure has been shown to reduce fertility in males.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESokol2005133,_passim-223]

In a child's developing brain, lead interferes with [synapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synapse) formation  in the [cerebral cortex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerebral_cortex), [neurochemical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurochemical) development (including that of neurotransmitters), and the organization of [ion channels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion_channel).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMycykHryhorczukAmitai2005462-224] Early childhood exposure has been linked with an increased risk of sleep disturbances and excessive daytime drowsiness in later childhood.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELiuLiuPakWang20151869–1874-225] High blood levels are associated with delayed puberty in girls.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoetersDen_HondDhoogeVan_Larebeke2008168–175-226] The rise and fall in exposure to airborne lead from the combustion of tetraethyl lead in gasoline during the 20th century [has been linked with historical increases and decreases in crime levels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead%E2%80%93crime_hypothesis).[^cite_note-227]

### Exposure sources

Lead exposure is a global issue since lead mining and smelting, and battery manufacturing, disposal, and [recycling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battery_recycling#Lead.E2.80.93acid_batteries), are common in many countries. Lead enters the body via inhalation, ingestion, or skin absorption. Almost all inhaled lead is absorbed into the body; for ingestion, the rate is 20–70%, with children absorbing a higher percentage than adults.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETarragó201216-228]

Poisoning typically results from ingestion of food or water contaminated with lead, and less commonly after accidental ingestion of contaminated soil, dust, or lead-based paint.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEToxicological_Profile_for_Lead20074-229] Seawater products can contain lead if affected by nearby industrial waters.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBremner2002101-230] Fruit and vegetables can be contaminated by high levels of lead in the soils they were grown in. Soil can be contaminated through particulate accumulation from lead in pipes, [lead paint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_paint), residual emissions from leaded gasoline.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAgency_for_Toxic_Substances_and_Disease_Registry2007-231]

The use of lead for water pipes is [a problem in areas with soft or acidic water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plumbosolvency).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEThorntonRautiuBrush200117-232] Hard water forms insoluble protective layers on the inner surface of the pipes, whereas soft and acidic water dissolves the lead pipes.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMoore1977109–115-233] Dissolved [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) in the carried water may result in the formation of soluble lead [bicarbonate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicarbonate); oxygenated water may similarly dissolve lead as [lead(II) hydroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead\(II\)_hydroxide). Drinking such water, over time, can cause health problems due to the toxicity of the dissolved lead. The [harder the water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_water) the more [calcium bicarbonate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium_bicarbonate) and [sulfate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium_sulfate) it contains, and the more the inside of the pipes are coated with a protective layer of lead carbonate or lead sulfate.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWibergWibergHolleman2001914-234]



[Kymographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kymograph) recording of the effect of lead acetate on frog heart experimental set up

Ingestion of applied lead-based paint is the major source of exposure for children: a direct source is chewing on old painted window sills. Additionally, as lead paint on a surface deteriorates, it peels and is pulverized into dust. The dust then enters the body through hand-to-mouth contact or contaminated food or drink. Ingesting certain [home remedies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_medicine#Home_remedies) may result in exposure to lead or its compounds.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETarragó201211-235]

Inhalation is the second major exposure pathway, affecting smokers and especially workers in lead-related occupations.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEOccupational_Safety_and_Health_Administration-216] [Cigarette smoke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobacco_smoke) contains, among other toxic substances, radioactive [lead-210](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotopes_of_lead).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECenters_for_Disease_Control_and_Prevention2015-236] "As a result of EPA's regulatory efforts, levels of lead in the air \[in the United States] decreased by 86 percent between 2010 and 2020."[^cite_note-EPSLAP-237] The concentration of lead in the air in the United States fell below the national standard of 0.15 μg/m<sup>3</sup>[^cite_note-EPAleadStandards-238] in 2014.[^cite_note-EPALeadTrends-239]

Skin exposure may be significant for people working with organic lead compounds. The rate of skin absorption is lower for inorganic lead.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWaniAraUsman201557,_58-240]

#### Lead in foods

Lead may be found in food when food is grown in soil that is high in lead, airborne lead contaminates the crops, animals eat lead in their diet, or lead enters the food either from what it was stored or cooked in.[^cite_note-241] Ingestion of lead paint and batteries is also a route of exposure for livestock, which can subsequently affect humans.[^cite_note-242] Milk produced by contaminated cattle can be diluted to a lower lead concentration and sold for consumption.[^cite_note-243]

In Bangladesh, lead compounds have been added to [turmeric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turmeric) to make it more yellow.[^cite_note-Stan2019-244] This is believed to have started in the 1980s and continues as of 2019.[^cite_note-Stan2019-244] It is believed to be one of the main sources of high lead levels in the country.[^cite_note-245] In Hong Kong the maximum allowed lead level in food is 6 parts per million in solids and 1 part per million in liquids.[^cite_note-246]

Lead-containing dust can settle on drying cocoa beans when they are set outside near polluting industrial plants.[^cite_note-247] In December 2022, [Consumer Reports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumer_Reports) tested 28 [dark chocolate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_chocolate) brands and found that 23 of them contained potentially harmful levels of lead, [cadmium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cadmium) or both. They have urged the chocolate makers to reduce the level of lead which could be harmful, especially to a developing fetus.[^cite_note-248]

In March 2024, the US [Food and Drug Administration](11632.md) recommended a voluntary recall on 6 brands of [cinnamon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinnamon) due to contamination with lead,[^cite_note-249] after 500 reports of child lead poisoning.[^cite_note-250] The FDA determined that cinnamon was adulterated with [lead chromate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_chromate).[^cite_note-251]

#### Lead in plastic toys

According to the United States [Center for Disease Control](6811.md), the use of lead in plastics has not been banned as of 2024. Lead softens the plastic and makes it more flexible so that it can go back to its original shape. Habitual chewing on colored plastic insulation from stripped electrical wires was found to cause elevated lead levels in a 46-year-old man.[^cite_note-252] Lead may be used in plastic toys to stabilize molecules from heat. Lead dust can be formed when plastic is exposed to sunlight, air, and detergents that break down the chemical bond between the lead and plastics.[^cite_note-253]

### Treatment

See also: [Chelation therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chelation_therapy)

Treatment for lead poisoning normally involves the administration of [dimercaprol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimercaprol) and [succimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Succimer).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPrasad2010651–652-254] Acute cases may require the use of [disodium calcium edetate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_calcium_edetate), the calcium [chelate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chelation), and the disodium salt of ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid ([EDTA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethylenediaminetetraacetic_acid)). It has a greater affinity for lead than calcium, with the result that lead chelate is formed by exchange and excreted in the urine, leaving behind harmless calcium.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMastersTrevorKatzung2008481–483-255]

### Disease burden

The global [disease burden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disease_burden) caused by lead is enormous, with various estimates attributing millions of annual deaths to lead exposure.[^cite_note-npr-taylor-256][^cite_note-lancet-larsen-257] The [Global Burden of Disease Study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Burden_of_Disease_Study) attributed 850,000 deaths in 2019 to [hypertension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypertension) caused by lead exposure, and a 2023 study in *[Lancet Planetary Health](463899.md)* estimated that nearly 5.5 million annual deaths from [cardiovascular disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiovascular_disease) (CVD) were caused by lead.[^cite_note-lancet-larsen-257] A 2022 study in the *[Journal of the American College of Cardiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_the_American_College_of_Cardiology)* estimated that lead exposure caused 1.57 million death worldwide in 2021, through "hypertension, stroke, coronary artery disease, peripheral artery disease, and other CVDs."[^cite_note-258]

Lead exposure is the primary environmental cause for [cognitive impairment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_impairment);[^cite_note-259] even slightly elevated lead levels around the age of 24 months are associated with intellectual and academic performance deficits at age 10 years.[^cite_note-260] Half of the U.S. population is estimated to have had a [blood lead level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_lead_level) over 5 µg/dL in early childhood, which caused an estimated cumulative loss of 824 million [IQ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence_quotient) points as of 2015.[^cite_note-261]

## Environmental effects

A dusty dump

Battery collection site in [Dakar](44251.md), Senegal, where at least 18 children died of lead poisoning in 2008

The extraction, production, use, and disposal of lead and its products have caused significant contamination of the Earth's soils and waters. Atmospheric emissions of lead were at their peak during the Industrial Revolution, and the leaded gasoline period in the second half of the twentieth century.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEUnited_Nations_Environment_Programme20104-262]

Lead releases originate from natural sources (i.e., concentration of the naturally occurring lead), industrial production, incineration and recycling, and mobilization of previously buried lead.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEUnited_Nations_Environment_Programme20104-262] In particular, as lead has been phased out from other uses, in the Global South, lead recycling operations designed to extract cheap lead used for global manufacturing have become a well documented source of exposure.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERenfrew20198-263] Elevated concentrations of lead persist in soils and sediments in post-industrial and urban areas; industrial emissions, including those arising from coal burning,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETrace_element_emission2012-264] continue in many parts of the world, particularly in the developing countries.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEUnited_Nations_Environment_Programme20106-265]

Lead can accumulate in soils, especially those with a high organic content, where it remains for hundreds to thousands of years. Environmental lead can compete with other metals found in and on plant surfaces potentially inhibiting [photosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis) and at high enough concentrations, negatively affecting plant growth and survival. Contamination of soils and plants can allow lead to ascend the food chain affecting microorganisms and animals. In animals, lead exhibits toxicity in many organs, damaging the nervous, [renal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kidney), reproductive, [hematopoietic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haematopoiesis), and cardiovascular systems after ingestion, inhalation, or skin absorption.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAssiHezmeeHaronSabri2016-266] Fish uptake lead from both water and sediment;[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWorld_Health_Organization1995-267] bioaccumulation in the food chain poses a hazard to fish, birds, and sea mammals.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEUK_Marine_SACs_Project1999-268]

Anthropogenic lead includes lead from [shot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shot_\(pellet\)) and [sinkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fishing_sinker). These are among the most potent sources of lead contamination along with lead production sites.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEUnited_Nations_Environment_Programme20109-269] In 2017, lead shot, bullets, and sinkers were banned for hunting and fishing on land managed by the USFWS ,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcCoy2017-270] but the ban was only effective for a month.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECama2017-271] A similar ban is being considered in the European Union.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELayton2017-272]

Analytical methods for the determination of lead in the environment include [spectrophotometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrophotometry), [X-ray fluorescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_fluorescence), [atomic spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_spectroscopy), and [electrochemical methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemistry). A specific [ion-selective electrode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion-selective_electrode) has been developed based on the ionophore *S*,*S'*-methylenebis(*N*,*N*-diisobutyl[dithiocarbamate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dithiocarbamate)).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHauser201749–60-273] An important biomarker assay for lead poisoning is [δ-aminolevulinic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%CE%94-aminolevulinic_acid) levels in plasma, serum, and urine.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELauwerysHoet2001115,_116–117-274]

## Restriction and remediation

An X-ray picture with numerous small pellets highlighted in white

Radiography of a swan found dead in [Condé-sur-l'Escaut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cond%C3%A9-sur-l'Escaut) (northern France), highlighting lead shot. There are hundreds of lead pellets (a dozen is enough to kill an adult swan within a few days). Such bodies are sources of environmental contamination by lead.

By the mid-1980s, there was significant decline in the use of lead in industry.[^cite_note-275] In the United States, environmental regulations reduced or eliminated the use of lead in non-battery products, including gasoline, paints, solders, and water systems. [Particulate control devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scrubber) were installed in [coal-fired power plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal-fired_power_station) to capture lead emissions.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETrace_element_emission2012-264] In 1992, U.S. Congress required the Environmental Protection Agency to reduce the blood lead levels of the country's children.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAuerKoverAidalaGreenwood20164-276] Lead use was further curtailed by the European Union's 2003 [Restriction of Hazardous Substances Directive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RoHS).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPetzelJuutiSugimoto2004122–124-277] A large drop in lead deposition occurred in the Netherlands after the 1993 national ban on use of lead shot for hunting and sport shooting: from 230 tonnes in 1990 to 47.5 tonnes in 1995.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDeltaresNetherlands_Organisation_for_Applied_Scientific_Research2016-278] The usage of lead in [Avgas 100LL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avgas#100LL_\(blue\)) for [general aviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_aviation) is allowed in the EU as of 2022.[^cite_note-q566-279]

In the United States, the [permissible exposure limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permissible_exposure_limit) for lead in the workplace, comprising metallic lead, inorganic lead compounds, and [lead soaps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_lead#Related_white_lead_compounds), was set at 50 μg/m<sup>3</sup> over an 8-hour workday, and the [blood lead level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_lead_level) limit at 5 μg per 100 g of blood in 2012.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAgency_for_Toxic_Substances_and_Disease_Registry2017-280] Lead may still be found in harmful quantities in stoneware,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEGrandjean1978303–321-281] [vinyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vinyl_group)[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELevinBrownKashtockJacobs20081288-282] (such as that used for tubing and the insulation of electrical cords), and Chinese brass.[^cite_note-284] Old houses may still contain lead paint.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELevinBrownKashtockJacobs20081288-282] White lead paint has been [withdrawn from sale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Lead_\(Painting\)_Convention,_1921) in industrialized countries, but specialized uses of other pigments such as yellow [lead chromate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead\(II\)_chromate) remain,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECrow2007-285] especially in road pavement marking paint.[^cite_note-286] Stripping old paint by sanding produces dust which can be inhaled.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMarinoLandriganGraefNussbaum19901183–1185-287] [Lead abatement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_abatement) programs have been mandated by some authorities in properties where young children live.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESchoch1996111-288] The usage of lead in [Avgas 100LL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avgas#100LL_\(blue\)) for [general aviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_aviation) is generally allowed in United States as of 2023.[^cite_note-289]

Lead waste, depending on the jurisdiction and the nature of the waste, may be treated as household waste (to facilitate lead abatement activities),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEUnited_States_Environmental_Protection_Agency2000-290] or potentially hazardous waste requiring specialized treatment or storage.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELead_in_Waste2016-291] Lead is released into the environment in shooting places and a number of lead management practices have been developed to counter the lead contamination.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEUnited_States_Environmental_Protection_Agency2005I-1-292] Lead migration can be enhanced in acidic soils; to counter that, it is advised soils be treated with lime to [neutralize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutralization_\(chemistry\)) the soils and prevent leaching of lead.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEUnited_States_Environmental_Protection_Agency2005III-5–III-6-293]

Research has been conducted on how to remove lead from [biosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BioSystems) by biological means: Fish bones are being researched for their ability to [bioremediate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioremediation) lead in contaminated soil.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFreeman2012a20–a21-294][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEYoung2012-295] The fungus *[Aspergillus versicolor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspergillus_versicolor)* is effective at absorbing lead ions from industrial waste before being released to water bodies.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEActon201394–95-296] Several bacteria have been researched for their ability to remove lead from the environment, including the [sulfate-reducing bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfate-reducing_microorganism) *[Desulfovibrio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desulfovibrio)* and *[Desulfotomaculum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desulfotomaculum)*, both of which are highly effective in aqueous solutions.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEParkBolanMegharaNaidu2011162–174-297] Millet grass *[Urochloa ramosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urochloa_ramosa)* has the ability to accumulate significant amounts of metals such as lead and [zinc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinc) in its shoot and root tissues making it an important plant for remediation of contaminated soils.[^cite_note-298]
