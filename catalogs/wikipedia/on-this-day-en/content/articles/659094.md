**Quantum information science** is an interdisciplinary field that combines the principles of [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics), [information theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory), and [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science) to explore how quantum phenomena can be harnessed for the processing, analysis, and transmission of information.[^cite_note-1] Quantum information science covers both theoretical and experimental aspects of quantum physics, including the limits of what can be achieved with [quantum information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_information). The term **quantum information theory** is sometimes used, but it refers to the theoretical aspects of information processing and does not include experimental research.[^cite_note-2]

At its core, quantum information science explores how [information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information) behaves when [stored](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_retrieval) and manipulated using quantum systems. Unlike classical information, which is encoded in bits that can only be 0 or 1, quantum information uses quantum bits or [qubits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qubit) that can exist simultaneously in multiple states because of [superposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_superposition).[^cite_note-3] Additionally, [entanglement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_entanglement)—a uniquely quantum linkage between particles—enables correlations that have no classical counterpart.[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6]

## Scientific and engineering studies

Quantum information science is inherently interdisciplinary, bringing together physics, [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), mathematics, and engineering. It involves developing theoretical frameworks, designing quantum algorithms, constructing quantum hardware, and implementing quantum communication protocols.[^cite_note-7]

[Quantum teleportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_teleportation), [entanglement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_entanglement) and the manufacturing of [quantum computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_computer) depend on a comprehensive understanding of quantum physics and engineering. [Google](1092923.md) and [IBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM), among others, have invested significantly in quantum computer hardware research, leading to significant progress in manufacturing quantum computers since the 2010s. Currently, it is possible to build a quantum computer with over 100 [qubits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qubit), but the error rates are high due to several factors including decoherence,[^cite_note-8] the lack of suitable hardware and materials for quantum computer manufacturing, which make it difficult to create a scalable quantum computer.[^cite_note-9]

[Quantum cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_cryptography) devices are now available for commercial use. The [one time pad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_time_pad), a cipher used by spies during the [Cold War](325329.md), uses a sequence of random keys for encryption. These keys can be securely exchanged using quantum entangled particle pairs, as the principles of the [no-cloning theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No-cloning_theorem) and [wave function collapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse) ensure the secure exchange of the random keys. The development of devices that can transmit quantum entangled particles is a significant scientific and engineering goal.

[Qiskit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qiskit), [Cirq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cirq) and [Q Sharp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q_Sharp) are popular quantum programming languages. Additional [programming languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Programming_languages) for quantum computers are needed, as well as a larger community of competent quantum programmers. To this end, additional learning resources are needed, since there are many fundamental differences in quantum programming which limits the number of skills that can be carried over from traditional programming.[^cite_note-10] OpenQASM (Open Quantum Assembly Language) is a machine-independent imperative programming language designed to describe quantum circuits. It is based on the quantum circuit model and represents quantum programs as ordered sequences of parameterized operations, including gates, measurements, resets, and real-time classical computations. Beyond implementing quantum algorithms, OpenQASM also enables the specification of circuits for tasks such as characterization, validation, and debugging of quantum processors.[^cite_note-11]

## Related mathematical subjects

[Quantum algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_algorithms) and [quantum complexity theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_complexity_theory) are two of the subjects in [algorithms](775.md) and [computational complexity theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_complexity_theory). In 1994, mathematician [Peter Shor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Shor) introduced a quantum algorithm for [prime factorization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factorization)[^cite_note-12] that, with a quantum computer containing 4,000 [logical qubits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_and_logical_qubits), could potentially break widely used ciphers like [RSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_\(cryptosystem\)) and [ECC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic-curve_cryptography), posing a major security threat. This led to increased investment in [quantum computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_computing) research and the development of [post-quantum cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-quantum_cryptography)[^cite_note-13] to prepare for the fault-tolerant quantum computing (FTQC) era.[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15]
