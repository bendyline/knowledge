# Hypernymy and hyponymy



An example of the relationship between hyponyms and hypernym

**Hypernymy** and **hyponymy** are the [semantic relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantics) between a generic term (*hypernym*) and a more specific term (*hyponym*). The hypernym is also called a *supertype*, *umbrella term*, or *blanket term*.[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4] The hyponym names a [subtype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subset) of the hypernym. The [semantic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic_field) of the hyponym is included within that of the hypernym.[^cite_note-5] For example, "pigeon", "crow", and "hen" are all hyponyms of "bird" and "animal"; "bird" and "animal" are both hypernyms of "pigeon", "crow", and "hen".[^cite_note-6]

## Discussion

In [linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics), [semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantics), [general semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_semantics), and [ontologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontology_components), hyponymy (from [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language)&#x20;*&#x20;*[ὑπό](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E1%BD%91%CF%80%CF%8C#Ancient_Greek)*&#x20;(*hupó*)* 'under' and&#x20;*&#x20;*[ὄνυμα](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E1%BD%84%CE%BD%CF%85%CE%BC%CE%B1#Ancient_Greek)*&#x20;(*ónuma*)* 'name') shows the relationship between a generic term (hypernym) and a specific instance of it (hyponym). A hyponym is a word or phrase whose semantic field is more specific than its hypernym. The semantic field of a hypernym, also known as a superordinate, is broader than that of a hyponym. An approach to the relationship between hyponyms and hypernyms is to view a hypernym as consisting of hyponyms. This, however, becomes more difficult with abstract words such as *imagine*, *understand* and *knowledge*. While hyponyms are typically used to refer to nouns, it can also be used on other parts of speech. Like nouns, hypernyms in verbs are words that refer to a broad category of actions. For example, verbs such as *stare*, *gaze*, *view* and *peer* can also be considered hyponyms of the verb *look*, which is their hypernym.

The meaning relation between hyponyms and hypernyms applies to lexical items of the same [word class (that is, part of speech)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Part_of_speech), and holds between [senses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_sense) rather than words. For instance, the word *screwdriver* as most immediately understood refers to the [screwdriver tool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screwdriver), and not to the [screwdriver drink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screwdriver_\(cocktail\)).

&#x20;Hypernymy and hyponymy are [converse relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Converse_relation). If X is a kind of Y, then X is a hyponym of Y and Y is a hypernym of X.[^cite_note-Maienborn-7] Hyponymy is a [transitive relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transitive_relation): if X is a hyponym of Y, and Y is a hyponym of Z, then X is a hyponym of Z.[^cite_note-8] For example, *[violet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Violet_\(color\))* is a hyponym of *[purple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purple)* and *purple* is a hyponym of *[color](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color)*; therefore *violet* is a hyponym of *color*. A word can be both a hypernym and a hyponym: for example *purple* is a hyponym of color but itself is a hypernym of the broad spectrum of shades of purple between the range of *crimson* and *violet*.

The hierarchical structure of semantic fields can be seen in hyponymy.[^cite_note-Gao-9] These can be conceptualized as a vertical arrangement, where the higher level is more general and the lower level is more specific.[^cite_note-Gao-9] For example, *living things* will be the highest level followed by *plants* and *animals*, and the lowest level may comprise *dog*, *cat* and *wolf*.[^cite_note-Gao-9]

### Taxonymy

Taxonymy (not to be confused with, though related to, *[taxonomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxonomy)*) is a sub-variety of hyponymy. Within the structure of a taxonomic lexical hierarchy, two types of hyponymic relation may be distinguished: the first—exemplified in "An X is a Y"—corresponds to so-called "simple" hyponymy; the second—that which is exemplified in "An X is a *kind/type of* Y"—is more discriminating, and functions as the "vertical" relation in the taxonomy. This latter relation is that which may be termed *taxonymy*.[^cite_note-The_Semantics_of_Relationships:_An_Interdisciplinary_Perspective-10]

A taxonomic lexical hierarchy is structured by, in addition to the above inclusion relations, its corresponding relations of exclusion: "A Z is not a Y", or *incompatibility*; and "A Z is a different kind/type of Y than X", or *co-taxonymy*.[^cite_note-The_Semantics_of_Relationships:_An_Interdisciplinary_Perspective-10]

### Co-hyponyms

If the hypernym Z consists of hyponyms X and Y, then X and Y are identified as co-hyponyms, also known as coordinate terms. Co-hyponyms are labelled as such when separate hyponyms share the same hypernym but are not hyponyms of one another, unless they happen to be synonymous.[^cite_note-Maienborn-7] For example, *screwdriver*, *scissors*, *knife*, and *hammer* are all co-hyponyms of one another and hyponyms of *tool*, but not hyponyms of one another: \*"A hammer is a type of [knife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knife)" is false.

Co-hyponyms are often but not always related to one another by the relation of incompatibility. For example, *apple*, *peach* and *plum* are co-hyponyms of *fruit*. However, an *apple* is not a *peach*, which is also not a *plum*. Thus, they are incompatible. Nevertheless, co-hyponyms are not necessarily incompatible in all [senses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_sense). A *queen* and *mother* are both hyponyms of *woman* but there is nothing preventing the *queen* from being a *mother*.[^cite_note-Meaning_in_Language-11] This shows that compatibility may be relevant.

### Autohyponyms



Three varieties of autohyponym

A word is an autohyponym if it is used for both a hypernym and its hyponym:[^cite_note-12] it has a stricter [sense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_sense) that is entirely a subset of a broader sense. For example, the word *dog* describes both the species *[Canis familiaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canis_familiaris)* and male individuals of *Canis familiaris*, so it is possible to say "That dog isn't a dog, it's a bitch" ("That hypernym Z isn't a hyponym Z, it's a hyponym Y"). The term "autohyponym" was coined by linguist [Laurence R. Horn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurence_R._Horn), in his 1984 paper "Ambiguity, negation, and the London School of Parsimony". Linguist [Ruth Kempson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruth_Kempson) had already observed that if there are hyponyms for one part of a set but not another, the hypernym can complement the existing hyponym by being used for the remaining part. For example, fingers describe all digits on a hand, but the existence of the word [thumb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thumb) for the first finger means that fingers can also be used for "non-thumb digits on a hand".[^cite_note-\:0-13] Autohyponymy is also called "vertical [polysemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polysemy)".[^cite_note-15]

Horn called this "licensed [polysemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polysemy)", but found that autohyponyms also formed even when there is no other hyponym. [Yankee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yankee) is autohyponymous because it is a hyponym (native of New England) and its hypernym (native of the United States), even though there is no other hyponym of Yankee (as native of the United States) that means "not a native of New England".[^cite_note-16][^cite_note-\:0-13] Similarly, the [verb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verb) to drink (a beverage) is a hypernym for to drink (an alcoholic beverage).[^cite_note-\:0-13]

In some cases, autohyponyms duplicate existing, distinct hyponyms. The hypernym "smell" (to emit any smell) has a hyponym "stink" (to emit a bad smell), but is autohyponymous because "smell" can also mean "to emit a bad smell", even though there is no "to emit a smell that isn't bad" hyponym.[^cite_note-\:0-13]

## Etymology

*Hyperonym* and *hypernym* mean the same thing, with both in use by linguists. The form *hypernym* interprets the *-o-* of *hyponym* as a part of *hypo*, such as in *hypertension* and *hypotension*. However, etymologically the *-o-* is part of the Greek stem *ónoma*. In other combinations with this stem, e.g. *synonym*, it is never elided. Therefore, *hyperonym* is etymologically more faithful than *hypernym*.[^cite_note-17] *Hyperonymy* is used, for instance, by [John Lyons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lyons_\(linguist\)), who does not mention *hypernymy* and prefers *superordination*.[^cite_note-18] The nominalization *hyperonymy* is rarely used, because the neutral term to refer to the relationship is *hypernymy*.

## Usage

[Computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science) often terms this relationship an "[is-a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Is-a)" relationship. For example, the phrase "Red is-a color" can be used to describe the hyponymic relationship between *red* and *color*.

Hyponymy is the most frequently encoded relation among [synsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synonym_ring) used in lexical databases such as [WordNet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WordNet). These semantic relations can also be used to compare [semantic similarity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic_similarity) by judging the distance between two synsets and to analyse [anaphora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaphora_\(linguistics\)).

As a hypernym can be understood as a more general word than its hyponym, the relation is used in [semantic compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic_compression) by generalization to reduce a level of [specialization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specialization_\(linguistics\)).

The notion of hyponymy is particularly relevant to [language translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_translation), as hyponyms are very common across languages. For example, in Japanese the word for older brother is *ani* (兄), and the word for younger brother is *otōto* (弟). An English-to-Japanese translator presented with a phrase containing the English word *brother* would have to choose which Japanese word equivalent to use. This would be difficult, because abstract information (such as the speakers' relative ages) is often not available during [machine translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_translation).
