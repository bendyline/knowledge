**Haredi Judaism** ([Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language): יהדות חֲרֵדִית, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Hebrew): *Yahadut Ḥaredit*, IPA: [\[χaʁeˈdi\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Hebrew)) is a sector of [Orthodox Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthodox_Judaism) that is characterized by its strict interpretation of religious sources and its accepted *[halakha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halakha)* (Jewish law) and traditions, in opposition to more accommodating values and practices. Its members are often referred to as ***ultra-Orthodox*** in English, a term considered [pejorative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pejorative) by many of its adherents, who prefer the term ***strictly Orthodox*** or ***Haredi*** (plural: ***Haredim***). Haredim regard themselves as the most authentic custodians of Jewish religious law and tradition which, in their opinion, is binding and unchangeable. Many consider all other contemporary [expressions of Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Movements_of_Judaism), including [Modern Orthodoxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Orthodox_Judaism), as "deviations from God's laws", although other movements of Judaism would disagree.[^cite_note-Rubel2010-1]

As of 2020, there were about 2.1 million Haredim globally, representing 14% of the world's Jewish population.[^cite_note-2]

## Terminology



Young Haredi Jews in Jerusalem, 2005

The term *Haredi* is a [Modern Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Hebrew) adjective derived from the [Biblical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblical_Hebrew) verb *hared*, which appears in the [Book of Isaiah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Isaiah) ([66\:2](https://mechon-mamre.org/p/pt/pt1066.htm#2); its plural *haredim* appears in Isaiah [66\:5](https://mechon-mamre.org/p/pt/pt1066.htm#5))[^cite_note-Stadler4-3] and is translated as "\[one who] trembles" at the word of God. The word connotes an awe-inspired fear to perform the will of God;[^cite_note-4] it is used to distinguish them from other Orthodox Jews (similar to the names used by Christian [Quakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quakers) and [Shakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakers) to describe their relationship to God).[^cite_note-Stadler4-3][^cite_note-5][^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7]

The term most commonly used by outsiders, for example most American news organizations, is *ultra-Orthodox* Judaism.[^cite_note-8] [Hillel Halkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hillel_Halkin) suggests the origins of the term may date to the 1950s, a period in which Haredi survivors of the [Holocaust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Holocaust) first began arriving in America.[^cite_note-\:1-9] However, [Isaac Leeser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Leeser) (1806–1868) was described in a 1916 biography as "ultra-Orthodox".[^cite_note-10]

The word *Haredi* is often used in the [Jewish diaspora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_diaspora) in place of the term *ultra-Orthodox*, which many view as inaccurate or offensive,[^cite_note-Ayalon1999-11][^cite_note-OthersPejorative-12][^cite_note-13] it being seen as a derogatory term suggesting extremism.[^cite_note-Shafran-14] English-language alternatives that have been proposed include *fervently Orthodox*,[^cite_note-15] *strictly Orthodox*,[^cite_note-OthersPejorative-12][^cite_note-16] or *traditional Orthodox*.[^cite_note-\:0-17] Others, however, dispute the characterization of the term as pejorative.[^cite_note-\:1-9] For example, [Ari L. Goldman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ari_L._Goldman), a professor at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University) in [United States](3434750.md), notes that the term simply serves a practical purpose to distinguish a specific part of the Orthodox community, and is not meant as pejorative.[^cite_note-\:0-17] Others, such as [Samuel Heilman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Heilman), criticized terms such as *ultra-Orthodox* and *traditional Orthodox*, arguing that they misidentify Haredi Jews as more authentically Orthodox than others, as opposed to adopting customs and practises that reflect their desire to separate from the outside world.[^cite_note-18][^cite_note-\:1-9]

The community has sometimes been characterized as *traditional Orthodox*, in contradistinction to the [Modern Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Orthodox_Judaism), the other major branch of Orthodox Judaism, and not to be confused with the movement represented by the [Union for Traditional Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_for_Traditional_Judaism), which originated in [Conservative Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_Judaism).[^cite_note-19][^cite_note-20]

Haredi Jews also use other terms to refer to themselves. Common [Yiddish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yiddish) words include *Yidn* (Jews), *erlekhe Yidn* (virtuous Jews),[^cite_note-Ayalon1999-11] *ben Torah* (son of the Torah),[^cite_note-Stadler4-3]

In [Israel](9282173.md), Haredi Jews are sometimes also called by the derogatory slang words *dos* (plural *dosim*), which mimics the traditional [Ashkenazi Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashkenazi_Hebrew) pronunciation of the word *datiyim* (religious),[^cite_note-21] and more rarely, *sh'chorim* (blacks), a reference to the black clothes they typically wear;[^cite_note-22] a related informal term used in English is *black hat*.[^cite_note-23]

## Population

Due to its imprecise definition, lack of data collection, and rapid change over time, estimating the global Haredi population is difficult. The true number of Haredim may be significantly underestimated due to their reluctance to participate in surveys and censuses.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEttinger2011a-24][^cite_note-25]

In 1992, out of a total of 1,500,000 Orthodox Jews worldwide, about 550,000 were Haredi (half of them in Israel).[^cite_note-Baumel-26] One estimate given in 2011 stated that there were approximately 1.3 million Haredi Jews globally.[^cite_note-Brown_2011-27] Studies have shown a very high growth rate, with a large young population.[^cite_note-28] Haredi population grew to 2.1 million in 2020 and is expected to double by 2040.[^cite_note-jpr.org.uk-29]

The vast majority of Haredi Jews are Ashkenazi. However, some 20% of the Haredi population are thought to belong to the Sephardic Haredi stream. In recent decades, Haredi society has grown due to the addition of a religious population that identifies with the [Shas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shas) movement. The percentage of people leaving the Haredi population has been estimated between 6% and 18%.[^cite_note-30] Not all leave the Orthodox community, as some become more [behaviorally modern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Orthodox_Judaism#The_behaviorally_modern) while still practicing some form of Orthodoxy.

Haredi Rabbis and students writing a [Torah scroll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sefer_Torah) (Haredi [settlement](15123.md) of [Beitar Illit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beitar_Illit), [Gush Etzion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gush_Etzion))

[Israel](9282173.md) has the largest Haredi population.[^cite_note-\:3-34] In 1948, there were about 35,000 to 45,000 Haredi Jews in Israel. By 1980, Haredim made up 4% of the Israeli population.[^cite_note-35] Haredim made up 9.9% of the Israeli population in 2009, with 750,000 out of 7,552,100; by 2014, that figure had risen to 11.1%, with 910,500 Haredim out of a total Israeli population of 8,183,400. According to a December 2017 study conducted by the [Israeli Democracy Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israeli_Democracy_Institute), the number of Haredi Jews in Israel exceeded 1 million in 2017, making up 12% of the population in Israel. In 2019, Haredim reached a population of almost 1,126,000;[^cite_note-36][^cite_note-hiddush-31] over the next year, it reached 1,175,000 (12.6% of total population).[^cite_note-37][^cite_note-toi-32] By the end of 2023, it reached almost 1,335,000, or 13.6% of total population;[^cite_note-38] at the end of 2024, it passed over 1,392,000, thus representing 13.9% of the population,[^cite_note-idi.org_2024-33] and by the end of 2025 it numbered over 1,452,000 or 14.3% of the total population of Israel.[^cite_note-renamed_from_2025_on_20260122130924-39]

The number of Haredi Jews in Israel continues to rise rapidly, with their current population growth rate being 4% per year.[^cite_note-timesofisrael-gross-40] The number of children per woman is 7.2, and the share of Haredim among those under the age of 20 was 16.3% in 2009 (29% of Jews).[^cite_note-cbs-41]

By 2030, the Haredi Jewish community is projected to make up 16% of the total population, and by 2065, a third of the Israeli population, including non-Jews. By then, one in two Israeli children would be Haredi.[^cite_note-timesofisrael-gross-40][^cite_note-tzvi-42][^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44] It is also projected that the number of Haredim in 2059 may be between 2.73 and 5.84 million, of an estimated total number of Israeli Jews between 6.09 and 9.95 million.[^cite_note-cbs-41][^cite_note-45]

The largest Israeli Haredi concentrations are in [Jerusalem](16043.md), [Bnei Brak](314671.md), [Modi'in Illit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modi'in_Illit), [Beitar Illit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beitar_Illit), [Beit Shemesh](323292.md), [Kiryat Ye'arim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiryat_Ye'arim), [Ashdod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashdod), [Rekhasim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rekhasim), [Safed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safed), and [El'ad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El'ad). Two Haredi cities, [Kasif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kasif,_Israel)[^cite_note-46] and [Tila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tila,_Israel)[^cite_note-47], are planned.

### United States

The [United States](3434750.md) has the second largest Haredi population, which has a growth rate on pace to double every 20 years. In 2000, there were 360,000 Haredi Jews in the US (7.2 per cent of the approximately 5 million Jews in the U.S.); by 2006, demographers estimate the number had grown to 468,000 (30% increase), or 9.4 percent of all U.S. Jews.[^cite_note-Wise-48] In 2013, it was estimated that there were 530,000 total ultra-Orthodox Jews in the United States, or 10% of all American Jews.[^cite_note-jppi-49] By 2011, 61% of all Jewish children in Eight-County [New York City metropolitan area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City_metropolitan_area) were Orthodox, with Haredim making up 49%.[^cite_note-Databank2011-50]

In 2020, it was estimated that there were approximately 700,000 total ultra-Orthodox Jews in the United States, or 12% of all American Jews.[^cite_note-jpr.org.uk-29] This number is expected to grow significantly in the coming years, due to high Haredi birth rates in America.

### United Kingdom

In 1998, the Haredi population in the [Jewish community of the United Kingdom](5380456.md) was estimated at 27,000 (13% of affiliated Jews).[^cite_note-Baumel-26] The largest communities are located in London, particularly [Stamford Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stamford_Hill#Haredi_Jewish_community), Golders Green, Hendon, Edgware; in [Salford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salford,_Greater_Manchester) and [Prestwich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prestwich) in [Greater Manchester](79819.md); and in [Gateshead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gateshead#Judaism). A 2007 study asserted that three out of four British Jewish births were Haredi, who then accounted for 17% of British Jews (45,500 out of around 275,000).[^cite_note-Wise-48] Another study in 2010 established that there were 9,049 Haredi households in the UK, which would account for a population of nearly 53,400, or 20% of the community.[^cite_note-98][^cite_note-99] The [Board of Deputies of British Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Board_of_Deputies_of_British_Jews) has predicted that the Haredi community will become the largest group in Anglo-Jewry within the next three decades: In comparison with the national average of 2.4 children per family, Haredi families have an average of 5.9 children, and consequently, the population distribution is heavily biased to the under-20-year-olds. By 2006, membership of Haredi synagogues had doubled since 1990.[^cite_note-100][^cite_note-economist-20150613-101] British Haredi fertility rate has also been estimated to be as high as 6.9 children per woman.[^cite_note-102]

An investigation by *[The Independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Independent)* in 2014 reported that more than 1,000 children in Haredi communities were attending illegal schools where secular knowledge is banned, and they learn only religious texts, meaning they leave school with no qualifications and often unable to speak any English.[^cite_note-103]

The 2018 Survey by the [Jewish Policy Research (JPR)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Jewish_Policy_Research) and the Board of Deputies of British Jews showed that the high birth rate in the Haredi and Orthodox community reversed the decline in the Jewish population in Britain.[^cite_note-104]

In 2020, it was estimated that there were approximately 76,000 total ultra-Orthodox Jews in the United Kingdom, or 25% of all British Jews, a significant increase from 1998 and 2010.[^cite_note-jpr.org.uk-29]

## History



Hasidic boys in [Łódź](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%81%C3%B3d%C5%BA), 1910

Throughout [Jewish history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_history), [Judaism](15624.md) has always faced internal and external challenges to its beliefs and practices which have emerged over time and produced counter-responses. According to its adherents, Haredi Judaism is a continuation of [Rabbinic Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabbinic_Judaism), and the immediate forebears of contemporary Haredi Jews were the Jewish religious traditionalists of [Central and Eastern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_and_Eastern_Europe) who fought against secular modernization's influence which reduced Jewish religious observance.[^cite_note-110] Indeed, adherents of Haredi Judaism, just like [Rabbinic Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabbinic_Jews), see their beliefs as part of an unbroken tradition which dates back to the [revelation at Sinai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_Commandments).[^cite_note-\:2-111] However, most historians of Orthodoxy consider Haredi Judaism, in its most modern incarnation, to date back to the beginning of the 20th century.[^cite_note-\:2-111][^cite_note-112][^cite_note-113]

For centuries, before [Jewish emancipation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_emancipation), European Jews were forced to live in [ghettos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_ghettos_in_Europe) where [Jewish culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_culture) and religious observance were preserved. Change began in the wake of the [Age of Enlightenment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment), when some European liberals sought to include the Jewish population in the emerging empires and [nation states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nation_state). The influence of the *[Haskalah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haskalah)* movement[^cite_note-114] (Jewish Enlightenment) was also evident. Supporters of the Haskalah held that Judaism must change, in keeping with the social changes around them. Other Jews insisted on strict adherence to *_[halakha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halakha)_* (Jewish law and custom).[^cite_note-\:3-34][^cite_note-115]

In Germany, the opponents of Reform rallied to [Samson Raphael Hirsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samson_Raphael_Hirsch), who led a secession from German Jewish communal organizations to form a strictly Orthodox movement, with its own network of [synagogues](48559.md) and [religious schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeshiva). His approach was to accept the tools of modern scholarship and apply them in defence of Orthodox Judaism. In the [Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish%E2%80%93Lithuanian_Commonwealth) (including areas [traditionally considered Lithuanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Duchy_of_Lithuania)), Jews true to traditional values gathered under the banner of *Agudas Shlumei Emunei Yisroel*.[^cite_note-116]

In 1877 [Moshe Schick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moshe_Schick) supported Hirsch's separatist policies in Germany. Schick's own son was enrolled in the [Hildesheimer Rabbinical Seminary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hildesheimer_Rabbinical_Seminary), headed by [Azriel Hildesheimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azriel_Hildesheimer), which taught secular studies. Hirsch, however, did not reciprocate, and expressed astonishment at Schick's *halakhic* contortions in condemning even those Status Quo communities that clearly adhered to *halakha*.[^cite_note-117] Lichtenstein opposed Hildesheimer, and his son Hirsh Hildesheimer, as they made use of the [German language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language) in sermons from the pulpit and seemed to lean in the direction of [Zionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zionism).[^cite_note-118]

Shimon Sofer was somewhat more lenient than Lichtenstein on the use of German in sermons, allowing the practice as needed for the sake of keeping cordial relations with the various governments. Likewise, he allowed extra-curricular studies of the [gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnasium_\(school\)) for students whose rabbinical positions would be recognized by the governments, stipulating the necessity to prove the strict adherence to the God-fearing standards per individual case.[^cite_note-119]



Haredi Jews from [Galicia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galicia_\(Eastern_Europe\)) at the [Karmelitermarkt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karmelitermarkt?action=edit\&redlink=1) in Vienna's second district, [Leopoldstadt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopoldstadt), 1915

In 1912, the [World Agudath Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Agudath_Israel) was founded, to differentiate itself from the [Torah Nationalist Mizrachi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_Zionism) and secular Zionist organizations. It was dominated by the [Hasidic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasidic_Judaism) *[rebbes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebbe)* and [Lithuanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuanian_Jews) rabbis and *[roshei yeshiva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosh_yeshiva)* (deans). The organization nominated rabbis who subsequently were elected as representatives in the Polish legislature [Sejm](28167.md), such as [Meir Shapiro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meir_Shapiro) and [Yitzhak-Meir Levin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yitzhak-Meir_Levin). Not all Hasidic factions joined the Agudath Israel, remaining independent instead, such as Machzikei Hadat of Galicia.[^cite_note-120]

In 1919, [Yosef Chaim Sonnenfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yosef_Chaim_Sonnenfeld) and [Yitzchok Yerucham Diskin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yitzchok_Yerucham_Diskin?action=edit\&redlink=1) founded the [Edah HaChareidis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edah_HaChareidis) as part of Agudath Israel in then-[Mandate Palestine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandate_Palestine).

In 1924, Agudath Israel obtained 75 percent of the votes in the Kehilla elections.[^cite_note-121]

The Orthodox community polled some 16,000 of a total 90,000 at the Knesseth Israel in 1929.[^cite_note-122] But Sonnenfeld lobbied Sir [John Chancellor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Chancellor_\(British_administrator\)), the High Commissioner, for separate representation in the Palestine Communities Ordinance from that of the Knesseth Israel. He explained that the Agudas Israel community would cooperate with the [Vaad Leumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaad_Leumi) and the [National Jewish Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Jewish_Council?action=edit\&redlink=1) in matters pertaining to the municipality, but sought to protect its religious convictions independently. The community petitioned the [Permanent Mandates Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permanent_Mandates_Commission) of the [League of Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Nations) on this issue. The one community principle was victorious, despite their opposition, but this is seen as the creation of the Haredi community in Israel, separate from the other Orthodox and Zionist movements.[^cite_note-123]

In 1932, Sonnenfeld was succeeded by [Yosef Tzvi Dushinsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yosef_Tzvi_Dushinsky_\(first_Dushinsky_rebbe\)), a disciple of the Shevet Sofer, one of the grandchildren of Moses Sofer. Dushinsky promised to build up a strong Jewish Orthodoxy at peace with the other Jewish communities and the non-Jews.[^cite_note-124]

### Post-Holocaust

In general, the present-day Haredi population originate from two distinct post-Holocaust waves.

The vast majority of Hasidic and [Litvak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuanian_Jews) communities were destroyed during [the Holocaust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Holocaust).[^cite_note-125][^cite_note-126] Although Hasidic customs have largely been preserved, the customs of Lithuanian Jewry, including its unique Hebrew pronunciation, have been almost lost. [Litvish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misnagdim#Litvishe) customs are still preserved primarily by the few older Jews who were born in Lithuania prior to the Holocaust. In the decade or so after 1945, there was a strong drive to revive and maintain these lifestyles by some notable Haredi leaders.

The [Chazon Ish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chazon_Ish) was particularly prominent in the early days of the State of Israel. [Aharon Kotler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aharon_Kotler) established many of the Haredi schools and [yeshivas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeshiva) in the United States and Israel; and [Joel Teitelbaum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joel_Teitelbaum) had a significant impact on revitalizing Hasidic Jewry, as well as many of the Jews who fled [Hungary](13275.md) during the [1956 revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_Revolution_of_1956) who became followers of his [Satmar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satmar_\(Hasidic_dynasty\)) dynasty, and became the largest Hasidic group in the world. These Jews typically have maintained a connection only with other religious family members. As such, those growing up in such families have little or no contact with non-Haredi Jews.[^cite_note-127][^cite_note-Wertheimer-128]

The second wave began in the 1970s associated with the religious revival of the so-called [baal teshuva movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baal_teshuva_movement),[^cite_note-DešenLiebman1995-129][^cite_note-130][^cite_note-131][^cite_note-132] although most of the newly religious become [Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthodox_Judaism), and not necessarily fully Haredi. The formation and spread of the [Sephardic Haredi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sephardic_Haredim) lifestyle movement also began in the 1980s by [Ovadia Yosef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ovadia_Yosef), alongside the establishment of the [Shas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shas) party in 1984. This led many Sephardi Jews to adopt the clothing and culture of the Lithuanian Haredi Judaism, though it had no historical basis in their own tradition. Many yeshivas were also established specifically for new adopters of the Haredi way of life.

The original Haredi population has been instrumental in the expansion of their lifestyle, though criticisms have been made of discrimination towards the later adopters of the Haredi lifestyle in *[shidduchim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shidduch)* (matchmaking)[^cite_note-133] and the school system.[^cite_note-134]

## Practices and beliefs

The Haredim represent the conservative, or pietistic, form of [Jewish fundamentalism](182557.md), distinct from the radical fundamentalism of [Gush Emunim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gush_Emunim),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESilberstein1993\[httpsbooksgooglecombooksidbmYTCgAAQBAJpgPA17_17\]-135] and emphasising withdrawal from, and disdain for, the secular world, and the creation of an alternative world which insulates the Torah and the life it prescribes from outside influences.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTETehranian1997324-136] Haredi Judaism is not an institutionally cohesive or homogeneous group, but comprises a diversity of spiritual and cultural orientations, generally divided into a broad range of Hasidic courts and Litvishe-Yeshivish streams from Eastern Europe, and Oriental [Sephardic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sephardic) Haredi Jews. These groups often differ significantly from one another in their specific ideologies and lifestyles, as well as the degree of stringency in religious practice, rigidity of religious philosophy, and isolation from the general culture that they maintain. Some Haredis encourage [outreach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_outreach) to less observant and unaffiliated Jews and *[hilonim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilonim)* (secular Israeli Jews).[^cite_note-137]

Some scholars, including some secular and Reform Jews, describe the Haredim as "radical fundamentalists".[^cite_note-146]

Efforts to keep clear of external influence are a core characteristic of Haredi Judaism. Historically, new mediums of communication such as books, newspapers and magazines, and later tapes, CDs and television, were dealt with by either transforming and controlling the content, or choosing to have rabbinic leadership censor it selectively or altogether. In the modern digital era, difficulty in censoring the Internet and conversely, the Internet's importance, resulted in a decades long and ongoing struggle of comprehension, adaptation, and regulation on the part of rabbinical leadership and community activists.[^cite_note-147]

These beliefs and practices, which have been interpreted as "isolationist", can bring them into conflict with authorities. In 2018, a Haredi school in the United Kingdom was rated as "inadequate" by the [Office for Standards in Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ofsted), after repeated complaints were raised about the censoring of textbooks and exam papers which contained mentions of [homosexuality](5488304.md), examples of women socializing with men, pictures showing women's shoulders and legs, or information that contradicts a [creationist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creationism) worldview.[^cite_note-148][^cite_note-149]

### Lifestyle and family



Haredi Jewish women and girls in [Mea Shearim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mea_Shearim), Jerusalem, 2013

Haredi life, like Orthodox Jewish life in general, is very family-centered and ordered. Boys and girls attend separate schools and proceed to higher [Torah study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torah_study), in a yeshiva or seminary, respectively, starting anywhere between the ages of 13 and 18. A significant proportion of young men remain in yeshiva until their marriage (often [arranged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arranged_marriage)). After marriage, many Haredi men continue their Torah studies in a [kollel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kollel).

Studying in secular institutions is often discouraged, although educational facilities for vocational training in a Haredi framework do exist. In North America and Europe, the majority of Haredi males are active in the workforce. For various reasons, in Israel, a majority (56%) of their male members do not work, though some of those are part of the unofficial workforce.[^cite_note-HarediMenWomenWorforcePercentageIsrael-150][^cite_note-151][^cite_note-152][^cite_note-153] Haredi families (and Orthodox Jewish families in general) are usually much larger than non-Orthodox families, with an average of seven children per family, but it is not unheard of for families to have twelve or more children.[^cite_note-Wertheimer-128] About 80% of female Haredi Jews in Israel work.[^cite_note-154]

Haredi Jews are typically opposed to the viewing of television and films,[^cite_note-155] and the reading of secular newspapers and books. There has been a strong campaign against the [Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet), and Internet-enabled mobile phones without filters have also been banned by leading rabbis.[^cite_note-156][^cite_note-huffingtonpost.com-157][^cite_note-158] In May 2012, 40,000 Haredim gathered at [Citi Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citi_Field), a [baseball park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baseball_park) in New York City, to discuss the dangers of unfiltered Internet.[^cite_note-huffingtonpost.com-157][^cite_note-159] The event was organized by the [Ichud HaKehillos LeTohar HaMachane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ichud_HaKehillos_LeTohar_HaMachane). The Internet has been allowed for business purposes, so long as filters are installed.

In some instances, forms of recreation which conform to Jewish law are treated as antithetical to Haredi Judaism. In 2013, the Rabbinical Court of the Ashkenazi Community in the Haredi settlement of [Beitar Illit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beitar_Illit) ruled against [Zumba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zumba) (a type of dance fitness) classes, although they were held with a female instructor and all-female participants.[^cite_note-160][^cite_note-Women-Only-161] The Court said in part: "Both in form and manner, the activity \[Zumba] is entirely at odds with both the ways of the Torah and the holiness of Israel, as are the songs associated to it."[^cite_note-Women-Only-161]

### *Shidduch* (matchmaking)

With Haredi Judaism having a heavy emphasis on marriage, especially while young, some members rely on the *[shidduch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shidduch)* (matchmaking) system. They employ a schadhan (a professional matchmaker) to support them in their search for a spouse. While there is no current statistical data showing how many people use the services of a schadhan, it is estimated that the vast majority of Haredi couples were paired by one.[^cite_note-162]

However, with the broader societal shift to online dating, matchmaking in Orthodox and Haredi Judaism has started making inroads online. Vastly different from the most popular online dating services, apps like Shidduch pair couples based on shared values and life goals. To do this, users fill out a digital resume. The app was made possible by a partnership between its developers and the [Orthodox Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthodox_Union) — the same group responsible for kosher food certification ("Circle-U").[^cite_note-163]

### Dress



Styles of Haredi dress



Typical Haredi dress for men and women



A Haredi couple in typical clothing

The standard mode of dress for males of the Lithuanian stream is a black or navy suit and a white shirt.[^cite_note-164] Headgear includes black [Fedora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fedora) or [Homburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homburg_\(hat\)) hats, with black [skull caps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yarmulka). Pre-war Lithuanian yeshiva students also wore light coloured suits, along with beige or grey hats,[^cite_note-165] and prior to the 1990s, it was common for Americans of the Lithuanian stream to wear coloured shirts throughout the week, reserving white shirts for [Shabbos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shabbat).[^cite_note-166]

Women adhere to the [laws of modest dress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tzniut), and wear long skirts and sleeves, high necklines, and, if married, some form of hair covering.[^cite_note-167] Haredi women never wear trousers, although most do wear pajama-trousers within the home at night.[^cite_note-peopleil-168]

It has become popular among some Haredi women to wear *[sheitels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheitel)* (wigs), that are thought to be more attractive than their own natural hair (drawing criticism from some more conservative Haredi rabbis). Mainstream Sephardi Haredi rabbi [Ovadia Yosef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ovadia_Yosef) forbade the wearing of wigs altogether.[^cite_note-169] Haredi women often dress more freely, and casually, within the home, as long as the body remains covered in accordance with *halakha*. More modernized Haredi women are somewhat more lenient in matters of their dress, and some follow the latest trends and fashions, while conforming to *halakha*.[^cite_note-peopleil-168]

### Neighborhoods

Haredi neighborhoods have been said by some to be safer, with less violent crime, although this is a generalization, and even that may apply to only specific communities, rather than all.[^cite_note-Kaplan2013-170]

In Israel, the entrances to some of the most extreme Haredi neighborhoods are fitted with signs that ask for modest clothing to be worn.[^cite_note-171] Some areas are known to have "modesty patrols",[^cite_note-172] and people dressed in ways perceived as immodest may suffer harassment, and advertisements featuring scantily dressed models may be targeted for vandalism.[^cite_note-173][^cite_note-174] These concerns are also addressed through public lobbying and legal avenues.[^cite_note-175][^cite_note-176]

During the week-long [Rio Carnival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rio_Carnival) in Rio de Janeiro, many of the city's 7,000 Orthodox Jews feel compelled to leave the town, due to the immodest exposure of participants.[^cite_note-177] In 2001, Haredi campaigners in Jerusalem succeeded in persuading the [Egged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egged_Ta'avura) bus company to get all their advertisements approved by a special committee.[^cite_note-178] By 2011, Egged had gradually removed all bus adverts that featured women, in response to their continuous defacement. A court order that stated such action was discriminatory led to Egged's decision not to feature people at all (neither male nor female).[^cite_note-179] Depictions of certain other creatures, such as [space aliens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grey_alien), were also banned, in order not to offend Haredi sensibilities.[^cite_note-180] Haredi Jews also campaign against other types of advertising that promote activities they deem offensive or inappropriate.[^cite_note-181]

Due to halakha, i.e., activities that Orthodox Jews believe are prohibited on [Shabbat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shabbat), most state-run buses in Israel do not run on Saturdays,[^cite_note-IIIDemerath2003-182] regardless of whether riders are Orthodox or even whether they are Jewish. In a similar vein, Haredi Jews in Israel have demanded that the roads in their neighborhoods be closed on Saturdays, vehicular traffic being viewed as an "intolerable provocation" upon their religious lifestyle (see [Driving on Shabbat in Jewish law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Driving_on_Shabbat_in_Jewish_law)). In most cases, the authorities granted permission after Haredi petitioning and demonstrations, some of them including fierce clashes between Haredi Jews and secular counter-demonstrators, and violence against police and motorists.[^cite_note-Rose2004-183]

While Jewish modesty law requires [gender separation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_separation_in_Judaism) under various circumstances, observers have contended that there is a growing trend among some groups of Hasidic Haredi Jews to extend its observance to the public arena.[^cite_note-Zeveloff_2011-186]

Since 1973, buses catering to Haredi Jews running from Rockland County and Brooklyn into Manhattan have had separate areas for men and women, allowing passengers to conduct on-board prayer services.[^cite_note-191] Although the lines are privately operated, they serve the general public, and in 2011, the set-up was challenged on grounds of discrimination, and the arrangement was deemed illegal.[^cite_note-192][^cite_note-193] During 2010–2012, there was much public debate in Israel surrounding the existence of segregated Haredi [Mehadrin bus lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehadrin_bus_lines) (whose policy calls for both men and women to stay in their respective areas: men in the front of the bus,[^cite_note-194] and women in the rear of the bus) following an altercation that occurred after a woman refused to move to the rear of the bus to sit among the women. A subsequent court ruling stated that while voluntary segregation should be allowed, forced separation is unlawful.[^cite_note-Katya_Alder-195] Israeli national airline [El Al](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Al) has agreed to provide gender-separated flights in consideration of Haredi requirements.[^cite_note-196]

Education in the Haredi community is strictly segregated by sex. [Yeshiva education for boys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeshiva) is primarily focused on the study of Jewish scriptures, such as the [Torah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanakh) and [Talmud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talmud) (*non-Hasidic* yeshivas in the United States teach secular studies in the afternoon); girls obtain studies both in Jewish religious education as well as broader secular subjects.[^cite_note-197]

### Newspapers and publications



[Tziporah Heller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tziporah_Heller), a weekly columnist for *[Hamodia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamodia)*

In 1930s Poland, the Agudath Israel movement published its own Yiddish-language paper, *Dos Yiddishe Tagblatt*. In 1950, the Agudah started printing *[Hamodia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamodia)*, a Hebrew-language Israeli daily.

Haredi publications tend to shield their readership from objectionable material,[^cite_note-198] and perceive themselves as a "[counterculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterculture)", desisting from advertising secular entertainment and events.[^cite_note-Simon1978-199] The editorial policy of a Haredi newspaper is determined by a rabbinical board, and every edition is checked by a rabbinical [censor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship).[^cite_note-200] A strict policy of modesty is characteristic of the Haredi press in recent years, and pictures of women are usually not printed.[^cite_note-Cohen80-201] In 2009, the Israeli daily *[Yated Ne'eman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yated_Ne'eman_\(Israel\))* doctored an Israeli cabinet photograph replacing two female ministers with images of men,[^cite_note-202] and in 2013, the *Bakehilah* magazine pixelated the faces of women appearing in a photograph of the [Warsaw Ghetto Uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warsaw_Ghetto_Uprising).[^cite_note-203] The mainstream Haredi political Shas party also refrains from publishing female images.[^cite_note-ReferenceA-204] Among Haredi publishers which have not adopted this policy is [ArtScroll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ArtScroll), which does publish pictures of women in their books.[^cite_note-205]

No coverage is given to serious crime, violence, sex, or drugs, and little coverage is given to non-Orthodox streams of Judaism.[^cite_note-206] Inclusion of "immoral" content is avoided, and when publication of such stories is a necessity, they are often written ambiguously.[^cite_note-Cohen80-201] The Haredi press generally takes an ambivalent stance towards Zionism and gives more coverage to issues that concern the Haredi community, such as the drafting of girls and yeshiva students into the army, autopsies, and Shabbat observance.[^cite_note-Simon1978-199] In Israel, it portrays the secular world as "spitefully anti-Semitic", and describes secular youth as "mindless, immoral, drugged, and unspeakably lewd".[^cite_note-207][^cite_note-208] Such attacks have led to Haredi editors being warned about libelous provocations.[^cite_note-209]

While the Haredi press is extensive and varied in Israel,[^cite_note-Simon1978-199] only around half the Haredi population reads newspapers. Around 10% read secular newspapers, while 40% do not read any newspaper at all.[^cite_note-210] According to a 2007 survey, 27% read the weekend Friday edition of *Hamodia*, and 26% the *Yated Ne'eman*.[^cite_note-Cohen_2012_111-211] In 2006, the most-read Haredi magazine in Israel was the *[Mishpacha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mishpacha)* weekly, which sold 110,000 copies.[^cite_note-Cohen_2012_111-211] Other popular Haredi publications include [Ami Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ami_Magazine) and The Flatbush Jewish Journal.

### Technology

Haredi leaders have at times suggested a ban on the internet and any internet-capable device,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDeutsch20094–5-212] their reasoning being that the immense amount of information can be corrupting, and the ability to use the internet with no observation from the community can lead to individuation.[^cite_note-213]

Some Haredi businessmen utilize the internet throughout the week, but they still observe Shabbat in every aspect by not accepting or processing orders from Friday evening to Saturday evening.[^cite_note-214] They utilize the internet under strict [filters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_filter) and guidelines. The [Kosher cell phone](19644137.md#Kosher_phone) was introduced to the Jewish public with the sole ability to call other phones. It was unable to utilize the internet, text other phones, and had no camera feature. In fact, a kosher phone plan was created, with decreased rates for kosher-to-kosher calls, to encourage community.[^cite_note-215][^cite_note-216]

### News hotlines

Main article: [Haredi news hotline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haredi_news_hotline)

News hotlines are an important source of news in the Haredi world. Since many Haredi Jews do not listen to the radio or have access to the internet, even if they read newspapers, they are left with little or no access to breaking news. News hotlines were formed to fill this gap, and many have expanded to additional fields over time.[^cite_note-217][^cite_note-\:01-218] Currently, many news lines provide rabbinic lectures, entertainment, business advice, and similar services, in addition to their primary function of reporting the news. Many Hasidic sects maintain their own hotlines, where relevant internal news is reported and the group's perspective can be advocated for. In the Israeli Haredi community, there are dozens of prominent hotlines, in both Yiddish and Hebrew. Some Haredi hotlines have played significant public roles.[^cite_note-219]

## In Israel

### Attitudes towards Zionism

See also: [Haredim and Zionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haredim_and_Zionism)

From the founding of Zionism in the 1890s, Haredi Jews leaders voiced objections to its secular orientation.[^cite_note-Sherman1993-220][^cite_note-Halpern2-221] After the establishment of the State of Israel, some Haredi Jews observed the Israeli [Independence Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_Day_\(Israel\)) as a day of mourning and referred to Israeli state-holidays as *byimey edeyhem* ("idolatrous holidays").[^cite_note-222]



Members of [Neturei Karta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neturei_Karta) protest against Israel (Washington, 2005)

The chief political division among Haredi Jews has been in their approach to the State of Israel. After Israeli independence, different Haredi movements took varying positions on it. Only a minority of Haredi Jews consider themselves to be Zionists. Haredim who do not consider themselves Zionists fall into two camps: non-Zionist and anti-Zionist. Non-Zionist Haredim, who comprise the majority, do not object to the State of Israel as an independent Jewish state, and many even consider it to be positive, but they do not believe that it has any religious significance. Anti-Zionist Haredim, who are a minority, but are more publicly visible than the non-Zionist majority, believe that any Jewish independence prior to the coming of the Messiah is a sin.[^cite_note-223][^cite_note-224]

The ideologically non-Zionist [United Torah Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Torah_Judaism) alliance comprising [Agudat Yisrael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agudat_Yisrael) and [Degel HaTorah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degel_HaTorah) (and the umbrella organizations World Agudath Israel and [Agudath Israel of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agudath_Israel_of_America)) represents a moderate and pragmatic stance of cooperation with the State of Israel, and participation in the political system. UTJ has been a participant in numerous coalition governments, seeking to influence state and society in a more religious direction and maintain welfare and religious funding policies. In general, their position is supportive of Israel.[^cite_note-225]

Haredim who are stridently anti-Zionist are under the umbrella of [Edah HaChareidis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edah_HaChareidis), who reject participation in politics and state funding of its affiliated institutions, in contradistinction to Agudah-affiliated institutions. [Neturei Karta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neturei_Karta) is a very small activist organization of anti-Zionist Haredim, whose controversial activities have been strongly condemned, including by other anti-Zionist Haredim.[^cite_note-226] Haredi support is often required to form coalition governments in the [Knesset](74656.md).[^cite_note-227][^cite_note-228]

In recent years, some rebbes affiliated with Agudath Israel, such as the [Sadigura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sadigura_\(Hasidic_dynasty\)) rebbe [Avrohom Yaakov Friedman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avrohom_Yaakov_Friedman_\(fifth_Sadigura_rebbe\)), have taken stances closer to the Israeli right wing on security, settlements and withdrawal from the Gaza Strip.[^cite_note-229]

[Shas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shas) represents Sephardi and Mizrahi Haredim, and, while having many points in common with Ashkenazi Haredim, differs from them by its more enthusiastic support for the State of Israel and the IDF. The [Sikirim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikrikim) group, an [anti-Zionist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Zionist) group composed of Haredi Jews, is considered a radical organization by Israelis.[^cite_note-Haaretz1.404855-230]

### Marriage

The purpose of marriage in the Haredi (and Orthodox) viewpoint is for the purpose of companionship, as well as for the purpose of having children.[^cite_note-231]

There is a high rate of marriage in the Haredi community. 83% are married, compared to the non-Haredi community in Israel of 63%.[^cite_note-shnaton-232] Marriage is viewed as holy, and as the natural home for a man and a woman to truly love each other.

### Divorce

In 2016, the divorce rate in Israel was 5% among the Haredi population, compared to the general population rate of 14%.[^cite_note-shnaton-232]

In 2016, Haaretz claimed that divorces among Haredim are increasing in Israel.[^cite_note-Haaretz1-233] In 2017, some predominantly Haredi cities reported the highest growth rates in divorce in the Israel, in the context of generally falling rates of divorce,[^cite_note-Lev2018-234] and in 2018, some predominantly Haredi cities reported drops in divorce, in the context of generally rising rates of divorce.[^cite_note-235]

When the divorce is linked to one spouse leaving the community, the one who chooses to leave is often [shunned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shunning) from his or her communities and forced to abandon their children, as most courts prefer keeping children in an established status quo.[^cite_note-Haaretz1-233][^cite_note-auto-236][^cite_note-auto1-237]

### Education

Main articles: [Cheder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheder), [Yeshiva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeshiva), and [Bais Yaakov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bais_Yaakov)

Between 2007 and 2017, the number of Haredim studying in higher education rose from 1,000 to 10,800.[^cite_note-tzvi-42]

In 2007, the Kemach Foundation was established to become an investor in the sector's social and economic development and provide opportunities for employment. Through the philanthropy of [Leo Noé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_No%C3%A9) of London, later joined by the [Wolfson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zev_Wolfson) family of New York and [Elie Horn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elie_Horn) from Brazil, Kemach has facilitated academic and vocational training. With a \$22 million budget, including government funding, Kemach provides individualized career assessment, academic or vocational scholarships, and job placement for the entire Haredi population in Israel. The Foundation is managed by specialists who, coming from the Haredi sector themselves, are familiar with the community's needs and sensitivities. By April 2014, more than 17,800 Haredim had received the services of Kemach, and more than 7,500 had received, or continue to receive, monthly scholarships to fund their academic or vocational studies. From 500 graduates, the net benefits to the government would be 80.8 million NIS if they work for one year, 572.3 million NIS if they work for 5 years, and 2.8 billion NIS (discounted) if they work for 30 years.[^cite_note-238]

The Council for Higher Education announced in 2012 that it was investing NIS 180 million over the following five years to establish appropriate frameworks for the education of Haredim, focusing on specific professions.[^cite_note-239] The largest Haredi campus in Israel is [The Haredi Campus - The Academic College Ono](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Haredi_Campus_-_The_Academic_College_Ono).

In the midst of a controversy surrounding the limited secular education in some Haredi yeshivas, [New York City](645042.md) mayor [Eric Adams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Adams) held up the Haredi yeshiva model as a model to emulate, arguing that "We need to ask, 'What are we doing wrong in our schools?' And learn what you are doing in the yeshivas to improve education."[^cite_note-240]

### Military

Main article: [Haredi conscription in Israel](82822441.md)

Haredi demonstration against the conscription of yeshiva pupils

Haredi demonstration against the conscription of yeshiva pupils

Upon the establishment of the State of Israel in 1948, universal conscription was instituted for all able-bodied Jewish males. However, military-aged Haredi men were [exempted from service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exemption_from_military_service_in_Israel) in the [Israel Defense Forces](39237.md) (IDF) under the principle of [Torato Umanuto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torato_Umanuto), which officially granted deferred entry into the IDF for yeshiva students, but in practice allowed young Haredi men to serve for a significantly reduced period of time or bypass military service altogether. At that time, the Haredi population was very low and only 400 individuals were affected.[^cite_note-241] However, the Haredi population rapidly grew.[^cite_note-242][^cite_note-243] In 2018, the Israel Democracy Institute estimated that the Haredim comprised 12% of Israel's total population and 15% of its Jewish population.[^cite_note-244] Haredim are also younger than the general population. Their absence from the IDF attracts significant resentment from secular Israelis. The most common criticisms of the exemption policy are:

- The Haredim can work in those 2–3 years of their lives in which they do not serve in the IDF, while most soldiers at the IDF are usually paid around \$80–250 a month, in addition to clothing and lodging.

  [^cite_note-245]

  &#x20;All the while, Haredi yeshiva students receive significant monthly funds and payments for their religious studies.

  [^cite_note-246]

- The Haredim, if they so choose, can study at that time.

  [^cite_note-247]

  [^cite_note-248]

Over the years, as many as 1,000 Haredi Jews have volunteered to serve in a Haredi Jewish unit of the IDF known as the [Netzah Yehuda Battalion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netzah_Yehuda_Battalion), or Nahal Haredi. The vast majority of Haredi men, however, continue to receive deferments from military service.[^cite_note-249] Haredim usually reject the practice of IDF service and contend that:

- A yeshiva student has an important role in protecting the Jewish people because Haredim believe that Torah study brings spiritual protection similar to how a soldier in the IDF brings physical protection. Haredim maintain that each role is important in protecting the Jewish people, and one who is a yeshiva student should not abandon his personal duty in spiritually protecting the Jewish people.

  [^cite_note-250]

  [^cite_note-251]

  [^cite_note-252]

  [^cite_note-253]

- The Israeli army is not conducive to a Haredi lifestyle. It is regarded as a "state-sponsored quagmire of [promiscuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Promiscuity)" due to Israel [conscripting both men and women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_the_Israel_Defense_Forces), and often grouping them together in military activities.

  [^cite_note-254]

  &#x20;Additionally, the keeping of military procedures makes it difficult to observe the Sabbath and many other Jewish practices.

  [^cite_note-255]

The *Torato Umanuto* arrangement was enshrined in the *[Tal Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tal_committee)* that came into force in 2002. The [High Court of Justice](1666510.md#High_Court_of_Justice) later ruled that it could not be extended in its current form beyond August 2012. A replacement was expected. The IDF was, however, experiencing a shortage of personnel, and there were pressures to reduce the scope of the *Torato Umanuto* exemption.[^cite_note-256] In March 2014, Israel's parliament approved legislation to end exemptions from military service for Haredi seminary students. The bill was passed by 65 votes to one, and an amendment allowing civilian national service by 67 to one.[^cite_note-257] In June 2024, the [Supreme Court of Israel](1666510.md) declared any continued exemption of IDF conscription unlawful. The army began drafting 3,000 Haredi men the following month.[^cite_note-TOI202407182-258]

There has been much uproar in Haredi society following actions towards [Haredi conscription](82822441.md). While some Haredim see this as a great social and economic opportunity,[^cite_note-259] others (including leading rabbis among them) strongly oppose this move.[^cite_note-260] Among the extreme Haredim, there have been some more severe reactions. Several Haredi leaders have threatened that Haredi populations would leave the country if forced to enlist.[^cite_note-261][^cite_note-262] Others have fueled public incitement against secular and National-Religious Jews, and specifically against politicians [Yair Lapid](2448072.md) and [Naftali Bennett](39754840.md), who support and promote Haredi enlistment.[^cite_note-263][^cite_note-264] Some Haredim have taken to threatening their fellows who agree to enlist,[^cite_note-265][^cite_note-266] to the point of physically attacking some of them.[^cite_note-267][^cite_note-268]

The Shahar program, also known as *Shiluv Haredim* (Ultra-Orthodox integration), allows Haredi men aged 22 to 26 to serve in the army for about a year and a half. At the beginning of their service, they study mathematics and English, which are often not well covered in Haredi boy schools. The program is partly aimed at encouraging Haredi participation in the workforce after military service. However, not all beneficiaries seem to be Haredim.[^cite_note-269]

### Employment

As of 2024, figures from the [Israel Democracy Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel_Democracy_Institute) on employment rates place Haredi women at 80%, close to the 83% for the non-Haredi Jewish women's national figure; while the percentage of working Haredi men is 54%, it is still far below the 87% of non-Haredi Jewish men nationwide. As of 2024, the number of Haredi men studying in yeshiva increased by 8.5% since January 2023, which was the largest increase seen in a year since 2015.[^cite_note-270]

The [Trajtenberg Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajtenberg_Committee), charged in 2011 with drafting proposals for economic and social change, called, among other things, for increasing employment among the Haredi population. Its proposals included encouraging military or national service and offering college prep courses for volunteers, creating more employment centers targeting Haredim and experimental matriculation prep courses after yeshiva hours. The committee also called for increasing the number of Haredi students receiving technical training through the [Industry, Trade, and Labor Ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industry,_Trade_and_Labour_Minister_of_Israel) and forcing Haredi schools to carry out standardized testing, as is done at other public schools.[^cite_note-271] It is estimated that half as many of the Haredi community are in employment as the rest of population. This has led to increasing financial deprivation, and 50% of children within the community live below the poverty line. This puts strain on each family, the community, and often the Israeli economy.

The demographic trend indicates the community will constitute an increasing percentage of the population, and consequently, Israel faces an economic challenge in the years ahead due to fewer people in the labor force. A report commissioned by the Treasury found that the Israeli economy may lose more than six billion shekels annually as a result of low Haredi participation in the workforce.[^cite_note-272] The [OECD](33853117.md) in a 2010 report stated that, "Haredi families are frequently jobless, or are one-earner families in low-paid employment. Poverty rates are around 60% for Haredim."[^cite_note-OECD_Reviews_of_Labour_Market_and_Social_Policies_OECD_Reviews_of_Labour_Market_and_Social_Policies:_Israel-273]

As of 2017, according to an Israeli finance ministry study, the Haredi participation rate in the labour force is 51%, compared to 89% for the rest of Israeli Jews.[^cite_note-veconomist-274]

A 2018 study by Oren Heller, a [National Insurance Institute of Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bituah_Leumi) senior economic researcher, has found that while [upward mobility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_mobility) among Haredim is significantly greater than the national average, unlike it, this tends not to translate into significantly higher pay.[^cite_note-275]

Haredi families living in Israel benefited from government-subsidized child care when the father studied Torah and the mother worked at least 24 hours per week. However, after [Israeli Finance Minister](7088294.md) [Avigdor Liberman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avigdor_Liberman) introduced a new policy in 2021, families in which the father is a full-time yeshiva student are no longer eligible for a daycare subsidy. Under this policy, fathers must also work at least part-time in order for the family to qualify for the subsidy. The move was denounced by Haredi leaders.[^cite_note-276]

### Work force and tax contribution

A 2025 Israel Democracy Institute study found that although Haredim made up 14% of Israel's working-age population in 2023, they generated only 4% of national tax revenue.[^cite_note-bill-277] As a result, the average non-Haredi worker is projected to pay an extra 3,540 shekels in taxes in 2025.[^cite_note-bill-277] Only 23% of Haredim pay income tax, compared to 62% of non-Haredi Jewish men and 46% of women.[^cite_note-bill-277] Employment among Haredi men declined to 54% in 2024, while rates for Haredi women rose to 81% in 2023—just 2% below non-Haredi women.[^cite_note-bill-277] Due to a lack of secular education, many Haredi men are poorly equipped for the labor market, leading to lower household incomes.[^cite_note-bill-277] Despite contributing less in taxes, Haredi households consume more state services, receiving transportation and municipal tax discounts, housing aid, and other benefits; the [Kohelet Policy Forum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kohelet_Policy_Forum) reported that 80% of Haredi households are net receivers of public funds.[^cite_note-bill-277] The IDI called this imbalance unsustainable.[^cite_note-bill-277]

### Other issues



Hasidim walk to the synagogue, [Rehovot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rehovot), Israel.

The Haredim in general are materially poorer than most other Israelis, but still represent an important market sector due to their bloc purchasing habits.[^cite_note-278] For this reason, some companies and organizations in Israel refrain from including women or other images deemed immodest in their advertisements to avoid Haredi consumer boycotts.[^cite_note-279][^cite_note-280] More than 50 percent of Haredim live below the poverty line, compared with 15 percent of the rest of the population.[^cite_note-Erlanger-281] Their families are also larger, with Haredi women having an average of 6.7 children, while the average Jewish Israeli woman has 3 children.[^cite_note-282] Families with many children often receive economic support through governmental child allowances, government assistance in housing, as well as specific funds by their own community institutions.[^cite_note-283]

In recent years, there has been a process of reconciliation and an attempt to merge Haredi Jews with Israeli society,[^cite_note-284] although employment discrimination is widespread.[^cite_note-285] Haredi Jews such as satirist [Kobi Arieli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kobi_Arieli?action=edit\&redlink=1), publicist Sehara Blau, and politician [Israel Eichler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel_Eichler) write regularly for leading Israeli newspapers.

Another important factor in the reconciliation process has been the activities of [ZAKA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZAKA), a Haredi organization known for providing emergency medical attention at the scene of [suicide bombings](18835454.md), and [Yad Sarah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yad_Sarah), the largest national volunteer organization in Israel established in 1977 by former Haredi mayor of [Jerusalem](16043.md), [Uri Lupolianski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uri_Lupolianski). It is estimated that Yad Sarah saves the country's economy an estimated \$320 million in hospital fees and long-term care costs each year.[^cite_note-286][^cite_note-jweekly-287]

### Pedophilia and sexual abuse cases

See also: [Adass Israel School sex abuse scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adass_Israel_School_sex_abuse_scandal); [FailedMessiah.com](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FailedMessiah.com); [Royal Commission into Institutional Responses to Child Sexual Abuse § Yeshiva, Melbourne and Yeshiva, Bondi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Commission_into_Institutional_Responses_to_Child_Sexual_Abuse#Yeshiva,_Melbourne_and_Yeshiva,_Bondi); and [Sexual abuse cases in Brooklyn's Haredi community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_abuse_cases_in_Brooklyn's_Haredi_community)

Cases of [pedophilia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedophilia), [sexual violence](731637.md), [assaults](218407.md), and [abuses](7397019.md) against women and [children](18721790.md) occur in roughly the same rates in Haredi communities as in the general population. However, such crimes are rarely discussed or reported to the authorities, and are also frequently downplayed by members of the communities.[^cite_note-Nyt2-288][^cite_note-VICE-289][^cite_note-The_Guardian-290][^cite_note-Independent-291][^cite_note-Haaretz2-292][^cite_note-WPost-293][^cite_note-ForwardJTA-294][^cite_note-Haaretz3-295]

### Divorce coercion

To receive a religious divorce, a Jewish woman needs her husband's consent in the form of a *[get](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Get_\(divorce_document\))* (Jewish divorce document). Without this consent, any future offspring of the wife would be considered *[mamzerim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamzer)* (bastards/impure). If the circumstances truly warrant a divorce, and the husband is unwilling, a [dayan (rabbinic judge)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dayan_\(rabbinic_judge\)) has the prerogative of instituting community shunning measures to "coerce him until he agrees", with physical force reserved only for the rarest of cases.[^cite_note-296][^cite_note-\:0-17][^cite_note-torture-297]

The [New York divorce coercion gang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_divorce_coercion_gang) was a Haredi Jewish group in [United States](3434750.md) that kidnapped, and in some cases tortured, Jewish men in the [New York metropolitan area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_metropolitan_area) to force them to grant their wives *gittin* (religious divorces). The [Federal Bureau of Investigation](11127.md) (FBI) broke up the group after conducting a [sting operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sting_operation) against the gang in October 2013. The sting resulted in the prosecution of four men, three of whom were convicted in late 2015.[^cite_note-298]

### Political controversies involving Haredi communities and parties in Israel

In January 2023, the Times of Israel reported that Haredi citizens in Israel pay just 2% of the country's total income tax revenues, despite making up 13.9% of the nation's population. Furthermore, the article's author described their communities as an "epicenter of poverty", with over 60% of Haredi households classified as "poor" on the government's socio-economic index, with that figure remaining nearly constant in every Haredi community.[^cite_note-299]

While this disparity has been present in Israel for decades, it has garnered more attention since December 2022 for numerous reasons. First, Haredi families have the highest fertility rate in Israel, at 6.6 births per woman. In comparison, the average fertility rate in Israel is much lower, at 2.9 per woman. Current projections estimate that the Haredi population will double by 2036, and they will comprise 16% of the total population by 2030.[^cite_note-300]

The second aspect of the controversy surrounds their political connections to Israel's Religious Zionist alliance. Historically, they have remained politically uninvolved, but since the 1990s, they have continuously engaged more. Today, members of Israel's ultra-Orthodox community have long enjoyed benefits: exemption from army service for Torah students, government stipends for those choosing full-time religious study over work, and separate schools that receive state funds, even though their curriculums often do not fully teach government-mandated subjects. Today, many Israeli Haredi men do not work, preferring to study the Torah full-time, thus resulting in their high poverty rate.[^cite_note-301]

### Haredi anti-draft rally

See also: [Protests against conscription of yeshiva students](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protests_against_conscription_of_yeshiva_students)

Hundreds of thousands of ultra-Orthodox men assembled in [Jerusalem](16043.md) in October 2025 to demonstrate against [conscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conscription_in_Israel) into the [Israel Defence Forces](39237.md) (IDF). Hebrew media sources have highlighted that, in recent years, this issue has been the sole matter that has brought together all sects and factions within the ultra-Orthodox community. As per Israel's [Channel 12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_12_\(Israel\)), the last protest that similarly unified the Haredi community was another anti-conscription rally approximately a decade earlier.[^cite_note-302]
