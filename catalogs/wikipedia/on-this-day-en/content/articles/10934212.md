**Air pollution** is the presence of substances in the [air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_Earth) that are harmful to humans, other living beings or the environment. Pollutants can be [gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas), like [ozone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground-level_ozone) or [nitrogen oxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_oxides), or small particles like [soot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soot) and dust. Both outdoor and indoor air can be polluted.

Outdoor air pollution comes from burning [fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel) for electricity and [transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport), [wildfires](56106.md), some industrial processes, waste management, [demolition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demolition) and agriculture. Indoor air pollution is often [from burning firewood or agricultural waste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_poverty_and_cooking) for cooking and heating. Other sources of air pollution include [dust storms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_storm) and [volcanic eruptions](8921015.md). Many sources of local air pollution, especially burning fossil fuels, also release [greenhouse gases](21350772.md) that cause [global warming](5042951.md). However, air pollution may limit warming locally.

Air pollution kills 7 to 8 million people each year. It is a significant [risk factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk_factor) for a number of diseases, including [stroke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stroke), [heart disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart_disease), [chronic obstructive pulmonary disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronic_obstructive_pulmonary_disease) (COPD), [asthma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asthma), [coronavirus](201983.md) and [lung cancer](18450.md). [Particulate matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particulate_matter) is the most deadly, both for indoor and outdoor air pollution. Ozone affects crops, and forests are damaged by the pollution that causes [acid rain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_rain). Overall, the [World Bank](36752.md) has estimated that welfare losses (premature deaths) and [productivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Productivity) losses (lost labor) caused by air pollution cost the [world economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_economy) over \$8 trillion per year.

Various technologies and strategies reduce air pollution. Key approaches include [clean cookers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clean_cooking), [fire protection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_protection), improved [waste management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waste_management), [dust control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust#Control), [industrial scrubbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scrubber), [electric vehicles](279350.md) and [renewable energy](25784.md). National air quality laws have often been effective, notably the 1956 [Clean Air Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clean_Air_Act_1956) in Britain and the 1963 [US Clean Air Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clean_Air_Act_\(United_States\)). International efforts have had mixed results: the [Montreal Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montreal_Protocol) almost eliminated harmful [ozone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone_layer)-depleting chemicals, while [international action on climate change](2119179.md) has been less successful.

## Sources

### Human sources

#### Industry and construction

[Demolition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demolition) of the cooling towers of a power station, [Athlone, Cape Town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athlone,_Cape_Town), South Africa, 2010.

A factory with chimneys releasing smoke.

Before [flue-gas desulfurization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flue-gas_desulfurization) was installed, the emissions from this power plant in [New Mexico](21649.md) contained excessive amounts of [sulfur dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfur_dioxide).

Burning fuel to [generate electricity](9540.md) causes air pollution; [lignite and coal produce the most air pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_and_environmental_impact_of_the_coal_industry), followed by oil, and then by [fossil gas](22131.md) and [biomass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomass).[^cite_note-1][^cite_note-OWID_SafestEnergy_2021-2] [Methane leaks](73651871.md) are common in oil and gas production,[^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4] and [oil refineries](195137.md) emit a wide range of pollutants.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESinghSingh2019166-5] Some hazardous air pollutants are produced in plastic and rubber production, whereas [chloroform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroform) can be produced during [water chlorination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_chlorination), and [arsenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arsenic_poisoning) is found in the mining industry.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHill2020103-6] Many polluting industries have been pushed out of richer nations, and China too has started to push its most polluting industries out of the country.[^cite_note-7]

[Construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Construction) and [demolition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demolition) produces dust, but also other pollutants. The direct particles from construction and demolition are relatively coarse.[^cite_note-DEFRA_PM-8][^cite_note-9] Construction also has an indirect impact on air quality, as [cement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cement) production is one of the main sources of particle pollution.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESinghSingh2019166-5] Though banned in many countries, [asbestos](21492663.md) persists in older buildings, where it poses a risk of lung disease when disturbed.[^cite_note-10] Building materials including carpeting and [plywood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plywood) emit [formaldehyde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formaldehyde), a gas which can cause difficulty breathing and [nausea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nausea).[^cite_note-\:6-11]

#### Transportation

See also: [Exhaust gas § Main motor vehicle emissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exhaust_gas#Main_motor_vehicle_emissions)

Road vehicles produce a significant amount of all air pollution. For instance, they may be responsible for a third to half of all [nitrogen dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_dioxide) emissions,[^cite_note-pmid31143547-12][^cite_note-pmid26142107-13] and are a major cause of [climate change](5042951.md).[^cite_note-14] Vehicles with petrol and diesel engines produce about half of their emissions from their [exhaust gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exhaust_gas), and the other half from [non-exhaust emissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-exhaust_emissions) (tire and brake wear and erosion or disturbance of the road surface); [electric vehicles](279350.md) produce no tailpipe emissions, but still produce the other emissions.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEAmato20184-15] [Diesel trains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diesel_locomotive), ships, and [planes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airplane) also cause air pollution.[^cite_note-16]

#### Agriculture and waste

Smoke rises from a fire, and is dispersed by wind

[Controlled burning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controlled_burning) of a field outside of [Statesboro, Georgia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statesboro,_Georgia), US, in preparation for spring planting.

Several people are working on a heap of rubbish, with smoke rising from it.

[E-waste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_waste) processing in [Agbogbloshie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agbogbloshie), Ghana, using open-burning of electronics to access valuable metals like copper.

[Agricultural emissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impact_of_agriculture), both from crops and from [animal agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_impacts_of_animal_agriculture), contribute substantially to air pollution.[^cite_note-17][^cite_note-10.1038/nature15371-18] For instance, [methane](18582230.md) is emitted by the [digestion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digestion) of food by [cattle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cattle), causing ground-level ozone.[^cite_note-19] Agriculture is also a major source of ammonia, which can form fine particulate matter.[^cite_note-20] Practices like [slash-and-burn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slash-and-burn) in forests like the [Amazon](48139.md) cause large air pollution alongside [deforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation).[^cite_note-21]

[Open dumps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_dump) of waste are a common source of air pollution in low-income countries. They can be a source of toxins and can promote the growth of microbes that pollute water and air. Through [open burning of waste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_burning_of_waste)—whether [self-ignited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_combustion) or burned on purpose—soot, methane, and other pollutants are released.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEUnited_Nations_Environmental_Programme202137–39-22] Organic waste in landfills itself also produces methane as it decomposes.[^cite_note-23] Globally, a quarter of [solid waste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Municipal_solid_waste) is not collected and another quarter is not disposed of properly.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTECookVelis20207-24]

#### Household sources

Main articles: [Household air pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Household_air_pollution), [Indoor air quality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indoor_air_quality), and [Energy poverty and cooking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_poverty_and_cooking)

a round metal open fire with small fish

Smoking fish over an open fire in Ghana, 2018.

As of 2023, more than 2.3 billion people in [developing countries](78449.md) rely on burning polluting fuels such as [firewood](481098.md), agricultural waste, [dry dung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dry_dung_fuel), [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal), or [charcoal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charcoal) for cooking, which causes harmful [household air pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Household_air_pollution).[^cite_note-25] [Kerosene](16992.md), another polluting fuel, is used in many countries for [lighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lighting) and sometimes for space heating or cooking. Globally, 12% of outdoor fine particle pollution comes from household cooking. Health effects are concentrated among women, who are likely to be responsible for cooking, and young children.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWorld_Health_Organization2016vii–xi-26]

[Gas stoves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_stove) for cooking contribute to indoor air pollution by emitting NO<sub>2</sub>, [benzene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzene), and carbon monoxide.[^cite_note-27] [Toasters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toaster) can produce particulate pollution.[^cite_note-28] Similarly, [heating systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heating_system) such as [furnaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Furnace_\(central_heating\)) and other types of fuel-burning heating devices release pollutants into the air.[^cite_note-29] In some developed countries, including the UK and Sydney, Australia, [wood stoves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood_stoves) are the major source of particulate pollution in urban areas.[^cite_note-30][^cite_note-auto-31] Wood stoves can also emit carbon monoxide and NO<sub>*x*</sub>.[^cite_note-\:6-11]

Other sources of indoor air pollution are building materials, biological material and tobacco smoke. Biological material, such as [dander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dander), [house dust mite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_dust_mite), [mold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mold) and [pollen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollen), can come from humans, animals or plants. Some of this material can trigger allergies, such as [allergic rhinitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allergic_rhinitis).[^cite_note-\:6-11] Fumes from [pesticides](48340.md), paints, [cleaning products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleaning_agent) and [personal care products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_care_products) can be substantial, and make up an increasing share of outdoor and indoor air pollution as transportation is getting cleaner.[^cite_note-32]

### Natural sources

A dust storm approaches a collection of houses, dwarfing them in height.

Dust storm approaching [Stratford, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratford,_Texas), in 1935.

[Dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust) from desert can cause poor air quality far from its source. For instance, dust from the [Gobi Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gobi_Desert) in China and Mongolia can reach [Hawaii](13270.md), and dust from the [Sahara](325363.md) reaches the [Amazon rainforest](48139.md) in South America.[^cite_note-33]

[Radon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radon) is a radioactive gas that can build up in buildings from the [soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil). It can cause [lung cancer](18450.md), especially in [smokers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoking). Levels are generally low, but can be elevated in buildings with "leaky" foundations or areas with soils rich in [uranium](31743.md).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarrisonHester201957–58-34] Volcanic eruptions can be a large source of sulfur dioxide and also produce particle pollution.[^cite_note-35]

[Vegetation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetation) can emit gases that contribute to ozone formation and particle pollution. This is especially true in warmer climates and during the growth season.[^cite_note-36] These gases react with human pollution sources to produce a seasonal haze.[^cite_note-37] [Black gum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_gum), poplar, oak and [willow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willow) emit gases that can raise ozone levels up to eight times more than low-impact tree species.[^cite_note-38] [Wildfires](56106.md), which have become more severe and more common due to climate change, release fine particles. They are a major source of air pollution.[^cite_note-39]

## Major pollutants

Main articles: [Pollutant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollutant) and [Greenhouse gas emissions](9528025.md)

Schematic showing (1) CO2 and CH4 from heating, driving and livestock causing warming (2) PM pollution from driving (3) CFCs from buildings depleting ozone, causing UV to come through (4) SO2 from combustion causing acid rain and (5) ground-level ozone from nitrous oxides and (6) nitrous oxides from car exhausts.

Major pollutants, their sources and effects: (1) [greenhouse gases](21350772.md), (2) [particulate pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particulate_matter), (3) [ozone-depleting gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone_depletion), (4) [acid rain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_rain), (5) [ground-level ozone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground-level_ozone), (6) [nitrogen oxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_oxides)

Air pollutants can be tiny solid or liquid particles dispersed in the air (called [aerosols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerosol)), or gases.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPearsonDerwent20221,_6-40] Pollutants are classified as primary or secondary. Primary pollutants are produced directly by a source and remain in the same chemical form after they have been emitted into the atmosphere. Examples include [carbon monoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide) gas from car exhausts and sulfur dioxide from factories. Secondary pollutants are not emitted directly. Rather, they form in the air when primary pollutants react with each other or with other parts of the atmosphere. [Ground-level ozone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground-level_ozone) is one example of a secondary pollutant. Some pollutants may be both primary and secondary — both are emitted directly and formed from other primary pollutants.[^cite_note-41]

### Ammonia

[Ammonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonia_pollution) (NH<sup></sup>\
<sub>3</sub>) is emitted mainly by overuse of synthetic nitrogen [fertilizers](37401.md) on farmland, and from [manure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manure) and urine from [livestock](25160767.md).[^cite_note-42] At typical concentrations in the air, it is not harmful to health directly. However, ammonia can react with other pollutants in the air to form [ammonium sulfate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonium_sulfate) or [nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonium_nitrate) salts, contributing to particulate matter pollution. Furthermore, when ammonia is [deposited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deposition_\(aerosol_physics\)) onto the soil, it can harm ecosystems via [eutrophication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eutrophication).[^cite_note-43]

### Carbon dioxide

[Carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) (CO<sub>2</sub>) is mainly emitted by the burning of fossil fuels.[^cite_note-44] CO<sub>2</sub> is sometimes called an air pollutant, because it is the main [greenhouse gas](21350772.md) responsible for [climate change](5042951.md).[^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46] Although the [World Health Organization](33583.md) recognizes CO<sub>2</sub> as a climate pollutant, it does not include the gas in its *Air Quality Guidelines* or set recommended targets for it.[^cite_note-47] This question of terminology has practical consequences, for example, in determining whether the U.S. [Clean Air Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clean_Air_Act_\(United_States\)) (which is designed to improve air quality) is deemed to regulate CO<sub>2</sub> emissions. The [Inflation Reduction Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation_Reduction_Act) of 2022 amended the Clean Air Act to define CO<sub>2</sub> from fossil fuel burning explicitly as an air pollutant.[^cite_note-48]

### Carbon monoxide

[Carbon monoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide) (CO) is a colorless, odorless, and toxic gas.[^cite_note-49] It is a product of [combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combustion) of fuel such as natural gas, coal, or wood. In the past, emissions from vehicles were the main source of CO, but modern vehicles do not emit much of it. Now, wildfires and [bonfires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonfire) are the main source of outdoors CO.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPearsonDerwent20222-50] Indoors, CO is a larger problem and mainly comes from cooking and heating.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPearsonDerwent202219–20-51] In poorly ventilated spaces, CO can [accumulate to dangerous levels](488743.md), and exposure may cause people to lose consciousness and die. When CO is destroyed in the atmosphere, it can raise levels of CO<sub>2</sub> and CH<sub>4</sub>.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHill202099-52]

### Ground-level ozone

leaves showing patches of white between the veins

Leaves damaged by exposure to ozone.

[Ground-level ozone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground-level_ozone) (O<sup></sup>\
<sub>3</sub>) is mostly created when NO<sup></sup>\
<sub>x</sub> and volatile organic compounds mix in the presence of sunlight. It can also form from carbon monoxide or methane.[^cite_note-EEA-ozone-53] Due to the influence of temperature and sunlight on this reaction, high ozone levels are most common on hot summer afternoons.[^cite_note-54] It is the main gas in [photochemical smog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photochemical_smog).[^cite_note-55]

O<sup></sup>\
<sub>3</sub> can be harmful to human health, but also to some materials, forests, plants, and crops.[^cite_note-56] Smog is a particular problem in big cities where it cannot easily be transported away by wind (e.g. cities built in valleys surrounded by mountains).[^cite_note-57] When ground-level ozone is produced, it can linger in the air for days or weeks, and therefore be transported far from where it was first formed.[^cite_note-EEA-ozone-53]

[Nitrogen oxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_oxides) (NO<sup></sup>\
<sub>x</sub>), particularly nitric oxide (NO), are mostly created by the burning of fossil fuels, and in lesser amounts by [lightning](61344.md). [Nitrogen dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_dioxide) (NO<sup></sup>\

### Particulate matter

[Particulate matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particulate_matter) (PM), also known as particle pollution, includes all airborne substances that are not gases.[^cite_note-DEFRA_PM-8][^cite_note-EPA_PM-62] It is a mix of microscopic solid particles or droplets suspended in a gas.[^cite_note-63]

Particulate matter can contain a large variety of materials and chemical compounds including toxic substances, which can vary strongly in size.[^cite_note-DEFRA_PM-8] Coarse PM (PM<sub>10</sub>) is 10 micrometer (μm) or smaller in diameter, fine PM (PM<sub>2.5</sub>) is smaller than 2.5 μm, and [ultrafine particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrafine_particle) are 0.1 μm or smaller.[^cite_note-\:16-64] Smaller particles pose more risk to health, as they can reach the bloodstream.[^cite_note-EPA_PM-62][^cite_note-\:16-64] A definitive link between fine particulate pollution and higher death rates in urban areas was established by the [Harvard Six Cities study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_Six_Cities_study), published in 1993.[^cite_note-laden-65]

[Sea spray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_spray), wildfires, volcanoes and [dust storms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_storm) are the main natural sources of PM. Meanwhile, human sources include the burning of biomass and fossil fuels, as well as road emissions and dust resuspension. Human PM is usually finer than natural PM.[^cite_note-66] Most particulate matter is formed in the atmosphere from precursor gases. For instance, [sulfate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfate) comes from SO<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub>, [nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrate) from NO<sup></sup>\
<sub>2</sub>, and [ammonium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonium) is formed from ammonia. [Soot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soot) on the other hand is directly emitted from combustion, and consists of black carbon and organic compounds.[^cite_note-67] [Particulate matter can have a cooling effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiative_forcing#Forcing_due_to_changes_in_albedo_and_aerosols) locally on the climate, as it reflects sunlight away from Earth's surface.[^cite_note-68][^cite_note-69][^cite_note-70][^cite_note-71][^cite_note-72]

### Sulfur dioxide

[Sulfur dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfur_dioxide) (SO<sub>2</sub>), an acidic and corrosive gas, is produced mostly by burning [crude oil](23195.md) and coal. These fossil fuels often contain sulfur compounds, and their combustion generates sulfur dioxide.[^cite_note-73] In Europe and North America, SO<sub>2</sub> is mostly found in areas with significant shipping and industry, as road traffic fuels are regulated.[^cite_note-74] Smaller amounts of SO<sub>2</sub> are released from [smelting](28716.md) and volcanoes.[^cite_note-usepaso2-75]

High concentrations of SO<sub>2</sub> in the air generally also lead to the formation of other sulfur oxides (SO<sub>x</sub>). SO<sub>x</sub> can react with other compounds in the atmosphere to form small particles and contribute to particulate matter pollution. At high concentrations, gaseous SO<sub>x</sub> can harm plants by damaging [leafs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaf) and decreasing growth.[^cite_note-usepaso2-75] Further oxidation of SO<sub>2</sub>, mostly taking place in cloud droplets, forms [sulfuric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfuric_acid) (H<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub>), which is one of the components of [acid rain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_rain).[^cite_note-76]



[Smog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smog) polluting the air.

### Volatile organic compounds

[Volatile organic compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volatile_organic_compounds) (VOCs) are a class of carbon-based chemicals that exist as gases at room temperature, found both indoors and outdoors.[^cite_note-epa-voc-77] They can cause [photochemical smog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photochemical_smog) and form aerosols impacting climate. The group includes methane, [acetone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetone), and [toluene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toluene). Some can [cause cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carcinogen), such as [butadiene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butadiene) and [benzene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzene),[^cite_note-78] with benzene being released from [cigarette smoking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobacco_smoking). Methane is a greenhouse gas and the second-largest driver of [global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming). Other VOCs contribute to climate warming because they help form ground-level ozone, a greenhouse gas.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEPearsonDerwent20224–5-79]

### Other pollutants

Some [heavy metals can be bad for health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal_toxicity). For instance, [lead](17747.md) exposure can lead to [learning disabilities](10970082.md) in children. In the atmosphere, heavy metals can exist in different states, such as particles or gases. [One of the forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexavalent_chromium) of [chromium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromium) can cause [cancer](105219.md). [Mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_\(element\)) is harmful both as an element and in an organic compound. In the atmosphere, it comes mostly from [cement production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cement_kiln), coal burning, and incinerators.[^cite_note-80]

[Persistent organic pollutants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persistent_organic_pollutant) (POPs) are organic compounds that are resistant to [environmental degradation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_degradation). They persist in the environment, are [capable of long-range transmission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grasshopper_effect), [bioaccumulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioaccumulate) in humans and animals, and [biomagnify](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomagnification) in food chains.[^cite_note-\:17-81] The [Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm_Convention_on_Persistent_Organic_Pollutants) identified [pesticides](48340.md) and other POPs of concern. These include [dioxins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polychlorinated_dibenzodioxins) and [furans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polychlorinated_dibenzofurans) which are created by waste combustion. POPs are usually either semi-volatile (gaseous only at higher temperatures) or non-volatile (emitted as particles). The harmful effects of the pesticide [DDT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DDT), a POP, were popularized by Rachel Carson's 1962 book *[Silent Spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silent_Spring)*.[^cite_note-82] [PFASs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PFASs) and [polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polycyclic_aromatic_hydrocarbon) (PAHs) are other examples of POPs.[^cite_note-83]

[Chlorofluorocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorofluorocarbons) (CFCs) are a group of compounds which harm the [ozone layer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone_layer). They were widely used in [aerosol sprays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerosol_sprays), refrigerants, and [fire suppression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_retardant). Due to their chemical stability, CFCs persist in the atmosphere and eventually reach the [stratosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratosphere) (the upper atmosphere).[^cite_note-84] There, they break down under the impact of [UV light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UV_light), which releases [chlorine](5667.md). This in turn reacts with ozone, destroying it. As the ozone layer blocks harmful UV radiation from reaching the Earth's surface, its depletion leads to health risks such as [skin ageing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skin_ageing) and [skin cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skin_cancer).[^cite_note-85]

Exposure to air pollution varies widely across the world and across groups.[^cite_note-86] Children, for example, are more exposed because they breathe more rapidly than adults and closer to the ground, where pollution from vehicle exhaust and dust is more concentrated.[^cite_note-HEI_2024_p21-23-87] Similarly, people engaging in strenuous exercise inhale more pollutants than those at rest.[^cite_note-\:7-88] People can reduce their exposure by wearing [high-quality face masks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N95_respirator) or by using [air purifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_purifier).[^cite_note-89]

most regions show that 100% of the population was exposed to unhealthy air pollution using the less strict 2017 WHO guidelines.

Share of the population exposed to air pollution levels above WHO guidelines, 2017.

For some pollutants, low exposure can be seen as safe, whereas other pollutants have negative health effects even at low levels.[^cite_note-WHO_energy_health-90] As evidence has grown that even very low levels of air pollutants hurt human health, the WHO halved its recommended safe limit for particulate matter from 10 μg/m<sup>3</sup> to 5 μg/m<sup>3</sup> in 2021. Under the new guideline, nearly the entire global population—97%—is classified as exposed to unsafe levels of fine particles (PM<sub>2.5)</sub>.[^cite_note-91] The new limit for [nitrogen dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_dioxide) (NO<sub>2</sub>) became 75% lower.[^cite_note-92] For all pollutants together, the World Health Organization concluded that 99% of the world population is exposed to harmful air pollution.[^cite_note-\:13-93]

For some pollutants such as [black carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_carbon), traffic related exposures may dominate total exposure despite short exposure times, since high concentrations coincide with proximity to major roads or participation in (motorized) traffic.[^cite_note-Dons_et_al.-94] A large portion of total daily exposure occurs as short peaks of high concentrations.[^cite_note-Dons_et_al._2019-95]

### By socioeconomic group

Main articles: [Toxic hotspot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toxic_hotspot) and [Environmental justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_justice)

While air pollution affects a variety of populations, some groups are more exposed. In many regions, there are disparities in exposure to pollution by race and income. This is especially true in countries with high [inequalities in incomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Income_inequality) and healthcare, such as the United States. Polluting industries and roads are more likely to be placed in poorer communities, and people in these communities are more likely to work outdoors, leading to additional exposure.[^cite_note-\:10-96] Residents in public housing, who are generally low-income and cannot easily move to healthier neighborhoods, are highly affected by nearby [refineries](339530.md) and chemical plants.[^cite_note-97] Additionally, lower-income communities more often perform polluting activities, such as using solid biofuels for cooking.[^cite_note-98][^cite_note-99] In the United States, Blacks and Latinos generally face more pollution than Whites and Asians.[^cite_note-Drury-100]

## Health effects

Further information: [Environmental health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_health)



The share of total deaths from indoor air pollution, 2017.

Air pollution is an important risk factor for various diseases, such as [COPD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COPD) (a common lung disease), [stroke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stroke), heart disease, [lung cancer](18450.md), and [pneumonia](52135.md). Indoor air pollution is also associated with [cataract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cataract).[^cite_note-WHO_energy_health-90] According to the WHO, 99% of the world's population lives in areas with air pollution that exceeds WHO recommended levels.[^cite_note-105] Even at very low levels (under the [World Health Organization](33583.md) recommended levels), fine particulates can continue to cause harm.[^cite_note-HEI_2024_p7-8-106]

Pollutants strongly linked to ill health include particulate matter,[^cite_note-107] carbon monoxide,[^cite_note-108] nitrogen dioxide (NO<sub>2</sub>), ozone (O<sub>3</sub>),[^cite_note-109] and sulfur dioxide (SO<sub>2</sub>). Fine particulates are especially damaging, as they can enter the [bloodstream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circulatory_system) via the lungs and reach other organs.[^cite_note-110][^cite_note-111] Air pollution causes disease by driving [inflammation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systemic_inflammation) and [oxidative stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidative_stress), suppressing the [immune system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immune_system), and by damaging [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA).[^cite_note-WHO_energy_health-90]

People living in [poverty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poverty), babies, and [older people](229060.md) are disproportionately affected by air pollution; pregnancy is also more risky when exposed to air pollution.[^cite_note-HEI_2024_p4-112] Communities with a low [socioeconomic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socioeconomic) status and [minority groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minority_group) are more vulnerable to pollution than more privileged communities.[^cite_note-Morello-Frosch_2011-113] Lower-income groups might for instance have less [access to healthcare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Access_to_healthcare).[^cite_note-\:10-96]

### Mortality



Estimates of yearly deaths from air pollution range from 6.7 million to 8.8 million. In comparison, war caused 50,000 yearly deaths and terrorism 25,000.

map showing low death rates in Europe and the Americas, and high death rates in South and South East Asia, and Africa

Deaths in 2021 from air pollution per 100,000 inhabitants ([IHME](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Health_Metrics_and_Evaluation)).

Estimates of deaths due to air pollution vary.[^cite_note-114] The 2024 [Global Burden of Disease Study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Burden_of_Disease_Study) estimates that air pollution contributed to 8.1 million deaths in 2021, which is more than 1 in 8 deaths. Outdoor particulate pollution ([PM<sub>2.5</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PM2.5)) was the largest cause of death (4.7 million), followed by [indoor particulate pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Household_air_pollution) (3.1 million) and ozone (0.5 million).[^cite_note-HEI_2024_p3-4-115]

The World Health Organization estimates that 6.7 million people die from air pollution each year, 4.2 million due to outdoor air pollution.[^cite_note-who2024-116] Roughly 68% of outdoor air pollution-related premature deaths were due to [coronary heart disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronary_artery_disease) and stroke, 14% due to COPD, and 14% due to lung infections ([lower respiratory tract infections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_respiratory_tract_infection)).[^cite_note-who2024-116]

A study published in 2019 estimated that, for 2015, the number was about 8.8 million, with 5.5 million of these premature deaths due to air pollution from human sources.[^cite_note-117][^cite_note-2019-03-12-guardian-118] The global mean [loss of life expectancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Years_of_potential_life_lost) from air pollution in 2015 was 2.9 years, substantially more than, for example, 0.3 years from all forms of direct violence.[^cite_note-10.1093/cvr/cvaa025-119]

#### By region

Regional deaths due to air pollution depend not only on the [regional exposure](#By_geographic_area), but also on how large and how old the population is, and the health of people overall.[^cite_note-120]

In some countries, more than 20% of deaths are attributed to air pollution (e.g. China, Nepal, Bangladesh, Laos, and North Korea). In South America, about 4% of deaths are from air pollution, while in countries such as Australia, Canada, and the US, this number is under 3%.[^cite_note-121]

In absolute number, India and China have the higher number of deaths from air pollution. In India, it contributed to 2.1 million deaths in 2021, whereas China saw 2.4 million deaths.[^cite_note-122] Annual premature European deaths from air pollution are estimated at 416,000[^cite_note-123] to 800,000.[^cite_note-2019-03-12-guardian-118] The UK saw some 17,000 deaths in 2021 due to air pollution and the US saw 64,000. Nigeria, Indonesia and Pakistan each saw over 200,000 deaths resulting from air pollution.[^cite_note-124]

### Cardiovascular disease

There is strong evidence that air pollution increases the risk of [cardiovascular disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiovascular_disease), including [stroke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stroke), [high blood pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypertension), and [coronary heart disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronary_artery_disease).[^cite_note-de_BontJaganathanDahlquist2022-131] According to the Global Burden of Disease Study, air pollution is responsible for 27% of deaths from strokes worldwide and 28% of coronary heart disease.[^cite_note-HEI_2024_p27-132] The risks are highest in regions with higher air pollution (e.g. Asia), for elderly and for people who are overweight.[^cite_note-de_BontJaganathanDahlquist2022-131]

Air pollution is a leading risk factor for stroke, particularly in developing countries where pollutant levels are highest.[^cite_note-HEI_2024_p27-132] A systematic analysis of 17 different risk factors in 188 countries found air pollution is associated with nearly one in three strokes (29%) worldwide (34% of strokes in developing countries versus 10% in developed countries).[^cite_note-Mayor2016-133][^cite_note-FeiginRothNaghavi2016-134] The mechanisms linking air pollution to increased cardiovascular mortality are not fully understood, but likely include [systemic inflammation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systemic_inflammation) and [oxidative stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidative_stress).[^cite_note-135]

### Lung disease

Air pollution is associated with increased development, hospitalization, mortality, and [COPD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronic_obstructive_pulmonary_disease) (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease).[^cite_note-\:22-136] COPD is a common disease which causes restricted airflow and breathing difficulties, and is the fourth-largest cause of death globally.[^cite_note-137] Nearly half of global COPD deaths are due to air pollution.[^cite_note-HEI_2024_p27-132] Fine particles (PM<sub>2.5</sub>) and NO<sub>2</sub> are associated with increased risk of developing COPD.[^cite_note-138] In children, air pollution can hinder lung development, which may increase their susceptibility to COPD later in life.[^cite_note-\:22-136]

Air pollution is further associated with increased risk of asthma and worsening of symptoms, and this effect seems stronger in children.[^cite_note-139] For adults, fine particles (PM<sub>2.5</sub>) or NO<sub>2</sub> seem linked to [asthma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asthma) onset too.[^cite_note-140] Short-term exposure to ozone makes asthma worse in children.[^cite_note-HEI_2024_p18-141] There is limited evidence on (almost) fatal asthma attacks in children: ground-level ozone and PM<sub>2.5</sub> seem to increase its risk.[^cite_note-142]

### Cancer

Dark factory clouds obscure the Clark Avenue Bridge in Cleveland, Ohio, July 1973.

Dark factory-emitted clouds obscuring the Clark Avenue Bridge in Cleveland, Ohio in July 1973.

About 265,000 lung cancer deaths were attributed globally in 2019 to exposure to [fine particulate matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PM2.5) (PM<sub>2.5</sub>) suspended in the air.[^cite_note-\:12-143] Exposure to indoor air pollution, including radon, caused another 170,000 lung cancer deaths.[^cite_note-\:12-143] Lung cancer was also more common among people exposed to NO<sub>2</sub> and black carbon.[^cite_note-144]

Outdoor air pollution may increase risk of other types of cancer too, but the evidence is not as clear as for lung cancer.[^cite_note-\:11-145] For example, there may be a relationship between [kidney cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kidney_cancer) and PM<sub>2.5</sub> and NO<sub>2</sub> levels.[^cite_note-146] Household air pollution – from cooking with solid fuels, but also from radon in building material – has been associated with [cervical](53338.md), [oral](414186.md), and [esophageal cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esophageal_cancer).[^cite_note-\:11-145]

### Pregnancy and children

[Stillbirths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stillbirth), [miscarriages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miscarriage), and [birth defects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_defect) are all more likely when the mother is exposed to air pollution during pregnancy.[^cite_note-HEI_2024_p21-23-87] Exposure to air pollution also raises the chance that a baby has a [low birth weight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_birth_weight). The impacts might be due to pollutants directly impacting the [placenta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Placenta) or [fetus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fetus), or indirectly via the mother's health (as air pollution can cause systemic inflammation and oxidative stress).[^cite_note-HEI_2024_p21-23-87]

Over a third of preterm births were associated with air pollution in 2021 globally. It causes more than half a million newborn deaths, a quarter of overall deaths.[^cite_note-HEI_2024_p21-23-87] The source of PM<sub>2.5</sub> differs greatly by region. In South and East Asia, pregnant women are frequently exposed to indoor air pollution because of wood and other [biomass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomass_heating_system) fuels being used for cooking, which are responsible for more than 80% of regional pollution. In the Middle East, North Africa, and West sub-Saharan Africa, fine PM comes from natural sources, such as [dust storms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_storm).[^cite_note-\:0-147]

For data including older children, polluted air resulted in the death of over 700,000 children in 2021 (709,000 under 5 years of age and 16,600 aged 5–14 years).[^cite_note-HEI_2024_p21-23-87] Children in low- or middle-income countries are exposed to higher levels of fine particulate matter than those in high income countries.[^cite_note-\:8-148] Further health effects of air pollution on children include asthma, pneumonia, and lower respiratory tract infections.[^cite_note-\:9-149] There is possibly a link between exposure to air pollution during pregnancy and after birth and [autism](29113700.md) in children.[^cite_note-150][^cite_note-151]

Many of these relationships could previously only be described as correlations, as study designs that demonstrate [causation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correlation_does_not_imply_causation) are difficult or impossible to conduct in environmental medicine. This would require a [randomized controlled trial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randomized_controlled_trial). Scientists at [BIPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leibniz_Institute_for_Prevention_Research_and_Epidemiology_%E2%80%93_BIPS) in Bremen were able to demonstrate a causal relationship for at least some health problems (e.g. diabetes and high blood pressure) using a special study design.[^cite_note-152]

### Brain health

See also: [Brain health and pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brain_health_and_pollution)

Air pollution is linked to various diseases of the [brain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brain).[^cite_note-153] It increases the risk of [dementia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dementia).[^cite_note-154][^cite_note-155] Indoor air pollution exposure during childhood may negatively affect cognitive function and neurodevelopment.[^cite_note-156][^cite_note-10.1016/j.pharmthera.2020.107523-157] [Prenatal exposure](#Prenatal_exposure) may also affect neurodevelopment.[^cite_note-158][^cite_note-159] Exposure to air pollution may contribute to [neurodegenerative diseases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurodegenerative_disease) such as Parkinson's disease.[^cite_note-10.1016/j.pharmthera.2020.107523-157]

Exposure to air pollution may also drive [mental health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_health) issues, such as [depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depression_\(mood\)) and [anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety_disorder).[^cite_note-160] In particular, air pollution from the use of solid fuels was associated with a higher depression risk.[^cite_note-161] Depression risk and [suicide](16831059.md) was more strongly linked to finer particulate matter (PM<sub>2.5</sub>), compared to coarser particles (PM<sub>10</sub>). The association was strongest for people over the age of 65.[^cite_note-162]

Problems with thinking (cognitive issues) are also associated with air pollution. In people over the age of 40, both NO<sub>x</sub> and PM<sub>2.5</sub> have been linked to general cognitive problems. PM<sub>2.5</sub> was also associated with reduced [verbal fluency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verbal_fluency_test) (for instance, number of animals one can list in a minute) and worse [executive functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_functions) (like [attention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attention) and [working memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Working_memory)). Similarly, children tended to fare worse in tests involving working memory when there was NO<sub>x</sub>, PM<sub>2.5</sub>, or PM<sub>10</sub> pollution.[^cite_note-163]

### Physical activity

The health benefits of [physical exercise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_exercise) may be modulated by air quality. A 2025 cross-national study involving 1.5 million adults demonstrated that high levels of ambient fine particulate matter (PM <sub>2.5</sub> μg/m<sup>3</sup>) can significantly diminish the protective effects of leisure-time physical activity against all-cause and cause-specific mortality.[^cite_note-ku-164]

The study identified a critical threshold of 25 μg/m<sup>3</sup> exposure; below this annual average concentration, regular exercise reduced all-cause mortality by approximately 30%. However, this benefit was halved (to 12–15%) when concentrations exceeded 25 μg/m<sup>3</sup> exposure.[^cite_note-ku-164]

## Social and environmental impacts

### Acid rain

Statue with a nose eroded away

Monument damaged by acid rain.

dead tree trunks which have lost all their leafs

Effects of acid rain in the [Jizera Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jizera_Mountains), Czech Republic.

Naturally, water in the atmosphere is slightly acidic. Some pollutants can form strong acids, making rainwater much more acidic. Key acids that cause [acid rain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_rain) are [nitric acid](21655.md) (HNO<sub>3</sub>), [sulfuric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfuric_acid) (H<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub>) and [hydrochloric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrochloric_acid) (HCl). HCl comes from [coal combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_combustion). H<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub> forms from SO<sub>2</sub>, which comes from the burning of coal and oil and from some industrial processes like smelting. HNO<sub>3</sub> forms from NO<sub>2</sub>, which is formed during high-temperature combustion. The term acid rain not only refers to rain, but also to pollution from hail, fog, and snow.[^cite_note-Seigneur_acid_rain-165]

Acid rain caused substantial damage in the 1970s, including [lake acidification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_acidification) and [forest diebacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_dieback) in Northern Europe. Due to the changed acidity in water bodies and soils, essential nutrients such as [magnesium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium) and [calcium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium) became soluble and could be washed away. Other elements, such as [aluminium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium), which were toxic to vegetation, became available for the roots to absorb. Acid rain also impacts buildings and statues made of specific stones (e.g. [marble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marble), [calcite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcite) or [freestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freestone_\(masonry\))), as the stone reacts chemically with the acid in the water and erodes.[^cite_note-Seigneur_acid_rain-165]

### Water and soil pollution

Air pollution can settle ([deposit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deposition_\(aerosol_physics\))) on the soil or in water, causing various problems. For instance, ammonia and nitric acid in the air can contribute to nutrient [pollution in water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_pollution), a process called [eutrophication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eutrophication). At first, the extra nutrients help plants grow, but dense plant growth blocks sunlight from reaching the bottom. Plants in the lower layers then die, and with fewer plants producing oxygen, the oxygen level drops. This harms organisms that need oxygen to live,[^cite_note-166] and can lead to the loss of sensitive species.[^cite_note-167]

### Agricultural effects

Various studies have estimated the impacts of air pollution on [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture), especially ozone. Ozone acts as an [oxidant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidant) and reduces [photosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis). One study estimated that for a 1% increase in ozone concentrations, there would be a global economic loss of \$10 billion each year. For PM<sub>2.5</sub>, a 1% increase in pollution levels would lead to about \$5 billion in losses, especially in colder climates.[^cite_note-Dong2023-168] After air pollutants enter the agricultural environment, they not only directly affect agricultural production and quality, but also enter agricultural waters and soil.[^cite_note-169] Air pollution further decreases the [productivity of laborers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_productivity) via health impacts.[^cite_note-Dong2023-168]

The [COVID-19 lockdowns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_lockdowns) created a natural experiment to examine the links between air quality and agricultural output. In India, the lockdown improved air quality, which enhanced surface greenness and photosynthetic activity. Both forests and crops saw positive effects; the improvement was most pronounced for crops.[^cite_note-170]

### Economic effects

Air pollution has a strong impact on the economy via its health effects – such as reduced [productivity at work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Productivity) and the costs of [healthcare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_care) – and its effects on crop yield. It also affects [tourism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourism), biodiversity, [forestry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forestry), and [water quality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_quality). Tourism may be negatively affected due to decreased [visibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visibility) and damage to [cultural heritage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_heritage).[^cite_note-171] People may be more prone to accidents due to air pollution. Increased NO<sub>2</sub> levels are for instance linked to construction site accidents.[^cite_note-Lavy_Rachkovski_Yoresh_2022_p.-172]

In terms of the welfare cost on human health (non-market costs), a [World Bank](36752.md) study found that PM<sub>2.5</sub> pollution in 2019 cost the [world economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_economy) over \$8 trillion, over 6% of global [GDP](12594.md). In India and China, the loss of GDP was over 10%. About 85% of this loss globally came from the loss of life, the rest from increased ill health.[^cite_note-\:15-173]<sup>: 23–24 </sup> The costs of lives lost are calculated using the [Value of Statistical Life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_of_statistical_life), a number that tries to estimate how much people would be [willing to pay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willingness_to_pay) to reduce their risk of dying.[^cite_note-\:15-173]<sup>: xiii </sup> This number differs by country and is difficult to estimate for [low- and middle-income countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-_and_middle-income_countries).[^cite_note-174]

The direct market impacts on productivity loss, healthcare use, and crop losses were estimated to rise to 1% of GDP by 2060, according to the OECD. The [Caspian region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caspian_region) and [China](5405.md) would see the largest impact.[^cite_note-175] Air pollution also has an impact on energy production, as it reduces the amount of sunlight that reaches [solar panels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_panel). It also causes the panels to become dirty, further reducing their energy output.[^cite_note-176]

## History of air pollution

Mummified remains of people in [Peru](170691.md), [Egypt](8087628.md) and Britain show that ancient people in these regions suffered from [blackening of the lungs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_lung_disease) caused by open fires in poorly ventilated homes. Recorded complaints of air pollution go back to the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece) and [Roman period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome). Outdoor air pollution became a problem with the rise of cities, caused by household smoke and by early industrial activities (such as smelting and [mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining)). In particular, lead levels, found in Arctic ice cores, were about ten times higher in the Roman period than in the period before.[^cite_note-177]

### Industrial Revolution

During the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution), outdoor air pollution started to rise strongly, mostly due to the large-scale burning of coal. This occurred first in Britain, then in the rest of Northern Europe and the United States. By the 19th century, buildings around industrial plants started to blacken, while plants and trees in public parks started to wither. Smoke-induced fogs reduced the amount of sunlight city-dwellers got, contributing to cases of [rickets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rickets), a childhood disease caused by lack of sunlight and poor diet.[^cite_note-\:14-178]

However, the business and political leadership of the industrial cities were enthusiastic backers of the industry: heavy black smoke meant prosperity, high profits, and high wages.[^cite_note-179]

Poster in the Soviet Union praising "The smoke of chimneys is the breath of Soviet Russia."

### Trends

Globally, there has been a trend towards improved air quality worldwide from 2013 to 2023 with PM2.5 levels decreasing, particularly in Southeast Asia, East Asia and Oceania.[^cite_note-180]

The United States has seen many improvements in air quality in every major pollutants with significant reductions in carbon monoxide, nitrogen dioxide, lead and more in the 21st century.[^cite_note-181] However smoke pollution from wildfires in the early 2020s has slowed or reversed the progress made on air quality in many states.[^cite_note-182]

### Miasma theory

The [miasma theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miasma_theory) was a prominent idea in the 18th and 19th centuries that gave a false explanation of how deadly epidemics like [cholera](7591.md), [yellow fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_fever), and [malaria](20423.md) ("bad air") originated and spread. It said that illnesses were caused by breathing in a mysterious "miasma," a harmful vapor that arose from decaying organic matter. Epidemics often came in the summer because that is when people spent more time outside. The theory motivated an enormous emphasis on public sanitation in major cities to remove smelly pollution, especially human and animal excrement, from streets and back alleys. The theory collapsed when physicians accepted the new [germ theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germ_theory) of disease in the late 19th century. Germs coughed up by an infected person or spread by certain types of [mosquitoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosquito) or [hookworms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hookworm) were the real reason people caught an infectious disease.[^cite_note-183]

### Anti-smoke action and modern protections

In the 1830s, anti-smoke groups emerged in Britain, followed by groups in the United States in the 1880s. Legislation against pollution was weak however, as it was seen to conflict with industrial interests. During the [interwar period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interwar_period) of 1920s and 1930s, a move from coal to gas and oil meant there was less air pollution, but this trend reversed when [World War II](32927.md) broke out.[^cite_note-\:14-178] The United Kingdom suffered its worst air pollution during the 1952 [Great Smog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Smog) of London, with some 12,000 deaths, which led to the [Clean Air Act 1956](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clean_Air_Act_1956).[^cite_note-EHP_112_1-184] The [1948 Donora smog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1948_Donora_smog) in the US, killing 20 people, prompted the US to start regulating air pollution.[^cite_note-\:14-178][^cite_note-185] Japan followed in 1960, but other heavily polluted regions, such as the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) and China, did not implement effective regulation.[^cite_note-\:14-178]

Technological disasters have caused severe problems with air pollution. The world's worst pollution disaster was the 1984 [Bhopal Disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhopal_Disaster) in India. Leaked industrial vapours from the [Union Carbide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_Carbide) factory (later bought by the [Dow Chemical Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dow_Chemical_Company)), killed at least 20,000 people and affected about 600,000.[^cite_note-Mosley54-186]

In the 1950s, smog in developed countries was regulated, but other pollutants were not. Acid rain, caused by sulfur dioxide, became a major issue as it spread across borders. In the 1990s, for instance, Japan experienced acid rain from Chinese and Korean industry. International cooperation was needed to curb acid rain, and various coalitions were started. In 1975, it was discovered that certain chemicals caused a [hole in the ozone layer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hole_in_the_ozone_layer); thanks to successful international negotiations, these chemicals were banned worldwide. There has been far less success in combatting climate change, and [greenhouse gas emissions](9528025.md), mostly from fossil fuels, continue to rise.[^cite_note-Raiser2020-187]

## Measurement and monitoring

### Monitoring

Further information: [Air pollution measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_pollution_measurement) and [Environmental monitoring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_monitoring)

Display showing poor air quality for PM2.5 and PM10

Air quality monitoring, [New Delhi, India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Delhi,_India).

Air pollution can be monitored using different techniques. For instance, [satellites](27683.md) and [remote sensing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remote_sensing) are used to track PM, NO<sub>2,</sub> and ozone.[^cite_note-188] Many regions have a network of monitoring stations, with good coverage in India, China, Europe, and the US. Poor coverage exists however for a number of highly polluted countries, such as Chad and Iran. The density of measurements is improving as there are more low-cost techniques to measure air pollution.[^cite_note-189] Low-cost monitors can also be used for indoor air quality monitoring.[^cite_note-190] Finally, air quality sensors can be incorporated into [drones](58900.md) to measure air pollution higher up in the air.[^cite_note-191] Some websites attempt to map air pollution levels using available data.[^cite_note-192][^cite_note-193]

[Air quality indexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_quality_index) (AQIs) offer a simple way to communicate changes in air quality and associated health risks to a wide audience. An AQI is essentially a health protection tool people can use to help reduce their short-term exposure to air pollution by adjusting activity levels during increased levels of air pollution. These indexes can indicate when air quality is good, when it is dangerous for sensitive groups (e.g. children with asthma) and when it is a general hazard.[^cite_note-194]

### Modelling and inventories

Main article: [Air quality modeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_quality_modeling)

High resolution map showing wildfire smoke and desert dust over Africa and the Atlantic, sea salt particles in cyclones over the world ocean and sulphate particle pollution from industrial sites over Europe, North America and China

Map of simulated particle pollution. Wildfire smoke is in green, desert dust is in orange, light blue is [sea salt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_salt), and white shows sulfate particle pollution.

When direct data is unavailable or when projecting future air pollutant levels, estimates can be derived using models or emission factors.[^cite_note-195] Air pollutant emission factors are typical values that link the amount of a pollutant released into the air to a related activity. This could for instance be the typical amount of particulate matter released from a coal-power station.[^cite_note-196] The [United States Environmental Protection Agency](58666.md) has published a compilation of air pollutant emission factors for a wide range of industrial sources,[^cite_note-197] as did the [European Environment Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Environment_Agency).[^cite_note-198]

Air quality models use [meteorological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteorology) and emissions data to [simulate how pollutants disperse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_dispersion_modeling) and react in the atmosphere. Regulatory agencies use them to assess whether a new source of air pollution would exceed acceptable pollution levels, for permitting purposes. They can also be used to predict future pollution levels under different policy scenarios.[^cite_note-199] There are models for local pollution, but also for cross-boundary pollution.[^cite_note-200]

## Pollution reduction by sector

Pollution prevention seeks to prevent pollution such as air pollution and could include adjustments to industrial and business activities such as [designing sustainable manufacturing processes (and the products' designs)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_design#Design_for_sustainable_manufacturing)[^cite_note-201] as well as efforts towards [renewable energy transitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renewable_energy_transition).[^cite_note-202][^cite_note-203]

### Industry and waste

a smokestack attached to a metal box

[Thermal oxidisers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_oxidiser) are air pollution abatement options for [hazardous air pollutants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazardous_air_pollutant) (HAPs), [volatile organic compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volatile_organic_compounds) (VOCs), and odorous emissions.

Various pollution control technologies and strategies are available to reduce air pollution. For instance, industrial plants can install [scrubbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scrubber), such as [flue-gas desulfurization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flue-gas_desulfurization) or [catalysts to remove NO<sub>x</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selective_catalytic_reduction). In the [power sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_sector), a very effective means to reduce air pollution is the [transition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_transition) to [renewable energy](25784.md) (e.g. solar and wind energy) or [nuclear power](22153.md).[^cite_note-\:2-204] Switching from coal-fired power plants to fossil gas reduces air pollution, but does not eliminate it.[^cite_note-205]

A growing number of countries regulate waste, through national or city-wide [waste management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waste_management) systems, opening managed [landfills](211285.md), [landfill gas capture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landfill_gas_utilization) (for electricity production), and [waste separation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waste_sorting).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEUnited_Nations_Environmental_Programme202137–39-22] In agriculture, air pollution can be minimized by not overusing fertilizers and by not feeding excess protein to livestock.[^cite_note-\:2-204]

### Transport

Further information: [Sustainable transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_transport)

a row of electric rickshaws with a couple of people inside

Electric [rickshaws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rickshaws) in Nepal.

see caption

Tram in [Alicante](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alicante), Spain.

The [avoid-shift-improve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avoid-Shift-Improve) framework groups efforts to cut pollution from vehicles into reducing travel, shifting to sustainable transport, and improving vehicle technology.[^cite_note-\:4-206] Reducing motor vehicle travel can curb pollution. One strategy is to build [compact cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_city), so that amenities are close by and cars are not needed.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTERingensonKramers2021218-207] Motor traffic can be reduced by creating more [walkable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walkability) cities and by investing in [cycling infrastructure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycling_infrastructure).[^cite_note-208] [Working from home](158555.md) is another way of avoiding motorized traffic.[^cite_note-\:4-206]

Traffic can be shifted to cleaner [modes of transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mode_of_transport) by increasing use of [public transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_transport),[^cite_note-209] for instance through higher [parking fees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parking_fee) or offering [free public transport](11163017.md). Tackling congestion, which increases fuel usage, with [congestion charging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congestion_pricing), also shifts people to use cleaner modes of transport. Finally, road vehicles can be improved from increased [fuel efficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_efficiency), improved quality of fuels, emission standards, and conversion to electric vehicles.[^cite_note-210] For example, buses in New Delhi, India, switched to [compressed natural gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compressed_natural_gas) after 2000, to reduce the city's thick [pea soup smog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pea_soup_fog).[^cite_note-\:9-149][^cite_note-211]

In 2006, [Lawrence D. Frank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_D._Frank) and his co-authors published a study with over 1,930 citations: *Many Pathways from Land Use to Health.*[^cite_note-212] The authors demonstrated that a 5% increase in [walkability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walkability) was associated with many benefits, including a 6.5% reduction in vehicle miles driven, a 5.6% reduction in grams of nitrogen oxides emissions, and a 5.5% emission reduction of volatile organic compounds (VOCs).[^cite_note-213]

### Cooking, lighting and heating

a parabolic mirror concentrates heat into a placeholder for kettles and pans.

A kettle heated by a [solar cooker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_cooker) at the [Phugtal Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phugtal_Monastery) in [Ladakh](303611.md), India.

Various technologies are available for clean cooking, to replace traditional biomass stoves or [three-stone fires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-stone_fires?action=edit\&redlink=1). For example, a switch to cooking with [biogas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biogas), [bioethanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioethanol), electricity, natural gas, or LPG ([liquified petroleum gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquified_petroleum_gas)) significantly reduces air pollution. [Improved cook stoves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Improved_cook_stoves), which use biomass more efficiently, improve air quality less, but can be an intermediate solution if clean cook stoves or their fuels are not available. These clean cooking devices, including those run on fossil fuels, usually have a smaller climate impact than traditional [biomass stoves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomass_stove).[^cite_note-214]

Kerosene for lighting can be replaced with efficient [LED lights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LED_light), for instance, solar-powered LED lights.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWorld_Health_Organization201652-215] Combustion of fossil fuels for space heating can be replaced by the use of electricity in [heat pumps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_pumps).[^cite_note-216] [Ventilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ventilation_\(architecture\)) improves indoor air quality, but leads to outdoor air pollution, which can in turn reduce local [indoor air quality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indoor_air_quality).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEWorld_Health_Organization201618-217]

## Policy and regulation

### Laws and regulations

Main articles: [Air quality law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_quality_law) and [Emission standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emission_standard)

Cityscape in a dense smog

Air pollution in Shanghai, China, in 2013. China launched a "[war against pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_on_Pollution?action=edit\&redlink=1)" in 2014, which drastically lowered particle pollution levels across the country.

[^cite_note-218]

Although a majority of countries have air pollution laws, 43% of countries lack a legal definition of air pollution, 34% lack outdoor air quality standards, and just 31% have laws for tackling pollution originating from outside their borders. Few countries have limits that are as strict as the World Health Organization's recommendations.[^cite_note-unep2021-219]

Some air pollution laws include specific air quality standards, such as the U.S. [National Ambient Air Quality Standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Ambient_Air_Quality_Standards) and E.U. Air Quality Directive,[^cite_note-220] which specify maximum atmospheric concentrations for specific pollutants. Other examples of air quality laws around the world include the [Clean Air Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clean_Air_Act_1956) in Britain, the [US Clean Air Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clean_Air_Act_\(United_States\)), and the [TA Luft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TA_Luft) in Germany.[^cite_note-221] Air pollution laws might also put limits on the *emissions* of air pollutants, e.g. from vehicles.[^cite_note-unep2021_p42-222]

The World Health Organization's [Global Air Quality Guidelines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_quality_guideline) encourage improvements in a similar way to national standards, but are "recommendations" and "good practice" rather than mandatory targets that countries must achieve.[^cite_note-who-guidelines-223]

Some air pollution action has been successful at the international level, such as the [Montreal Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montreal_Protocol),[^cite_note-\:20-224] which phased out harmful ozone-depleting chemicals. It was ratified worldwide. On the other hand, [international action on climate change](2119179.md), has been less successful.[^cite_note-\:21-225] The 1997 [Kyoto Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto_Protocol) introduced modest reduction targets for some countries but lacked strong enforcement,[^cite_note-Mosley86-226] while the 2015 [Paris Agreement](30242372.md) set no binding limits, instead encouraging all countries to raise their ambition over time.[^cite_note-Raiser2020-187]

### Clean air as a human right

In 2022, the [UN General Assembly](31957.md) passed a resolution recognizing the right to a clean, healthy, and sustainable environment as a human right. The resolution is not legally binding. This resolution followed the declaration from the [UN Human Rights Council](635790.md) published earlier that year.[^cite_note-227]

While many countries have air pollution laws, they differ in how they can be enforced via [litigation](113258.md). In the European Union, individual countries, including France, have been fined by the EU for not complying with air quality rules. The revised [Ambient Air Quality Directive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambient_Air_Quality_Directive) also makes it possible for individuals in the EU to seek compensation.[^cite_note-228] While China allows litigation on environmental grounds, it is rare as it is seen as risky.[^cite_note-229] In [Chile](5489.md), the right to a healthy environment is part of the [constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution), and the Supreme Court found that the government has to act to provide clear air because of this.[^cite_note-230]
