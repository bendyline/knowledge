# Art theft

This article is about theft of physical works of art. For plagiarism of art, sometimes also called "art theft", see [Plagiarism § In the arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plagiarism#In_the_arts).



The [Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isabella_Stewart_Gardner_Museum) was [robbed in 1990](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isabella_Stewart_Gardner_Museum_theft), losing paintings and items valued at over \$500 million.

**Art theft** is the stealing of [paintings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paintings), [sculptures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sculpture), or other forms of [visual art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_arts) from [galleries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_gallery), [museums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_museum) or other public and private locations. Stolen [art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art) is often resold or used by criminals as [collateral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collateral_\(finance\)) to secure loans.[^cite_note-1] Only a small percentage of stolen art is recovered—an estimated 10%.[^cite_note-2] Many nations operate police squads to investigate art theft and illegal trade in stolen art and [antiquities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiquities).[^cite_note-police-3]

Some famous art theft cases include the robbery of the *[Mona Lisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mona_Lisa)* from the [Louvre](17546.md) in 1911 by employee [Vincenzo Peruggia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincenzo_Peruggia).[^cite_note-monalisa25-4] Another was the theft of *[The Scream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Scream)*, stolen from the [Munch Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munch_Museum) in 2004, but recovered in 2006.[^cite_note-bbctheft-5] The largest-value art theft occurred at the [Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isabella_Stewart_Gardner_Museum) in Boston, when 13 works, worth a combined \$500 million [were stolen in 1990](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isabella_Stewart_Gardner_Museum_theft). The case remains unsolved. Large-scale art thefts include the [Nazi looting of Europe](3005707.md) during World War II and the [Russian looting of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_theft_and_looting_by_Russia_during_the_invasion_of_Ukraine) during the [2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine](79400268.md).[^cite_note-Ukraine-6] More recently [French crown jewels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_crown_jewels) were [stolen from the Louvre in 2025](81376506.md).

## Individual theft

Many thieves are motivated by the fact that valuable art pieces are worth millions of dollars and weigh only a few kilograms at most. Also, while most high-profile museums have extremely tight security, many places with multimillion-dollar art collections have disproportionately poor security measures.[^cite_note-7] That makes them susceptible to thefts that are slightly more complicated than a typical [smash-and-grab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smash-and-grab), but offer a huge potential payoff. Thieves sometimes target works based on their own familiarity with the artist, rather than the artist's reputation in the art world or the theoretical value of the work.[^cite_note-rembrandts-8]

Unfortunately for the thieves, it is extremely difficult to sell the most famous and valuable works without getting caught, because any interested buyer will almost certainly know the work is stolen and advertising it risks someone contacting the authorities. It is also difficult for the buyer to display the work to visitors without it being recognized as stolen, thus defeating much of the point of owning the art. Many famous works have instead been held for ransom from the legitimate owner or even returned without ransom, due to the lack of black-market customers. Returning for ransom also risks a [sting operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sting_operation).[^cite_note-rembrandts-8]



[Jean-Baptiste Oudry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Oudry)'s *The White Duck*, which was stolen in 1990

For those with substantial collections, such as the [Marquess of Cholmondeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Cholmondeley,_7th_Marquess_of_Cholmondeley) at [Houghton Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houghton_Hall), the risk of theft is neither negligible nor negotiable.[^cite_note-9] [Jean-Baptiste Oudry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Oudry)'s *White Duck&#x20;*&#x20;was stolen from the Cholmondeley collection at Houghton Hall in 1990. The canvas is still missing.[^cite_note-10]

## Prevention in museums

Museums can take numerous measures to prevent the theft of artworks include having enough guides or guards to watch displayed items, avoiding situations where security-camera sightlines are blocked, and fastening paintings to walls with hanging wires that are not too thin and with locks.[^cite_note-rkhhii-11]

### Art theft education

The Smithsonian Institution sponsors the National Conference on Cultural Property Protection, held annually in Washington, D. C. The conference is aimed at professionals in the field of cultural property protection.

Since 1996, the Netherlands-based Museum Security Network has disseminated news and information related to issues of cultural property loss and recovery. Since its founding the Museum Security Network has collected and disseminated over 45,000 reports about incidents with cultural property. The founder of the Museum Security Network, Ton Cremers, is recipient of the National Conference on Cultural Property Protection Robert Burke Award.

2007 saw the foundation of the Association for Research into Crimes against Art (ARCA). ARCA is a nonprofit [think tank](37101.md) dedicated principally to raising the profile of art crime ([art forgery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_forgery) and [vandalism](32676.md), as well as theft) as an academic subject. Since 2009, ARCA has offered an unaccredited postgraduate certificate program dedicated to this field of study. The [Postgraduate Certificate Program in Art Crime and Cultural Heritage Protection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postgraduate_Certificate_Program_in_Art_Crime_and_Cultural_Heritage_Protection) is held from June to August every year in Italy. A few American universities, including [New York University](http://arthistory.as.nyu.edu/page/home), also offer courses on art theft.

## Recovery

In the public sphere, [Interpol](14338129.md), the [FBI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI) Art Crime Team, London's [Metropolitan Police](192450.md) [Art and Antiques Unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_and_Antiques_Unit), [New York Police Department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Police_Department)'s special frauds squad[^cite_note-police-3] and numerous other law enforcement agencies worldwide maintain "squads" dedicated to investigating thefts of this nature and recovering stolen works of art.

According to [Robert King Wittman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_King_Wittman), a former FBI agent who led the Art Crime Team until his retirement in 2008, the unit is very small compared with similar law-enforcement units in Europe, and most art thefts investigated by the FBI involve agents at local offices who handle routine property theft. "Art and antiquity crime is tolerated, in part, because it is considered a victimless crime," Wittman said in 2010.[^cite_note-rkhhii-11]

In response to a growing public awareness of art theft and recovery, a number of not-for-profit and private companies now act both to record information about losses and oversee recovery efforts for claimed works of art. Among the most notable are:

- [IFAR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Foundation_for_Art_Research)

- [Commission for Looted Art in Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commission_for_Looted_Art_in_Europe)

- [Holocaust Claims Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocaust_Claims_Conference)

- [Art Loss Register](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_Loss_Register)

- [Art Recovery Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_Recovery_Group)

In January 2017, Spain's [Interior Ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interior_Ministry) announced that police from 18 European countries, with the support of Interpol, [Europol](525329.md), and [Unesco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unesco), had arrested 75 people involved in an international network of art traffickers. The pan-European operation had begun in October 2016 and led to the recovery of about 3,500 stolen items including archaeological artifacts and other artwork. The ministry did not provide an inventory of recovered items or the locations of the arrests.[^cite_note-12]

In 1969 the Italian Ministry of Cultural Heritage and Activities and Tourism formed the Comando Carabinieri Tutela Patrimonio Culturale (TPC), better known as the Carabinieri Art Squad. In 1980, the TPC established the database Leonardo, with information about more than 1 million stolen artworks, and accessible to law enforcement agencies around the world.[^cite_note-13]

In December 2021 Michael Steinhardt, an American [hedge-fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedge-fund) [billionaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billionaire), was ordered to surrender 180 looted and illegally smuggled [antiquities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiquities) valued at 70 million U.S. dollars. The antiquities will be returned to their rightful owners and Steinhardt is banned for life from acquiring any other [relics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relic#Cultural_relics).[^cite_note-14]

In June 2026, French narcotics police discovered a stolen £13 million Pablo Picasso painting, a 1937 portrait of [Marie-Thérèse Walter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie-Th%C3%A9r%C3%A8se_Walter), during a routine drug raid in an [Ormesson-sur-Marne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ormesson-sur-Marne) home. A 37-year-old security guard from the art storage facility was arrested after the artwork was found alongside 17kg of [cannabis resin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis_resin) and luxury goods.[^cite_note-15]

## State theft, wartime looting and misappropriation by museums

From 1933 through the end of World War II, the [Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi) regime maintained a policy of [looting art](3005707.md) for sale or for removal to museums in the Third Reich. [Hermann Göring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_G%C3%B6ring), head of the [Luftwaffe](17885.md), personally took charge of hundreds of valuable pieces, generally stolen from Jews and other victims of [the Holocaust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Holocaust).

In early 2011, about 1,500 art masterpieces, assumed to have been stolen by the [Nazis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazis) during and before World War II, were [confiscated from a private home in Munich, Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornelius_Gurlitt_\(art_collector\)#Discovery_of_collection). The confiscation was not made public until November 2013.[^cite_note-16] With an estimated value of \$1 billion, their discovery is considered "astounding",[^cite_note-17] and includes works by [Pablo Picasso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pablo_Picasso), [Henri Matisse](60203.md), [Marc Chagall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marc_Chagall), [Paul Klee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Klee), [Max Beckmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Beckmann) and [Emil Nolde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emil_Nolde), all of which were considered lost.[^cite_note-guardian-18]

The looted, mostly [Modernist](163543.md) art was banned by the Nazis when they came to power, on the grounds that it was "un-German" or Jewish Bolshevist in nature.[^cite_note-19] Descendants of Jewish collectors who were robbed of their works by the Nazis may be able to claim ownership of many of the works.[^cite_note-guardian-18] Members of the families of the original owners of [these artworks](7208744.md) have, in many cases, persisted in claiming title to their pre-war property.

The 1964 film [*The Train*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Train_\(1964_film\)), starring [Burt Lancaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burt_Lancaster), is based on the true story of works of art which had been placed in storage for protection in France during the war, but was looted by the Germans from French museums and private art collections, to be shipped by train back to Germany. Another film, *[The Monuments Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Monuments_Men)* (2014), co-produced, co-written and directed by [George Clooney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Clooney), is based on a similar true-life story. In this film, U.S. soldiers are tasked with saving over a million pieces of art and other culturally important items throughout Europe, before their destruction by [Nazi plunder](3005707.md).

In 2006, after a protracted court battle in the United States and Austria (see *[Republic of Austria v. Altmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Austria_v._Altmann)*), five paintings by [Austrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrians) artist [Gustav Klimt](12425.md) were returned to [Maria Altmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Altmann), the niece of pre-war owner, Ferdinand Bloch-Bauer. Two of the paintings were portraits of Altmann's aunt, Adele. The more famous of the two, the gold *[Portrait of Adele Bloch-Bauer I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portrait_of_Adele_Bloch-Bauer_I)*, was sold in 2006 by Altmann and her co-heirs to philanthropist [Ronald Lauder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Lauder) for \$135 million. At the time of the sale, it was the highest known price ever paid for a painting. The remaining four [restituted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restitution) paintings were later sold at [Christie's New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christie's_East) for over \$190 million.

Because antiquities are often regarded by the country of origin as national treasures, there are numerous cases where artworks (often displayed in the acquiring country for decades) have become the subject of highly charged and political controversy. One prominent example is the case of the [Elgin Marbles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elgin_Marbles), which were moved from the [Parthenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenon) to the [British Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Museum) in 1816 by the [Earl of Elgin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Bruce,_7th_Earl_of_Elgin). Many different [Greek governments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Greece) have called for the [repatriation](1481067.md) of the marbles.[^cite_note-20]

Similar controversies have arisen over [Etruscan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etruscan_civilization), [Aztec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aztec), and [Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_art) artworks, with advocates of the originating countries generally alleging that the artifacts taken form a vital part of the countries [cultural heritage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_heritage). [Yale University](34273.md)'s [Peabody Museum of Natural History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peabody_Museum_of_Natural_History) is engaged (as of November 2006) in talks with the [government of Peru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Peru) about possible [repatriation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_repatriation) of [artifacts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artifact_\(archaeology\)) taken during the excavation of [Machu Picchu](80019.md) by Yale's [Hiram Bingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiram_Bingham_III). Likewise, the Chinese government considers Chinese art in foreign hands to be stolen and there may be a clandestine repatriation effort underway.[^cite_note-21]

In 2006, New York's [Metropolitan Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Museum) reached an agreement with Italy to return many [disputed pieces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illicit_antiquities). The [Getty Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getty_Museum) in Los Angeles is also involved in a [series of cases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marion_True) of this nature. The artwork in question is of Greek and ancient Italian origin. The museum agreed on November 20, 2006, to return 26 contested pieces to Italy. One of the Getty's signature pieces, a statue of the goddess [Aphrodite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aphrodite), is the subject of particular scrutiny.

In January 2013, after investigations by Interpol, FBI and The U.S. Department of Homeland Security, police in Canada arrested [John Tillmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Tillmann) for an enormous spate of art thefts. It was later determined that Tillmann in conjunction with his Russian wife, had for over twenty years stolen at least 10,000 different art objects from museums, galleries, archives and shops around the world. While not the largest art heist in total dollar value, Tillmann's case may be the largest ever in number of objects stolen.

Since its [invasion of Ukraine](79400268.md) in 2022, [Russia has stolen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_theft_and_looting_by_Russia_during_the_invasion_of_Ukraine) tens of thousands of art pieces.[^cite_note-Ukraine-6] Experts state that this is the largest art theft since the Nazi looting of Europe in World War II.[^cite_note-Ukraine-6]

## In fiction

Genres such as [crime fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime_fiction) often portray fictional art thefts as glamorous or exciting raising generations of admirers. Most of these sources add adventurous, even heroic element to the theft, portraying it as an achievement. In literature, a niche of the [mystery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mystery_\(fiction\)) genre is devoted to art theft and forgery. In film, a [caper story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caper_story) usually features complicated heist plots and visually exciting getaway scenes. In many of these movies, the stolen art piece is a [MacGuffin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacGuffin).[^cite_note-101]

### Literature

- *False Idols* by Patrick Lohier, Lisa Klink, and Diana Renn is a thriller about antiquities theft that starts in Cairo and spans the globe. The serial novel was written with input from famous [FBI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI) art detective [Robert King Wittman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_King_Wittman).

- Author [Iain Pears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iain_Pears) has a series of novels known as the *Art History Mysteries*, each of which follows a fictional shady dealing in the art history world.

- *St. Agatha's Breast* by T. C. Van Adler follows an order of monks attempting to track the theft of an early [Poussin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poussin) work.

- *The Man Who Stole the Mona Lisa* by Robert Noah is a [historical fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_fiction) speculating on the motivations behind the actual theft.

- *[Inca Gold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_Gold)* by [Clive Cussler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clive_Cussler) is a Dirk Pitt adventure about pre-Columbian art theft.

- Author [James Twining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Twining) has written a trio of novels featuring an art thief called Tom Kirk. The third book, *The Gilded Seal* is centered on a fictional theft of Da Vinci works, specifically, the *Mona Lisa*.

- [Ian Rankin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_Rankin)'s novel *[Doors Open](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doors_Open)* centers on an art heist organised by a bored businessman.

- *The Art Thief* by Noah Charney, a fiction quoting art thefts in history, some plots are based on the real theft of missing Caravaggio from Palermo. Through a character's mouth the author also gave his conclusion as how to narrow the circle of suspects for the famous robbery of the Boston Gardner Museum.

- *[Chasing Vermeer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chasing_Vermeer)* by Blue Balliett

- In *The Tenth Chamber* by Glenn Cooper, a fictional town hijacks a train and steals, among other artifacts, the *[Portrait of a Young Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portrait_of_a_Young_Man_\(Raphael\))* by Raphael (missing in real life), offering a fictional explanation as to its disappearance.

- *[Heist Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heist_Society)* by [Ally Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ally_Carter) is a [young adult fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_adult_fiction) novel depicting teens who rob the Henley.

- In the manga *[From Eroica With Love](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/From_Eroica_With_Love)*, British Earl, Dorian Red, Earl of Gloria, is the notorious art thief, *Eroica*.

- Art Historian Noah Charney's 2011 monograph, "The Thefts of the Mona Lisa: On Stealing the Worlds Most Famous Painting" (ARCA Publications) is a full account of the 1911 theft of the Mona Lisa from the Louvre Museum.

- In *If Tomorrow Comes* by [Sidney Sheldon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidney_Sheldon), a very cunning plan to steal a painting by [Francisco Goya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_Goya) was watched closely by an Interpol officer, but eventually succeeded.

- B.A. Shapiro's 2012 novel *The Art Forger* is a fictional modern literary thriller involving art-forgery and fraud years after the historical art-heist at the Isabella Steward Gardener Museum.

### Film

- *[Topkapi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topkapi_\(film\))* (1964) starring [Melina Mercouri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melina_Mercouri), [Maximilian Schell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximilian_Schell), and [Peter Ustinov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Ustinov), depicts the meticulously planned theft of an emerald-encrusted dagger from the Topkapi Museum in Istanbul.

- *[How to Steal a Million](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_to_Steal_a_Million)* (1966) starring [Peter O'Toole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_O'Toole) and [Audrey Hepburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audrey_Hepburn), about the theft from a Paris museum of a fake [Cellini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellini) sculpture to prevent its exposure as a forgery.

- *[Gambit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gambit_\(1966_film\))* (1966), starring [Michael Caine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Caine) and [Shirley MacLaine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shirley_MacLaine)

- *[Once a Thief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Once_a_Thief_\(1991\))* (1991), directed by [John Woo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Woo), follows a trio of art-thieves in Hong Kong who stumble across a valuable cursed painting.

- *[Hudson Hawk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hudson_Hawk)* (1991) centers on a cat burglar who is forced to steal Da Vinci works of art for a world domination plot.

- In *[Entrapment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entrapment_\(film\))* (1999), an insurance agent is persuaded to join the world of art theft by an aging master thief.

- *[Ocean's Twelve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean's_Twelve)* (2004) involves the theft of four paintings (including Blue Dancers by [Edgar Degas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Degas)) and the main plot revolves around a competition to steal a [Fabergé egg](327475.md).

- [Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vinci_\(film\)) (2004), a Polish art thief is hired to steal [Lady with an Ermine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lady_with_an_Ermine) by [Leonardo da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci) from the [Czartoryski Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czartoryski_Museum) in [Kraków](16815.md) and gets his former partner-turned police officer friend to help him.

- *[The Maiden Heist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Maiden_Heist)* (2009), three museum security guards who devise a plan to steal back the artworks to which they have become attached after they are transferred to another museum.

- *[Headhunters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Headhunters_\(film\))* (2011), a corporate recruiter who doubles as an art thief sets out to steal a [Rubens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Paul_Rubens) painting from one of his job prospects.

- *[Doors Open](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doors_Open)* (2012), a British television movie based on the novel by [Ian Rankin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_Rankin).

- *[Trance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trance_\(2013_film\)) (2013)* Simon, an art auctioneer, becomes involved in the theft of a painting, Goya's Witches in the Air, from his own auction house.

- *[The Thomas Crown Affair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Thomas_Crown_Affair_\(1999_film\))* (1999), When the painting of San Giorgio Maggiore at Dusk by Monet is stolen from the Metropolitan Museum of Art, the insurers of the \$100 million artwork send investigator Catherine Banning (Rene Russo) to assist NYPD Detective Michael McCann (Denis Leary) in solving the crime.

- *[Belphegor, Phantom of the Louvre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belphegor,_Phantom_of_the_Louvre)* (2001), A rare collection of artifacts from an archaeological dig in Egypt are brought to the famous Musée du Louvre in Paris. While experts are using a laser scanning device to determine the age of a sarcophagus, a ghostly spirit escapes and makes its way into the museum's electrical system.

- *[Woman in Gold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woman_in_Gold_\(film\))* (2015), historical drama about the efforts of Maria Altmann's decade-long battle to reclaim Gustav Klimt's painting of her aunt, *[Portrait of Adele Bloch-Bauer I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portrait_of_Adele_Bloch-Bauer_I)*.

- *[St. Trinian's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Trinian's_\(film\))* (2007), A group of schoolgirls scheme to steal Johannes Vermeer's *[Girl with a Pearl Earring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girl_with_a_Pearl_Earring)* and use the profits to save their school from closure.

### Television

- *[White Collar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Collar_\(TV_series\))* (2009–2014), Neal Caffrey, an art thief and suave con artist, teams up with FBI Agent Peter Burke to catch criminals using his expertise. However, throughout the course of the series, Neal continues to occasionally steal art under a variety of circumstances. Multiple seasons involve a plot arch that revolves around a cache of Nazi-looted art.

- *[Leverage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leverage_\(American_TV_series\))* (2008–2012), A crew of semi-reformed criminals form a Robin Hood-style organization that helps people no one else can help. Many members of the group have flashbacks to various instances of art theft in which they participated. At times, they are required to steal art in order to complete their jobs of aiding desperate people.

- *[The Blacklist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Blacklist)* (2013–2023), artwork and antiquities (stolen or otherwise) is often a big part, if not a central theme, to many episodes in the series. Raymond Reddington has also admitted to brokering many deals revolving around stolen art, sculptures, coins, and many other small items of artistic value during his time as a criminal mastermind.

- *[Cat's Eyes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cat's_Eyes_\(TV_series\))* (2024), a drama series about three sisters from Paris who are art thieves trying to collect all the works belonging to their missing father while trying to discover the real reasons behind his disappearance.

### Manga and anime

- *[Cat's Eye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cat's_Eye_\(manga\))* (1981–1985), a manga about three sisters who are formidable art thieves trying to collect all the works belonging to their missing father. It was adapted into a televised [anime series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Cat's_Eye_episodes) originally broadcast from 1983 to 1984.
