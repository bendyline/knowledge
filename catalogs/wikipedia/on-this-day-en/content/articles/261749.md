The **Main Directorate of the General Staff of the Armed Forces of the Russian Federation**,[^cite_note-1] formerly the **Main Intelligence Directorate**,[^cite_note-2] and commonly known by its previous abbreviation **GRU**,[^cite_note-3][^cite_note-gu-4] is the foreign military intelligence agency of the [General Staff of the Armed Forces of the Russian Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Staff_of_the_Armed_Forces_of_the_Russian_Federation) tasked with advancing military intelligence through collecting and analyzing intelligence from around the world and conducting clandestine and covert operations. The GRU controls the military intelligence service and maintains its own [special forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spetsnaz_GRU) units.&#x20;

Unlike Russia's other [security and intelligence agencies](35960622.md) – such as the [Foreign Intelligence Service](614404.md) (SVR), the [Federal Security Service](385990.md) (FSB), and the [Federal Protective Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Protective_Service_\(Russia\)) (FSO) – whose heads report directly to the [president of Russia](239646.md) (see [Intelligence agencies of Russia](35960622.md)), the director of the GRU is subordinate to the Russian military command, reporting to the [Minister of Defence](30730416.md) and the [Chief of the General Staff](13271754.md).

The directorate is reputedly Russia's largest foreign intelligence agency,[^cite_note-5] and is distinguished among its counterparts for its willingness to execute riskier "complicated, high stakes operations".[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7] According to unverified statements by [Stanislav Lunev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanislav_Lunev), a defector from the GRU, in 1997 the agency deployed six times as many agents in foreign countries as the SVR, and commanded some 25,000 [Spetsnaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spetsnaz) troops.[^cite_note-8]

### Origins and early history



GRU Official emblem (until 2009) with motto engraved: *"Greatness of the Motherland in your glorious deeds"*

The first Russian body for military intelligence dates from 1810, in the context of the [Napoleonic Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars) raging across Europe, when [War Minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_War_of_the_Russian_Empire) [Michael Andreas Barclay de Tolly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Andreas_Barclay_de_Tolly) proposed to Emperor [Alexander I of Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_I_of_Russia) the formation of the Expedition for Secret Affairs under the War Ministry ([Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language): Экспедиция секретных дел при военном министерстве); two years later, it was renamed the Special Bureau ([Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language): Особая канцелярия).[^cite_note-ImperialGRUhistory-9][^cite_note-GRUagenturaRU-10][^cite_note-GRUhistory-11][^cite_note-12]

In 1815, the Bureau became the First Department under the General Chief of Staff. In 1836, the intelligence functions were transferred to the Second Department under the General Chief of Staff. After many name-changes through the years, in April 1906, the Military intelligence was carried out by the Fifth Department under the General Chief of Staff of the War Ministry.[^cite_note-ImperialGRUhistory-9][^cite_note-GRUagenturaRU-10][^cite_note-GRUhistory-11]

&#x20;The GRU's first predecessor in Soviet Russia was established by the secret order signed on 5 November 1918 by [Jukums Vācietis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jukums_V%C4%81cietis), the first [commander-in-chief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commander-in-chief) of the [Red Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Army) (RKKA), and by [Ephraim Sklyansky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephraim_Sklyansky), deputy to [Leon Trotsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_Trotsky), the civilian leader of the Red Army.[^cite_note-redstarfounded-13][^cite_note-14] (Since 2006, the Russian Federation has officially observed the date of 5 November as the [professional holiday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_holidays_in_Russia#List_of_other_public_holidays,_commemorative_and_professional_days) of military intelligence in Russia.)[^cite_note-15][^cite_note-16] The military [human intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_intelligence) service thus established was originally known as the Registration Agency (*Registrupravlenie*, or *Registrupr*; [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language): Региструпр) of the Field Headquarters of the Revolutionary Military Council of the Republic; [Semyon Aralov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semyon_Aralov) was its first head.[^cite_note-redstarfounded-13] Its early history was marked by a series of reorganisations influenced by the [Soviet-Polish War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet-Polish_War), the consolidation and restablisation of the Soviet Union, and the general reorganisation of the Red Army; this included changes to its name, status, and responsibilities.

> Throughout most of the interwar period, the men and women who worked for Red Army Intelligence called it either the Fourth Department, the Intelligence Service, the *Razvedupr*, or the RU. \[…] As a result of the re-organisation \[in 1926], carried out in part to break up Trotsky's hold on the army, the Fourth Department seems to have been placed directly under the control of the State Defense Council (Gosudarstvennaia komissiia oborony, or GKO), the successor of the RVSR. Thereafter its analysis and reports went directly to the GKO and the Politburo, apparently even bypassing the Red Army Staff.

— Raymond W. Leonard[^cite_note-17]

The first head of the Fourth Directorate was [Yan Karlovich Berzin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yan_Karlovich_Berzin), who remained in the post from March 1924 until April 1935 (in 1938, he was arrested and executed as a [Trotskyite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trotskyism) during the [Stalinist purges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Purge)). Military intelligence was known for its fierce independence from the rival ["internal intelligence organizations"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secret_police), such as the [NKVD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NKVD), and later [KGB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KGB); however, public statements of Soviet military intelligence veterans state the Fourth Directorate, and later GRU, had always been operationally subordinate to the KGB.[^cite_note-mlechin-18] Military intelligence was headquartered in a small and nondescript complex west of the Kremlin, whereas the NKVD was in the very centre of Moscow, next to the building that housed [People's Commissariat for Foreign Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Foreign_Affairs_\(Soviet_Union\)) at the bottom of [Kuznetsky Most](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuznetsky_Most).[^cite_note-\:1-19] Consequently, Soviet military intelligence came to be known in Soviet diplomats' [cant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cant) as *distant neighbours* ([Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language): дальние соседи) as opposed to the *near neighbours* of the NKVD/KGB.[^cite_note-mlechin-18][^cite_note-20]

### Cold War

The GRU was created under its current name and form by [Joseph Stalin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Stalin) in February 1942, less than a year after the [invasion of the Soviet Union by Nazi Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Barbarossa). From April 1943 the GRU handled human intelligence exclusively outside the USSR.[^cite_note-21][^cite_note-22] In addition to operations against the Axis powers, GRU is credited with having infiltrated the British nuclear weapon programme and up to 70 American government and scientific institutions.[^cite_note-\:1-19]

During the [Cold War](325329.md), the GRU, like many of its Western rivals, maintained [*rezidenturas*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resident_spy), or resident spies, worldwide; these included both "legal" agents, based at Soviet embassies with [official diplomatic cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Official_cover), and ["illegal"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-official_cover) officers [without cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-official_cover). It also maintained a [signals intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signals_intelligence) (SIGINT) [station in Lourdes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lourdes_SIGINT_Station), Cuba and other [Soviet-bloc countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Bloc). Though less well known than the KGB, with which it shared a fierce rivalry, GRU is known to have been involved in several high-profile episodes; this included opening backchannel negotiations with the U.S. government during the [Cuban Missile Crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_Missile_Crisis) and contributing to the [Profumo scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Profumo_affair) that partly contributed to the fall of a British administration.[^cite_note-\:1-19] GRU was distinguished for its "closer ties with revolutionary movements and terrorist groups, greater experience with weapons and explosives, and even tougher training for recruits"; new recruits were allegedly shown footage of a traitorous officer being fed into a crematorium alive.[^cite_note-\:1-19]

The existence of the GRU was not publicized during the Soviet era, though it was mentioned in the 1931 memoirs of the first [OGPU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OGPU) defector, [Georges Agabekov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Agabekov), and described in detail in the 1939 autobiography, *I Was Stalin's Agent,* by [Walter Krivitsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Krivitsky), the most senior Red Army intelligence officer ever to defect.[^cite_note-23] GRU became widely known in Russia, and outside narrow confines of the Western [intelligence community](501244.md), during *[perestroika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perestroika)*, due partly to the writings of "[Viktor Suvorov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viktor_Suvorov)" ([Vladimir Rezun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Rezun)), a GRU officer who defected to [Great Britain](31717.md) in 1978 and wrote about his experiences in the Soviet military and intelligence services. According to Suvorov, even the [General Secretary of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Secretary_of_the_Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union), the country's *de facto* leader, needed to undergo a security screening to enter GRU headquarters.

### Post-Soviet period

Following the [dissolution of the USSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_USSR) in December 1991, the GRU continued as an important part of Russia's intelligence services, especially since it was the only one to more or less maintain operational and institutional continuity:[^cite_note-reuters.com-24] the KGB had been dissolved after aiding a [failed coup in 1991](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_coup_attempt_of_1991) against the then Soviet President [Mikhail Gorbachev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Gorbachev). It is now succeeded by the [Foreign Intelligence Service](614404.md) (SVR) and the [Federal Security Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Security_Service_of_the_Russian_Federation) (FSB).

Evidencing its growing strategic profile, in 2006 the GRU moved to a new headquarters complex at [Khoroshovskoye Shosse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khoroshovskoye_Shosse?action=edit\&redlink=1), which cost 9.5 billion rubles to build and incorporates 70,000 square meters.[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26] In April 2009, President [Dmitry Medvedev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dmitry_Medvedev) fired then-GRU head [Valentin Korabelnikov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valentin_Korabelnikov), who had headed the GRU since 1997, reportedly over Korabelnikov's objections to proposed reforms.[^cite_note-reuters.com-24][^cite_note-27] Pursuant to these reforms, the following year, the official name of the unit was changed from "GRU" to the "Main Directorate of the Russian General Staff", or "G.U."; however, "GRU" continues to be commonly used in media.[^cite_note-gu-4][^cite_note-meduza-28] The GRU underwent severe reductions in funding and personnel following the 2008 [Russo-Georgian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Georgian_War), during which it failed to discover the more advanced anti-aircraft weapons obtained by Georgia. However, it continued to play a key role in several Russian operations, including in [Russia's intervention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_military_intervention_in_Ukraine_\(2014%E2%80%93present\)) in eastern Ukraine in 2014 and the subsequent [annexation of Crimea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annexation_of_Crimea_by_the_Russian_Federation).[^cite_note-\:1-19] GRU agents were also implicated in numerous cyberwarfare operations across the West, including in the U.S., France, and Germany.[^cite_note-\:1-19] Many of its successes took place during the tenure of [Igor Sergun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igor_Sergun), who headed the service from late 2011 until his death in early January 2016.[^cite_note-29] Sergun's sudden death shortly after the restoration of the GRU's influence led to speculations of foul play by Russian adversaries.[^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31]

The tenure of Sergun's successor, [Igor Korobov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igor_Korobov), was marked by what some news media[^cite_note-32] construed as multiple high-profile setbacks, such as the thwarted [2016 coup d'état attempt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montenegrin_coup_plot) in [Montenegro](20760.md), the failed [2018 Salisbury poisoning](56823699.md), and an unprecedented number of disclosed GRU agents.[^cite_note-guardknewlot-33] Korobov died on 21 November 2018, "after a serious and prolonged illness", according to the official Defence Ministry statement.[^cite_note-34][^cite_note-Tass-Died-35] His death provoked speculations and unverified reports of him having fallen ill in October that year following a harsh dressing-down from President [Vladimir Putin](32817.md).[^cite_note-guardknewlot-33][^cite_note-bbcskripaldied-36][^cite_note-37] However, former CIA station-chief Daniel Hoffman cautioned in 2017 that some of the Russian intelligence's recent operations that appeared to be botched might have been intended for discovery.[^cite_note-38][^cite_note-39] Similarly, in 2019, [Eerik-Niiles Kross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eerik-Niiles_Kross), a former Estonian intelligence official, opined that GRU's apparent sloppiness "has become part of the [psychological warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_warfare). It's not that they have become that much more aggressive. They want to be felt. It's part of the game."[^cite_note-Schwirtz20191008-40]

On 2 November 2018, while marking the GU's 100th anniversary, President Putin proposed restoring the agency's former name: Главное разведывательное управление (GRU).[^cite_note-41]

## Organizational structure

Since 1977, the director of the [Intelligence Directorate of the Main Staff of the Russian Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence_Directorate_of_the_Main_Staff_of_the_Russian_Navy) has also typically been the deputy director of the GRU. The Navy Intelligence Directorate was made subordinate to the fifth directorate of the GRU.[^cite_note-42]

### 1997 organization

The GRU is organized into numerous directorates, directions, and sections.[^cite_note-FAS-43] According to the data available in open sources in 1997, the structure of the Main Directorate consists of at least 12 known directorates and several other auxiliary departments.

- The First Directorate is responsible for intelligence in Europe.

- The Second Directorate is geographically responsible for the Western Hemisphere.

- The Third Directorate is geographically responsible for Asia.

- The Fourth Directorate is geographically responsible for Africa and the Middle East.

- The Fifth Directorate is responsible for [military operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_operations) intelligence, including naval and air force intelligence.

- The Sixth Directorate is responsible for [signals intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signals_intelligence) (SIGINT) and space intelligence.

  [^cite_note-44]

  &#x20;It uses over 20 different types of aircraft, a fleet of 60 SIGINT collection vessels, satellites, and ground stations to collect signals intelligence. Together with [FAPSI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FAPSI), the GRU operates SIGINT collection facilities in over 60 diplomatically protected facilities throughout the world. These agencies also operate ground collection facilities within former Soviet states' territory.

- The Seventh Directorate is responsible specifically for [NATO](21133.md).

- The Eighth Directorate deals with special purpose administration.

- The Ninth Directorate is responsible for military technology.

- The Tenth Directorate is the department of [war economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_economy).

- The Eleventh Directorate is the department of strategic doctrines and arms.

- The Twelfth Directorate is responsible for [information warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_warfare).

The American [Congressional Research Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congressional_Research_Service), based on interviews with various experts, gives the following organization of the GRU, although it acknowledges that the organization's true structure is "a closely guarded secret."[^cite_note-CRS1-45]

4 Regional Directorates:

- First Directorate: [European Union](9317.md)

- Second Directorate: [North](21139.md) and [South America](26769.md), the [United Kingdom](31717.md), [Australia](4689264.md), and [New Zealand](4913064.md)

- Third Directorate: [Asia](689.md)

- Fourth Directorate: [Africa](5334607.md)

11 Mission-Specific Directorates:

- Fifth Directorate: Operational Intelligence

- Sixth Directorate: [Electronic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ELINT)/[Signals intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signals_intelligence)&#x20;

  - GRU [cyber capabilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyberwarfare), Unit 26165 (allegedly the hacking group [Fancy Bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fancy_Bear)/[APT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_persistent_threat)28/STRONTIUM), and Unit 74455 (allegedly the [Sandworm Team](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandworm_\(hacker_group\)))

- Seventh Directorate: [NATO](21133.md)

- Eighth Directorate: Russian [Spetsnaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spetsnaz) ([special forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_forces))

- Ninth Directorate: [Military technology](90815.md)

- Tenth Directorate: Military economy

- Eleventh Directorate: [Strategic doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_doctrine)

- Twelfth Directorate: [Information Operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_warfare) (separate from American [information operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_operations))

- Space Intelligence Directorate

- Operational and Technical Directorate

- External Relations Department

The *New York Times* in 2026 identified the Twentieth Directorate as operating in Tokyo, Japan.[^cite_note-nyt-bsg-26-46]

### Units

#### Unit 26165

Main article: [Unit 26165](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_26165)

Unit 26165, also known as Fancy Bear, STRONTIUM, and APT28, is a [cyber operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyberwarfare)/[hacking group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hacker). Unit 26165 was originally created during the [Cold War](325329.md) as the 85th Main Special Service Center, responsible for [military intelligence](146678.md) [cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptography).[^cite_note-CRS1-45] The [Netherlands](21148.md) has accused Unit 26165 of also being involved in the attempted 2018 [OPCW](36832.md) hack[^cite_note-47] and targeting its investigation into the 2014 downing of [Malaysia Airlines Flight 17](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysia_Airlines_Flight_17) (MH17), for which the [Dutch investigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysia_Airlines_Flight_17#Criminal_investigation) blames [pro-Russian Ukrainian separatists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_pro-Russian_unrest_in_Ukraine) armed with [surface-to-air missiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface-to-air_missiles) by Russia.[^cite_note-48][^cite_note-49]

#### Unit 29155

Main article: [Unit 29155](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_29155)

Unit 29155 is tasked with foreign assassinations and other covert activities aimed at destabilizing European countries.[^cite_note-Schwirtz20191008-40] The Unit is thought to have operated in secret since at least 2008, though its existence only became publicly known in 2019.[^cite_note-Schwirtz20191008-40][^cite_note-Schwirtz-2-50] It is commanded by Maj. Gen. [Andrei Vladimirovich Averyanov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrei_Vladimirovich_Averyanov?action=edit\&redlink=1) and based at the headquarters of the 161st Special Purpose Specialist Training Center in eastern Moscow.[^cite_note-Schwirtz20191008-40][^cite_note-Schwirtz-2-50] Its membership included decorated veterans from the Soviet [war in Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet%E2%80%93Afghan_War) and Russia's [most recent series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Chechen_War) of [wars in Chechnya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Chechen_War) and [Ukraine](42085878.md). It has been linked to the [2014 Russian annexation of Crimea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annexation_of_Crimea_by_the_Russian_Federation), the 2015 [poisonings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisoning) of [Bulgarian](3415.md) [arms dealer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arms_dealer) Emilian Gebrev (also spelled Emilyan), the [2016 Montenegro coup attempt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Montenegrin_coup_allegations), and the [poisoning of Russian defector Sergei Skripal](56823699.md). Unit 29155 operatives have also been tracked to [Switzerland](26748.md) during the time (early 2018) other GRU units hacked the [World Anti-Doping Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Anti-Doping_Agency) (then investigating [state-sponsored doping by Russian Olympians](55980489.md)) and attempted to hack the [Organisation for the Prohibition of Chemical Weapons](36832.md) (then investigating the [Douma chemical attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douma_chemical_attack) by Russia-backed [Bashar al-Assad](364813.md) and evidence in the Skripal case).[^cite_note-51] [Spain](26667.md) has also investigated the travel of Unit 29155 member Denis Sergeev (who has also used the name Sergei Fedotov) to [Barcelona](4443.md) in 2017 around the time of the [2017 Catalan independence referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_Catalan_independence_referendum).[^cite_note-CRS1-45] The unit is also accused of being behind the alleged [Russian bounty program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_bounty_program) where [Taliban](30635.md) militants were paid to kill American troops, although the program's existence is uncertain, unproven, and unverified.[^cite_note-52][^cite_note-53][^cite_note-CRS1-45]

The FBI, [CISA](59407369.md), and [NSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Security_Agency) concluded that cyber actors linked to the GRU's 161st Specialist Training Center (Unit 29155) had conducted cyber operations targeting global entities for espionage, sabotage, and reputational harm since at least 2020. Starting on 13 January 2022, these actors deployed the WhisperGate malware against several Ukrainian organizations. The advisory detailed the tactics and techniques used by Unit 29155 and offered further analysis of WhisperGate.[^cite_note-54]

#### Unit 35555

Unit 35555 is a socio-psychological research laboratory linked to supporting Wagner and other private military companies.[^cite_note-55]

#### Unit 54777

Main article: [Unit 54777](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_54777)

Unit 54777, alternately called the 72nd Special Service Center,[^cite_note-CRS1-45] is one of the GRU's primary [psychological warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_warfare) capabilities.[^cite_note-WapoDec28-56] Unit 54777 retains several [front organizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Front_organizations), including InfoRos and the Institute of the Russian Diaspora.[^cite_note-WapoDec28-56] The unit originated from Soviet [GLAVPUR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GLAVPUR) (*Glavnoye Politicheskoye Upravlenie*, or the Main Political Department) and was created in early 1990s and notably employed colonel Aleksandr Viktorovich Golyev, whose memoirs were published in 2020 along with other GRU documents. In the 1990s, the unit focused on pro-Soviet disinformation in [newly split republics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union) such as Lithuania and Chechnya. In later years the unit covered a broad range of activities from running NGOs targeting Russian expatriates in Western countries (InfoRos, Institute of the Russian Diaspora, World Coordinating Council of Russian Compatriots Living Abroad, Foundation for Supporting and Protecting the Rights of Compatriots Living Abroad) and manipulating public opinion in Russia and abroad in preparation for armed conflicts such as in Georgia, Donbas or Syria.[^cite_note-57]

#### Unit 74455

Main article: [Unit 74455](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_74455)

Unit 74455, also known as the Sandworm Team or the Main Center for Technologies, used various fictitious online identities ([DCLeaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DCLeaks) and [Guccifer 2.0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guccifer_2.0)) to coordinate the release of the politically sensitive stolen documents with [WikiLeaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WikiLeaks) for "maximum political impact" starting on the eve of the [2016 Democratic National Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Democratic_National_Convention). Its guilt has been reported by American media and a [Senate Intelligence Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Senate_Select_Committee_on_Intelligence) investigation. In October 2020, the [United States Department of Justice](52563.md) indicted six Unit 74455 GRU officers for multiple cyberattacks, including the [December 2015 Ukraine power grid cyberattack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/December_2015_Ukraine_power_grid_cyberattack), the [2017 Macron e-mail leaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_Macron_e-mail_leaks), the [2017 NotPetya attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petya_\(malware\)), the [2018 Winter Olympics hack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Winter_Olympics_opening_ceremony#Hacking_of_computer_systems) (for which the GRU attempted to frame [North Korea](21255.md)), several 2018 attacks on Skripal case investigators, and a 2018–2019 [cyberattack](37925700.md) campaign against [Georgian](48768.md) media and the [Georgian Parliament](3471146.md).[^cite_note-58][^cite_note-CRS1-45]

### SATCOM

Since the mid-1970s the GRU has maintained a [satellite communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_communications) interception post near Andreyevka, located approximately 80 kilometres (50 miles) from [Spassk-Dalny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spassk-Dalny), [Primorsky Krai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primorsky_Krai).[^cite_note-59][^cite_note-60][^cite_note-61]

## GRU illegals

According to a Western assessment of the GRU seen by [Reuters](18998750.md) in the autumn of 2018, the GRU had a long-running program to run "illegal" spies, i.e. those who work [without diplomatic cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-official_cover) and who live under an assumed identity in foreign countries for years.[^cite_note-gu-4] The assessment said: "It plays an increasingly important role in Russia's development of Information Warfare (both defensive and offensive). It is an aggressive and well-funded organization which has the direct support of – and access to – President Vladimir Putin, allowing freedom in its activities and leniency with regards to diplomatic and legislative scrutiny."[^cite_note-gu-4]

The [United States](3434750.md) alleges that the GRU, as well as the [SVR](614404.md) (its civilian foreign intelligence counterpart), makes use of both [legal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Official_cover) (intelligence officers with diplomatic protection/official government roles) and illegal operatives.[^cite_note-CRS1-45]

The "Havana syndrome," which affected U.S. diplomats and spies worldwide, was possibly linked to GRU’s Unit 29155, as reported by the Insider. Symptoms included migraines and dizziness. Investigations suggested incidents might have occurred as early as 2016, with potential prior events in Frankfurt, Germany. The U.S. Congress passed the Havana Act in 2021 to provide aid to affected personnel and families.[^cite_note-62]

## Special Forces of the Main Directorate

Main article: [Special Forces of the Main Directorate of the General Staff of the Russian Armed Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Forces_of_the_Main_Directorate_of_the_General_Staff_of_the_Russian_Armed_Forces)

Commonly known as the *[Spetsnaz GRU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spetsnaz_GRU)*, it was formed in 1949. Following the [dissolution of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union) in 1991, the Spetsnaz GRU remained intact as part of the Russian GRU until 2010, when it was reassigned to other agencies. In 2013, however, the decision was reversed and Spetsnaz GRU units were reassigned to GRU divisions and placed under GRU authority again.[^cite_note-McDermott-63]

## Education

GRU officers train at a [Ministry of Defence](7875884.md) military academy at 50 Narodnoe Opolchenie Street, with intelligence agents receiving additional training at the Cherepovets Higher Military School of Radio Electronics.[^cite_note-meduza-28] The [A.F. Mozhaysky Military-Space Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A.F._Mozhaysky_Military-Space_Academy) has also been used to train GRU officers.[^cite_note-meduza-28]

## Activities by country

According to the [Federation of American Scientists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federation_of_American_Scientists): "Though sometimes compared to the US [Defense Intelligence Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defense_Intelligence_Agency), \[the GRU's] activities encompass those performed by nearly all joint US military intelligence agencies as well as other national US organizations. The GRU gathers [human intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HUMINT) through military attaches and foreign agents. It also maintains significant signals intelligence ([SIGINT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SIGINT)) and imagery reconnaissance ([IMINT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IMINT)) and satellite imagery capabilities."[^cite_note-64] Soviet GRU Space Intelligence Directorate had put more than 130 SIGINT satellites into orbit. GRU and KGB SIGINT network employed about 350,000 specialists.[^cite_note-Mitrokhin-65]

### Colombia

In December 2020, [Migración Colombia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Migraci%C3%B3n_Colombia) confirmed the expulsion of two Russian diplomats accused of espionage.[^cite_note-83] One of the assailants was identified as Aleksandr Nikolayevich Belousov who, according to the [National Intelligence Directorate](53071204.md) of Colombia, is a GRU officer that had been credited by the Russian Embassy in Bogotá as a secretary.[^cite_note-84] Nikolayevich, along with an [SVR](614404.md) officer, had reportedly tried to gather intelligence on the country's electricity infrastructure on behalf of Venezuela's [Maduro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Maduro) government.[^cite_note-85]

### Czech Republic

### Estonia

In 2007, Deniss Metsavas, a [Lasnamäe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lasnam%C3%A4e)-born member of the [Estonian Land Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estonian_Land_Forces), was targeted with a [honey trapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honey_trapping) operation while visiting [Smolensk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smolensk). His father, Pjotr Volin, was also recruited by GRU agents as leverage against Deniss, and would serve as a courier for classified information.[^cite_note-AtlanticJune26-87]

In May 2017, Russian citizen Artem Zinchenko was convicted of spying on Estonia for the GRU.[^cite_note-MoscowTimes-88][^cite_note-Janes-89] In 2018, Zinchenko was [traded](17209653.md) back to Russia in exchange for Raivo Susi, an Estonian imprisoned for espionage. In 2022, Zinchenko fled Russia to seek [asylum](1621432.md) in Estonia, citing personal opposition to the [2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine](79400268.md).[^cite_note-90][^cite_note-91]

On 5 September 2018, Major Deniss Metsavas and Pjotr Volin were charged with giving classified information to the GRU[^cite_note-92][^cite_note-93] The two were convicted in February 2019.[^cite_note-AtlanticJune26-87]

### Finland

In September 2018, Finnish police ran a large scale operation against numerous sites owned by Airiston Helmi Oy company that over years accumulated land plots and buildings close to nationally significant key straits, ports, oil refineries and other strategic locations as well as two Finnish Navy vessels. The security operation was run in parallel in multiple locations, involving Finnish National Bureau of Investigation, local police, Tax Administration, Border Guard, and Finnish Defence Forces. During the operation, a no-fly zone was declared over Turku Archipelago where key objects were located. While official cause given for the raid was multi-million euro money laundering and tax fraud, media speculated that the company had been a cover for GRU preparing infrastructure for a surprise attack on Finnish locations in case of a conflict situation.[^cite_note-94][^cite_note-95]

### France

Viktor Ilyushin, a GRU operative working as an Air Force deputy attaché, was expelled from France in 2014 for attempted espionage of the staff of [François Hollande](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_Hollande).[^cite_note-meduza-28]

In August 2015, a GRU unit posing as [Islamic State of Iraq and the Levant](9087364.md) supporters called CyberCaliphate took [TV5Monde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TV5Monde) offline for approximately 18 hours.[^cite_note-WapoDec28-56]

GRU's APT – [Fancy Bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fancy_Bear) used fake [Facebook](7529378.md) accounts to pose as associates of [Emmanuel Macron](43671127.md)'s campaign staff, with the goal of interfering with the [2017 French presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_French_presidential_election).[^cite_note-ReutersFR-96] Georgy Petrovich Roshka, a member of the GRU's Unit 26165 was involved in the theft of Macron's emails, and subsequent distribution via [WikiLeaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WikiLeaks).[^cite_note-meduza-28][^cite_note-rfe-97]

In December 2019, *[Le Monde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Monde)* reported that the joint effort by British, Swiss, French and U.S. intelligence agencies had discovered an apparent "rear base" of GRU in southeastern France, which was presumably used by GRU for the clandestine operations carried out throughout Europe. Investigators had identified 15 agents – all of them members of GRU's [Unit 29155](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_29155) – who visited [Haute-Savoie](90605.md) in the [Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes](45093325.md), [region](58846.md) of France from 2014 to 2018, including [Alexander Petrov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Mishkin) and [Ruslan Boshirov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatoliy_Chepiga), who are believed to be behind [the poisoning](56823699.md) of the former GRU colonel and British double agent [Sergei Skripal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergei_Skripal) in Salisbury in 2018.[^cite_note-dwsavoie-98][^cite_note-99]

### Germany

In February 2021, Germany charged German citizen Jens F., a worker whose company maintained Bundestag electrical equipment, with espionage, accusing him of providing the building's floor plans to GRU operatives in the [Russian embassy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embassy_of_Russia,_Berlin) in 2017. In September 2021, the German [foreign ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Foreign_Office) warned Russia against the continuation of a pre-election [cyberattack](37925700.md) campaign targeting German legislators, claiming it had "reliable information" linking the [Ghostwriter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghostwriter_\(hacker_group\)) group behind the attacks to the GRU. ### The Netherlands and Switzerland

In mid-September 2018 the Swiss press reported that two men allegedly working for the GRU had been arrested in [The Hague](30269.md), the Netherlands in the spring that year, after the [Salisbury poisoning incident](56823699.md), for planning to hack the computer systems of the [Spiez Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiez_Laboratory), a Swiss institute analyzing chemical weapon attacks for the [Organisation for the Prohibition of Chemical Weapons](36832.md) (OPCW).[^cite_note-upi-125][^cite_note-126] In early October 2018, the government of the Netherlands announced they had arrested four GRU operatives on 13 April: Aleksei Morenets, Evgenii Serebriakov, Oleg Sotnikov, and Aleksey Minin.[^cite_note-127] The Russians allegedly attempted to launch a major "close access" cyberattack[^cite_note-128] against the headquarters of the OPCW in the Hague and also intended to travel onwards to the Spiez laboratory in Switzerland, which was testing [Novichok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novichok_agent) samples from Salisbury at the time.[^cite_note-129][^cite_note-130] Investigation conducted by [open-source intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-source_intelligence) outlets in the aftermath of the Dutch government's revelations that used Russian road police databases led to identification of further 305 GRU officers whose private cars were registered at GRU headquarters in Moscow.[^cite_note-131] GRU officer Denis Vyacheslavovich Sergeev has also been documented as operating in [Geneva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geneva) and [Lausanne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lausanne).[^cite_note-132]

#### Alleged attempt to infiltrate International Criminal Court

Main article: [Sergey Vladimirovich Cherkasov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergey_Vladimirovich_Cherkasov)

In June 2022, the Dutch [AIVD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Intelligence_and_Security_Service) stated that GRU intelligence officer Sergey Vladimirovich Cherkasov, under the alias of Viktor Muller Ferreira, was denied entry to the Netherlands after arriving for an internship with the [International Criminal Court](14880.md). The AIVD described Cherkasov as a deep-cover [illegal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-official_cover), publishing a document he is alleged to have written in 2010 reminding himself of his cover identity. As Ferreira, Cherkasov is alleged to have attended university in the United States and [Republic of Ireland](14560.md), building a cover identity for years as a Brazilian national with an interest in international affairs. AIVD head [Erik Akerboom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erik_Akerboom?action=edit\&redlink=1) described the attempted infiltration as a "long-term, multi-year GRU operation that cost a lot of time, energy and money," calling it a "high-level threat."[^cite_note-133][^cite_note-bbcc-134]

### Norway

In December 2020, the [Norwegian Police Security Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_Police_Security_Service) (PST) stated that hackers linked to [Fancy Bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fancy_Bear) and the GRU's 85th Special Services Center (GTsSS) were likely responsible for a breach of the [Storting](172897.md)'s email system earlier in the year. [Bellingcat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bellingcat) investigator [Christo Grozev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christo_Grozev) identified Mikushin as a GRU colonel while the Russian embassy in Norway denied any knowledge of Mikushin, calling his arrest a part of "spy mania."[^cite_note-136][^cite_note-137]

### Poland

In June 2014, Poland [expelled](521241.md) Russian deputy [military attaché](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_attach%C3%A9) Eduard Shishmakov (alias Eduard Shirokov) and three other Russian citizens accused of spying over a [2014 wiretap scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wprost#Role_in_the_2014_government_bugging_scandal) involving the publication of wiretapped conversations between senior Polish officials. Shishmakov, an accused GRU operative, later became a key suspect in the [2016 Montenegrin coup allegations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Montenegrin_coup_allegations).[^cite_note-138]

In October 2014, Poland arrested two alleged GRU spies. Polish Lt. Col. Zbigniew J. worked for the GRU for "several years" feeding information on unit morale and troop movements while lawyer-lobbyist Stanisław Szypowski influenced governmental circles and sought a job in the [Economy Ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Economy_\(Poland\)) while providing information on the [energy sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_in_Poland). Both met with GRU operatives under [official cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Official_cover) in [Warsaw](32908.md) and were monitored by Polish counterintelligence.[^cite_note-139]

In July 2019, a Warsaw court sentenced former Economy Ministry employee Marek W. to three years in prison for passing classified information on the [energy sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_in_Poland) to the GRU from 2015 to 2016.[^cite_note-140]

In May 2020, Polish journalists, supported by former intelligence officials, accused the GRU of conducting a 700-email bomb threat campaign against Polish schools as part of a [hybrid warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hybrid_warfare) strategy. Polish and Russian intelligence services did not comment on the accusations.[^cite_note-141]

In March 2022, the Polish [Internal Security Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_Security_Agency_\(Poland\)) (ABW) arrested reporter Pablo Gonzalez, whom they identified as "an agent of the \[GRU]," as he planned to cross the Polish-Ukrainian border. Gonzalez, a Spanish citizen of Russian origin, was found with two passports of different names and detained on suspicion of espionage. The ABW accused Gonzalez of "\[carrying] out activities for Russia using his journalistic status" and traveling to worldwide zones of conflict and political instability.[^cite_note-142][^cite_note-143]

In January 2023, Warsaw authorities arrested a Russian and a Belarusian national. The [SKW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Counterintelligence_Service_\(Poland\)), Poland's military [counterintelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterintelligence) agency, accused the pair of spying on Polish military facilities for the GRU since 2017.[^cite_note-144][^cite_note-145]

### Russia

[Dmitry Kozak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dmitry_Kozak) and [Vladislav Surkov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladislav_Surkov), members of the [Vladimir Putin](32817.md) administration, reportedly served in the GRU. Two Chechens, [Said-Magomed Kakiev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Said-Magomed_Kakiev) and former [warlord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warlord) [Sulim Yamadayev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulim_Yamadayev) were commanders of [Special Battalions Vostok and Zapad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Battalions_Vostok_and_Zapad) ("East" and "West") that were controlled by the GRU. The battalions each included close to a thousand fighters until their disbandment in 2008.[^cite_note-149]

Approximately 300 commandos, [intelligence officers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence_officer) and other GRU personnel died during the fighting in Chechnya.[^cite_note-150]

GRU detachments from [Chechnya](6095.md) were transferred to [Lebanon](17771.md) independently of the [United Nations Interim Force in Lebanon](787766.md) after the [2006 Lebanon War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_Lebanon_War).[^cite_note-151]

GRU officers have also been accused of creating criminal [death squads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_squads).[^cite_note-Korolkov-152]

### Slovakia

In early 2022, Slovakia arrested four Slovak nationals described as a "Russian spying network \[seeking] information about NATO and Ukraine." Two were charged with spying and bribery, with Slovak authorities alleging [undercover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-official_cover) GRU officers at the Russian Embassy paid tens of thousands of euros for the "highly sensitive" information about Slovakia, the [Slovak military](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slovak_Armed_Forces), and NATO.[^cite_note-153] The men charged were a Slovak military academy rector and disinformation blogger; the other two were released without charge.[^cite_note-154]

### Slovenia

In January 2023, the [Slovenian Intelligence and Security Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slovenian_Intelligence_and_Security_Agency) arrested and charged two individuals in [Ljubljana](42374.md) with espionage on behalf of the GRU and using false documents. Both were reportedly operating under assumed identities with ties to Argentina. Media variously described those charged as either a husband and wife[^cite_note-155][^cite_note-156] or two men.[^cite_note-157][^cite_note-158]

### Spain

According to reporting by [Bellingcat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bellingcat), *[El País](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Pa%C3%ADs)* and the Civica Media Foundation, the [Audiencia Nacional](2811690.md) is investigating a GRU group known as [Unit 29155](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GRU_Unit_29155) and its operations in Spain.[^cite_note-Pais-159] GRU members Denis Sergeev, Alexey Kalinin and Mikhail Opryshko are reported to have been operating in [Barcelona](4443.md) around the time of the [2017 Catalan independence referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_Catalan_independence_referendum).[^cite_note-Pais-159]

### Sweden

In late 2021, Swedish authorities arrested brothers Peyman and Payam Kia for aggravated espionage on behalf of the GRU. Peyman had worked with the [Swedish Security Service](368897.md) and [Swedish Armed Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Armed_Forces); both brothers were jailed in January 2023.[^cite_note-160][^cite_note-161]

In late 2022, elite police arrested Russian-born Swedish citizen Sergey Skvortsov, accusing him of nearly 10 years of "gross illegal intelligence activities against Sweden and against a foreign power" (later identified as the United States) on behalf of the GRU. Skvortsov reportedly worked to illegally transfer Western technology to Russia.[^cite_note-162][^cite_note-163]

### Syria

See also: [Russian military intervention in the Syrian Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_military_intervention_in_the_Syrian_Civil_War)

The Sixth Directorate was responsible for maintaining the [Center S](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_S) covert listening post in [Syria](7515849.md) prior to its loss to the [Free Syrian Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Syrian_Army) in 2014.[^cite_note-164][^cite_note-165][^cite_note-166] The Sixth Directorate also operates a signals intelligence listening post at [Hmeimim Air Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khmeimim_\(air_base\)) near [Latakia](195594.md).[^cite_note-167]

In 2015 GRU special forces soldiers have reportedly appeared in [Aleppo](159244.md) and [Homs](77533.md).[^cite_note-168][^cite_note-169] GRU officials have also visited [Qamishli](524369.md), near the border with Turkey.[^cite_note-170]

### Turkey

In 2018 the Turkish government published CCTV videos from assassination of a Chechen commander Abdulvahid Edelgiriev, who was killed in 2015 in Istanbul,[^cite_note-171] claiming the perpetrator was the same person as [Anatoliy Chepiga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatoliy_Chepiga) ("Ruslan Boshirov") from Skripal assassination in UK.[^cite_note-172]

### Ukraine

The [Spetsnaz GRU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Forces_of_the_Main_Directorate_of_the_General_Staff_of_the_Russian_Armed_Forces) were involved in the [annexation of Crimea by the Russian Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annexation_of_Crimea_by_the_Russian_Federation) and in the [war in Donbas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_in_Donbas_\(2014%E2%80%932022\)).[^cite_note-meduza-28] During the November 2018, [Kerch Strait incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerch_Strait_incident), the GRU's Unit 54777 sent [text messages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Text_messaging) to Ukrainian men in the border region calling on them to report for military service.[^cite_note-WapoDec28-56]

[News media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_media) and private [cybersecurity](7398.md) firms allege that the GRU hacked the [computer networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_networks) of Ukrainian energy company [Burisma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burisma), a key player in the 2020 [Biden–Ukraine conspiracy theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biden%E2%80%93Ukraine_conspiracy_theory).[^cite_note-CRS1-45]

Spetsnaz GRU are involved in the [2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine](79400268.md). Their first reported casualty was Captain Alexey Gluschak, killed in action [in Mariupol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Mariupol) on 8 March.[^cite_note-WashEx14.3.22-173]

### United Kingdom

Main article: [Poisoning of Sergei and Yulia Skripal](56823699.md)

In September 2018 the [Crown Prosecution Service](383064.md) formally named two Russian nationals, Alexander Petrov and Ruslan Boshirov (the names used by the men when entering the UK), as suspected perpetrators of the assassination attempt of the former GRU officer [Sergei Skripal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergei_Skripal) and his daughter in March 2018.[^cite_note-BBCcharges-174] As part of the charge announcement Scotland Yard released a detailed track of the individuals' 48 hours in the UK. This covered their arrival in the UK at [Gatwick Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gatwick_Airport), trip to Salisbury the day before the attack, trip to Salisbury on the day of the attack and return to Moscow via [Heathrow Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heathrow_Airport).[^cite_note-175] The two men stayed both nights in the City Stay Hotel on [Bow Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bow_Road), East London and [Novichok agent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novichok_agent) was found in their room after police sealed it off on 4 May 2018.[^cite_note-176][^cite_note-177] British Prime Minister Theresa May told the Commons the same day that the suspects were part of the G.U. intelligence service (formerly known as GRU) and the assassination attempt was not a rogue operation and was "almost certainly" approved at a senior level of the Russian state.[^cite_note-BBCcharges-174][^cite_note-178]

As a side effect of the Skripal poisoning investigation, Russian and Western media reported conclusions made by [open-source intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-source_intelligence) outlets that claimed that GRU operatives were issued Russian foreign travel passports with certain characteristics that would allow their tentative identification.[^cite_note-179] Through further research, in the autumn of 2018, "Boshirov" was publicly exposed as [Anatoliy Chepiga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatoliy_Chepiga), a decorated GRU officer,[^cite_note-180][^cite_note-181][^cite_note-182] and "Petrov" as [Alexander Mishkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Mishkin).[^cite_note-183][^cite_note-184]

### United States

GRU officer [Stanislav Lunev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanislav_Lunev), who defected to the U.S. in 1992 while he was posted in Washington under the cover of a TASS news agency correspondent, in the 1990s publicized his claims that small nuclear weapons that could be fit into a knapsack or a briefcase or suitcase had been secretly pre-positioned in the U.S. and other countries around the world to be used for [sabotage](29462.md) by Russia's agents in the event of war.[^cite_note-Lunev-185] [U.S. Congressman](19468510.md) [Curt Weldon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curt_Weldon) pursued these claims publicly while admitting that they had been found largely spurious by the FBI.[^cite_note-186] Searches of the areas identified by Lunev – who admitted he had never planted any weapons in the US – have been conducted, "but law-enforcement officials have never found such weapons caches, with or without portable nuclear weapons".[^cite_note-187]

#### Electoral interference

See also: [Russian interference in the 2016 United States elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_interference_in_the_2016_United_States_elections) and [Russian interference in the 2018 United States elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_interference_in_the_2018_United_States_elections)

United States Deputy Attorney General [Rod Rosenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rod_Rosenstein) announcing in 2018 a grand jury indictment of 12 Russian intelligence officers for hacking offenses related to the 2016 U.S. presidential election

On 29 December 2016 the [White House](33057.md) sanctioned the nine entities and individuals, including the GRU as well as the FSB, for their alleged activities to disrupt and spread disinformation during the [2016 US presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_United_States_presidential_election).[^cite_note-WHelectionsSanctions-188] In addition, the [United States State Department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_State_Department) also declared 35 Russian diplomats and officials *[persona non grata](521241.md)* and denied Russian government officials access to two Russian-owned installations in [Maryland](18858.md) and [New York](8210131.md).[^cite_note-WHelectionsSanctions-188] On 13 July 2018, an indictment to several GRU Staffers was issued.[^cite_note-189] GRU Unit 26165 and Unit 74455 are alleged to be behind the [DCLeaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DCLeaks) website, and were indicted for obtaining access and distributing information from data about 500,000 voters from a state election board website as well as the email accounts of [John Podesta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Podesta), Hillary Clinton, and volunteers and employees of the United States Presidential Campaign of Hillary Clinton, the [Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Congressional_Campaign_Committee), and the [Democratic National Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Democratic_National_Committee_email_leak) (DNC).[^cite_note-NYT180713-190][^cite_note-Indictment180713-191] According to information leaked by [Reality Winner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reality_Winner), the GRU attempted to hack the voting machine manufacturer [VR Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VR_Systems), as well as local election officials.[^cite_note-int1-192]

In July 2018 Deputy Attorney General [Rod Rosenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rod_Rosenstein) released an indictment returned by a grand jury charging twelve GRU officers with conspiring to [interfere](52846360.md) in the 2016 elections.[^cite_note-193][^cite_note-cnbc-194][^cite_note-195]

According to [Microsoft](19001.md) VP Tom Burt, a GRU-run group dubbed Strontium (alternatively known as APT28, Sofacy, and Pawn Storm, and Fancy Bear)[^cite_note-196] has been engaged in [spear phishing attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phishing#Spear_phishing) against at least three campaigns in the [2018 midterm elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_United_States_elections).[^cite_note-197]

On 19 November 2021, the Coordinating Council of Russian Compatriots in the United States (CCORC) or (KCOPC) closed and on 9 March 2022 Elena Branson was accused of working as a foreign agent by the FBI.[^cite_note-198][^cite_note-199][^cite_note-200][^cite_note-201][^cite_note-202]

### Yemen

In August 2024, *[Middle East Eye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East_Eye)*, citing a US official, reported that personnel of GRU were stationed in [Houthi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houthi_movement)-controlled parts of [Yemen](350939.md) to assist the [militia's attacks on merchant ships](75207766.md) during the [Gaza war](74998519.md).[^cite_note-203]
