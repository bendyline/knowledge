# Mile run world record progression

The [world record](3443107.md) in the [mile run](5227492.md) is the fastest time set by a [runner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Running) in the [middle-distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_distance_running) [track and field](194904.md) event. [World Athletics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Athletics) is the official body which oversees the records. [Josh Kerr](54894149.md) is the current men's record holder with his time of 3\:42.66,[^cite_note-1] while [Faith Kipyegon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faith_Kipyegon) has the women's record of 4\:07.64.[^cite_note-2] Since 1976, the [mile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mile) has been the only non-[metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_system) distance recognized by World Athletics (formerly the International Association of Athletics Federations (IAAF)) for record purposes. In international competitions such as the Olympics the term "[metric mile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_mile)" is sometimes used to refer to a distance of 1,500 meters, which is 109.344 meters shorter than an Imperial mile, even though four "full" laps of a 400 meter track is equal to 1,600 meters.

Accurate times for the mile run (1.609344 km) have been recorded since 1850, when the first precisely measured [running tracks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Running_track) were built. Progression of the mile record accelerated in the 1930s as [newsreel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newsreel) coverage greatly popularized the sport, making stars out of milers such as [Jules Ladoumègue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jules_Ladoum%C3%A8gue), [Jack Lovelock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Lovelock), and [Glenn Cunningham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glenn_Cunningham_\(runner\)). In the 1940s, Swedes [Arne Andersson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arne_Andersson) and [Gunder Hägg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunder_H%C3%A4gg) lowered the record to 4\:01.4 while racing was curtailed during World War II in the combatant countries. After the war, [Roger Bannister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Bannister) of the United Kingdom and [John Landy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Landy) of Australia vied to be the first to break the fabled [four-minute mile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-minute_mile) barrier. Roger Bannister did it first on May 6, 1954, and John Landy followed 46 days later.

On the women's side, the first sub-5\:00 mile was achieved by the UK's [Diane Leather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diane_Leather) 23 days after Bannister's first sub-4\:00 mile. However, the [International Association of Athletics Federations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Association_of_Athletics_Federations) (IAAF) did not recognize women's records for the distance until 1967, when [Anne Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_Smith_\(middle-distance_runner\)) of the UK ran 4\:37.0.[^cite_note-Sears2015-3]

The "Time" column indicates the ratified mark; the "Auto" column indicates a fully automatic time that was also recorded in the event when hand-timed marks were used for official records, or which was the basis for the official mark, rounded to the 10th of a second, depending on the rules then in place.

Records for the mile were rounded up to the nearest tenth of a second commencing January 1, 1957. Previously, records were rounded up to the nearest fifth of a second. Those rounded-up marks were: Cunningham's 4\:06.8 (timed at 4\:06.7); Hägg's 4\:06.2 (4\:06.1); Hägg's 4\:01.4 (4\:01.3); Landy's 3\:58.0 (3\:57.9). Landy's mark was not retroactively adjusted when the new rule came into effect.[^cite_note-wrp-34]<sup>: vii, 69–70 </sup> Auto times to the hundredth of a second were accepted by the IAAF for events up to and including 10,000 m beginning in 1981.[^cite_note-iaaf-6]

During the world record-setting race in 1999, [Noah Ngeny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noah_Ngeny) came in second place to Hicham El Guerrouj with a time of 3\:43.40, which stood as the second fastest mile in history until the new record was set by Josh Kerr in July 2026.[^cite_note-35]

## Road men

### IAAF era

The Road Mile became an official world record event after September 1, 2023, on World Athletics Certified Courses only (i.e: elevation gradient must not exceed one meter per kilometer, start and finish line must not be more than half a mile apart). Faster times have been recorded in non-compliant events such as the [Fifth Avenue Mile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fifth_Avenue_Mile). The winning times from the 2023 U.S. Road Mile Championships, on 25 April, were ratified by World Athletics as the inaugural road mile world records.[^cite_note-\:0-37][^cite_note-\:1-38]
