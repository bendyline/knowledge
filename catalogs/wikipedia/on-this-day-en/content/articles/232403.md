# Ditch

For other uses, see [Ditch (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ditch_\(disambiguation\)).

A **ditch** is a small-to-moderate size trench created to channel [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water). A ditch can be used for [drainage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage), to drain water from low-lying areas, alongside [roadways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Road) or fields, or to channel water from a more distant source for plant [irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrigation). Ditches are commonly seen around [farmland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farmland_\(farming\)), especially in areas that have required drainage, such as [The Fens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Fens) in eastern [England](9316.md) and much of the [Netherlands](21148.md).

Roadside ditches may provide a hazard to [motorists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motorist) and [cyclists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclist), whose vehicles may crash into them and get damaged, flipped over, or stuck and cause major injury, especially in poor [weather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weather) conditions and [rural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rural) areas.

## Etymology

In [Anglo-Saxon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English_language), the word *dïc* already existed and was pronounced [\[diːk\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Old_English) ("deek") in northern England and [\[diːtʃ\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Old_English) ("deetch") in the south. The origins of the word lie in digging a [trench](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trench) and forming the upcast soil into a bank alongside it. This practice has meant that the name *dïc* was given to either the excavation or the bank, and evolved to both the words ["dike"/"dyke"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dike_\(construction\)) and "ditch".

Thus [Offa's Dyke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offa's_Dyke) is a combined structure and [Car Dyke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Car_Dyke) is a trench, though it once had raised banks as well. In the English [Midlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midlands) and [East Anglia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Anglia), a dyke is what a ditch is in the south of England, a property-boundary marker or drainage channel. Where it carries a stream, it may be called a running dike as in *Rippingale Running Dike*, which leads water from the [catchwater drain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catchwater_drain), Car Dyke, to the South Forty Foot Drain in [Lincolnshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincolnshire) (TF1427). The Weir Dike is a [soak dike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soak_dike) in [Bourne North Fen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourne,_Lincolnshire), near [Twenty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twenty,_Lincolnshire) and alongside the [River Glen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Glen,_Lincolnshire).

## Sustainability of drainage ditches

Drainage ditches play major roles in [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture) throughout the [world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World). Improper drainage systems accelerate [water contamination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_contamination), excessively desiccate soils during seasonal [drought](37335.md), and become a financial burden to maintain. Industrial earth-moving equipment facilitates maintenance of straight drainage trenches, but [entrenchment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trench) results in increasing environmental and eventually profound economic costs over time.[^cite_note-1]

Sustainable channel design can result in ditches that are largely self-maintaining due to natural [geomorphological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geomorphological) equilibrium. Slowed net [siltation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siltation) and [erosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erosion) result in net reduction in [sediment transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sediment_transport). Encouraging development of a natural [stream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stream) [sinuosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinuosity) and a multi-terraced channel cross section appear to be key to maintain both peak ditch drainage capacity, and minimum net [pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollution) and [nutrient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutrient) transport.[^cite_note-2]

[Flooding](50482.md) can be a major cause of recurring crop loss—particularly in heavy soils—and can severely disrupt [urban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_area) economies as well. Subsurface drainage to ditches offers a way to remove excess water from agricultural fields, or vital urban spaces, without the erosion rates and pollution transport that results from direct [surface runoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_runoff). However, excess drainage results in recurring drought induced [crop yield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop_yield) losses and more severe [urban heat island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island) or [desiccation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desiccation) issues.

[Controlled subsurface drainage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controlled_subsurface_drainage?action=edit\&redlink=1) from sensitive areas to vegetated drainage ditches makes possible a better balance between water drainage and water retention needs. The initial investment allows a community to draw down local [water tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_table) when and where necessary without exacerbating drought problems at other times.[^cite_note-3]

## Water diversion ditches

Particularly in [Colorado](5399.md), the term ditch is also applied to open [aqueducts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqueduct_\(water_supply\)) that traverse hillsides as part of [transbasin diversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transbasin_diversion) projects.

Examples include the [Grand Ditch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Ditch) over [La Poudre Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Poudre_Pass), the [Berthoud Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berthoud_Pass) Ditch, and the [Boreas Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boreas_Pass) Ditch.

## Herbicides

[Herbicides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbicide) may be used to maintain a ditch. Primarily this is done to deny refuge to [weeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weed) that would progress into the adjacent field, but may instead involve only [broadleaf herbicides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadleaf_herbicide) specifically to produce forage and/or hay.[^cite_note-U-Minn-Ext-4][^cite_note-Az-herbicide-5][^cite_note-PNW-Pest-Man-HB-6]

## For forage and hay

Ditches can provide [forage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forage) or be harvested for [hay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hay). If herbicides are used, however, the resulting [manure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manure) cannot necessarily be used in crop fields, because in some cases herbicides will pass through and produce crop injury.[^cite_note-U-Minn-Ext-4]
