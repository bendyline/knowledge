**Censorship in Turkey** is [regulated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_regulation_in_Turkey) by domestic and international legislation, the latter (in theory) taking precedence over domestic law, according to [Article 90](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Constitution%20of%20the%20Republic%20of%20Turkey#90) of the [Constitution of Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Turkey) (so amended in 2004).[^cite_note-1]

Despite legal provisions, [freedom of the press](105323.md) in [Turkey](11125639.md) has steadily deteriorated from 2010 onwards, with a precipitous decline following the [attempted coup in July 2016](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Turkish_coup_d'%C3%A9tat_attempt).[^cite_note-FH-2][^cite_note-3] The Turkish government of [Recep Tayyip Erdoğan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recep_Tayyip_Erdo%C4%9Fan) has arrested hundreds of journalists, closed or taken over dozens of media outlets, and prevented journalists and their families from traveling. By some accounts, Turkey currently accounts for one-third of all journalists imprisoned around the world.[^cite_note-4]

Since 2013, [Freedom House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_House) ranks Turkey as "Not Free".[^cite_note-FH-2] In 2015 [Reporters Without Borders](526054.md) ranked Turkey at the 149th place out of over 180 countries, between [Mexico](3966054.md) and [DR Congo](76762.md), with a score of 44.16.[^cite_note-RSF-5] In 2025, according to the same organisation 90% of the media is controlled by the government and Turkey ranks 159th out of 180 countries in terms freedom of press.[^cite_note-6]

In the third quarter of 2015, the independent Turkish press agency [Bianet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bianet) recorded a strengthening of attacks on opposition media under the [Justice and Development Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justice_and_Development_Party_\(Turkey\)) (AKP) interim government.[^cite_note-2015Q3-7] Bianet's final 2015 monitoring report confirmed this trend and underlined that, once the AKP had regained a majority in parliament after the AKP interim government period, the Turkish government further intensified its pressure on the country's media.[^cite_note-auto-8]

According to Freedom House,

> The government enacted new laws that expanded both the state's power to block websites and the surveillance capability of the [National Intelligence Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Intelligence_Organization_\(Turkey\)) (MİT). Journalists faced unprecedented legal obstacles as the courts restricted reporting on corruption and national security issues. The authorities also continued to aggressively use the penal code, criminal defamation laws, and the antiterrorism law to crack down on journalists and media outlets. Verbal attacks on journalists by senior politicians—including Recep Tayyip Erdoğan, the incumbent prime minister who was elected president in August—were often followed by harassment and even death threats against the targeted journalists on social media. Meanwhile, the government continued to use the financial and other leverage it holds over media owners to influence coverage of politically sensitive issues. Several dozen journalists, including prominent columnists, lost their jobs as a result of such pressure during the year, and those who remained had to operate in a climate of increasing self-censorship and media [polarization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_polarization_in_Turkey).[^cite_note-FH-2]

In 2012 and 2013 the [Committee to Protect Journalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_to_Protect_Journalists) (CPJ) ranked Turkey as the worst journalist jailer in the world (ahead of [Iran](14653.md) and [China](5405.md)), with 49 journalists sitting in jail in 2012 and 40 in 2013.[^cite_note-9][^cite_note-10] [Twitter](9988187.md)'s 2014 Transparency Report showed that Turkey filed over five times more content removal requests to Twitter than any other country in the second half of 2014, with requests rising another 150% in 2015.[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-reuters-12]

During its rule since 2002 the ruling AKP has gradually expanded its control over media.[^cite_note-Aksera+Baybars-Hawks-13] Today, numerous newspapers, TV channels and internet portals dubbed as *Yandaş Medya* ("Partisan Media") or *Havuz Medyası* ("Pool Media") continue their heavy pro-government propaganda.[^cite_note-\:1-14] Several media groups receive preferential treatment in exchange for AKP-friendly editorial policies.[^cite_note-\:2-15] Some of these media organizations were acquired by AKP-friendly businesses or directly by Erdogan's own family through questionable funds and processes.[^cite_note-\:3-16][^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18] Media not friendly to AKP, on the other hand, are threatened with intimidation, inspections and fines.[^cite_note-\:4-19] These media group owners face similar threats to their other businesses.[^cite_note-\:5-20] An increasing number of columnists have been fired for criticizing the AKP leadership.[^cite_note-\:6-21][^cite_note-\:7-22][^cite_note-\:8-23][^cite_note-\:9-24]

The AKP leadership has been criticized by multiple journalists over the years because of censorship.[^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26][^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28][^cite_note-29][^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31][^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33][^cite_note-34][^cite_note-35]

## History

Further information: [Multi-party period of Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-party_period_of_Turkey) and [Media censorship and disinformation during the Gezi Park protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_censorship_and_disinformation_during_the_Gezi_Park_protests)

See also: [Politics of Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics_of_Turkey)

Regional censorship predates the establishment of the Republic of Turkey. On 15 February 1857, the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md) issued law governing printing houses (*Basmahane Nizamnamesi*); books first had to be shown to the governor, who forwarded them to commission for education (*Maarif Meclisi*) and the police. If no objection was made, the Sultan would then inspect them. Without censure from the Sultan books could not be legally issued.[^cite_note-nedim-36] On 24 July 1908, at the beginning of the [Second Constitutional Era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Constitutional_Era), censorship was lifted; however, newspapers publishing stories that were deemed a danger to interior or exterior State security were closed.[^cite_note-nedim-36] Between 1909 and 1913 four journalists were killed—[Hasan Fehmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasan_Fehmi_\(journalist\)), [Ahmet Samim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmet_Samim), Zeki Bey, and Hasan Tahsin (Silahçı).[^cite_note-37]

Following the [abolition of the Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolition_of_the_Caliphate) in 1924, the [Sheikh Said rebellion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheikh_Said_rebellion) broke out as part of the complex ethnic conflict that erupted with the creation of a [secular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secularism_in_Turkey) Turkish nationalist identity that was rejected by many Kurds, who had long been loyal subjects of the [Caliph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliph). Sheikh Said, a [Naqshbandi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naqshbandi) sheikh, accused [Turkish nationalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_nationalism) of having "reduced the Caliph to the state of a parasite". The uprising was crushed brutally and martial law was imposed on 25 February 1925. Disagreement in the ruling [Republican People's Party](404748.md) ultimately favored more hardline measures and under [İsmet İnönü](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%B0smet_%C4%B0n%C3%B6n%C3%BC)'s leadership, the *Takrir-i Sükun Kanunu* was proposed on 4 March 1925.[^cite_note-38] This law granted the government unchecked powers, and had a number of consequences including the closure of all newspapers except for *[Cumhuriyet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumhuriyet)* and *Hakimiyet-i Milliye* (both were official or semi-official state publications). The effect was to censor any criticism of the ruling party, and socialists and communists were arrested and tried by the [Independence Tribunals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_Tribunal) that were established in Ankara under the law. *Tevhid-i Efkar*, *[Sebilürreşad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sebil%C3%BCrre%C5%9Fad)*, *Aydınlık*, *[Resimli Ay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resimli_Ay)*, and *Vatan* were among the newspapers closed and several journalists arrested and tried at the tribunals.[^cite_note-nedim-36] The tribunals also closed down the offices of opposition party *[Terakkiperver Cumhuriyet Fırkası](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terakkiperver_Cumhuriyet_F%C4%B1rkas%C4%B1)* on 3 June 1925, under the pretext that their openly stated support for the protection of religious customs had contributed to the Sheikh Said rebellion.[^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40]

During [World War II](32927.md) (1939–1945) many newspapers were ordered shut, including the dailies *Cumhuriyet* (5 times, for 5 months and 9 days), *[Tan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tan_\(newspaper\))* (7 times, for 2 months and 13 days), and *Vatan* (9 times, for 7 months and 24 days).[^cite_note-nedim-36]

When the [Democrat Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democrat_Party_\(Turkey,_1946%E2%80%931961\)) under [Adnan Menderes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adnan_Menderes) came to power in 1950, censorship entered a new phase. The Press Law changed, sentences and fines were increased. Several newspapers were ordered shut, including the dailies *Ulus* (unlimited ban), *Hürriyet*, *Tercüman*, and *Hergün* (two weeks each). In April 1960, a so-called [investigation commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_of_Inquest) (*Tahkikat Komisyonu*) was established by the Grand National Assembly of Turkey. It was given the power to confiscate publications, close papers and printing houses. Anyone not following the decisions of the commission were subject to imprisonment, between one and three years.[^cite_note-nedim-36]

Freedom of speech was heavily restricted after the [1980 military coup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980_Turkish_coup_d'%C3%A9tat) headed by General [Kenan Evren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenan_Evren). During the 1980s and 1990s, approaching the topics of [secularism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secularism_in_Turkey), [minority rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights_in_Turkey) (in particular the [Kurdish issue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurds_in_Turkey)), and the role of the [military in politics](30205.md) risked reprisal.[^cite_note-HRW02-41]

Article 8 of the [Anti-Terror Law](4700071.md) (Law 3713), slightly amended in 1995 and later repealed,[^cite_note-PAR04-42] imposed three-year prison sentences for "separatist propaganda". Despite its name, the Anti-Terror Law punished many non-violent offences.[^cite_note-HRW02-41] [Pacifists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacifism) have been imprisoned under Article 8. For example, publisher Fatih Tas was prosecuted in 2002 under Article 8 at Istanbul [State Security Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_Security_Court_\(Turkey\)) for translating and publishing writings by [Noam Chomsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noam_Chomsky), summarizing the history of [human rights violations in southeast Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights_of_Kurdish_people_in_Turkey); he was acquitted, however, in February 2002.[^cite_note-HRW02-41] Prominent female publisher [Ayşe Nur Zarakolu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ay%C5%9Fe_Nur_Zarakolu), who was described by *[The New York Times](30680.md)* as "\[o]ne of the most relentless challengers to Turkey's press laws", was imprisoned under Article 8 four times.[^cite_note-43][^cite_note-Independent-44]

Since 2011, the [AKP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justice_and_Development_Party_\(Turkey\)) government has increased restrictions on freedom of speech, freedom of the press and internet use,[^cite_note-nytimes.com-45] and television content,[^cite_note-Al-Monitor-46] as well as the right to free assembly.[^cite_note-spiegel.de-47] It has also developed links with media groups, and used administrative and legal measures (including, in the case of [Doğan Holding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Do%C4%9Fan_Holding), a \$2.5 billion tax fine) against critical media groups and critical journalists: "over the last decade the AKP has built an informal, powerful, coalition of party-affiliated businessmen and media outlets whose livelihoods depend on the political order that Erdogan is constructing. Those who resist do so at their own risk."[^cite_note-ForeignPolicy-48] Since his time as prime minister through to his presidency Erdoğan has sought to control the press, forbidding coverage, restricting internet use and stepping up repression on journalists and media outlets.[^cite_note-49]



[NTV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NTV_\(Turkish_TV_channel\)) broadcast van covered with protest graffiti during the [Gezi Park protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gezi_Park_protests), in response to relative lack of coverage of mainstream media of the protests, 1 June 2013

Foreign media noted that, particularly in the early days (31 May – 2 June 2013) of the [Gezi Park protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gezi_Park_protests), the events attracted relatively little mainstream media coverage in Turkey, due to either government pressure on media groups' business interests or simply ideological sympathy by media outlets.[^cite_note-guardian-resign-50][^cite_note-BBCTurksdeprived-51] The BBC noted that while some outlets are aligned with the AKP or are personally close to Erdoğan, "most mainstream media outlets – such as TV news channels [HaberTürk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habert%C3%BCrk_TV) and [NTV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NTV_\(Turkish_TV_channel\)), and the major centrist daily *[Milliyet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milliyet)* – are loath to irritate the government because their owners' business interests at times rely on government support. All of these have tended to steer clear of covering the demonstrations."[^cite_note-BBCTurksdeprived-51] [Ulusal Kanal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulusal_Kanal) and [Halk TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halk_TV) provided extensive live coverage from Gezi park.[^cite_note-dw\.de-52]

Turkey's Journalists Union estimated that at least "72 journalists had been fired or forced to take leave or had resigned in the past six weeks since the start of the [unrest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gezi_Park_protests)" in late May 2013 due to pressure from the [AKP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justice_and_Development_Party_\(Turkey\)) government. Kemal Kilicdaroglu, head of the [Republican People's Party](404748.md) (CHP) party, said 64 journalists have been imprisoned and "We are now facing a new period where the media is controlled by the government and the police and where most media bosses take orders from political authorities." The government says most of the imprisoned journalists have been detained for serious crimes, like membership in an armed terrorist group, that are not related to journalism.[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55]

[Bianet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bianet)'s periodical reports on freedom of the press in Turkey published in October 2015 recorded a strengthening of attacks on the opposition media during the [AKP interim government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_interim_election_government_of_Turkey) in the third quarter of 2015. Bianet recorded the [censorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship) of 101 websites, 40 Twitter accounts, 178 news; attacks against 21 journalists, three media organs, and one printing house; civil pursuits against 28 journalists; and the six-fold increase of arrests of media representatives, with 24 journalists and 9 distributors imprisoned.[^cite_note-2015Q3-7] The increased criminalisation of the media follows the freezing of the [Kurdish peace process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2013%E2%80%932015_PKK%E2%80%93Turkey_peace_process) and the failure of AKP to obtain an outright majority at the [June 2015 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/June_2015_Turkish_general_election) and to achieve the [presidentialisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidential_system) of the political system. Several journalists and editors are tried for being allegedly members of unlawful organisations, linked to either Kurds or the [Gülen movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%BClen_movement), others for alleged insults to religion and to the [President](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_Turkey). In 2015, *[Cumhuriyet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumhuriyet)* and *[Doğan Holding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Do%C4%9Fan_Holding)* were investigated for "terror", "espionage" and "insult". On the date of [Bianet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bianet)'s publication, 61 people, of whom 37 journalists, were convict, defendant or suspect for having insulted or personally attacked the then-PM, now-President [Recep Tayyip Erdoğan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recep_Tayyip_Erdo%C4%9Fan). The [European Court of Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Court_of_Human_Rights) condemned Turkey for violation of the freedom of expression in the Abdurrahman Dilipak case ([Sledgehammer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sledgehammer_\(coup_plan\)) investigation),[^cite_note-56][^cite_note-57] and the Turkish Constitutional Court upheld the violation of the freedom of expression of five persons, including a journalist. [RTÜK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_and_Television_Supreme_Council) could not yet choose its president; it still warned companies five times and fined them six times. The [Supreme Electoral Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Electoral_Council_\(Turkey\)) ordered 65 channels twice to stop broadcasting the results of the [June 2015 election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/June_2015_Turkish_general_election) before the end of the publishing ban.

Attacks on media freedom went far beyond the AKP interim government period. The January 2016 updated *Bianet* report confirmed this trend, underlining that the whole 2014 figure of arrested journalists increased in 2015, reaching the number of 31 journalists arrested (22 in 2014).[^cite_note-auto-8] Once the AKP regained the majority in the elections on 1 November 2015, the Turkish government intensified the pressure on the country's media, for example by banning some TV channels, in particular those linked to the [Fethullah Gülen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fethullah_G%C3%BClen) movement, from digital platforms and by seizing control of their broadcasting. In November 2015, [Can Dündar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Can_D%C3%BCndar), *[Cumhuriyet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumhuriyet)*'s editor in chief and its Ankara representative [Erdem Gül](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erdem_G%C3%BCl) were arrested on charges of belonging to a terror organisation, espionage, and for having allegedly disclosed confidential information. Investigation against the two journalists were launched after the newspaper documented the transfer of weapons from Turkey to Syria in trucks of the [National Intelligence Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Intelligence_Organization_\(Turkey\)) previously involved in the [MİT trucks scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_National_Intelligence_Organisation_scandal_in_Turkey). Dündar and Gül were released in February 2016 when the Supreme Court decided that their detention was unlawful.[^cite_note-58] In July 2016, on the occasion of the launch of the campaign "I'm a journalist", Mehmet Koksal, project officer of the [European Federation of Journalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Federation_of_Journalists) declared that "Turkey has the largest number of journalists in jail out of all the countries in the [Council of Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Europe)."[^cite_note-59]

The situation further deteriorated as a consequence of the [2016 Turkish coup d'état attempt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Turkish_coup_d'%C3%A9tat_attempt) of 15 July 2016 and the subsequent government reaction, leading to an increase of attacks targeting the media in Turkey. Mustafa Cambaz, a photojournalist working for the daily Yeni Şafak was killed during the coup. Turkish soldiers attempting to overthrow the government took control of several newsrooms, including the Ankara-based headquarters of the state broadcaster [TRT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_Radio_and_Television_Corporation), where they forced anchor [Tijen Karaş](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tijen_Kara%C5%9F) to read a statement at gunpoint while members of the editorial board were held hostage and threatened. Soldiers also seized the Istanbul offices of Doğan Media Center which hosted several media outlets, including the *Hürriyet* daily newspaper and the private TV station [CNN Türk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CNN_T%C3%BCrk), holding journalists and other professionals hostage for many hours overnight. During the coup, in the streets of Istanbul, a photojournalist working for *Hürriyet* and the Associated Press was assaulted by civilians that were demonstrating against the coup.[^cite_note-60] In the following days, after the government regained power, the state regulatory authority, known as the Information Technologies and Communications Authority, shut down 20 independent online news portals. On 19 July, the Turkish Radio and Television Supreme Council decided to revoke the licence of 24 TV channels and radio stations for being allegedly connected to the [Gülen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%BClen_movement) community, without providing much details on this decision. Also, following the decision of declaring the state of emergency for three months taken on 21 July,[^cite_note-61] a series of limitations to freedom of expression and freedom of the media were imposed. The measures within the regime of emergency included the possibility of banning the printing, copying, publishing and distributing of newspapers, magazines, books and leaflets.[^cite_note-62]

An editorial criticizing press censorship published on 22 May 2015[^cite_note-NYT52215-63] and inclusion of Erdoğan as one of a rising class of "soft" dictators in an op-ed published in May 2015 in *[The New York Times](30680.md)*[^cite_note-NYT52415-64] resulted in a strong reaction by Erdoğan.[^cite_note-NYT52815-65] In an interview Dündar gave in July 2016, before the coup attempt and the government reaction, the journalist stated that "Turkey is going through its darkest period, journalism-wise. It has never been an easy country for journalists, but I think today it has reached its lowest point and is experiencing unprecedented repression".[^cite_note-66]

In October 2022, Turkey passed a law in which the government was given greater powers over social media and online news. The law, according to [Human Rights Watch](46530.md), will allow more power for the government to censor journalists as well as access to information.[^cite_note-\:26-67] At the time, 65 employees in media, including journalists were in prison or detention.[^cite_note-\:26-67] 25 [Kurdish](3979351.md) journalists were detained under suspicion of "membership of a terrorist organization" according to Human Rights Watch.[^cite_note-\:26-67] *Voice of America* and *Deutche Welle* were blocked in Turkey by a Turkish court in June 2022.[^cite_note-\:26-67]

## Legislative framework

The [Constitution of Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Turkey), at art. 28, states that the press is free and shall not be censored. Expressions of non-violent opinion are safeguarded by Article 10 of the [European Convention on Human Rights and Fundamental Freedoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Convention_on_Human_Rights_and_Fundamental_Freedoms), ratified by Turkey in 1954, and various provisions of the [International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Covenant_on_Civil_and_Political_Rights), signed by Turkey in 2000.[^cite_note-HRW02-41] Many Turkish citizens convicted under the laws mentioned below have applied to the [European Court of Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Court_of_Human_Rights) (ECHR) and won their cases.[^cite_note-HRW02-41]

Yet, Constitutional and international guarantees are undermined by restrictive provisions in the Criminal Code, Criminal Procedure Code, and anti-terrorism laws, effectively leaving prosecutors and judges with ample discretion to repress ordinary journalistic activities.[^cite_note-FH-2] The 2017 [Council of Europe Commissioner for Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commissioner_for_Human_Rights)' report on freedom of expression and media freedom in Turkey reiterated that censorship problems stem mainly from the Turkish Criminal Code and the Turkish Anti- Terrorism Law No. 3713.[^cite_note-68][^cite_note-\:10-69][^cite_note-\:11-70] Prosecutors continued to bring a number of cases for terrorism or membership of an armed organization mainly based on certain statements of the accused, as coinciding with the aims of such organization.[^cite_note-\:10-69]

Beside the [Article 301](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_301_\(Turkish_penal_code\)), amended in 2008, and [Article 312](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_312_\(Turkish_Penal_Code\)), more than 300 provisions constrained freedom of expression, religion, and association, according to the [Turkish Human Rights Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_Human_Rights_Association) (2002).[^cite_note-HRW02-41] Article No. 299 of the Turkish Criminal Code provides for criminal defamation of the Head of the State, and it is being increasingly enforced. 18 persons were imprisoned for this offense as of June 2016.[^cite_note-\:10-69][^cite_note-\:11-70] Article No. 295 of the Criminal Code is increasingly being enforced as well, with a "press silence" (*Yayın Yasağı*) being imposed for topics of relevant public interest such as terrorist attacks and bloody blasts.[^cite_note-71] The silence can be imposed on television, print, and radio, as well as on online content, web hosting, and internet service providers. Violations can result in up to three years of detention.[^cite_note-72]

Many of the repressive provisions found in the Press Law, the Political Parties Law, the Trade Union Law, the Law on Associations, and other legislation, were imposed by the military junta after its [coup in 1980](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_military_coup,_1980). As for the Internet, the relevant law is [Law No. 5651](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_Internet_Law) of 2007.[^cite_note-\:22-73]

According to the Council of Europe Commissioner and to the [Venice Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venice_Commission) for Democracy through Law, the decrees issued under the state of emergency since July 2016, conferred an almost limitless discretionary power to the Turkish executive to apply sweeping misure against NGOs, the media and the public sector.[^cite_note-\:10-69][^cite_note-\:12-74][^cite_note-\:13-75] Specifically, many NGOs were closed, the media organizations seized or shut down and public sector employees as well as journalists and media workers arrested or intimidated.[^cite_note-\:10-69]

## Article 301

Main article: [Article 301 (Turkish Penal Code)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_301_\(Turkish_Penal_Code\))

Article 301 is a provision in the Turkish penal code that, since 2005 made it a punishable offense to insult [Turkishness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkishness) or various official Turkish institutions. Charges were brought in more than 60 cases, some of which were high-profile.[^cite_note-guardian-richard-83]

The article was amended in 2008, including changing "Turkishness" into "the Turkish nation", reducing maximum prison terms to 2 years, and making it obligatory to get the approval of the [Minister of Justice](10283737.md) before filing a case.[^cite_note-84][^cite_note-85] Changes were deemed "largely cosmetic" by [Freedom House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_House),[^cite_note-FH-2] although the number of prosecutions dropped. Although only few persons were convicted, trials under Art. 301 are seen by human rights watchdogs as a punitive measure in themselves, as time-consuming and expensive, thus exerting a chilling effect on free speech.[^cite_note-FH-2]

- Novelist [Orhan Pamuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orhan_Pamuk), at the time a Nobel Prize candidate, was prosecuted under Article 301 for discussing the [Armenian genocide](140376.md); Pamuk subsequently won the prize.

- [Perihan Mağden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perihan_Ma%C4%9Fden), a columnist for the newspaper *[Radikal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radikal)*, was tried under the article for provocation, and acquitted on July 27, 2006; Mağden had broached the topic of [conscientious objection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conscientious_objection) to [mandatory military service](5735.md) as an abuse of human rights.

## Article 312

[Article 312](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_312_of_the_Turkish_Penal_Code) is a controversial article of the [Turkish Penal Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_Penal_Code) relating to inciting racial or religious hatred encouraging people to disobey the law. It has been used to prosecute journalists for writing about Kurdish issues.[^cite_note-91][^cite_note-92]

Article 312 imposes three-year prison sentences for incitement to commit an offence and incitement to [religious or racial hatred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hate_speech). In 1999 the mayor of Istanbul and current president of the [Turkish Republic](11125639.md) [Recep Tayyip Erdogan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recep_Tayyip_Erdogan) was sentenced to 10 months' imprisonment under Article 312 for reading a few lines from a poem that had been authorized by the [Ministry of National Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_National_Education_\(Turkey\)) for use in schools, and consequently had to resign.[^cite_note-Article_312_of_the_Turkish_Penal_Code_HRW02-93] In 2000 the chairman of the [Human Rights Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Rights_Association_\(Turkey\)), [Akin Birdal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akin_Birdal), was imprisoned under Article 312 for a speech in which he called for "peace and understanding" between Kurds and Turks,[^cite_note-Article_312_of_the_Turkish_Penal_Code_HRW02-93] and thereafter forced to resign, as the Law on Associations forbids persons who breach this and several other laws from serving as association officials.[^cite_note-Article_312_of_the_Turkish_Penal_Code_HRW02-93]

On February 6, 2002, a "mini-democracy package" was voted by [Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_\(Turkey\)), altering wording of Art. 312. Under the revised text, incitement can only be punished if it presents "a possible threat to [public order](3905736.md)".[^cite_note-Article_312_of_the_Turkish_Penal_Code_HRW02-93]

## Other

[Defamation](28661.md) and [libel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libel) remain criminal charges in Turkey (Article 125 of the Penal Code). They often result in fines and jail terms. Bianet counted 10 journalists convicted of defamation, [blasphemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blasphemy) or incitement to hatred in 2014.[^cite_note-FH-2]

[Article 216 of the Penal Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_216_\(Turkish_Penal_Code\)?action=edit\&redlink=1), banning incitement of hatred and violence on grounds of ethnicity, class or religion (with penalties of up to 3 years), is also used against journalists and media workers.[^cite_note-FH-2]

[Article 314 of the Penal Code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_314_\(Turkish_Penal_Code\)?action=edit\&redlink=1) is often invoked against journalists, particularly Kurds and leftists, due to its broad definition of terrorism and of membership in an armed organisation. It carries a minimum sentence of 7,5 years. According to the OSCE, most of 22 jailed journalists as of June 2014 had been charged or condemned based on Art. 314.

Article 81 of the Political Parties Law (imposed by the military junta in 1982) forbids parties from using any language other than Turkish in their written material or at any formal or public meetings. This law is strictly enforced.[^cite_note-HRW02-41] Kurdish deputy [Leyla Zana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leyla_Zana) was jailed in 1994, ostensibly for membership to the PKK.

In 1991, laws outlawing [communist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist) (Articles 141 and 142 of the criminal code) and [Islamic fundamentalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_fundamentalist) ideas (Article 163 of the criminal code) were repealed.[^cite_note-HRW02-41] This package of legal changes substantially freed up expression of leftist thought, but simultaneously created a new offence of "separatist propaganda" under Article 8 of the Anti-Terror Law.[^cite_note-HRW02-41] Prosecutors also began to use Article 312 of the criminal code (on religious or racial hatred) in place of Article 163.[^cite_note-HRW02-41]

The 1991 antiterrorism law (the Law on the Fight against Terrorism) has been invoked to charge and imprison journalists for activities that [Human Rights Watch](46530.md) define as "nonviolent political association" and speech. The [European Court of Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Court_of_Human_Rights) has in multiple occasions found the law to amount to [censorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship) and breach of freedom of expression.[^cite_note-FH-2]

Constitutional amendments adopted in October 2001 removed mention of "language forbidden by law" from legal provisions concerning free expression. Thereafter, university students began a campaign for optional courses in Kurdish to be put on the university curriculum, triggering more than 1,000 detentions throughout Turkey during December and January 2002.[^cite_note-HRW02-41] Actions have also been taken against the [Laz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laz_people) minority.[^cite_note-HRW02-41] According to the 1923 [Treaty of Lausanne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Lausanne), Turkey only recognizes the language rights of the Jewish, Greek and Armenian minorities.[^cite_note-HRW02-41] The government ignores Article 39(4) of the Treaty of Lausanne, which states that: "\[n]o restrictions shall be imposed on the free use by any Turkish national of any language in private intercourse, in commerce, religion, in the press or in publications of any kind or at public meetings."[^cite_note-HRW02-41] Pressured by the EU, Turkey has promised to review the Broadcasting Law.[^cite_note-HRW02-41]

Other legal changes in August 2002 allowed for the teaching of languages, including Kurdish.[^cite_note-Amnesty03-94] However, limitations on Kurdish broadcasting continue to be strong: according to the EU Commission (2006), "time restrictions apply, with the exception of films and music programmes. All broadcasts, except songs, must be subtitled or translated in Turkish, which makes live broadcasts technically cumbersome. Educational programmes teaching the Kurdish language are not allowed. The [Turkish Public Television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_Public_Television) (TRT) has continued broadcasting in five languages including Kurdish. However, the duration and scope of TRT's national broadcasts in five languages is very limited. No private broadcaster at national level has applied for broadcasting in languages other than Turkish since the enactment of the 2004 legislation."[^cite_note-EU-95] TRT broadcasts in Kurdish (as well as in [Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language) and [Circassian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Caucasian_languages) dialect) are symbolic,[^cite_note-96] compared to satellite broadcasts by channels such as controversial [Roj TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roj_TV), based in [Denmark](76972.md).

In 2003, Turkey adopted a [freedom of information law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_information_law?action=edit\&redlink=1). Yet, state secrets that may harm national security, economic interests, state investigations, or intelligence activity, or that "violate the private life of the individual," are exempt from requests. This has made accessing official information particularly difficult.[^cite_note-FH-2]

Amendments in 2013 (the Fourth Judicial Reform package), spurred by the EU accession process and a renewed Kurdish peace process, amended several laws. Antiterrorism regulations were tweaked so that publication of statements of illegal groups would only be a crime if the statement included coercion, violence, or genuine threats. Yet, the reform was deemed as not reaching international human rights standards, since it did not touch upon problematic norms such as the Articles 125, 301 and 314 of the Penal Code.[^cite_note-FH-2] In 2014, a Fifth Judicial Reform package was passed, which among others reduced the maximum period pretrial detention from 10 to 5 years. Consequently, several journalists were released from jail, pending trial.[^cite_note-FH-2]

New laws in 2014 were nevertheless detrimental to freedom of speech.[^cite_note-FH-2]

- February 2014 amendments to the [Law No. 5651 ("Internet Law")](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_Internet_Law) allowed the Telecommunication Authority ([TİB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C4%B0B?action=edit\&redlink=1)) powers to block websites on vague grounds of privacy protection, with only ex-post court intervention within 48h to confirm the block. A September 2014 amendment to Law no. 5651 had also allowed TİB to block websites “for national security, the restoration of public order, and the prevention of crimes”; this was later overturned by the Constitutional Court in October.

  [^cite_note-FH-2]

- April 2014 amendments to secret service regulations (Law Amending the Law on State Intelligence Services and the National Intelligence Organization) granted more powers to the [MİT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%B0T), including the faculty to access any personal data without a court order, as well as personal [legal immunity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_immunity) for breaches of the law. It also made it a crime, punished with up to 9 years in prison, to acquire or publish information on MİT activities.

  [^cite_note-FH-2]

- December 2014 amendments to the Penal and Criminal Procedure Codes made it possible to search persons or premises under simple “reasonable suspicion,” rather than “strong suspicion based on concrete evidence.” Police resorted to such grounds already in October, even before their actual approval, to raid the home of journalist Aytekin Gezici in [Adana](185243.md), after he had criticised the government on [Twitter](9988187.md).

  [^cite_note-FH-2]

- August 2016, Turkey closed the Presidency of Telecommunication and Communication which had been tasked with regulation of censorship and surveillance orders since 2005. The transfer of executive powers to the Information and Communication Technologies Authority eliminated ministerial oversight of [internet blocking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Block_\(Internet\)) orders as part of a wider set of reforms to introduce an executive presidency.

  [^cite_note-97]

In June 2018, [Esenyurt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esenyurt) municipality in [Istanbul](3391396.md) has taken down [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic) shop signs, citing a new regulation stipulating that shop signs must include at least 75 percent Turkish words. Esenyurt had one of the highest populations of Syrian refugees in Istanbul after the start of the [Syrian civil war](30741795.md) and many Syrian businesses started to pop up.[^cite_note-98]

On 14 October 2022, the parliament of Turkey adopted a legislative proposal that adds a new article, Article 217/A, to the Turkish Penal Code. Under the title *Halkı yanıltıcı bilgiyi alenen yayma suçu* ("Crime of publicly spreading misleading information"), the article sets a penalty of imprisonment for up to three years for publicly disseminating false information in a way that is "suitable for disrupting the public peace" for the purpose of creating "anxiety, fear or panic".[^cite_note-guardian20221013-99] Critics have pointed out that the law contains no clear definition of "false" or "misleading" information, opening the door to further abuse by courts to crack down on dissent.[^cite_note-100][^cite_note-guardian20221013-99] As formulated by a coalition of twenty-two international media freedom organizations, the bill "provides a framework for extensive censorship of online information and the criminalisation of journalism, which will enable the government to further subdue and control public debate in the lead up to Turkey's general elections in 2023".

## ECHR oversight

See also: [List of ECHR cases concerning Article 10 in Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_ECHR_cases_concerning_Article_10_in_Turkey)

Turkey is one of the [Council of Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Europe) member states with the greatest number of [ECHR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Court_of_Human_Rights)-recognised violations of rights included in the [European Convention on Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Convention_on_Human_Rights). Of these, several concern [Article 10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_10_of_the_European_Convention_on_Human_Rights) of the convention, on [freedom of expression](21401843.md).

- The *Tanıyan v. Turkey* case (no. 29910/96) concerned the confiscation orders were issued for 117 of the 126 issues of the *Yeni Politika* daily published in 1995, either under the Prevention of Terrorism Act or under Article 312 of the Criminal Code. The Turkish government struck a [friendly settlement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friendly_settlement) with Necati Tanıyan in 2005, paying EUR 7,710 in damages and recognising the "interference" and the need "to ensure that the amended Article 312 will be applied in accordance with the requirements of Article 10 of the Convention as interpreted in the Court's case-law".

  [^cite_note-101]

- The *Halis Doğan et al. v. Turkey* case (no. 50693/99) concerned 6 journalists (including [Ragıp Zarakolu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rag%C4%B1p_Zarakolu)) who worked for the Turkish daily newspaper *Özgür Bakış*. The newspaper was banned from the provinces of south-east Anatolia ([OHAL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OHAL)) in which a state of emergency had been declared on 7 May 1999. The ECHR struck the decision as unmotivated, arbitrary, and lacking a mechanism of judicial appeal.

  [^cite_note-102]

- The *Demirel and Ateş v. Turkey* case (no. 10037/03 and 14813/03), concerned the editor and owner of the weekly newspaper *Yedinci Gündem* (Seventh Order of the Day), twice fined in 2002 for publishing statements and an interview with members of the PKK ([Workers’ Party of Kurdistan](69680.md)). The paper was also temporarily closed down. The ECHR condemned Turkey in 2007, as the controversial contents did not incite violence or constitute [hate speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hate_speech).

  [^cite_note-103]

- The *[Ürper and Others v. Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%9Crper_and_Others_v._Turkey?action=edit\&redlink=1)* cases (2007) concerned 26 Turkish citizens, either owners or directors and journalists of four daily newspapers (*Ülkede Özgür Gündem*, *Gündem*, *Güncel* and *Gerçek Demokrasi*) which were repeatedly suspended for up to one month each between November 2006 and October 2007, as being considered PKK propaganda outlets. The applicants were also criminally prosecuted. The ECHR in 2009 condemned the suspension of future publications based on assumptions as an unjustifiable restriction to press freedom.

  [^cite_note-104]

- [Özgür Gündem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%96zg%C3%BCr_G%C3%BCndem) case (2000): Özgür Gündem is a pro- Kurdish and leftist media outlet based in Istanbul. From the beginning of the ‘90's, the newspaper has been subject to raids and legal actions, with many journalists being arrested and even killed. The paper remained closed from 1994 to 2011 due to a court order. These facts were the bases for the *[Özgür Gündem v. Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%96zg%C3%BCr_G%C3%BCndem_v._Turkey?action=edit\&redlink=1)* case before the ECtHR.

  [^cite_note-\:19-105]

## Attacks and threats against journalists

### Physical attacks and assassinations of journalists

Main article: [List of journalists killed in Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_journalists_killed_in_Turkey)

See also: [Attempted assassination of Pope John Paul II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attempted_assassination_of_Pope_John_Paul_II)

The physical [safety of journalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safety_of_journalists) in Turkey is at risk.

Several journalists died in the 1990s at the height of the [Kurdish–Turkish conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurdish%E2%80%93Turkish_conflict_\(1978%E2%80%93present\)). Soon after the pro-Kurdish press had started to publish the first daily newspaper by the name of "[Özgür Gündem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%96zg%C3%BCr_G%C3%BCndem)" (Free Agenda) killings of Kurdish journalists started. Hardly any of them have been clarified or resulted in sanctions for the assailants. "Murder by unknown assailants" (tr: *faili meçhul*) is the term used in Turkish to indicate that the perpetrators were not identified because of them being protected by the State and cases of disappearance. The list of names of distributors of Özgür Gündem and its successors that were killed (while the perpetrators mostly remained unknown) includes 18 names.[^cite_note-109] Among the 33 journalists that were killed between 1990 and 1995, most were working for the so-called Kurdish Free Press.

The killings of journalists in Turkey since 1995 are more or less individual cases. Most prominent among the victims is [Hrant Dink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hrant_Dink), killed in 2007, but the death of [Metin Göktepe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metin_G%C3%B6ktepe) also raised great concern, since police officers beat him to death. The death of [Metin Alataş](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metin_Alata%C5%9F?action=edit\&redlink=1) in 2010 is also a source of disagreement: though the autopsy claimed it was suicide, his family and colleagues demanded an investigation. He had formerly received death threats and had been violently assaulted.[^cite_note-110] Since 2014, several Syrian journalists who were working from Turkey and reporting on the rise of [Daesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daesh) have been assassinated.

In 2014, journalists suffered obstruction, [tear gas](1202646.md) injuries, and physical assault by the police while covering several major protests: the February protests against internet censorship; the [May Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_Day) demonstrations; and the [Gezi Park protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gezi_Park_protests) anniversaries (when CNN correspondent [Ivan Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Watson) was shortly detained and physically mistreated). Turkish security forces fired [tear gas](1202646.md) at journalists reporting from the border close to the Syrian town of [Kobane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kobane) in October.[^cite_note-FH-2]

- The [CPJ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_to_Protect_Journalists) counted one media-related killing in 2014, the one of Kadir Bağdu who was shot in [Adana](185243.md) while delivering the pro-Kurdish daily *[Azadiya Welat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azadiya_Welat)*.

  [^cite_note-FH-2]

- The general secretary of the Turkish Journalists’ Union, [Mustafa Kuleli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mustafa_Kuleli?action=edit\&redlink=1), as well as journalist Hasan Cömert, were attacked in February 2014 by unknown assailants.

- The journalist Mithat Fabian Sözmen had to seek medical care after a physical attack in March 2014.

  [^cite_note-FH-2]

### Arrests of journalists

Main article: [List of arrested journalists in Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_arrested_journalists_in_Turkey)

Despite the 2004 Press Law only foreseeing fines, other restrictive laws have led to several journalists and writers being put behind bars. According to a report published by the [Committee to Protect Journalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_to_Protect_Journalists) (CPJ), at least seven journalists remained in prison by the end of 2014. The independent Turkish press agency [Bianet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bianet) counted 22 journalists and 10 publishers in jail - most of them [Kurds](17068.md), charged with association with an illegal organisation.[^cite_note-FH-2]

In 2016, Turkey became the country with the highest population of jailed journalists. As to the committee to Protect Journalists (CPJ) rank, Turkey was the first country ever to jail 81 journalists, editors and media practitioners in one year.[^cite_note-111]

According to a CPJ report, Turkish authorities are engaging in widespread criminal prosecution and jailing of journalists, and are applying other forms of severe pressure to encourage self-censorship in the press. The CPJ found that this pressure takes the form of highly repressive laws, particularly in the penal code and anti-terror law; a criminal procedure code that greatly favors the state; and a harsh anti-press tone set at the highest levels of government. Turkey's press freedom situation has reached a crisis point.[^cite_note-Turkey's_Press_Freedom_Crisis-112] This reports mentions 3 types of journalists targeted :

- Investigative and critical reporters: victims of the anti-state prosecutions: The government's broad inquiry into the [Ergenekon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergenekon_\(allegation\)) plot ensnared investigative reporters. But the evidence, rather than revealing conspirators, points to a government intent on punishing critical reporters.

- Kurdish journalists: Turkish authorities conflate support for the Kurdish cause with terrorism itself. When it comes to Kurdish journalists, newsgathering activities such as fielding tips, covering protests, and conducting interviews are evidence of a crime.

- Collateral damages of the general assault on the press: The authorities are waging one of the world's biggest anti-press campaigns in recent history. Dozens of writers and editors are in prison, nearly all on terrorism or other anti-state charges.

  [^cite_note-Turkey's_Press_Freedom_Crisis-112]

[Kemalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kemalist) and/or nationalist journalists were arrested on charges referring to the [Ergenekon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergenekon_\(organization\)) case and several left-wing and Kurdish journalists were arrested on charges of engaging in propaganda for the [PKK](69680.md) listed as a terrorist organization. In short, writing an article or making a speech can still lead to a court case and a long prison sentence for membership or leadership of a terrorist organisation. Together with possible pressure on the press by state officials and possible firing of critical journalists, this situation can lead to a widespread self-censorship.[^cite_note-113][^cite_note-114]

In November 2013, three journalists were sentenced to [life in prison](148681.md) as senior members of the illegal [Marxist–Leninist Communist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxist%E2%80%93Leninist_Communist_Party_\(Turkey/Northern_Kurdistan\)) - among them the founder of [Özgür Radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%96zg%C3%BCr_Radio?action=edit\&redlink=1), [Füsun Erdoğan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F%C3%BCsun_Erdo%C4%9Fan). They had been arrested in 2006 and held until 2014, when they were released following legal reforms on pre-trial detention terms. An appeal is still pending.[^cite_note-FH-2]

In February 2017, German-Turkish journalist [Deniz Yücel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deniz_Y%C3%BCcel) was jailed in Istanbul.[^cite_note-115][^cite_note-116][^cite_note-117]

On April 10, 2017, the Italian journalist [Gabriele Del Grande](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriele_del_Grande) was arrested in Hatay and jailed in Mugla.[^cite_note-\:21-118] He was in Turkey in order to write a book on the war in Syria. He went on hunger strike on April 18, 2017[^cite_note-\:21-118] and was freed six days later, on April 24, 2017.[^cite_note-119]

### Judicial prosecution

Main article: [List of prosecuted Turkish writers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_prosecuted_Turkish_writers)

[Defamation](28661.md) and [libel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libel) remain criminal charges in Turkey. They often result in fines and jail terms. Bianet counted 10 journalists convicted of defamation, [blasphemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blasphemy) or incitement to hatred in 2014.[^cite_note-FH-2]

Courts' activities on media-related cases, particularly those concerning the corruption scandals surrounding Erdoğan and his close circle, have cast doubts on the independence and impartiality of the judiciary in Turkey. The [Turkish Journalists' Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_Journalists'_Association) and the [Turkish Journalists' Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_Journalists'_Union?action=edit\&redlink=1) counted 60 new journalists under prosecution for this single issue in 2013, for a total number of over 100 lawsuits.[^cite_note-FH-2]

### Hostile public rhetoric and smear campaigns

Particularly since 2013, the President Erdoğan and other governmental officials have resorted to hostile public rhetoric against independent journalists and media outlets, which is then echoed in the pro-governmental press and TV, accusing foreign media and interest groups of conspiring to bring down his government.[^cite_note-FH-2]

- The *[Economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Economist)* correspondent, [Amberin Zaman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amberin_Zaman), was publicly denounced as a "shameless militant" by Erdoğan at a pre-electoral rally in August 2014. Erdoğan tried to intimidate her by telling her to "know \[her] place". She was then subjected to a deluge of abuse and threats on social media by AKP supporters in the following months.

  [^cite_note-FH-2]

- In September 2014 *[The New York Times](30680.md)* reporter Ceylan Yeğinsu was publicly smeared and depicted as a traitor for a photograph caption in a reportage on ISIS recruitment in Turkey. The U.S. State Department criticized Turkey for such intimidation attempts.

  [^cite_note-FH-2]

### Arbitrary denial of access

Turkish authorities have been reported as denying access to events and information to journalists for political reasons.[^cite_note-FH-2]

- In December 2013, after the press had unveiled an alleged corruption scandal involving top government officials, the police department announced the closure of two press rooms in Istanbul and declared that journalists would not be allowed to enter police facilities unless strictly for formal press conferences.

  [^cite_note-FH-2]

- 2014 saw a worsening of discriminatory accreditation policies. AKP meetings were off-limits for critical journalists. In case of visits abroad, foreign officials had to hold separate press conferences to allow unaccredited media correspondents.

## Government control over the media

See also: [Transparency of media ownership in Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transparency_of_media_ownership_in_Turkey) and [Concentration of media ownership in Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentration_of_media_ownership_in_Turkey)

Since 2011, the [AKP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justice_and_Development_Party_\(Turkey\)) government has increased restrictions on freedom of speech, freedom of the press and internet use,[^cite_note-nytimes.com-45] and television content,[^cite_note-Al-Monitor-46] as well as the right to free assembly.[^cite_note-spiegel.de-47] It has also developed links with media groups, and used administrative and legal measures (including, in [one case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Do%C4%9Fan_Holding), a billion tax fine) against critical media groups and critical journalists: "over the last decade the AKP has built an informal, powerful, coalition of party-affiliated businessmen and media outlets whose livelihoods depend on the political order that Erdoğan is constructing. Those who resist do so at their own risk."[^cite_note-ForeignPolicy-48]

These behaviours became particularly prominent in 2013 in the context of the Turkish media coverage of the [Gezi Park protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gezi_Park_protests). The BBC noted that while some outlets are aligned with the AKP or are personally close to Erdoğan, "most mainstream media outlets - such as TV news channels HaberTurk and NTV, and the major centrist daily *Milliyet* - are loth to irritate the government because their owners' business interests at times rely on government support. All of these have tended to steer clear of covering the demonstrations."[^cite_note-BBCTurksdeprived-51] Few channels provided live coverage – one that did was [Halk TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halk_TV).[^cite_note-dw\.de-52] Several private media outlets were reported as engaging in self-censorship due to political pressures. The 2014 [local](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Turkish_local_elections) and [presidential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Turkish_presidential_election) elections exposed the extent of biased coverage by pro-government media.[^cite_note-FH-2]

### Direct control over state media

The state-run [Anadolu Agency](43356543.md) and the [Turkish Radio and Television Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_Radio_and_Television_Corporation) (TRT) have also been criticized by media outlets and opposition parties, for acting more and more like a mouthpiece for the ruling [AKP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justice_and_Development_Party_\(Turkey\)), a stance in stark violation of their requirement as public institutions to report and serve the public in an objective way.[^cite_note-126]

In 2014, the TRT, the state broadcaster, as well as the state-owned [Anadolu Agency](43356543.md), were subject to stricter controls. Even [RTÜK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_and_Television_Supreme_Council) warned TRT for disproportionate coverage of the AKP; the Supreme Board of Elections fined the public broadcaster for not reporting at all on presidential candidates other than Erdoğan, between August 6 and 8. The [Council of Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Europe) observers reported concern about the unfair media advantage for the incumbent ruling party.[^cite_note-FH-2]

### Pro-governmental "Pool Media"

During its 12-year rule, the ruling [AKP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justice_and_Development_Party_\(Turkey\)) has gradually expanded its control over media.[^cite_note-Aksera+Baybars-Hawks-13] Today, numerous newspapers, TV channels and internet portals also dubbed as *Yandaş Medya* ("Partisan Media") or *Havuz Medyası* ("Pool Media") continue their heavy pro-government propaganda.[^cite_note-\:1-14] Several media groups receive preferential treatment in exchange for AKP-friendly editorial policies.[^cite_note-\:2-15] Some of these media organizations were acquired by AKP-friendly businesses through questionable funds and processes.[^cite_note-\:3-16]

Leaked telephone calls between high ranking AKP officials and businessmen indicate that government officials collected money from businessmen in order to create a "pool media" that will support [AKP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justice_and_Development_Party_\(Turkey\)) government at any cost.[^cite_note-127][^cite_note-128] Arbitrary tax penalties are assessed to force newspapers into bankruptcy—after which they emerge, owned by friends of the president. According to an investigation by [Bloomberg](3057255.md),[^cite_note-129] Erdoğan forced a sale of the once independent daily [*Sabah*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabah_\(newspaper\)) to a consortium of businessmen led by his son-in-law.[^cite_note-130]

### Direct pressures and self-censorship of major media outlets

Major media outlets in Turkey belong to certain group of influential businessman or holdings. In nearly all cases, these holding companies earn only a small fraction of their revenue from their media outlets, with the bulk of profits coming from other interests, such as construction, mining, finance, or energy.[^cite_note-131] Therefore, media groups usually practice [self-censorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-censorship) to protect their wider business interests.

Media not friendly to the AKP are threatened with intimidation, inspections and fines.[^cite_note-\:4-19] These media group owners face similar threats to their other businesses.[^cite_note-\:5-20] An increasing number of columnists have been fired for criticizing the AKP leadership.[^cite_note-\:6-21][^cite_note-\:7-22][^cite_note-\:8-23][^cite_note-\:9-24]

In addition to the censorship practiced by pro-government media such as *[Sabah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabah_\(newspaper\))*, *[Yeni Şafak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeni_%C5%9Eafak)*, and *[Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_\(Turkish_newspaper\))*, the majority of other newspapers, such as *[Sözcü](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%B6zc%C3%BC)*, *[Zaman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaman_\(newspaper\))*, *[Milliyet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milliyet)*, and *[Radikal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radikal)* have been reported as practicing self-censorship to protect their business interests and using the market share (65% of the total newspapers sold daily in Turkey as opposed to pro-government media[^cite_note-132]) to avoid retaliatory action by the AKP government of [Recep Tayyip Erdoğan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recep_Tayyip_Erdo%C4%9Fan).[^cite_note-133]

During the period before the [Turkish local elections of 2014](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Turkish_local_elections), a number of phone calls between prime minister [Recep Tayyip Erdoğan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recep_Tayyip_Erdo%C4%9Fan) and media executives were leaked to the internet.[^cite_note-134] Most of the recordings were between Erdoğan and [Habertürk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habert%C3%BCrk) newspaper & TV channel executive Fatih Saraç. In those recordings, it can be heard that Erdoğan was calling Fatih Saraç when he was unhappy about a news item published in the newspaper or broadcast on TV. He was demanding Fatih Saraç to be careful next time or censor any particular topics he is not happy about.[^cite_note-135] At another leaked call, Erdoğan gets very upset and angry over a news published at [Milliyet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milliyet) newspaper and reacts harshly to Erdoğan Demirören, owner of the newspaper. Later, it can be heard that Demirören is reduced to tears.[^cite_note-136] During a call between Erdoğan and editor-in-chief of [Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_\(Turkish_newspaper\)) daily Mustafa Karaalioğlu, Erdoğan lashes out at Karaalioğlu for allowing [Mehmet Altan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehmet_Altan) to continue writing such critical opinions about a speech the prime minister had delivered recently. In the second conversation, Erdoğan is heard grilling Karaalioğlu over his insistence on keeping [Hidayet Şefkatli Tuksal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hidayet_%C5%9Eefkatli_Tuksal), a female columnist in the paper despite her critical expressions about him.[^cite_note-137] Later, both Altan and Tuksal got fired from Star newspaper. Erdoğan acknowledged that he called media executives.[^cite_note-138]

In 2014, direct pressures from the executive and the Presidency have led to the dismissal of media workers for their critical articles. [Bianet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bianet) records over 339 journalists and media workers being laid off or forced to quit in the year - several of them due to political pressures.[^cite_note-FH-2]

- In August 2014 [Enis Berberoğlu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enis_Berbero%C4%9Flu), the editor-in-chief of *[Hürriyet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C3%BCrriyet)* newspaper, quit the paper right before the [2014 Turkish presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_Turkish_presidential_election). It has been reported that he was forced to resign after a clash with the publishing company [Doğan Holding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Do%C4%9Fan_Holding), due to Berberoğlu's refusal to fire a columnist. The day before, Erdoğan had publicly criticized the Doğan group. Hürriyet denied pressures related to the case.

  [^cite_note-FH-2]

### Removing channels from government-controlled TV satellites

[Türksat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C3%BCrksat_\(company\)) is the sole communications satellite operator in Turkey. There have been allegations that TV channels critical of the [AKP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justice_and_Development_Party_\(Turkey\)) party and [President Erdoğan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recep_Tayyip_Erdo%C4%9Fan) have been removed from Türksat's infrastructure, and that Türksat's executive board is dominated by pro-Erdoğan figures.

In October 2015 a video recording emerged of a 2 February 2015 conversation between Mustafa Varank, advisor to President Erdoğan and board member of Türksat, and some journalists in which Varank states that he had urged Türksat to drop certain TV channels because "they are airing reports that harm the government's prestige". Later that year the TV channels Irmak TV, [Bugün TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koza_%C4%B0pek_Holding?action=edit\&redlink=1), and Kanaltürk, known for their critical stance against the government, were notified by Türksat that their contracts would not be renewed as of November 2015, and were told to remove their platforms from Türksat's infrastructure.[^cite_note-173]

Türksat dropped TV channels critical of the government from its platform in November 2015. The broadcasting of TV stations—including Samanyolu TV, Mehtap TV, S Haber and Radio Cihan—that are critical of the ruling AKP government were halted by Türksat because of a “legal obligation” to the order of a prosecutor's office, based on the suspicion that the channels are supporting a terrorist organization. Among the TV and radio stations removed were Samanyolu Europe, Ebru TV, Mehtap TV, Samanyolu Haber, Irmak TV, Yumurcak TV, Dünya TV, MC TV, Samanyolu Africa, Tuna Shopping TV, Burç FM, Samanyolu Haber Radio, Mehtap Radio and Radio Cihan.[^cite_note-174]

The critical Bugün and Kanaltürk TV channels, which were seized by a government-initiated move in October 2015, were also dropped from Türksat in November 2015. Later on 1 March 2016 these two seized channels closed due to financial reasons by government trustees.[^cite_note-175]

In March 2016 the two TV channels from other wings of the politics were also removed from Türksat, namely, Turkish Nationalist [Benguturk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benguturk?action=edit\&redlink=1) and Kurdish Nationalist [IMC TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IMC_TV).[^cite_note-BT-IMC-176]

On 25 September 2017, Turkey decided to remove broadcaster [Rudaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudaw_Media_Network), which is affiliated to the [Kurdistan Region](3949386.md), from its satellite broadcasting on the same day voting took place on an [independence referendum in the KRG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_Kurdistan_Region_independence_referendum).[^cite_note-177]

## Censorship of the media

See also: [Mass media in Turkey](2945560.md) and [Concentration of media ownership in Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentration_of_media_ownership_in_Turkey)

Censorship of sensitive topics in Turkey happens both online and offline. Kurdish issues, the [Armenian genocide](140376.md), as well as subjects controversial for Islam or the Turkish state are often censored. Enforcement remains arbitrary and unpredictable.[^cite_note-FH-2] Also, defamation of the Head of the State is a crime provision increasingly used for censoring critical voices in Turkey.[^cite_note-\:11-70]

In the 2026 [Reporters Without Borders](526054.md) [World Press Freedom Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Press_Freedom_Index), Turkey is ranked in the 163rd place out of 180 countries.[^cite_note-\:0-178] The situation for [free expression](21401843.md) has always been troubled in Turkey.[^cite_note-179][^cite_note-180] The situation dramatically deteriorated after the [2013 Gezi protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_censorship_and_disinformation_during_the_Gezi_Park_protests),[^cite_note-181] reaching its peak after the 15 July [2016 coup attempt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Turkish_coup_d'%C3%A9tat_attempt). From that moment on, a [state of emergency](298608.md) is in force,[^cite_note-182] tens of thousand of journalists, academics, public officials and intellectuals have been arrested or charged, mainly with terrorist charges, sometimes following some statement or writing of them.[^cite_note-\:0-178]

The [Council of Europe Commissioner for Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commissioner_for_Human_Rights)' report on freedom of expression and media freedom in Turkey, after his 2016 visits to Turkey, noted that the violations to freedom of expression in Turkey have created a distinct [chilling effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chilling_effect), manifesting in [self- censorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-censorship) both among the remaining media and among ordinary citizens.[^cite_note-\:10-69] In addition, the Commissioner wrote that the main obstacle to an improvement of the situation of freedom of expression and media freedom in Turkey is the lack of political will both to acknowledge and to address such problems.[^cite_note-\:10-69]

### Reporting bans and gag orders

In 2017, the Council of Europe Commissioner for Human Rights noted that with regard to judicial harassment restricting freedom of expression the main issues consist in:[^cite_note-\:10-69]

- [Backsliding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_backsliding) in the case-law of the Turkish judiciary;

- Issues related to the independence of the judiciary and of the judicial culture;

- Defamation remains a criminal offence and causes dangerous [chilling effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chilling_effect), in particular defamation of the President of the Republic and of public officials;

- Harassment restricted the parliamentary debate, after the lift of the immunity of parliamentarians. Most of the opposition [HD Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peoples'_Democratic_Party_\(Turkey\)) MPs are under investigations, if not in prison;

- Great restrictions of academic freedoms: many academics were dismissed, forced to resign, suspended or taken into police custody;

- Harassment involves all sectors of Turkish society, e.g. human rights defenders. There are frequent impositions of media bans or blackouts concerning events of clear public interest and an excessive use of detention on remand.

As to January 18, 2017, more than 150 media outlets were closed and their assets liquidated by governmental decrees.[^cite_note-\:12-74][^cite_note-\:13-75][^cite_note-\:15-169] Under [emergency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Turkish_coup_d'%C3%A9tat_attempt) decree No. 687 of February 9, 2017, Turkey's [Saving Deposit Insurance Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savings_Deposit_Insurance_Fund_of_Turkey) (TMSF) will be authorized to sell companies seized by the state through the appointment of [trustees](506701.md).[^cite_note-\:16-170][^cite_note-\:17-171] Also, through the use of emergency decrees- such as Nos. 668 (July 27, 2016), 675 (October 29, 2016) and 677 (November 22, 2016), 178 media organizations were closed down being charged of having terrorist affiliations. As to November 2016, Twenty-four of these shut-down media organizations were radio stations, twenty- eight televisions, eighty newspapers.[^cite_note-\:18-172]

In 2014, Turkish regulators issued several reporting bans on public interest issues.[^cite_note-FH-2]

- In February 2014 it was forbidden to report on allegations of MİT involvement in the transfer of weapons to Syria.

- In March 2014 leaked audio recordings of a national security meeting at the Foreign Ministry were put under gag order.

- In May 2014 the [Radio and Television Supreme Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_and_Television_Supreme_Council) (RTÜK) warned broadcasters to refrain from showing materials deemed “disrespectful to feelings of the families of victims” after the [Soma mine disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soma_mine_disaster). The country worst mining disaster, causing 301 deaths, remained absent from most mainstream media outlets.

- In June 2014 a reporting ban was issued concerning the [kidnapping by ISIL of 49 Turkish citizens from the Turkish consulate in Mosul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Mosul#Aftermath), Iraq.

- In November 2014 a court in Ankara issued an unprecedented reporting ban on a parliamentary inquiry into corruption allegations concerning four former ministers.

- In September 2014 the premises of the online newspapers *Gri Hat* and *Karşı Gazete* were raided and searched by police after they had published information on the alleged corruption scandal. The police demanded the removal of online information, despite only having a search warrant.

  [^cite_note-FH-2]

In 2012, as part of the Third Reform Package, all previous bans on publications were cancelled unless renewed by court - which happened for most leftist and Kurdish publications.[^cite_note-FH-2]

Academics are also affected by government's censorship. In this regard, the case of the [Academics for Peace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academics_for_Peace) is particularly relevant:[^cite_note-barisicinakademisyenler.net-89] on January 14, 2016, 27 academics were detained for interrogations after having signed a petition with more than other 1.000 people asking for peace in the south-east of the country, where there are ongoing violent clashes between the Turkish army and the PKK.[^cite_note-The_Guardian-90]

## Internet censorship

See also: [Category\:Internet censorship in Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category\:Internet_censorship_in_Turkey), [Internet regulation in Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_regulation_in_Turkey), and [Block of Wikipedia in Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Block_of_Wikipedia_in_Turkey)

Turkey's Internet censorship regime shifted from "moderate" to "severe" in late 2016 following a series of social media shutdowns, regional [Internet blackouts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_blackout) and restrictions on [VPN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_private_network) and [Tor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tor_\(anonymity_network\)) circumvention tools documented by independent digital rights watchdog [Turkey Blocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey_Blocks).[^cite_note-242][^cite_note-243] Months earlier, human rights research group [Freedom House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_House) had already downgraded its outlook of internet freedom in the country to "Not Free", noting in its report that the assessment was made before further restrictions following the abortive military coup in July.[^cite_note-244]

With regard to Internet censorship, in the 2017 Report on media freedom and freedom of expression in Turkey, the [Commissioner for Human Rights of the Council of Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commissioner_for_Human_Rights) found out:[^cite_note-\:10-69]

- the increase of blocking and filtering of web pages;

- an increased practice of resorting to bandwidth throttling during times of domestic crises, making certain social media and platforms inaccessible.;

- cases of full internet shutdowns;

- Increase of prosecutions and detentions for online activities causing a great chilling effect (a.k.a. self-censorship).

In earlier years, the Turkish government implemented legal and institutional reforms driven by the country's ambitions to become a [European Union](9317.md) member state. At the same time Turkey demonstrated its high sensitivity to defamation and other "inappropriate" online content, resulting in the closure of a number of local and international Web sites. All Internet traffic passes through [Türk Telekom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C3%BCrk_Telekom)'s infrastructure, allowing centralized control over online content and facilitating the implementation of shutdown decisions.[^cite_note-245][^cite_note-246]

In December 2010 the [OpenNet Initiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenNet_Initiative), a non-partisan organization based in Canada and the United States that investigates, analyzes, and exposes Internet filtering and surveillance practices, classified Internet censorship in Turkey as selective (third lowest of four classifications) in the political, social, and Internet tools areas and found no evidence of censorship in the conflict/security area.[^cite_note-ONICP-Turkey-247] However, also in 2010, [Reporters Without Borders](526054.md) added Turkey to its list of 16 countries "under surveillance" (the less serious of two Internet censorship lists that it maintains), saying:

> The year 2010 was marked by the widely covered deblocking of the video-sharing website [YouTube](3524766.md) which, unfortunately, did not equate to a lifting of online censorship in Turkey. In a country where taboo topics abound, several thousand websites are still inaccessible and legal proceedings against online journalists persist.[^cite_note-248]

In July 2010 the [Alternative Informatics Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternative_Informatics_Association) organized one of the first and largest street protests against Internet censorship in Istanbul. A second protest took place in May 2011 with demonstrations in 30 cities in Turkey.[^cite_note-249]

In its *[Freedom on the Net](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_on_the_Net) 2016* report, Freedom House gave Turkey a "freedom on the net status" of "not free" saying that:[^cite_note-250]

- Mobile and internet connections were repeatedly suspended in [Yuksekova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y%C3%BCksekova), [Cizre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cizre), Sur, [Silopi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silopi), and other cities in the southeast of the country during raids by security agencies against militants; Twitter, Facebook, and YouTube were temporarily blocked on numerous occasions—typically in the aftermath of terrorist attacks—until they restricted access to specific posts or accounts;

- Turkey accounted for almost 90 percent of all content that was locally restricted by Twitter in the second half of 2015. Turkey's regulator fined the company TRY 150,000 (US\$51,000) for refusing to remove what it termed "terrorist propaganda" from the site;

- Pro-government trolls have escalated their campaigns to harass opposition voices and organizations on social media through smear campaigns and fake accounts;

- Journalists such as Hayri Tunç, [Aytekin Gezici](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aytekin_Gezici?action=edit\&redlink=1), and [Bülent Keneş](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%BClent_Kene%C5%9F) received lengthy prison sentences for "insulting" public officials or spreading "terrorism propaganda";

- A 14-day cyberattack brought almost 400,000 Turkish websites offline and temporarily suspended retail banking services in the country.

The *Freedom on the Net 2015* report, tracked that over 60,000 websites remain blocked in Turkey, and that TIB blocked 22,645 websites without prior court order only in 2014. [Twitter](9988187.md) was blocked for two weeks and [YouTube](3524766.md) for two months in 2014.[^cite_note-FH-2][^cite_note-FOTN-2015-Turkey-251] On March 21, 2014, Twitter access for Turkish users was blocked for two weeks in the run-up to local elections to prevent a stream of leaked wiretapped recordings of senior officials that had appeared on the site, prompting Prime Minister [Recep Tayyip Erdoğan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recep_Tayyip_Erdo%C4%9Fan) to declare he would "root out" the network.[^cite_note-telegraph-252] In March 2014, Google claimed that Turkish ISPs had set up servers that were masquerading as Google's DNS, in order to block services such as YouTube and Twitter.[^cite_note-253]

In the 11th biannual transparency report published on September 19, 2017, [Twitter](9988187.md) said that Turkey was the first among countries where about 90 percent of removal requests came from.[^cite_note-254] Also, Turkey has submitted the highest volume of removal requests to Twitter in 2014,[^cite_note-reuters-12] 2015,[^cite_note-bloomberg-255][^cite_note-256] and 2016.[^cite_note-bloomberg-255]

During the [2016–17 purges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016%E2%80%9317_purges_in_Turkey), the secure [instant messaging](55951.md) app [ByLock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ByLock_\(application\)) was accused by the Turkish government of being used primarily by members of the [Gülen movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%BClen_movement), which it classifies as a terrorist organization, during the failed coup. The government launched investigations of over 23,000 citizens for connections to Gülen, based solely on evidence that they had downloaded or used ByLock. Some of these investigations resulted in arrests and detainment. However, in December 2017, the government announced that it would investigate 11,480 phone numbers had been falsely accused of ties to ByLock and Gülen, after finding that the accusations were induced by unrelated apps embedding a [web beacon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_beacon) pointing to the ByLock website from within. An arrest warrant was also issued against the developer of one of these apps.[^cite_note-257][^cite_note-258]

According to the decision published in the *[T.C. Resmî Gazete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T.C._Resm%C3%AE_Gazete)* on 1 August 2019, online media service providers such as [Netflix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netflix), BluTV and [Puhutv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puhutv), which broadcast series on the Internet, came under the control of [RTÜK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RT%C3%9CK). Following this decision, digital broadcasting platforms were obliged to obtain a broadcast license to continue broadcasting.[^cite_note-259] With the decision, the RTÜK administration specified that the violation of the rules of broadcasting could result in sanctions for the broadcasters.[^cite_note-260]

On 1 July 2020, in a statement made to his party members, Erdoğan announced that the government would introduce new measures and regulations to control or shut down social media platforms such as [YouTube](3524766.md), [Twitter](9988187.md) and [Netflix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netflix). Through these new measures, each company would be required to appoint an official representative in the country to respond to legal concerns. The decision comes after a number of Twitter users insulted his daughter [Esra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esra_Erdo%C4%9Fan) after she welcomed her fourth child.[^cite_note-261]

### Legal framework

[Internet Law No. 5651](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_Internet_Law) was enacted in 2007 Turkey with the declared objective of protecting families and minors.[^cite_note-\:22-73][^cite_note-\:24-262] The way for its enactment was paved after the ban imposed on Youtube.com in 2007, because of a video insulting the Turkish Republic's founder [Kemal Atatürk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mustafa_Kemal_Atat%C3%BCrk).[^cite_note-\:24-262] Since then, such law was enforced in a restrictive manner, often causing episodes of censorship against common citizens, journalists and media outlets.[^cite_note-263] For this reason, experts consider Law No. 5651 particularly controversial.[^cite_note-264]

On 5 February 2014 the Turkish Parliament adopted a controversial bill amending the [Internet regulation in Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_regulation_in_Turkey). It allows the telecommunications authority ([TIB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidency_of_Telecommunication_and_Communication)) to block any website within 4 hours without first seeking a court ruling, and requires Internet providers to store all data on web users' activities for two years and make it available to the authorities upon request.[^cite_note-265] After the 15 July 2016 coup attempt, TIB’S power were transferred to the Technology and Communications Authority (*[Information and Communication Technologies Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_and_Communication_Technologies_Authority_\(Turkey\))*– BTK), which previously oversaw the TIB's operations.[^cite_note-266]

Internet Law No. 5651 prohibits:

- crimes against [Atatürk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atat%C3%BCrk) (Article 8/b),

- offering or promoting prostitution,

- providing place and opportunity for gambling,

- unauthorized online gambling and betting,

- sexual abuse of children,

- encouraging suicide,

- supplying drugs that are dangerous for health, and

- facilitation of the abuse of drugs.

Web sites are also blocked for the following reasons:

- downloading of MP3 and movies in violation of copyright laws,

- insults against state organisations and private persons

- crimes related to terrorism

- violation of trademark regulations

- unfair trade regulated under the Turkish Commercial Code

- violation of Articles 24, 25, 26, and 28 of the Constitution (freedoms of religion, expression, thought, and freedom of press).

Since the 2015 amendments, national security is also a basis for broad access bans.[^cite_note-267]

Decisions to block a website can be appealed, but usually only after a site has been blocked. Nevertheless, due to the public profile of the major websites banned and the lack of juridical, technical, or ethical arguments to justify the censorship, the blocked sites are often available using [proxies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proxy_server) or by changing [DNS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domain_Name_System) servers.

In September 2017, Turkey's Supreme Court ruled that having ByLock, a mobile messaging application, installed on one's phone is enough evidence to convict a suspect as a member of the [Gülen movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%BClen_movement).[^cite_note-268]

## Civil society initiatives

See also: [Access to public information in Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Access_to_public_information_in_Turkey)

- [Initiative for Freedom of Expression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initiative_for_Freedom_of_Expression?action=edit\&redlink=1) is an Istanbul-based association and movement of civil disobedience, working on the right to freedom of expression. It is a member of the global network [IFEX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IFEX_\(organization\)).

  [^cite_note-361]

  [^cite_note-362]

  &#x20;Since 2000, it publishes annual reports on the situation of freedom of expression in Turkey and distributes them among the main Non-Governmental Organizations, as well as to the media institutions. Every week, the Initiative publishes a Weekly Bulletin

  [^cite_note-363]

  &#x20;in Turkish and in English. Since 1997, it organizes biennial "[Gatherings for Freedom of Expression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gatherings_for_Freedom_of_Expression?action=edit\&redlink=1)" in Istanbul. Together with other stakeholders, it created the ÇTL database ([Current Trial Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_Trial_Library?action=edit\&redlink=1)),

  [^cite_note-364]

  &#x20;recording thought crime cases. It opened a virtual and interactive [Museum of the Crimes of Thought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_the_Crimes_of_Thought?action=edit\&redlink=1).

  [^cite_note-365]

- [Turkey Blocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey_Blocks) monitors access to social media services and online mass-communication networks around Turkey's main population areas. It provides real time reporting of online incidents that may impact the safety, access to information and online business operations.

  [^cite_note-366]

- [Turkey Uncensored](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey_Uncensored?action=edit\&redlink=1) is an [Index on Censorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_on_Censorship) project to publish articles from censored Turkish writers, artists and translators.

  [^cite_note-367]

  &#x20;Index on Censorship also curates the [Mapping Media Freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mapping_Media_Freedom?action=edit\&redlink=1) project - a database identifying threats, violations and limitations faced by members of the press throughout European Union member states, candidates for entry and neighbouring countries where threats on Turkish journalists and foreign journalists in Turkey are regularly monitored.

  [^cite_note-368]

- The [Platform for Independent Journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platform_for_Independent_Journalism?action=edit\&redlink=1) (P24) is a timely initiative to support and promote editorial independence in the Turkish press at a time when the journalistic profession is under fierce commercial and political pressure.

  [^cite_note-369]

- İfade Özgürlüğü Derneği (İFÖD - Freedom of Expression Association) published an annual report entitled [EngelliWeb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EngelliWeb?action=edit\&redlink=1),

  [^cite_note-370]

  &#x20;providing detailed information on Internet censorship and blocked websites from Turkey. The association also provides legal support to anyone facing criminal charges involving political speech.

- Siyah Bant (Black Tape) was established in 2011.

  [^cite_note-371]

  &#x20;The website contains an archive of cases of art censorship after the year 2000, and continues to produce research, documentation and discussions of cases of censorship in the arts in Turkey.

  [^cite_note-372]
