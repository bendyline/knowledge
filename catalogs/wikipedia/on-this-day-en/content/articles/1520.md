Location of Aachen in the Meuse (Dutch and German: *Maas*) river system ([Wurm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wurm_\(Rur\))→ [Rur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rur_\(river\))→ [Meuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meuse)→ [North Sea](21179.md))

**Aachen**[^cite_note-5] is the [13th-largest city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cities_in_North_Rhine-Westphalia_by_population) in [North Rhine-Westphalia](38414.md) and the [27th-largest city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cities_in_Germany_by_population) of Germany, with around 264,000 inhabitants.[^cite_note-pop2025-6] It is the western-most major city in Germany, lying approximately 61 km (38 mi) west of [Cologne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cologne) and [Bonn](3295.md), directly bordering [Belgium](3343.md) in the southwest, and the [Netherlands](21148.md) in the northwest. The city lies in the [Meuse–Rhine Euroregion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meuse%E2%80%93Rhine_Euroregion) and is the seat of the [district of Aachen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen_\(district\)) *(Städteregion Aachen)*.

The once [Celtic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celts) settlement was equipped with several *[thermae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermae)* in the course of colonization by [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_people) pioneers settling at the warm [Aachen thermal springs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen_thermal_springs) around the 1st century. After the withdrawal of the Roman troops, the [vicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vicus) *Aquae Granni* was [Frankized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franks) around the 5th century, by which it came under [Merovingian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merovingian) and later [Carolingian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carolingian) rule. With the completion of the Carolingian [Palace of Aachen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_Aachen) at the transition to the 9th century, Aachen was constituted as the main royal residence of the [Frankish Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankish_Empire) ruled by [Charlemagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlemagne). Because of that, the city is sometimes called the "cradle of Europe".[^cite_note-7] After the [Treaty of Verdun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Verdun), the city was within the borders of [Middle Francia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Francia), until it became part of [East Francia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Francia) after the [Treaty of Meerssen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Meerssen) (870). It subsequently was part of the [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire) and was granted city rights in 1166 by Emperor [Frederick Barbarossa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Barbarossa), becoming an [imperial city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_city). It served as the coronation site where 31 Holy Roman Emperors were crowned [Kings of the Germans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_German_monarchs) from 936 to 1531, until [Frankfurt](10992.md) became the preferred place of coronation.

One of Germany's leading institutes of higher education in technology, the [RWTH Aachen University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RWTH_Aachen_University) *(Rheinisch-Westfälisch Technische Hochschule Aachen)*, is located in the city. Its university hospital [Uniklinikum Aachen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniklinikum_Aachen) is Europe's largest single-building hospital. Aachen's industries include science, engineering and information technology. In 2009, Aachen was ranked eighth among cities in Germany for innovation.

Aachen is located at the northern foothills of the [High Fens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Fens) and the [Eifel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eifel) Mountains. It sits on the [Wurm River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wurm_\(Rur\)), a tributary of the [Rur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rur_\(river\)), and together with [Mönchengladbach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%B6nchengladbach), it is the only larger German city in the [drainage basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage_basin) of the [Meuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meuse). The [regional dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen_dialect) spoken in the city is a [Central Franconian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Franconian), [Ripuarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ripuarian_language) variant with strong [Limburgish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limburgish) influences from the dialects in the neighbouring Netherlands. As a [Rhenish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhenish) city, Aachen is one of the main centres of [carnival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnival) celebrations [in Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnival_in_Germany), along with [Cologne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cologne_Carnival) and [Mainz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainz_carnival). The [culinary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culinary) specialty for which the city is best known is [Aachener Printen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachener_Printen), a type of [gingerbread](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gingerbread).

## Etymology

The name *Aachen* is a modern descendant, like southern German *Ach(e)*, German: *Aach*, meaning "river" or "stream", from [Old High German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_High_German) *ahha*, meaning "water" or "stream", which directly translates (and etymologically corresponds) to [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) *Aquae*, referring to the springs. The location has been inhabited by humans since the [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic) era, about 5,000 years ago, attracted to its warm [mineral springs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineral_spring). Latin *Aquae* figures in Aachen's [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome) name *Aquae granni*, which meant "waters of [Grannus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grannus)", referring to the [Celtic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celts) god of healing who was worshipped at the springs.[^cite_note-Munro_1995-8][^cite_note-tourism-9] This word became *Åxhe* in [Walloon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walloon_language) and *Aix* in French, and subsequently *Aix-la-Chapelle* to distinguish it from [Aix-en-Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aix-en-Provence), after [Charlemagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlemagne) had his [palatine chapel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palatine_Chapel,_Aachen) built there in the late 8th century and then made the city his empire's capital.

## History

Further information: [Timeline of Aachen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_Aachen)

See also: [Free Imperial City of Aachen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Imperial_City_of_Aachen)

### Early history

[Flint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flint) quarries on the [Lousberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lousberg), Schneeberg, and Königshügel, first used during [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic) times (3000–2500 BC), attest to the long occupation of the site of Aachen, as do recent finds under the modern city's *Elisengarten* pointing to a former settlement from the same period. [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age) (around 1600 BC) settlement is evidenced by the remains of barrows (burial mounds) found, for example, on the Klausberg. During the [Iron Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age), the area was settled by Celtic peoples[^cite_note-15] who were perhaps drawn by the marshy Aachen basin's [hot sulphur springs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hot_spring) where they worshipped [Grannus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grannus), god of light and healing. Proofs of a [Celts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celts) settlements on Aachens territories at the beginning of the [Gallic War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallic_Wars) do not exist at the moment.[^cite_note-16]

The 25-hectare Roman spa resort town of Aquae Granni was, according to legend, founded by Grenus, under [Hadrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadrian), around 124 AD.[^cite_note-17] Grenus refers to the Celtic god, and it seems it was the Roman 6th Legion at the start of the 1st century AD that first channelled the hot springs into a spa at Büchel,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBridgwaterAldrich1968-18][^cite_note-19] adding at the end of the same century the *Münstertherme* spa,[^cite_note-Anon_2013-20] two water pipelines, and a probable sanctuary dedicated to Grannus. A kind of forum, surrounded by colonnades, connected the two spa complexes.[^cite_note-21] There was an extensive residential area. The Romans built bathhouses near [Burtscheid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burtscheid). A temple precinct called *Vernenum* was built near the modern [Kornelimünster/Walheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kornelim%C3%BCnster/Walheim). Today, remains have been found of three bathhouses,[^cite_note-McClendon_1996-22] including two fountains in the *Elisenbrunnen* and the Burtscheid bathhouse.

Roman civil administration in Aachen eventually broke down as the baths and other public buildings (along with most of the [villae rusticae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villae_rusticae) of the surrounding countryside) were destroyed around AD 375 at the start of the [migration period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Migration_period). The last Roman coin finds are from the time of Emperor [Gratian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gratian) (AD 375–383).[^cite_note-23] Rome withdrew its troops from the area, but the town remained populated. By 470, the town came to be ruled by the [Ripuarian Franks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ripuarian_Franks)[^cite_note-Coll-24] and subordinated to their capital, [Cologne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cologne). During the Roman period, Aachen was the site of a flourishing Jewish community.[^cite_note-jewishencyclopedia.com-25]

### Middle Ages

[Pepin the Short](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pepin_the_Short) had a castle residence built in the town, due to the proximity of the hot springs and also for strategic reasons as it is located between the [Rhineland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhineland) and northern France.[^cite_note-McClendon_1996a-26] [Einhard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einhard) mentions that in 765–766 Pepin spent both Christmas and Easter at *Aquis villa* *(*Et celebravit natalem Domini in Aquis villa et pascha similiter*)*[^cite_note-27] ("and \[he] celebrated the birth of the Lord \[Christmas] in the town Aquis, and similarly Easter"), which must have been sufficiently equipped to support the royal household for several months. In the year of his coronation as king of the [Franks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francia), 768, [Charlemagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlemagne) came to spend Christmas at Aachen for the first time.[^cite_note-29] He remained there in a mansion which he may have extended, although there is no source attesting to any significant building activity at Aachen in his time, apart from the building of the [Palatine Chapel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palatine_Chapel_in_Aachen) (since 1930, cathedral) and the [Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_Aachen).

Charlemagne spent most winters in Aachen between 792 and his death in 814. Aachen became the focus of his court and the political centre of his empire. During the [Carolingian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carolingian) empire, a Jewish community lived near the royal palace. In Jewish texts, the city of Aachen was called *Aish* or *Ash* (אש). In 797, Isaac, a Jewish merchant, accompanied two ambassadors of [Charlemagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlemagne) to the court of [Harun al-Rashid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harun_al-Rashid). He returned to Aachen in July 802, bearing an elephant called [Abul-Abbas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abul-Abbas) as a gift for the emperor.[^cite_note-30] After Charlemagne's death, he was buried in the church which he had built;[^cite_note-McClendon_1996a-4-31] his original tomb has been lost, while his alleged remains are preserved in the *[Karlsschrein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karlsschrein)*, the shrine where he was reburied after being declared a saint; his saintliness, however, was never officially acknowledged by the Roman Curia as such.&#x20;

By the terms of the [Treaty of Verdun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Verdun) 834, Aachen became part of [Middle Francia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Francia), governed by emperor [Lothair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lothair_I). He was succeeded by [Lothair II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lothair_II) as king of [Lotharingia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lotharingia) by the terms of the [Treaty of Prüm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Pr%C3%BCm). By the [Treaty of Meerssen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Meerssen) 870, Aachen came to [East Francia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Francia). Within East Francia, later the [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire) until 1166, Aachen was part of the Duchy of [Lower Lotharingia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Lotharingia).

In 936, [Otto I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_I,_Holy_Roman_Emperor) was crowned king of [East Francia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Francia) in the collegiate church built by Charlemagne. During the reign of [Otto II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_II), the nobles revolted and the [West Franks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Francia) under [Lothair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lothair_of_France)[^cite_note-dupuy-32] [raided Aachen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franco-German_war_of_978%E2%80%93980) in 978.[^cite_note-33] Aachen was attacked again by [Odo of Champagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odo_II,_Count_of_Blois), who attacked the [imperial palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_Aachen) while [Conrad II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conrad_II,_Holy_Roman_Emperor) was absent. Odo relinquished it and was [killed afterwards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Bar-le-Duc).[^cite_note-34] The palace and town of Aachen had fortifying walls built by order of Emperor [Frederick Barbarossa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_I,_Holy_Roman_Emperor) between 1172 and 1176.[^cite_note-McClendon_1996-22] Over the next 500 years, most kings of [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Germany) who ruled the [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire) were crowned in Aachen. The original audience hall built by Charlemagne was torn down and replaced by the current [city hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen_Rathaus) in 1330.[^cite_note-35][^cite_note-McClendon_1996-22] During the 13th century, many Jews converted to Christianity, as shown in the records of the [Aachen Minster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen_Cathedral) (today's Cathedral). In 1486, the Jews of Aachen offered gifts to [Maximilian I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximilian_I,_Holy_Roman_Emperor) during his coronation ceremony. The last king to be crowned here was [Ferdinand I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_I,_Holy_Roman_Emperor) in 1531.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBridgwaterAldrich1968-18][^cite_note-Ranson_1998-36]

During the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), Aachen remained a city of regional importance, due to its proximity to [Flanders](10878.md); it achieved a modest position in the trade in [woollen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wool) cloths, favoured by imperial privilege. The city remained a [free imperial city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_imperial_city), subject to the emperor only, but was politically far too weak to influence the policies of any of its neighbours. The only dominion it had was over [Burtscheid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burtscheid), a neighbouring territory ruled by a [Benedictine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedictine) [abbess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbess), which was forced to accept that all of its traffic must pass through the "Aachener Reich".

As an imperial city, Aachen held certain political privileges that allowed it to remain independent of the troubles of Europe for many years. It remained a direct vassal of the Holy Roman Empire throughout most of the Middle Ages. It was also the site of many important church councils, including the [Council of 837](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_837?action=edit\&redlink=1)[^cite_note-37] and the [Council of 1166](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_1166?action=edit\&redlink=1), a council convened by the [antipope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antipope) [Paschal III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paschal_III).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBayer2000?-38]

Parts of Aachen belonged to [Imperial Abbey of Burtscheid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burtscheid_Abbey),[^cite_note-39] [Reichsabtei Kornelimünster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kornelim%C3%BCnster_Abbey),[^cite_note-40] [Duchy of Juelich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_J%C3%BClich) and the [Duchy of Limburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Limburg).

#### Manuscript production

Aachen was an important site for the production of historical manuscripts. Under Charlemagne's purview, both the [Ada Gospels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen_Gospels_\(Ada_School\)) and the [Coronation Gospels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronation_Gospel) may have been produced in Aachen.[^cite_note-McKitterick_1996-41] In addition, quantities of the other texts in the court library were also produced locally. During the reign of [Louis the Pious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_the_Pious) (814–840), substantial quantities of ancient texts were produced at Aachen, including legal manuscripts such as the leges scriptorium group, [patristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patristic) texts including the five manuscripts of the [Bamberg Pliny Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bamberg_Pliny_Group?action=edit\&redlink=1).[^cite_note-McKitterick_1996-41] Finally, under [Lothair I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lothair_I) (840–855), texts of outstanding quality were still being produced. This however marked the end of the period of manuscript production at Aachen.[^cite_note-McKitterick_1996-41]

### 16th–18th centuries



The [siege of Aachen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Aachen_\(1614\)) by the Spanish Army of Flanders under Ambrogio Spinola in 1614



View of Aachen in 1690

In 1598, following the invasion of [Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Empire) troops from the [Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Netherlands), [Rudolf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_II,_Holy_Roman_Emperor) deposed all [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant) office holders in Aachen and went as far as expelling them from the city.[^cite_note-42] From the early 16th century, Aachen started to lose its power and influence. First the [coronations of emperors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronation_of_the_Holy_Roman_Emperor) were moved from Aachen to [Frankfurt](10992.md). 1614 Aachen became theatre of the [Siege of Aachen (1614)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Aachen_\(1614\)) within the [War of the Jülich Succession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_J%C3%BClich_Succession). Although religious changes were forced several times in Aachen and [Protestant communities were banned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counter-Reformation), hardly any fighting took place during the [Thirty Years' War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirty_Years'_War).[^cite_note-43]

The [religious wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_wars_of_religion) had been followed by the great fire of 1656.[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-eb-45] After the destruction of most of the city in 1656, the rebuilding was mostly in the [Baroque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baroque_architecture) style.[^cite_note-McClendon_1996-22] The decline of Aachen culminated in 1794, when the French, led by General [Charles Dumouriez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Dumouriez),[^cite_note-Coll-24] [occupied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_First_Coalition) Aachen.[^cite_note-Ranson_1998-36] The area of Aachen had been scene of battles within the Flanders Campaign in autumn 1794: [Battle of Ourthe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Sprimont), [Battle of Aldenhoven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Aldenhoven_\(1794\)), [Siege of Maastricht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Maastricht_\(1794\)).

In 1542, the Dutch [humanist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_humanism) and physician [Francis Fabricius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Fabricius) published his study of the health benefits of the hot springs in Aachen.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEJourdan182192-46] By the middle of the 17th century, the city had developed a considerable reputation as a spa, although this was in part because Aachen was then – and remained well into the 19th and early 20th century – a place of high-level prostitution.[^cite_note-47] Traces of this hidden agenda of the city's history are found in the 18th-century guidebooks to Aachen as well as to the other spas.

The main [indication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indication_\(medicine\)) for visiting patients, ironically, was [syphilis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syphilis); only by the end of the 19th century had [rheumatism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rheumatism) become the most important object of cures at Aachen and Burtscheid.

Aachen was chosen as the site of several important congresses and peace treaties: the [first congress of Aachen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_Aix-la-Chapelle_\(1668\)) (often referred to as the Congress of Aix-la-Chapelle in English) on 2 May 1668,[^cite_note-dupuy1-48] leading to the [First Treaty of Aachen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Aix-la-Chapelle_\(1668\)) in the same year which ended the [War of Devolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_Devolution).[^cite_note-49] The [second congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_Aix-la-Chapelle_\(1748\)) ended with the [second treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Aix-la-Chapelle_\(1748\)) in 1748, ending the [War of the Austrian Succession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_Austrian_Succession).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBridgwaterAldrich1968-18][^cite_note-50] In 1789, there was a constitutional crisis in the Aachen government,[^cite_note-51] and in 1794 Aachen lost its status as a [free imperial city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_imperial_city).[^cite_note-McClendon_1996-22]

In 1629, the Aachen Jewish community was expelled from the city. In 1667, six Jews were allowed to return. Most of the Aachen Jewish community settled in Burtscheid. As recently as the late 18th century the Abbess of Burtscheid was still prevented from building a road linking her territory to the neighbouring estates of the [duke of Jülich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_J%C3%BClich); the city of Aachen deployed its handful of soldiers to chase away road-diggers.

### 19th century



The modern [Elisabethhalle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elisabethhalle) pool

On 9 February 1801, the [Peace of Lunéville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_of_Lun%C3%A9ville) removed the ownership of Aachen and the entire "left bank" of the Rhine from Germany (the [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire)) and granted it to France.[^cite_note-Coll-24] In 1815, control of the town was passed to the [Kingdom of Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Prussia) through an agreement reached by the [Congress of Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_Vienna).[^cite_note-McClendon_1996-22][^cite_note-Ranson_1998-36] The [third congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_Aix-la-Chapelle_\(1818\)) took place in 1818, to decide the fate of occupied [Napoleonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon_I_of_France) France.

By the middle of the 19th century, industrialisation had swept away most of the city's medieval rules of production and commerce, although the remains of the city's medieval constitution were kept in place until 1801, when Aachen became the "[chef-lieu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefectures_in_France) du [département de la Roer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roer_\(department\))" in Napoleon's [First French Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_French_Empire). The current territory of Aachen belonged entirely to the Arrondissement d'Aix-la-Chapelle.[^cite_note-52] In 1815, after the [Second Treaty of Paris, finishing the Napoleonic Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Paris_\(1815\)), the [Kingdom of Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Prussia) took over within the new [German Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Confederation). The city was one of its most socially and politically backward centres until the end of the 19th century.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBridgwaterAldrich1968-18] The [Kingdom of Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Prussia) created the "Regierungsbezirk Aachen"[^cite_note-g045-53] (governmental district) and integrated it into the Prussian province [Grand Duchy of the Lower Rhine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Duchy_of_the_Lower_Rhine), from 1822 the [Rhine Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhine_Province). By 1880 the population was 80,000. Starting in 1838, the railway from [Cologne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cologne) to [Belgium](3343.md) passed through Aachen.[^cite_note-54] By "Gesetz betreffend die Vereinigung der Stadtgemeinde Burtscheid mit der Stadtgemeinde Aachen vom 29. März 1897 nebst Vertrag" Aachen and [Burtscheid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burtscheid) merged 1897.[^cite_note-55] The city suffered extreme overcrowding and deplorable sanitary conditions until 1875, when the medieval fortifications were abandoned as a limit to building and new, better housing was built in the east of the city, where sanitary drainage was easiest. In December 1880, the [Aachen tramway network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Aachen) was opened, and in 1895 it was electrified.[^cite_note-56] In the 19th century and up to the 1930s, the city was important in the production of railway locomotives and carriages, iron, pins, [needles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sewing_needle), buttons, tobacco, woollen goods, and silk goods.

### 20th century

#### Prewar Period

Aachen was enlarged 1 April 1906 by "Gesetz betreffend die Vereinigung der Gemeinde Forst mit der Stadtgemeinde Aachen vom 31. März 1906 nebst Vertrag"[^cite_note-57] by incorporation of Forst.

#### World War I, Interwar Period, World War II

Further information: [Battle of Aachen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Aachen)

Films shot on 13, 14 and 15 October 1944 in Aachen by US forces

The [Belgium Campaign within World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_invasion_of_Belgium_\(1914\)) started in Aachen.[^cite_note-58]

After [World War I](4764461.md), Aachen was occupied by the Entente until 1930, along with the rest of German territory west of the Rhine.[^cite_note-Ranson_1998-36] Aachen was one of the locations involved in the [Rhenish Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhenish_Republic). On 21 October 1923, an armed mob took over the city hall. Similar actions took place in [Mönchengladbach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%B6nchengladbach), [Duisburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duisburg), and [Krefeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krefeld). This republic lasted about a year.[^cite_note-59]

During the interwar period, Aachen was assigned some areas of the former [district of Eupen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eupen).

Also the [Western Campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_France) of [World War II](32927.md) started nearby Aachen with the [Battle of Maastricht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Maastricht).

Aachen was heavily damaged during [World War II](32927.md). Philip W. Blood[^cite_note-60] mentioned more than 120 air strikes on Aachen between 1940 an 1944. According to [Jörg Friedrich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%B6rg_Friedrich_\(author\)) in *[The Fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Fire:_The_Bombing_of_Germany,_1940%E2%80%931945)* (2002), two Allied air raids on 11 April and 24 May 1944 "radically destroyed" the city. The first killed 1,525, including 212 children, and bombed six hospitals. During the second, 442 aircraft hit two railway stations, killed 207, and left 15,000 homeless. The raids destroyed [Aachen-Eilendorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eilendorf_\(Aachen\)) and [Aachen-Burtscheid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burtscheid).[^cite_note-61]

In autumn 1944 Aachen and its surroundings were reached by the [Western Allied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Allies) [Siegfried Line campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siegfried_Line_campaign).[^cite_note-62]

The city and its fortified surroundings were besieged from 12 September to 21 October 1944 by the US 1st Infantry Division[^cite_note-Stanton_2006-63] with the 3rd Armored Division assisting from the south.[^cite_note-64] Around 13 October the US 2nd Armored Division, coming from the north, and got as close as [Würselen](2031511.md),[^cite_note-65] while the 30th Infantry Division completed the encirclement of Aachen on 16 October 1944.[^cite_note-66] With reinforcements from the US 28th Infantry Division[^cite_note-67] the battle continued involving direct assaults through the heavily defended city, which forced the German garrison to surrender on 21 October 1944.[^cite_note-Stanton_2006-63]

Aachen was the first German city to be captured by the Western Allies, and its residents welcomed the soldiers as liberators.[^cite_note-baker2004-68] What remained of the city was destroyed—in some areas completely—during the fighting,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBridgwaterAldrich1968-18] mostly by American [artillery](2508.md) fire and demolitions carried out by the [Waffen-SS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waffen-SS) defenders. Damaged buildings included medieval churches and the [Rathaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen_Rathaus) (city hall), although [Aachen Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen_Cathedral) was largely unscathed. 4,000 inhabitants remained in the city; the rest had followed evacuation orders. Its first Allied-appointed mayor, [Franz Oppenhoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Oppenhoff), was assassinated by an SS commando unit.[^cite_note-Rempel-1989-69]

#### Expulsion of Aachen Jews



View of the [Old Synagogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Synagogue_\(Aachen\)) after its destruction on [Kristallnacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kristallnacht), November 1938

On 16 May 1815, the [Jewish community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_community) of the city offered an homage in its synagogue to the Prussian king, [Friedrich Wilhelm III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_William_III_of_Prussia).[^cite_note-70] In 1862, a large synagogue was built, later called the [Old Synagogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Synagogue_\(Aachen\)). By 1933, 1,345 Jews lived in the city. On *[Kristallnacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kristallnacht)* in 1938, the synagogue was destroyed.[^cite_note-71] By the onset of [World War II](32927.md) in 1939, many Jews had emigrated or were arrested, and only 782 remained in the city. At the end of the war in 1945, only 62 Jews lived in the city. As of 2003, 1,434 Jews were again living in Aachen.

#### Postwar period

Subsequent the German surrender finishing the [Battle of Aachen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Aachen) 21 October 1944, the [Supreme Headquarters Allied Expeditionary Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Headquarters_Allied_Expeditionary_Force) acted as military government.[^cite_note-72] Aachen within the northern part of [Rhine Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhine_Province) became part of [British occupation zone in Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_occupation_zone_in_Germany). Aachen also belonged to the [British zone under Belgian control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgian_Forces_in_Germany). By Ordinance No. 46,[^cite_note-73] British Military Government created the "Land [Nordrhein/Westfalen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nordrhein-Westfalen)", including the "Regierungsbezirk Aachen", 23 August 1946. 23 May 1949 Aachen became part of the [Federal Republic of Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Germany). With the Aachen Law of 1971, Aachen was merged with [Kornelimünster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kornelim%C3%BCnster), [Brand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brand_\(Aachen\)), Walheim, [Laurensberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurensberg), [Richterich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richterich), [Haaren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haaren_\(Aachen\)), [Eilendorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eilendorf_\(Aachen\)).[^cite_note-74] After the abolition of the Aachen governmental district, August 1972, Aachen became part of the [Cologne governmental district](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cologne_\(region\)).[^cite_note-75]

### 21st century

The city of Aachen has developed into a technology hub as a by-product of hosting one of the leading [universities of technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_Technology) in Germany with the [RWTH Aachen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RWTH_Aachen) (Rheinisch-Westfälische Technische Hochschule), known especially for mechanical engineering, automotive and manufacturing technology as well as for its research and academic hospital [Klinikum Aachen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klinikum_Aachen), one of the largest medical facilities in Europe.[^cite_note-76]

## Geography



View towards Aachen at the foothills of the [High Fens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Fens), with the [university hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniklinikum_Aachen) visible, from the [Vaalserberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaalserberg), the highest elevation in Aachen and of the European part of the Netherlands.



Physiogeographical location of Aachen

Aachen is located in the middle of the [Meuse–Rhine Euroregion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meuse%E2%80%93Rhine_Euroregion), close to [the border tripoint of Germany, the Netherlands, and Belgium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaalserberg#Three-Country_Point). The town of [Vaals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaals) in the Netherlands lies nearby at about 6 km (4 mi) from Aachen's city centre, while the Dutch city of [Heerlen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heerlen) and [Eupen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eupen), the capital of the [German-speaking Community of Belgium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German-speaking_Community_of_Belgium), are both located about 20 km (12 mi) from Aachen city centre. Aachen lies near the head of the open valley of the [Wurm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wurm_\(Rur\)) (which today flows through the city in canalised form), part of the larger basin of the [Meuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meuse), and about 30 km (19 mi) north of the [High Fens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Fens), which form the northern edge of the [Eifel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eifel) uplands of the [Rhenish Massif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhenish_Massif).

The maximum dimensions of the city's territory are 21.6 km (13+3⁄8 mi) from north to south, and 17.2 km (10+3⁄4 mi) from east to west. The city limits are 87.7 km (54+1⁄2 mi) long, of which 23.8 km (14+3⁄4 mi) border Belgium and 21.8 km (13+1⁄2 mi) the Netherlands. The highest point in Aachen, located in the far southeast of the city, lies at an elevation of 410 m (1,350 ft) above sea level. The lowest point, in the north, and on the border with the Netherlands, is at 125 m (410 ft).

### Climate

As the westernmost city in Germany[^cite_note-Munro_1995-8] (and close to the Low Countries), Aachen and the surrounding area belongs to a [temperate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperate_climate) [climate zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanic_climate) ([Cfb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification)), with humid weather, mild winters, and warm summers. Because of its location north of the [Eifel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eifel) and the [High Fens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Fens) and its subsequent prevailing westerly weather patterns, rainfall in Aachen (on average 805 mm/year) is comparatively higher than, for example, in [Bonn](3295.md) (with 669 mm/year). Another factor in the local weather forces of Aachen is the occurrence of [Foehn winds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foehn_winds) on the southerly air currents, which results from the city's geographic location on the northern edge of the Eifel.

Because the city is surrounded by hills, it suffers from [inversion-related](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inversion_\(meteorology\)) smog. Some areas of the city have become [urban heat islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_heat_island) as a result of poor heat exchange, both because of the area's natural geography and from human activity. The city's numerous cold air corridors, which are slated to remain as free as possible from new construction, therefore play an important role in the urban climate of Aachen.[^cite_note-77]

The January average is 3.0 °C (37 °F), while the July average is 18.5 °C (65 °F). Precipitation is almost evenly spread throughout the year.

The city's oceanic climate provides comparably mild winters: While Aachen falls within the coldest extents covered by [USDA plant hardiness zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardiness_zone) 8b in the 1991–2020 period, having an average yearly minimum of −9.22 °C (15.4 °F), the Canadian city of [Regina, Saskatchewan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regina,_Saskatchewan) which is located at a similar latitude but at the heart of the North American landmass, far away from the sea's moderating effects, is classified as being in zone 3a.[^cite_note-78]

In the 1991–2020 period, the record latest freeze (at 2 m above ground) of spring occurred on 28 April and the record first fall freeze on 13 October.[^cite_note-79] The average last freeze date for the 1991–2020 period is 6 April, though it varies significantly from year to year and by location[^cite_note-80] (note that the climate station was relocated in 2011).

- Highest Temperature 38.6 °C (101.5 °F) on [25 July 2019](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_European_heatwaves#Germany).

- Warmest Minimum 24.5 °C (76.1 °F) on 29 July 1947.

- Coldest Maximum −12.8 °C (9.0 °F) on 22 January 1940.

- Lowest Temperature −20.4 °C (−4.7 °F) on 11 January 1945.

- Highest Daily Precipitation 98.7 mm (3.89 in) on 14 July 2021.

## Geology



Layered [sandstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandstone) and [claystone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claystone) [formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formation_\(stratigraphy\)) from the [Devonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devonian) period below St. Adalbert Church in Aachen

The geology of Aachen is very structurally heterogeneous. The oldest occurring rocks in the area surrounding the city originate from the [Devonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devonian) period and include [carboniferous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carboniferous) [sandstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandstone), [greywacke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greywacke), [claystone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claystone) and [limestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limestone). These formations are part of the [Rhenish Massif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhenish_Massif), north of the High Fens. In the [Pennsylvanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvanian_\(geology\)) subperiod of the [Carboniferous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carboniferous) geological period, these rock layers were narrowed and folded as a result of the [Variscan orogeny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variscan_orogeny). After this event, and over the course of the following 200 million years, this area has been continuously flattened.[^cite_note-87]

During the [Cretaceous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cretaceous) period, the ocean penetrated the continent from the direction of the [North Sea](21179.md) up to the mountainous area near Aachen, bringing with it clay, sand, and chalk deposits. While the clay (which was the basis for a major [pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pottery) industry in nearby [Raeren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raeren)) is mostly found in the lower areas of Aachen, the hills of the [Aachen Forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen_Forest) and the [Lousberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lousberg) were formed from upper Cretaceous sand and chalk deposits. More recent sedimentation is mainly located in the north and east of Aachen and was formed through [Tertiary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertiary_\(period\)) and [Quaternary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternary) river and wind activities.

Along the major [thrust fault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrust_fault) of the [Variscan orogeny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variscan_orogeny), there are over 30 [thermal springs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_spring) in Aachen and [Burtscheid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burtscheid). Additionally, the subsurface of Aachen is traversed by numerous [active faults](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_fault) that belong to the Rurgraben fault system, which has been responsible for numerous earthquakes in the past, including the 1756 [Düren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%BCren) earthquake[^cite_note-duerenEQ-88] and the [1992 Roermond earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1992_Roermond_earthquake),[^cite_note-Roermond-89] which was the strongest earthquake ever recorded in the [Netherlands](21148.md).

## Main sights

### Cathedral

Main article: [Aachen Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen_Cathedral)



Aachen Cathedral

Aachen Cathedral was erected on the orders of [Charlemagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlemagne). Construction began *c.* AD 796,[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBayer2000?-38] and it was, on completion *c.* 798,[^cite_note-McClendon_1996a-2-95] the largest cathedral north of the [Alps](981.md). It was modelled after the [Basilica of San Vitale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_of_San_Vitale), in [Ravenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ravenna), Italy,[^cite_note-Ranson_1998-36] and was built by [Odo of Metz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odo_of_Metz).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEBayer2000?-38] Charlemagne also desired for the chapel to compete with the [Lateran Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lateran_Palace), both in quality and authority.[^cite_note-Gaehde_1996-96] It was originally built in the [Carolingian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carolingian_architecture) style, including marble covered walls, and mosaic inlay on the dome.[^cite_note-McClendon_1996a-3-97] On his death, Charlemagne's remains were interred in the cathedral and can be seen there to this day. The cathedral was extended several times in later ages, turning it into a curious and unique mixture of building styles. The throne and gallery portion date from the [Ottonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottonian_dynasty), with portions of the original [opus sectile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opus_sectile) floor still visible.[^cite_note-McClendon_1996a-3-97] The 13th century saw gables being added to the roof, and after the fire of 1656, the dome was rebuilt. Finally, a [choir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choir_\(architecture\)) was added around the start of the 15th century.[^cite_note-McClendon_1996a-4-31]

After [Frederick Barbarossa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Barbarossa) [canonised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonisation) Charlemagne in 1165 the chapel became a [destination for pilgrims](23763.md).[^cite_note-McClendon_1996a-4-31] For 600 years, from 936 to 1531, Aachen Cathedral was the church of coronation for 30 German kings and 12 queens. The church built by Charlemagne is still the main attraction of the city.[^cite_note-98] In addition to holding the remains of its founder, it became the burial place of his successor [Otto III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_III,_Holy_Roman_Emperor). In the upper chamber of the gallery, Charlemagne's marble throne is housed.[^cite_note-Young-99] Aachen Cathedral has been designated as a [UNESCO World Heritage Site](44940.md).[^cite_note-100]

Most of the marble and columns used in the construction of the cathedral were brought from Rome and [Ravenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ravenna), including the [sarcophagus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarcophagus) in which Charlemagne was eventually laid to rest.[^cite_note-Gaehde_1996-96] A bronze bear from [Gaul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaul) was placed inside, along with an equestrian statue from Ravenna, believed to be [Theodric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodric), in contrast to a wolf and a statue of [Marcus Aurelius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Aurelius) in the [Capitoline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitoline).[^cite_note-Gaehde_1996-96] Bronze pieces such as the doors and railings, some of which have survived to present day, were cast in a local foundry. Finally, there is uncertainty surrounding the bronze pine cone in the chapel, and where it was created. Wherever it was made, it was also a parallel to a piece in Rome, this in [Old St. Peter's Basilica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_St._Peter's_Basilica).[^cite_note-Gaehde_1996-96]

### Cathedral Treasury

Main article: [Aachen Cathedral Treasury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen_Cathedral_Treasury)



[Cross of Lothair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_of_Lothair), Aachen Cathedral Treasury

Aachen Cathedral Treasury has housed, throughout its history, a collection of liturgical objects. The origin of this [church treasure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_treasure) is in dispute as some say Charlemagne himself endowed his chapel with the original collection, while the rest were collected over time. Others say all of the objects were collected over time, from such places as [Jerusalem](16043.md) and [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople).[^cite_note-Gaehde_1996-96] The location of this treasury has moved over time and was unknown until the 15th century when it was located in the Matthiaskapelle (St. Matthew's Chapel) until 1873, when it was moved to the Karlskapelle (Charles' Chapel). From there it was moved to the Hungarian Chapel in 1881 and in 1931 to its present location next to the Allerseelenkapelle (Poor Souls' Chapel).[^cite_note-Gaehde_1996-96] Only six of the original [Carolingian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carolingian_Empire) objects have remained, and of those only three are left in Aachen: the [Aachen Gospels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen_Gospels_\(Ada_School\)), a [diptych](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diptych) of Christ, and an early [Byzantine silk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_silk). The [Coronation Gospels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronation_Gospels) and a [reliquary burse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Stephen's_Purse) of [St. Stephen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Stephen) were moved to [Vienna](55866.md) in 1798 and the [Talisman of Charlemagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talisman_of_Charlemagne) was given as a gift in 1804 to [Josephine Bonaparte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephine_Bonaparte) and subsequently to [Rheims Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rheims_Cathedral).[^cite_note-Gaehde_1996-96] 210 documented pieces have been added to the treasury since its inception, typically to receive in return legitimisation of linkage to the heritage of Charlemagne. The [Lothar Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lothar_Cross), the [Gospels of Otto III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospels_of_Otto_III) and multiple additional Byzantine silks were donated by [Otto III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_III). Part of the [Pala d'Oro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen_Cathedral#Pala_d'Oro) and a covering for the [Aachen Gospels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen_Gospels_\(Ada_School\)) were made of gold donated by [Henry II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_II,_Holy_Roman_Emperor).[^cite_note-Gaehde_1996-96] [Frederick Barbarossa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_I,_Holy_Roman_Emperor) donated the candelabrum that adorns the dome and also once "crowned" the [Shrine of Charlemagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karlsschrein), which was placed underneath in 1215. [Charles IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_IV,_Holy_Roman_Emperor) donated a pair of reliquaries. [Louis XI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_XI) gave, in 1475, the crown of [Margaret of York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_of_York), and, in 1481, another arm reliquary of Charlemagne. [Maximilian I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximilian_I,_Holy_Roman_Emperor) and [Charles V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_V,_Holy_Roman_Emperor) both gave numerous works of art by [Hans von Reutlingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_von_Reutlingen).[^cite_note-Gaehde_1996-96] Continuing the tradition, objects continued to be donated until the present, each indicative of the period of its gifting, with the last documented gift being a chalice from 1960 made by [Ewald Mataré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ewald_Matar%C3%A9).[^cite_note-Gaehde_1996-96]

### Rathaus

Main article: [Aachen Rathaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen_Rathaus)



Aachen Rathaus seen from the south

The Aachen Rathaus, (English: Aachen City Hall or Aachen Town Hall) dated from 1330,[^cite_note-Merkl_2007-28] lies between two central squares, the *Markt* (marketplace) and the *Katschhof* (between city hall and cathedral). The coronation hall is on the first floor of the building. Inside one can find five frescoes by the Aachen artist [Alfred Rethel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Rethel) which show legendary scenes from the life of Charlemagne, as well as Charlemagne's signature. Also, precious replicas of the [Imperial Regalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Regalia_of_the_Holy_Roman_Empire) are kept here.[^cite_note-Young-99]

Since 2009, the city hall has been a station on the *Route Charlemagne*, a tour programme by which historical sights of Aachen are presented to visitors. At the city hall, a museum exhibition explains the history and art of the building and gives a sense of the historical coronation banquets that took place there. A portrait of [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon) from 1807 by [Louis-André-Gabriel Bouchet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis-Andr%C3%A9-Gabriel_Bouchet) and one of his wife [Joséphine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9phine_de_Beauharnais) from 1805 by [Robert Lefèvre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Lef%C3%A8vre) are viewable as part of the tour.

As before, the city hall is the seat of the mayor of Aachen and of the city council, and annually the [Charlemagne Prize](344825.md) is awarded there.[^cite_note-101]

### Other sights

The *Grashaus*, a late medieval house at the *Fischmarkt*, is one of the oldest non-religious buildings in central Aachen. It hosted the city archive, and before that, the Grashaus was the city hall until the present building took over this function.

The *Elisenbrunnen* is one of the most famous sights of Aachen. It is a neo-classical hall covering one of the city's famous fountains. It is just a minute away from the cathedral. Just a few steps in a south-easterly direction lies the 19th-century [theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theater_Aachen).

Also of note are two remaining city gates, the *[Ponttor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ponttor)* (Pont gate), 800 metres (1⁄2 mile) northwest of the cathedral, and the *Marschiertor* (marching gate), close to the central railway station. There are also a few parts of both medieval city walls left, most of them integrated into more recent buildings, but some others still visible. There are even five towers left, some of which are used for housing.

[St. Michael's Church, Aachen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Michael's_Church,_Aachen) was built as a church of the Aachen [Jesuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_of_Jesus) Collegium in 1628. It is attributed to the Rhine [mannerism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mannerism), and a sample of a local [Renaissance architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_architecture). The rich façade remained unfinished until 1891, when the architect Peter Friedrich Peters added to it. The church is a [Greek Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Orthodox_Church) church today, but the building is used also for concerts because of its good acoustics.

The synagogue in Aachen, which was destroyed on the [Night of Broken Glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Night_of_Broken_Glass) ([Kristallnacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kristallnacht)), 9 November 1938, was reinaugurated on 18 May 1995.[^cite_note-102][^cite_note-103] One of the contributors to the reconstructions of the synagogue was [Jürgen Linden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%BCrgen_Linden), the Lord Mayor of Aachen from 1989 to 2009.

There are numerous other notable churches and [monasteries](45856.md), a few remarkable 17th- and 18th-century buildings in the particular [Baroque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baroque_architecture) style typical of the region, a synagogue, a collection of statues and monuments, park areas, cemeteries, among others. Among the museums in the town are the [Suermondt-Ludwig Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suermondt-Ludwig_Museum), which has a fine sculpture collection and the [Aachen Museum of the International Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen_Museum_of_the_International_Press?action=edit\&redlink=1), which is dedicated to newspapers from the 16th century to the present.[^cite_note-EB-104] The area's industrial history is reflected in dozens of 19th- and early 20th-century manufacturing sites in the city.

## Economy



[Ford](30433662.md) Research Center, Aachen

Aachen is the administrative centre for the coal-mining industries in neighbouring places to the northeast.[^cite_note-McClendon_1996-22]

Products manufactured in Aachen include electrical goods, [fine woolen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen_fine_cloth) textiles, foodstuffs (chocolate and candy), glass, machinery, rubber products, furniture, metal products.[^cite_note-Cohen-105] Also in and around Aachen chemicals, plastics, cosmetics, and needles and pins are produced.[^cite_note-Ranson_1998-36] Though once a major player in Aachen's economy, today glassware and textile production make up only 10% of total manufacturing jobs in the city.[^cite_note-Kerner_2013-10] There have been a number of spin-offs from the university's [IT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_technology) technology department.

### Electric vehicle manufacturing



StreetScooter *Work* as DHL delivery van (2016)

In June 2010, Achim Kampker, together with Günther Schuh, founded a small company to develop electric powered light utility vehicles; in August 2014, it was renamed [StreetScooter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/StreetScooter) GmbH. This started as a privately organised research initiative at the [RWTH Aachen University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RWTH_Aachen_University), before becoming the independent company in Aachen. Kampker was also the founder and chairman of the European Network for Affordable and Sustainable Electromobility. In May 2014, the company announced that the city of Aachen, the city council Aachen and the savings bank Aachen had ordered electric vehicles from the company. In late 2014, approximately 70 employees were manufacturing 200 vehicles annually in the premises of the [Waggonfabrik Talbot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waggonfabrik_Talbot), the former Talbot/Bombardier plant in Aachen.[^cite_note-106]

In December 2014 [DHL Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DHL_Group) purchased the StreetScooter company from Günther, operating it as a wholly owned subsidiary.[^cite_note-107]

In 2015, Günther founded a new electric vehicle company, [e.GO Mobile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E.GO_Mobile), which started producing the e.GO Life electric passenger car and other vehicles in April 2019.

By April 2016, StreetScooter announced that it would produce 2000 of its electric vans, branded the Work, in Aachen by the end of the year, and would be scaling up to manufacture approximately 10,000 Works annually, starting in 2017, also in Aachen.[^cite_note-108] At the time, this target would make it the largest electric light utility vehicle manufacturer in Europe, surpassing [Renault](162292.md)'s smaller [Kangoo Z.E.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renault_Kangoo)[^cite_note-109]

## Culture

## Education



The main building of RWTH Aachen University



Typical Aachen street with early 20th-century [Gründerzeit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gr%C3%BCnderzeit) houses



Another example of Aachen early 20th-century Gründerzeit houses

[RWTH Aachen University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RWTH_Aachen_University), established as Polytechnicum in 1870, is one of [Germany's Universities of Excellence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Universities_Excellence_Initiative) with strong emphasis on technological research, especially for electrical and mechanical engineering, computer sciences, physics, and chemistry. The university clinic attached to the RWTH, the [Klinikum Aachen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klinikum_Aachen), is the biggest single-building hospital in Europe.[^cite_note-113] Over time, a host of software and computer industries have developed around the university. It also maintains a [botanical garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botanical_garden) (the [Botanischer Garten Aachen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botanischer_Garten_Aachen)).

[FH Aachen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FH_Aachen), Aachen University of Applied Sciences (AcUAS) was founded in 1971. The AcUAS offers a classic engineering education in professions such as [mechatronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechatronics), construction engineering, mechanical engineering or electrical engineering. German and international students are educated in more than 20 international or foreign-oriented programmes and can acquire German as well as international degrees (Bachelor/Master) or *Doppelabschlüsse* (double degrees). Foreign students account for more than 21% of the student body.

The Katholische Hochschule Nordrhein-Westfalen – Abteilung Aachen (Catholic University of Applied Sciences Northrhine-Westphalia – Aachen department)[^cite_note-114] offers its some 750 students a variety of degree programmes: social work, childhood education, nursing, and co-operative management. It also has the only programme of study in Germany especially designed for mothers.[^cite_note-115]

The [Hochschule für Musik und Tanz Köln](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hochschule_f%C3%BCr_Musik_und_Tanz_K%C3%B6ln) ([Cologne University of Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cologne_University_of_Music)) is one of the world's foremost performing arts schools and one of the largest music institutions for higher education in Europe[^cite_note-116] with one of its three campuses in Aachen.[^cite_note-117] The Aachen campus substantially contributes to the Opera/Musical Theatre master's programme by collaborating with the [Theater Aachen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theater_Aachen) and the recently established musical theatre chair through the Rheinische Opernakademie.

The [German Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Army)'s technical school *(Ausbildungszentrum Technik Landsysteme)* is located in Aachen.[^cite_note-MeerOpitz1998-118]

## Sports



[New Tivoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Tivoli), home ground of [Alemannia Aachen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alemannia_Aachen)

The annual [CHIO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CHIO_Aachen) (short for the French term *Concours Hippique International Officiel*) is the biggest [equestrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equestrianism) meeting of the world and among horsemen is considered to be as prestigious for equitation as the tournament of [Wimbledon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Championships,_Wimbledon) for tennis. Aachen hosted the [2006 FEI World Equestrian Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_FEI_World_Equestrian_Games).

The local [football](10568.md) team [Alemannia Aachen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alemannia_Aachen) had a short run in Germany's [first division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundesliga), after its promotion in 2006. However, the team could not sustain its status and is now back in the third division. The stadium ["Tivoli"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Tivoli), opened in 1928, served as the venue for the team's home games and was well known for its incomparable atmosphere throughout the whole of the second division.[^cite_note-GdawietzLeroi2007-119] Before the old stadium's demolition in 2011, it was used by amateurs, whilst the Bundesliga Club held its games in the new stadium "Neuer Tivoli" – meaning [New Tivoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Tivoli)—a couple of metres down the road. The building work for the stadium which has a capacity of 32,960, began in May 2008 and was completed by the beginning of 2009.

The Ladies in Black women's volleyball team (part of the "PTSV Aachen" sports club since 2013) has played in the first German volleyball league (DVL) since 2008.

In June 2022, the local basketball club BG Aachen e.V. was promoted to the 1st regional league.

## Transport



[Aachen Central Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen_Central_Station)

### Rail

Aachen's railway station, the [Hauptbahnhof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen_Central_Station) (Central Station), was constructed in 1841 for the [Cologne–Aachen railway line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cologne%E2%80%93Aachen_high-speed_railway). In 1905, it was moved closer to the city centre. It serves main lines to Cologne, [Mönchengladbach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen%E2%80%93M%C3%B6nchengladbach_railway) and [Liège](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HSL_3) as well as branch lines to [Heerlen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heerlen), [Alsdorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alsdorf), [Stolberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stolberg_\(Rhineland\)) and [Eschweiler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eschweiler). [ICE high speed trains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intercity-Express) from [Brussels](3708.md) via [Cologne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cologne) to [Frankfurt am Main](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankfurt_am_Main) and [Eurostar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurostar) trains from Paris to Cologne also stop at Aachen Central Station. Four [RE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional-Express) lines and two [RB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regionalbahn) lines connect Aachen with the [Ruhrgebiet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruhr), Mönchengladbach, Spa (Belgium), [Düsseldorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%BCsseldorf) and the [Siegerland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siegerland). The *[Euregiobahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euregiobahn)*, a regional railway system, reaches several minor cities in the Aachen region.

There are four smaller stations in Aachen: *[Aachen West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen_West_station)*, *[Aachen Schanz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen_Schanz_station)*, *[Aachen-Rothe Erde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen-Rothe_Erde_station)* and *[Eilendorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eilendorf_station)*. Slower trains stop at these. Aachen West has gained in importance with the expansion of [RWTH Aachen University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RWTH_Aachen_University).

### Intercity bus stations

There are two stations for [intercity bus services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intercity_bus_service) in Aachen: [Aachen West station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen_West_station), in the north-west of the city, and Aachen Wilmersdorfer Straße, in the north-east.[^cite_note-120]

### Public transport



[Bi-articulated bus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bi-articulated_bus) of the city's transit authority ASEAG, at the [university hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klinikum_Aachen) bus stop

The first horse tram line in Aachen opened in December 1880. After electrification in 1895, it attained a maximum length of 213.5 kilometres (132+5⁄8 miles) in 1915, thus becoming the fourth-longest tram network in Germany. Many tram lines extended to the surrounding towns of [Herzogenrath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herzogenrath), [Stolberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stolberg_\(Rheinland\)), [Alsdorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alsdorf) as well as the Belgian and Dutch communes of [Vaals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaals), [Kelmis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelmis) (then *Altenberg*) and [Eupen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eupen). The Aachen tram system was linked with the [Belgian national interurban tram system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vicinal_tramway). Like many tram systems in Western Europe, the Aachen tram suffered from poorly maintained infrastructure and was so deemed unnecessary and disrupting for car drivers by local politics. On 28 September 1974, the last line 15 (Vaals–Brand) operated for one last day and was then replaced by buses. A proposal to reinstate a tram/light rail system under the name *Campusbahn* was dropped after a referendum.

Today, the ASEAG (*Aachener Straßenbahn und Energieversorgungs-AG*, literally "Aachen Tram and Power Supply Company") operates a 1,240.8-kilometre-long (771 mi) bus network with 68 bus routes. Because of the location at the border, many bus routes extend to Belgium and the Netherlands. Lines 14 to Eupen, Belgium and 44 to Heerlen, Netherlands are jointly operated with [Transport en Commun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soci%C3%A9t%C3%A9_R%C3%A9gionale_Wallonne_du_Transport) and [Veolia Transport Nederland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veolia_Transport_Nederland), respectively. ASEAG is one of the main participants in the Aachener Verkehrsverbund (AVV), a tariff association in the region. Along with ASEAG, city bus routes of Aachen are served by private contractors such as Sadar, Taeter, Schlömer, or [DB Regio Bus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DB_Regio_Bus). Line 350, which runs from [Maastricht](20125.md), also enters Aachen.

### Roads

Aachen is connected to the [Autobahn](1764276.md) [A4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundesautobahn_4) (west-east), [A44](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundesautobahn_44) (north-south) and A544 (a smaller motorway from the A4 to the *Europaplatz* near the city centre). There are plans to eliminate traffic jams at the Aachen road interchange.

### Airport

[Maastricht Aachen Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maastricht_Aachen_Airport) ([IATA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IATA_airport_code): **MST**, [ICAO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ICAO_airport_code): **EHBK**) is the main airport of Aachen and [Maastricht](20125.md). It is located around 15 nautical miles (28 kilometres; 17 miles) northwest of Aachen. There is a shuttle-service between Aachen and the airport. However, the airport only offers limited European destinations. The nearest major international airports are [Cologne Bonn Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cologne_Bonn_Airport), located 88 km (55 mi) east and [Düsseldorf Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%BCsseldorf_Airport), located 97.1 km (60 mi) northeast of Aachen.

Recreational aviation is served by the (formerly military) [Aachen Merzbrück Airfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen_Merzbr%C3%BCck_Airfield).

## Charlemagne Prize

Main article: [Charlemagne Prize](344825.md)



[Chancellor of Germany](20890626.md) [Angela Merkel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angela_Merkel), wearing the Charlemagne Prize awarded to her in 2008

Since 1950, a committee of Aachen citizens annually awards the Charlemagne Prize (German: *Karlspreis*) to personalities of outstanding service to the unification of Europe. It is traditionally awarded on [Ascension Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascension_Day) at the [City Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aachen_Rathaus). In 2016, the Charlemagne Award was awarded to [Pope Francis](1687680.md).

The International Charlemagne Prize of Aachen was awarded in the year 2000 to US president [Bill Clinton](3356.md), for his special personal contribution to co-operation with the states of Europe, for the preservation of peace, freedom, democracy and [human rights in Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights_in_Europe), and for his support of the enlargement of the European Union. In 2004, [Pope John Paul II](23805.md)'s efforts to unite Europe were honoured with an "Extraordinary Charlemagne Medal", which was awarded for the only time ever.
