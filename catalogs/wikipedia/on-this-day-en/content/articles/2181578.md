The **Arizona State Capitol** is the [seat of government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seat_of_government) for the [U.S. state](18618239.md) of [Arizona](21883824.md) and is located in its [capital city](181337.md) of [Phoenix](49121.md). The capitol comprises the original State Capitol building; two buildings housing the legislative chambers for the [Arizona State Legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arizona_State_Legislature) made up of the [House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arizona_House_of_Representatives) and the [Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arizona_Senate); and Executive Tower containing offices for the [Governor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governor_of_Arizona). It was originally built in 1900 as the capitol for the [Arizona Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arizona_Territory), and became the state capitol after Arizona was [admitted into the Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Admission_to_the_Union) in 1912. The 1900 capitol is maintained as the **Arizona Capitol Museum** with a focus on the history and culture of Arizona.&#x20;

## History

### Earlier capitols

On February 24, 1863, the [Arizona Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arizona_Territory) was created by splitting the existing [New Mexico Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Mexico_Territory) along a north–south line.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFarish1915321–322-2][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFarish1915323-3] Initially, the [bill](52047975.md) specified the capital was to be located in [Tucson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tucson,_Arizona), but that stipulation was removed before the final passage of the bill.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFarish1915323-3] The [first session](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1st_Arizona_Territorial_Legislature) of the [new Territorial Legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arizona_Territorial_Legislature) convened in [Prescott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prescott,_Arizona) on September 26, 1864 in a "long, one-story log-house" built for the legislature.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFarish191690-4][^cite_note-address-5] The building was roughly built, and due to its inadequate [chinking](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/chinking), notably drafty and uncomfortable in the winter. The [second legislative session](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2nd_Arizona_Territorial_Legislature) was held at nearby [saloon](272207.md), while the [third](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3rd_Arizona_Territorial_Legislature) was held in an "old courthouse and jail".[^cite_note-address-5] The legislature voted on September 27, 1867 to the relocate the capital "permanently" to Tucson by November 1.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFarish191828-6] The move was considered controversial and was subject to allegations of fraud on the part of Governor [Richard McCormick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_C._McCormick).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEFarish191828-6] While in Tucson, the legislature met in a series of existing [adobe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adobe) buildings.[^cite_note-address-5]

In January 1877, it was voted to move the capital back to Prescott at the start of the next session in January 1879 where the legislature then resided in "large, frame building" known as Curtis Hall.[^cite_note-address-5][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKelly192667–68-7][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEhrlich1981232-8] Due to its remoteness, its harsh winters and lack of direct railroad connections, by the next legislative session in 1881, members were already debating the suitability of Prescott as the Arizona capital and if it should be moved back to Tucson or elsewhere.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEEhrlich1981232-8] By 1889, the legislature came to a decision and voted to relocate to Phoenix on January 29. It immediately adjourned in Prescott to reconvene a week later on February 6 at the city hall in Phoenix.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKelly1926132–133-9]

### Territorial capitol

As part of an effort to demonstrate that the Arizona Territory was ready for [statehood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Admission_to_the_Union), a [bond](60737.md) issue for \$100,000 was approved for the construction of a territorial capitol.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKelly1926183-10][^cite_note-FOOTNOTESilverstein2010-11] An additional \$25,000 for the construction was authorized in 1899. [James Riely Gordon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Riely_Gordon) was chosen to design the new capitol building by winning a [design contest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architectural_design_competition). His original plan called for the capitol to be much larger, with a more prominent rotunda and large wings for both houses of the legislature on each side of the current building.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESilverstein2010-11] Funding deficits meant the project had to be reduced, so the legislative wings were discarded from the plan and a small lead-alloy top substituted for Gordon's decorative dome.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESilverstein2010-11]

Construction of the Capitol began when ground was broken in March 1899 with the work undertaken by the contractor [Tom Lovell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Lovell_\(building_contractor\)) of [Denton, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denton,_Texas).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE''The_Arizona_Graphic''19011-12] Unusually, a [cornerstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornerstone) was not used in the construction of the capitol as when the site was prepared for one, and when a ceremony would have been held, it was during the hot, desert summer when outdoor activity was minimized.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE''The_Arizona_Graphic''19011-12] The completed capitol building was turned over to the territorial government on May 1, 1900.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTE''The_Arizona_Graphic''19011-12] The legislature began meeting in the new capitol on January 21, 1901 at the start of the legislative session, and it was publicly dedicated in ceremony of February 24.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKelly1926215-13]

### State capitol

In 1918 and 1938, expansions were added on the west side of the building, which had the same architecture and increased the total square footage from the original 40,000 to a final 123,000. It was home to the Legislature until 1960, when the current house and senate buildings were constructed, and the Governor's Office until 1974, when the executive tower was built. The state at that time had a plan of converting the original Capitol into a museum dedicated to Arizona's history. The original opening of the Arizona Capitol Museum was announced by Governor Bruce Babbitt at his inauguration in 1978 and hosted over 40,000 schoolchildren in that first year in 1979. After a restoration, the building was re-opened as a museum in 1981.[^cite_note-14] In the 1990s, more than \$3 million was spent to renovate the Capitol and rooms were restored to their original design. Again, due to budget deficits, construction was stopped on a few rooms on the third floor and they remain incomplete. The 1900 Capitol was listed on the [National Register of Historic Places](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Register_of_Historic_Places) on October 29, 1974.[^cite_note-nara-1]

After the [Great Recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Recession) created a \$3.2 billion deficit in a state's budget, the Arizona government put up for sale a number of buildings and state [prisons](19008450.md), as well as the Capitol.[^cite_note-nyt-15] The sale, which included both legislature buildings and Executive Tower, closed on January 13 and resulted in \$735 million in revenue for the government who would then [leaseback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaseback) the properties.[^cite_note-16] It was estimated that the arrangement would cost Arizona \$1.5 billion in lease payments over the 20-year lease.[^cite_note-nyt-15] The original capitol building was not a part of the transaction. During the 2019 [State of the State address](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_of_the_State_address), Governor [Doug Ducey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doug_Ducey) announced that as part of a refinancing, the state would regain ownership of the Capitol buildings sold in 2010.[^cite_note-17] Under terms of the initial sale, the earliest refinancing could occur was in July 2020.

On the night of June 24, 2022, and into the morning of June 25, police and pro-choice activists [clashed outside the building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Arizona_Capitol_protests) in protest of the Supreme Court ruling in *[Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dobbs_v._Jackson_Women's_Health_Organization)*.[^cite_note-\:0-18] The protesters numbered over 5,000 persons.[^cite_note-\:0-18] The [Arizona Department of Public Safety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arizona_Department_of_Public_Safety) deployed [tear gas](1202646.md) and [flash bang grenades](662807.md) against the protesters, forcing them to disperse.[^cite_note-19] Arizona Senate Republicans accused the protestors of attempting to storm the building, with GOP State Senator [Kelly Townsend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelly_Townsend) tweeting that the legislators were "being held hostage" and comparing the protest to the [2021 United States Capitol attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_United_States_Capitol_attack). She also called for a "J24 Committee" in reference to the [January 6 committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/January_6_committee).[^cite_note-20]

## Arizona Capitol Museum



View of the original capitol building

Museum exhibits, events, and programs focus on the evolution of Arizona from Territory to State. The Arizona Takes Shape exhibit provides school-age visitors curriculum-related information for [Arizona State History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Arizona) and government studies. The museum has over 20 exhibits featuring contemporary, historical and artifacts from the Arizona state-owned collections. Permanent exhibits include the sinking of USS *Arizona*, the formal silver service from USS *Arizona*, a timeline of events pivotal in making Arizona a state, the Governor and Secretary of State's original offices, the historical senate, and house.[^cite_note-21]

Visitors can enter the Historic House Chamber where the people can sit at the desks. There is also a room dedicated to the 140 changes in the [Arizona Constitution](https://www.azleg.gov/constitution/) over 100 years of statehood. The Governor's office on the second floor includes artifacts from several of Arizona's governors as well as a flag used by Teddy Roosevelt's Rough Riders.[^cite_note-22]

One impressive display shows the enormous silver and copper punchbowl service from [USS *Arizona*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Arizona), as well as a bronze sculpture that was ensconced outside the Admiral's stateroom and used as a centerpiece at state dinners wherever USS *Arizona* was docked. Both of these historical artifacts survived the sinking of *Arizona* because they had been removed from the ship for cleaning prior to the [attack on Pearl Harbor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attack_on_Pearl_Harbor). The punchbowl service is the only one of its kind and is composed of etched copper panels depicting desert scenes set into a silver bowl ornamented with mermaids, dolphins, waves, and other nautical themes.[^cite_note-23]

Of particular interest is the display of a collection of gifts received by Arizona as part of the [Merci Train](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merci_Train) sent by France to the United States following World War II. The French wanted to thank America for sending the [Friendship Train](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friendship_Train): 250 railroad cars full of fuel, oil, and food in 1948 during a time that the European countries were devastated by World War II. Tens of thousands of French citizens donated objects to be sent to the United States and it was decided that because the outpouring of goods was so great, one boxcar would be sent to each state with one being shared between the District of Columbia and the Territory of Hawaii. All of the items were to be loaded in "Forty and Eight"-type boxcars, named after the sign painted on them which stated that 40 men or 8 horses could be loaded inside. Each car was to be adorned with the coats of arms of all of the provinces of France. The capitol building displays work by the noted Arizona artist [Lon Megargee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lon_Megargee). The train car can be found at the [McCormick-Stillman Railroad Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McCormick-Stillman_Railroad_Park) in [Scottsdale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottsdale,_Arizona).[^cite_note-24][^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26]

## Architecture



View from the east at sunset. (2007). The Senate building is on the left and the House building is on the right.

The building is made largely from materials indigenous to Arizona, including [tufa stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tufa), [malapai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malpa%C3%ADs_\(landform\)), [granite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granite), and the [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper) of the dome. The design is optimized for the desert climate of Arizona, with thick masonry walls that insulate the interior, skylights, and round "bullseye" [clerestory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clerestory) windows to let heat out of the legislative chambers. The building is topped with a weather vane similar to the [Winged Victory of Samothrace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winged_Victory_of_Samothrace), visible through a skylight from within the [rotunda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotunda_\(architecture\)).

## Capitol Mall renovation proposal

As Arizona's population has grown, the Capitol complex itself has become increasingly crowded. The Senate and House buildings, opened in 1960, have been deteriorating. The Senate, in particular, is prone to constant plumbing problems, and occasionally a broken pipe floods the entire building. The Capitol itself is now used exclusively as a museum, and serves over 70,000 visitors each year, including more than 50,000 school children. In the past complaints had been made that the site was not pleasing aesthetically, and compare the Senate and House buildings as oversized "bunkers" which eclipse the beauty of the Capitol. A task force appointed by the state legislature in 2007 reported that the complex is "barely" adequate to suit the state's current needs and "wholly" inadequate to suit the state's future needs.[^cite_note-27] As a result, proposals were made in 2008 to renovate or rebuild the Capitol site, to a grander site, as well as a site that will serve the needs of the government more adequately. To date, keeping the integrity of the 1901 building in original condition, including the "cramped" spaces occupied by some of the Senate and the House staff have been the main focus for the Capitol Museum administrator and staff. The building's original usage is part of the state history.

Proposals for relocating some office and meeting space back into the Capitol included the House and Senate buildings undergo either a drastic rebuilding and expansion or a complete demolition and construction of new facilities for the House and Senate. A past [Arizona State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arizona_State_University) study planned a comprehensive redesign for the entire Capitol mall and complex.[^cite_note-28]
