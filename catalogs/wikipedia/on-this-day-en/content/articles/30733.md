A **tram**, also known as a **tramcar**, **streetcar** or **trolley** is a form of [urban rail transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_rail_transit) in which [vehicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolling_stock), whether individual [railcars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railcar) or [multiple-unit trains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple-unit), run on [tramway tracks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tramway_track) laid on or parallel to public streets, or operate in [segregated right-of-ways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_right-of-way).[^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4]

Trams are a type of [light rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_rail)[^cite_note-CED-5][^cite_note-ORR-6][^cite_note-\:1-7] and are generally the smallest-scale form of such rail transit; they operate in mixed traffic with other [motor vehicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motor_vehicle), provide lower local-scale capacity, and operate with limited [signal-priority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic_signal_preemption).[^cite_note-ORR-6] However, their larger vehicle size and steel wheels allow for greater capacity and improved [energy efficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_efficiency_in_transport) in comparison to [diesel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diesel_engine) or [electric buses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_bus).[^cite_note-\:1-7] Their units are typically capable of forming [motor coaches or motorcars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motor_coach_\(rail\)), which allows for the operation of longer trains.[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9]

Trams are usually lighter and shorter than [main line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_line_\(railway\)) and [rapid transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapid_transit) trains or metros. Most trams use electrical power, fed by a [pantograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantograph_\(transport\)) sliding on an [overhead line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overhead_line); older systems may use a [trolley pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trolley_pole) or a [bow collector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bow_collector). In some cases, a [contact shoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contact_shoe) on a [third rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_rail) is used, or the tram runs on [battery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_engine) or diesel motors.

Trams are primarily operated as [local public transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_transport#Urban_rail_transit), but can also operate as a hybrid [tram-train](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tram-train), running [at-grade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/At-grade_railway) in a [city center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_centre) and switching to segregated right-of-ways in [suburbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suburb). Occasionally, they may also carry [freight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freight_\(rail\)). The differences between these modes of rail transport are often indistinct, and systems may combine multiple features or operation patterns.

One of the advantages over earlier forms of transit was the low [rolling resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolling_resistance) of metal wheels on [steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel) rails, allowing the trams to haul a greater load for a given effort. Another factor which contributed to the rise of trams was the high [total cost of ownership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_cost_of_ownership) of horses. Electric trams largely replaced animal power in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Improvements in other vehicles such as [buses](4146.md) led to the decline of trams in the early to mid 20th century. However, trams have seen a resurgence since the 1980s.

## Etymology and terminology



A sign in [Portland](23503.md) that reads "go by streetcar". Trams are often called streetcars in North America.

The English terms *tram* and *tramway* are derived from the [Scots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scots_language) word *tram*,[^cite_note-10] referring respectively to a type of truck ([goods wagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goods_wagon) or freight [railroad car](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railroad_car)) used in [coal mines](208413.md) and the tracks on which they ran. The word *tram* probably derived from [Middle Flemish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Dutch) *trame* ("beam, handle of a barrow, bar, rung"). The identical word *trame* with the meaning "crossbeam" is also used in the [French language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language). Etymologists believe that the word *tram* refers to the wooden beams the railway tracks were initially made of before the railroad pioneers switched to the much more wear-resistant tracks made of iron and, later, steel.[^cite_note-11] The word *tram-car* is attested from 1873.[^cite_note-12]

### Alternatives



A trackless train is also called tram in U.S. English.

Although the words *tram* and *tramway* have been adopted by many languages, they are not used universally in English; North Americans prefer *streetcar*, *trolley*, or *trolleycar*. The term *streetcar* is first recorded in 1840, and originally referred to [horsecars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horsecar).

The terms *streetcar* and *trolley* are often used interchangeably in the [United States](3434750.md), with *trolley* being the preferred term in the eastern US and *streetcar* in the western US. *Streetcar* is preferred in [English Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Canada), while *tramway* is preferred in [Quebec](7954867.md). In parts of the United States, internally powered [buses](4146.md) made to resemble a streetcar are often referred to as "trolleys". To avoid further confusion with [trolley buses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trolley_bus), the [American Public Transportation Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Public_Transportation_Association) (APTA) refers to them as "[trolley-replica buses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trolley-replica_bus)". In the United States, the term *tram* has sometimes been used for rubber-tired [trackless trains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trackless_train), which are unrelated to other kinds of trams.

A widely held belief holds the word *trolley* to derive from the *troller* (said to derive from the words *traveler* and *roller*), a four-wheeled device that was dragged along dual overhead wires by a cable that connected the troller to the top of the car and collected electrical power from the [overhead wires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overhead_wire);[^cite_note-13] this [portmanteau derivation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portmanteau_word) is, however, most likely [folk etymology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_etymology). "Trolley" and variants refer to the verb *troll*, meaning "roll" and probably derived from [Old French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_French),[^cite_note-14] and cognate uses of the word were well established for handcarts and horse drayage, as well as for nautical uses.[^cite_note-15]

The alternative North American term 'trolley' may strictly speaking be considered incorrect, as the term can also be applied to cable cars, or [conduit cars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conduit_car) that instead draw power from an underground supply. Conventional diesel [tourist buses](4146.md) decorated to look like streetcars are sometimes called *trolleys* in the US ([tourist trolley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourist_trolley)). Furthering confusion, the term *tram* has instead been applied to open-sided, low-speed [segmented vehicles on rubber tires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trackless_train) generally used to ferry tourists short distances, for example on the [Universal Studios backlot tour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Studios_Backlot_Tour) and, in many countries, as tourist transport to major destinations. The term may also apply to an aerial ropeway, e.g. the [Roosevelt Island Tramway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roosevelt_Island_Tramway).

### Trolleybus

Although the use of the term *trolley* for tram was not adopted in Europe, the term was later associated with the *[trolleybus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trolleybus)*, a rubber-tired vehicle running on hard pavement, which draws its power from pairs of overhead wires. These electric buses, which use twin trolley poles, are also called *trackless trolleys* (particularly in the northeastern US), or sometimes simply *trolleys* (in the UK, as well as the [Pacific Northwest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Northwest), including [Seattle](11388236.md), and [Vancouver](32706.md)).

### Creation

The history of passenger trams, streetcars and trolley systems, began in the early nineteenth century. It can be divided into several distinct periods defined by the principal means of power used. Precursors to the tramway included the wooden or stone [wagonways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wagonway) that were used in central Europe to transport [mine carts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mine_cart) with unflanged wheels since the 1500s, and the paved limestone trackways designed by the Romans for heavy horse and ox-drawn transportation. By the 1700s, paved [plateways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plateway) with [cast iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cast_iron) rails were introduced in England for transporting coal, stone or iron ore from the mines to the urban factories and docks.

### Horse-drawn

Main article: [Horsecar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horsecar)



A horse-drawn tram operated by [Swansea and Mumbles Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swansea_and_Mumbles_Railway), 1870. Established in 1804, the railway service was the world's first.



Inauguration of the [Milan](36511.md)–[Monza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monza) tramway, 1876.

The world's first passenger train or tram was the [Swansea and Mumbles Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swansea_and_Mumbles_Railway), in [Wales](69894.md), UK. The British Parliament passed the Mumbles Railway Act in 1804, and horse-drawn service started in 1807.[^cite_note-16] The service closed in 1827, but was restarted in 1860, again using horses.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDunbar196715-17] It was worked by steam from 1877, and then, from 1929, by very large (106-seat) electric tramcars, until closure in 1960.[^cite_note-18] The Swansea and Mumbles Railway was something of a one-off however, and no street tramway appeared in Britain until 1860 when one was built in [Birkenhead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birkenhead_Corporation_Tramways) by the American [George Francis Train](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Francis_Train).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDunbar196723-19]

Street railways developed in America before Europe, due to the poor [paving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Road_surface) of the streets in American cities which made them unsuitable for [horse-drawn omnibuses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horse-drawn_omnibus), which were then common on the well-paved streets of European cities. Running the horsecars on rails allowed for a much smoother ride. There are records of a street railway running in [Baltimore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltimore) as early as 1828, however the first authenticated streetcar in America, was the [New York and Harlem Railroad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_and_Harlem_Railroad) developed by the Irish coach builder [John Stephenson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Stephenson_\(coachbuilder\)), in New York City which began service in the year 1832.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDunbar196716–17-20][^cite_note-21] The New York and Harlem Railroad's [Fourth Avenue Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Avenue_Line_\(Manhattan_surface\)) ran along the [Bowery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowery) and [Fourth Avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Avenue_\(Manhattan\)) in New York City. It was followed in 1835 by the [New Orleans and Carrollton Railroad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Orleans_and_Carrollton_Railroad) in [New Orleans, Louisiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Orleans,_Louisiana),[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDunbar196718-22] which still operates as the [St. Charles Streetcar Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Charles_Streetcar_Line). Other American cities did not follow until the 1850s, after which the "animal railway" became an increasingly common feature in the larger towns.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDunbar196718-22]

The first permanent tram line in continental Europe was opened in Paris in 1855 by [Alphonse Loubat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphonse_Loubat) who had previously worked on American streetcar lines.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEDunbar196721-23] The tram was developed in numerous cities of Europe (some of the most extensive systems were found in Berlin, [Budapest](36787.md), [Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_Corporation_Tramways), [Saint Petersburg](24320051.md), [Lisbon](18091.md), [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_County_Council_Tramways), [Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Corporation_Tramways), [Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tramways_in_Paris#History), [Kyiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Kyiv)). The first tram in South America opened in 1858 in [Santiago, Chile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_town_tramway_systems_in_Chile). The first trams in Australia opened in 1860 in [Sydney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Sydney). Africa's first tram service started in [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Alexandria) on 8 January 1863. The first trams in Asia opened in 1869 in [Batavia (Jakarta), Netherlands East Indies (Indonesia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Asia).

Limitations of horsecars included the fact that any given animal could only work so many hours on a given day, had to be housed, groomed, fed and cared for day in and day out, and produced prodigious amounts of manure, which the streetcar company was charged with storing and then disposing. Since a typical horse pulled a streetcar for about a dozen miles a day and worked for four or five hours, many systems needed ten or more horses in stable for each horsecar. In 1905 the British newspaper *Newcastle Daily Chronicle* reported that, "A large number of London's discarded horse tramcars have been sent to [Lincolnshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincolnshire) where they are used as sleeping rooms for [potato pickers](10721543.md)".[^cite_note-24]



Horse-drawn trams continued to be used in [New York City](645042.md) until 1917.

Horses continued to be used for light shunting well into the 20th century, and many large metropolitan lines lasted into the early 20th century. New York City had a regular horsecar service on the [Bleecker Street Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bleecker_Street_Line) until its closure in 1917.[^cite_note-25] [Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh,_Pennsylvania), had its Sarah Street line drawn by horses until 1923. The last regular mule-drawn cars in the US ran in [Sulphur Rock, Arkansas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulphur_Rock,_Arkansas), until 1926 and were commemorated by a [U.S. postage stamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._postage_stamp) issued in 1983.[^cite_note-encyclopediaofarkansas-26] The last mule tram service in [Mexico City](18987.md) ended in 1932, and a mule tram in [Celaya, Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celaya,_Mexico), survived until 1954.[^cite_note-morrison_celaya-27] The last horse-drawn tram to be withdrawn from public service in the UK took passengers from [Fintona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fintona) railway station to Fintona Junction one mile away on the main Omagh to Enniskillen railway in Northern Ireland. The tram made its last journey on 30 September 1957 when the Omagh to Enniskillen line closed. The "van" is preserved at the [Ulster Transport Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_Transport_Museum).

Horse-drawn trams still operate on the 1876-built [Douglas Bay Horse Tramway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Bay_Horse_Tramway) on the [Isle of Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isle_of_Man), and at the 1894-built [horse tram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Harbor_Horse_Drawn_Tram) at [Victor Harbor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Harbor,_South_Australia) in [South Australia](26716.md). New horse-drawn systems have been established at the [Hokkaido](58092.md) Museum in Japan and also in [Disneyland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disneyland). A horse-tram route in [Polish](22936.md) [gmina Mrozy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gmina_Mrozy), first built in 1902, was reopened in 2012.

### Steam

Main articles: [Tram engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tram_engine) and [Steam dummy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_dummy)



A steam [tram engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tram_engine) from the [Cologne-Bonn Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cologne_Stadtbahn), pulling a train through [Brühl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Br%C3%BChl_\(Rhineland\)) marketplace, c. 1900

The first mechanical trams were powered by [steam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_engine).[^cite_note-28] Generally, there were two types of steam tram. The first and most common had a small [steam locomotive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_locomotive) at the head of a line of one or more carriages, similar to a small train. Systems with such steam trams included [Christchurch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christchurch), New Zealand; Sydney, Australia; other city systems in [New South Wales](21654.md); [Munich](19058.md), Germany (from August 1883 on),[^cite_note-Muenchen1964-29] [British India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohamedali_Tramways_Company) (from 1885) and the Dublin & Blessington Steam Tramway (from 1888) in Ireland. Steam tramways also were used on the suburban tramway lines around [Milan](36511.md) and [Padua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Padua); the last *Gamba de Legn* ("Peg-Leg") tramway ran on the [Milan-Magenta-Castano Primo route](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milan-Magenta/Castano_Primo_Tramway?action=edit\&redlink=1) in late 1957.[^cite_note-Milan1957-30]

The other style of steam tram had the steam engine in the body of the tram, referred to as a [tram engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tram_engine) (UK) or [steam dummy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_dummy) (US). The most notable system to adopt such trams was in Paris. French-designed steam trams also operated in [Rockhampton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Rockhampton), in the Australian state of [Queensland](59051.md) between 1909 and 1939. [Stockholm](26741.md), Sweden, had a steam tram line at the island of [Södermalm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%B6dermalm) between 1887 and 1901.

Tram engines usually had modifications to make them suitable for street running in residential areas. The wheels, and other moving parts of the machinery, were usually enclosed for safety reasons and to make the engines quieter. Measures were often taken to prevent the engines from emitting visible smoke or steam. Usually the engines used [coke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coke_\(fuel\)) rather than coal as fuel to avoid emitting smoke; [condensers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensing_steam_locomotive) or [superheating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superheater) were used to avoid emitting visible steam. A major drawback of this style of tram was the limited space for the engine, so that these trams were usually underpowered. Steam trams faded out around the 1890s to 1900s, being replaced by electric trams.

### Cable-hauled

Main article: [Cable car (railway)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cable_car_\(railway\))



Winding drums of the [London and Blackwall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_and_Blackwall_Railway) cable-operated railway

Another motive system for trams was the cable car, which was pulled along a [fixed track](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_track) by a moving steel cable, the cable usually running in a slot below the street level. The power to move the cable was normally provided at a "powerhouse" site a distance away from the actual vehicle. The [London and Blackwall Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_and_Blackwall_Railway), which opened for passengers in east London, England, in 1840 used such a system.[^cite_note-31]

The first practical cable car line was tested in [San Francisco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_cable_car_system), in 1873. Part of its success is attributed to the development of an effective and reliable [cable grip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cable_grip) mechanism, to grab and release the moving cable without damage. The second city to operate cable trams was [Dunedin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunedin_cable_tramway_system), from 1881 to 1957.[^cite_note-32]

The most extensive cable system in the US was built in [Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cable_cars_in_Chicago) in stages between 1859 and 1892. New York City developed multiple cable car lines, that operated from 1883 to 1909.[^cite_note-33] Los Angeles also had several cable car lines, including the [Second Street Cable Railroad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Street_Cable_Railroad), which operated from 1885 to 1889,[^cite_note-34] and the Temple Street Cable Railway, which operated from 1886 to 1898.[^cite_note-35]



The first [cable car service in Melbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melbourne_cable_tramway_system), 1885. Melbourne operated one of the largest cable car networks in the world.

From 1885 to 1940, the city of [Melbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melbourne_cable_tramway_system), Victoria, Australia operated one of the largest cable systems in the world, at its peak running 592 trams on 75 kilometres (47 mi) of track. There were also two isolated cable lines in [Sydney](27862.md), New South Wales, Australia; the North Sydney line from 1886 to 1900, and the King Street line from 1892 to 1905.[^cite_note-36]

In [Dresden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dresden), Germany, in 1901 an elevated [suspended](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suspension_railway) cable car following the *Eugen Langen one-railed floating tram system* started operating. [Cable cars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highgate_Hill_Cable_Tramway) operated on Highgate Hill in North London and [Kennington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kennington) to [Brixton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brixton) Hill in South London. They also worked around "Upper Douglas" in the [Isle of Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isle_of_Man) from 1897 to 1929 (cable car 72/73 is the sole survivor of the fleet).[^cite_note-37]

In Italy, in [Trieste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trieste), the [Trieste–Opicina tramway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trieste%E2%80%93Opicina_tramway) was opened in 1902, with the steepest section of the route being negotiated with the help of a [funicular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funicular) and its cables.[^cite_note-38]

Cable cars suffered from high [infrastructure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrastructure) costs, since an expensive system of [cables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wire_rope), [pulleys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulley), [stationary engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stationary_engine) and lengthy underground vault structures beneath the rails had to be provided. They also required physical strength and skill to operate, and alert operators to avoid obstructions and other cable cars. The cable had to be disconnected ("dropped") at designated locations to allow the cars to coast by inertia, for example when crossing another cable line. The cable then had to be "picked up" to resume progress, the whole operation requiring precise timing to avoid damage to the cable and the grip mechanism. Breaks and frays in the cable, which occurred frequently, required the complete cessation of services over a cable route while the cable was repaired. Due to overall wear, the entire length of cable (typically several kilometres) had to be replaced on a regular schedule. After the development of reliable electrically powered trams, the costly high-maintenance cable car systems were rapidly replaced in most locations.



The [San Francisco cable car](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_cable_car_system)'s effectiveness on hills partially explains its continued use.

Cable cars remained especially effective in hilly cities, since their nondriven wheels did not [lose traction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_adhesion) as they climbed or descended a steep hill. The moving cable pulled the car up the hill at a steady pace, unlike a low-powered steam or horse-drawn car. Cable cars do have wheel brakes and [track brakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Track_brake), but the cable also helps restrain the car to going downhill at a constant speed. Performance in steep terrain partially explains the survival of cable cars in San Francisco.

The [San Francisco cable cars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_cable_car_system), though significantly reduced in number, continue to provide regular transportation service, in addition to being a well-known [tourist attraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourist_attraction). A single cable line also survives in [Wellington](33804.md) (rebuilt in 1979 as a [funicular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funicular) but still called the "[Wellington Cable Car](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellington_Cable_Car)"). Another system, with two separate cable lines and a shared power station in the middle, operates from the Welsh town of [Llandudno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llandudno) up to the top of the [Great Orme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Orme) hill in [North Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Wales), UK.

### Internal combustion



The only petrol-driven tram of [Stockholms Spårvägar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholms_Sp%C3%A5rv%C3%A4gar_\(1915\)), on line 19, in the 1920s

[Hastings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hastings) and some other tramways, for example [Stockholms Spårvägar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholms_Sp%C3%A5rv%C3%A4gar_\(1915\)) in Sweden and some lines in [Karachi](17123.md), used [petrol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrol_engine) trams. [Galveston Island Trolley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galveston_Island_Trolley) in Texas operated diesel trams due to the city's hurricane-prone location, which would have resulted in frequent damage to an electrical supply system. Although [Portland, Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portland,_Victoria) promotes its [tourist tram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portland_Cable_Tram)[^cite_note-39] as being a cable car it actually operates using a diesel engine. The tram, which runs on a circular route around the town of Portland, uses dummies and salons formerly used on the [Melbourne cable tramway system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melbourne_cable_tramway_system) and since restored.

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries a number of systems in various parts of the world employed trams powered by gas, [naphtha](184342.md) gas or [coal gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_gas) in particular. Gas trams are known to have operated between [Alphington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphington,_Victoria) and [Clifton Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifton_Hill,_Victoria) in the northern suburbs of [Melbourne](17306237.md), Australia (1886–1888); in Berlin and [Dresden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dresden), Germany; in [Estonia](28222445.md) (1921–1951); between Hirschberg and Hermsdorf, Germany, from 1897 (now [Jelenia Góra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jelenia_G%C3%B3ra), Cieplice, and [Sobieszów](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sobiesz%C3%B3w) in Poland); and in the UK at [Lytham St Annes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lytham_St_Annes), [Trafford Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trafford_Park), Manchester (1897–1908) and [Neath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neath_Corporation_Tramways), Wales (1896–1920).

Comparatively little has been published about gas trams. However, research on the subject was carried out for an article in the October 2011 edition of "The Times", the historical journal of the Australian Association of Timetable Collectors, later renamed the Australian Timetable Association.[^cite_note-40][^cite_note-Ceti-41]



[Gross-Lichterfelde Tram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross-Lichterfelde_Tramway) in [Berlin](3354.md) in 1882. First lacking [overhead wires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overhead_wire), it became the first tram with those in 1883.

### Electric

Main article: [List of tram systems by gauge and electrification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tram_systems_by_gauge_and_electrification)

The world's first electric tram line operated in [Sestroretsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sestroretsk) near [Saint Petersburg](24320051.md) invented and tested by inventor [Fyodor Pirotsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fyodor_Pirotsky) in 1875.[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43] Later, using a similar technology, Pirotsky put into service the first public electric tramway in St. Petersburg, which operated only during September 1880.[^cite_note-guarnieri_1-44] The [second demonstration tramway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siemens_locomotive_of_1879) was presented by Siemens & Halske at the 1879 Berlin Industrial Exposition. The first public electric tramway used for permanent service was the [Gross-Lichterfelde tramway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross-Lichterfelde_tramway) in [Lichterfelde](13312807.md) near Berlin in Germany, which opened in 1881. It was built by [Werner von Siemens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_von_Siemens) who contacted Pirotsky. This was the world's first commercially successful electric tram. It drew current from the rails at first, with [overhead wire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overhead_line) being installed in 1883.[^cite_note-45]



Between 1962 and 1992 [Blackpool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackpool) had the only urban tramway in the UK.

In Britain, [Volk's Electric Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volk's_Electric_Railway) was opened in 1883 in Brighton. This two kilometer line along the seafront, [re-gauged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Track_gauge_conversion) to 2 ft 8+1⁄2 in (825 mm) in 1884, remains in service as the oldest operating electric tramway in the world. Also in 1883, [Mödling and Hinterbrühl Tram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%B6dling_and_Hinterbr%C3%BChl_Tram) was opened near [Vienna](55866.md) in Austria. It was the first tram in the world in regular service that was run with electricity served by an overhead line with [pantograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantograph_\(transport\)) [current collectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_collector). The [Blackpool Tramway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackpool_Tramway) was opened in Blackpool, UK on 29 September 1885 using conduit collection along Blackpool Promenade. This system is still in operation in modernised form.[^cite_note-46]

The earliest tram system in Canada was built by [John Joseph Wright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Joseph_Wright_\(engineer\)?action=edit\&redlink=1), brother of the famous mining entrepreneur [Whitaker Wright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitaker_Wright), in [Toronto](64646.md) in 1883, introducing electric trams in 1892. In the US, multiple experimental [electric trams](#Electric) were exhibited at the 1884 [World Cotton Centennial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Cotton_Centennial) World's Fair in [New Orleans, Louisiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Orleans,_Louisiana), but they were not deemed good enough to replace the [Lamm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emile_Lamm) fireless engines then propelling the [St. Charles Streetcar Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Charles_Streetcar_Line) in that city. The first commercial installation of an electric streetcar in the United States was built in 1884 in [Cleveland, Ohio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleveland,_Ohio), and operated for a period of one year by the East Cleveland Street Railway Company.[^cite_note-mileston-47] The first city-wide electric streetcar system was implemented in 1886 in [Montgomery, Alabama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montgomery,_Alabama), by the [Capital City Street Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_City_Street_Railway) Company, and ran for 50 years.[^cite_note-mileston-47]



[Zürich tram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Z%C3%BCrich) [multiple-unit train](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple-unit_train)

In 1888, the [Richmond Union Passenger Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richmond_Union_Passenger_Railway) began to operate trams in [Richmond, Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richmond,_Virginia), that [Frank J. Sprague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_J._Sprague) had built. Sprague later developed [multiple unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_unit) control, first demonstrated in Chicago in 1897, allowing multiple cars to be coupled together and operated by a single motorman. This gave rise to the modern [subway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapid_transit) train. Following the improvement of an overhead "trolley" system on streetcars for collecting electricity from [overhead wires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overhead_wire) by Sprague, electric tram systems were rapidly adopted across the world.[^cite_note-48]

Earlier electric trains proved difficult or unreliable and experienced limited success until the second half of the 1880s, when new types of current collectors were developed.[^cite_note-guarnieri_1-44] Siemens' line, for example, provided power through a live rail and a return rail, like a [model train](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_train), limiting the [voltage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltage) that could be used, and delivering [electric shocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_shock) to people and animals crossing the tracks.[^cite_note-49] Siemens later designed his own version of overhead current collection, called the [bow collector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bow_collector). One of the first systems to use it was in [Thorold, Ontario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorold,_Ontario), opened in 1887, and it was considered quite successful. While this line proved quite versatile as one of the earliest fully functional electric streetcar installations, it required horse-drawn support while climbing the [Niagara Escarpment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niagara_Escarpment) and for two months of the winter when [hydroelectricity](381399.md) was not available. It continued in service in its original form into the 1950s.

[Sidney Howe Short](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidney_Howe_Short) designed and produced the first [electric motor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_motor) that operated a streetcar without gears. The motor had its [armature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armature_\(electrical_engineering\)) direct-connected to the [streetcar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streetcar)'s [axle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axle) for the driving force.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin1924122–123-50][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHammond2011142-51][^cite_note-FtWorth1894-52][^cite_note-Grace-53][^cite_note-Topeka1894-54] Short pioneered "use of a conduit system of concealed feed" thereby eliminating the necessity of [overhead wire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overhead_line) and a [trolley pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trolley_pole) for street cars and railways.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMalone1928128-55][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin1924122–123-50][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHammond2011142-51] While at the University of Denver he conducted experiments which established that [multiple unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_unit) powered cars were a better way to operate trains and trolleys.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEMartin1924122–123-50][^cite_note-FOOTNOTEHammond2011142-51]

[Sarajevo](26786.md) built a citywide system of electric trams in 1895.[^cite_note-59] [Budapest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budapesti_K%C3%B6zleked%C3%A9si_Zrt.) established [its tramway system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Budapest) in 1887, and its ring line has grown to be the busiest tram line in Europe, with a tram running once per minute at rush hour. [Bucharest](36877.md) and [Belgrade](55904.md)[^cite_note-60] ran a regular service from 1894.[^cite_note-61][^cite_note-62] [Ljubljana](42374.md) introduced [its tram system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ljubljana_tram_system) in 1901 – it closed in 1958.[^cite_note-63] [Oslo](22309.md) had the first tramway in [Scandinavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scandinavia), starting operation on 2 March 1894.[^cite_note-64]

The first electric tramway in Australia was a Sprague system demonstrated at the 1888 [Melbourne Centennial Exhibition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melbourne_Centennial_Exhibition) in [Melbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Melbourne); afterwards, this was installed as a commercial venture operating between the outer Melbourne suburb of [Box Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Box_Hill,_Victoria) and the then tourist-oriented country town [Doncaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doncaster,_Victoria) from 1889 to 1896.[^cite_note-65] Electric systems were also built in [Adelaide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Adelaide), [Ballarat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Ballarat), [Bendigo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Bendigo), [Brisbane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Brisbane), [Fremantle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Fremantle), [Geelong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Geelong), [Hobart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Hobart), [Kalgoorlie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Kalgoorlie), [Launceston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Launceston), [Leonora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonora,_Western_Australia), [Newcastle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Newcastle,_New_South_Wales), [Perth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Perth), and [Sydney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Sydney).

By the 1970s, the only full tramway system remaining in Australia was the Melbourne tram system. The [Southern California Railway Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_California_Railway_Museum) in [Perris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perris,_California) has the largest collection of [Pacific Electric Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Electric_Railway) streetcars (known as the Red Cars).

Two rare but significant alternatives were [conduit current collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conduit_current_collection), which was widely used in London, Washington, D.C., and New York City, and the [surface contact collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stud_contact_system) method, used in [Wolverhampton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolverhampton) (the Lorain system), [Torquay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torquay_Tramways) and [Hastings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hastings) in the UK (the Dolter stud system), and in [Bordeaux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tramway_de_Bordeaux), France (the [ground-level power supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground-level_power_supply) system).&#x20;

The convenience and economy of electricity resulted in its rapid adoption once the technical problems of production and transmission of electricity were solved. Electric trams largely replaced animal power and other forms of motive power including cable and steam, in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.



Tram line 3 [Socimi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socimi) in front of [Colosseum](49603.md) in [Rome](25458.md).

There was one particular hazard associated with trams powered from a trolley pole off an overhead line on the early electrified systems. Since the tram relies on contact with the rails for the current return path, a problem arises if the tram is derailed or (more usually) if it halts on a section of track that has been heavily sanded by a previous tram, and the tram loses electrical contact with the rails. In this event, the underframe of the tram, by virtue of a circuit path through ancillary loads (such as interior lighting), is live at the full supply voltage, typically 600 volts DC. In British terminology, such a tram was said to be 'grounded'—not to be confused with the US English use of the term, which means the exact opposite. Any person stepping off the tram and completing the earth return circuit with their body could receive a serious electric shock. If "grounded", the driver was required to jump off the tram (avoiding simultaneous contact with the tram and the ground) and pull down the trolley pole, before allowing passengers off the tram. Unless derailed, the tram could usually be recovered by running water down the running rails from a point higher than the tram, the water providing a conducting bridge between the tram and the rails. With improved technology, this ceased to be a problem.

In the 2000s, several companies introduced catenary-free designs: Alstom's Citadis line uses a third rail, Bombardier's PRIMOVE LRV is charged by contactless induction plates embedded in the trackway and CAF URBOS tram uses ultracaps technology[^cite_note-74][^cite_note-75]

### Battery

Main article: [List of battery operated trams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_battery_operated_trams)



Recharging battery-powered trams of [Paris and Seine Tramway Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_and_Seine_Tramway_Company?action=edit\&redlink=1), late 1890s.

As early as 1834, [Thomas Davenport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Davenport_\(inventor\)), a Vermont blacksmith, had invented a battery-powered electric motor which he later patented. The following year he used it to operate a small model [electric car](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battery_electric_multiple_unit) on a short section of track four feet in diameter.[^cite_note-76][^cite_note-77]

Attempts to use [batteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead-acid_battery) as a source of electricity were made from the 1880s and 1890s, with unsuccessful trials conducted in among other places [Bendigo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Bendigo) and [Adelaide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Adelaide) in Australia. Starting in 1890 the *accutram* of [HTM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTM_Personenvervoer) in [The Hague](30269.md) in the Netherlands was used in regular passenger service for about 14 years. The first trams in Bendigo, Australia, in 1892, were battery-powered, but within as little as three months they were replaced with horse-drawn trams. In [New York City](645042.md) some minor lines also used storage batteries. Then, during the 1950s, a longer battery-operated tramway line ran from [Milan](36511.md) to [Bergamo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bergamo).

In China there is a [Nanjing battery Tram line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Nanjing) and has been running since 2014.[^cite_note-78] In 2019, the [West Midlands Metro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Midlands_Metro) in [Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham), England adopted battery-powered trams on sections through the city centre close to [Grade I listed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Listed_building) [Birmingham Town Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_Town_Hall). Since 2024, battery trams are running in Istanbul on the [tourist tramway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourist_tramway).[^cite_note-79] In the region of [Coventry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coventry), a [battery powered tram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coventry_Very_Light_Rail) is being tested since 2025.

### Compressed air

Paris and Berne (Switzerland) operated trams that were powered by [compressed air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pneumatics) using the [Mekarski system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mekarski_system).[^cite_note-80][^cite_note-81] Trials on street tramways in Britain, including by the [North Metropolitan Tramway Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_County_Council_Tramways#Acquisition_of_tramways) between Kings Cross and Holloway, London (1883), achieved acceptable results but were found not to be economic because of the combined coal consumption of the stationary compressor and the onboard steam boiler.[^cite_note-82][^cite_note-83]

### Hybrid system

The [Trieste–Opicina tramway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trieste%E2%80%93Opicina_tramway) in [Trieste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trieste) operates a hybrid funicular tramway system. Conventional electric trams are operated in [street running](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_running) and on [reserved track](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reserved_track) for most of their route. However, on one steep segment of track, they are assisted by cable tractors, which push the trams uphill and act as brakes for the downhill run. For safety, the cable tractors are always deployed on the downhill side of the tram vehicle.

Similar systems were used elsewhere in the past, notably on the [Queen Anne Counterbalance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Anne_Counterbalance) in Seattle and the [Darling Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darling_Street) wharf line in Sydney.

### Modern development



[Hyundai Rotem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyundai_Rotem)'s Hydrogen fuel cell tram train for [Daejeon Metro Line 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daejeon_Metro_Line_2)

In the mid-20th century many tram systems were disbanded, replaced by buses, [trolleybuses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trolleybus), automobiles or [rapid transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapid_transit). The [General Motors streetcar conspiracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Motors_streetcar_conspiracy) was a case study of the decline of trams in the United States. In the 21st century, trams have been re-introduced in cities where they had been closed down for decades (such as [Tramlink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tramlink) in London), or kept in heritage use (such as [Spårväg City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sp%C3%A5rv%C3%A4g_City) in Stockholm). Most trams made since the 1990s (such as the [Bombardier Flexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombardier_Flexity) series and [Alstom Citadis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alstom_Citadis)) are articulated [low-floor trams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-floor_tram) with features such as [regenerative braking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regenerative_braking).

In March 2015, [China South Rail Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CSR_Corporation_Limited) (CSR) demonstrated the world's first hydrogen [fuel cell vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_cell_vehicle) tramcar at an assembly facility in [Qingdao](250171.md). The chief engineer of the CSR subsidiary [CSR Sifang Co Ltd.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CSR_Sifang_Co_Ltd.), Liang Jianying, said that the company is studying how to reduce the running costs of the tram.[^cite_note-84][^cite_note-85]

In July 2024, 38 hydrogen fuel cell trams manufactured by [Hyundai Rotem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyundai_Rotem) were selected to operate on [Daejeon Metro Line 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daejeon_Metro_Line_2). The supply contract has already been signed, and delivery is scheduled to begin in the second half of 2026. The line is expected to enter service in 2028. It means hydrogen trams will run on a 38.8 km route.[^cite_note-86]

## Operation



A dedicated right of way lane in [Toronto](64646.md). The tram on the left is about to enter a track operating in mixed traffic.

There are two main types of tramways, the classic tramway built in the early 20th century with the tram system operating in mixed traffic, and the later type which is most often associated with the tram system having its own right of way. Tram systems that have their own right of way are often called [light rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_rail) but this does depends on the region. Though these two systems differ in their operation, their equipment is similar.

### Headway

A typical headway for a tram or light rail line is 5 minutes during peak hours to 15 minutes off-peak. The optimal headway for bus, tram and metro is 3 to 6 minutes. The lowest recorded interval is 1.5 minutes for the Hong Kong tramway. In the case where coupled articulated tram vehicles have a headway of 2 minutes, the capacity is about 10,000 passengers per hour per direction.[^cite_note-87]

### Controls

Trams were traditionally operated with separate levers for applying power and brakes. More modern vehicles use a [locomotive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locomotive)-style controller which incorporate a [dead man's switch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_man's_switch). The success of the [PCC streetcar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PCC_streetcar) had also seen trams use automobile-style [foot controls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automobile_pedal) allowing hands-free operation, particularly when the driver was responsible for fare collection.

### Power supply

Main articles: [Railway electrification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railway_electrification), [Current collector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_collector), and [List of tram systems by gauge and electrification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tram_systems_by_gauge_and_electrification)



[Overhead lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overhead_line) are used for most tram and light rail systems.

Electric trams use various devices to collect power from [overhead lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overhead_line). The most common device is the [pantograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantograph_\(transport\)), while some older systems use [trolley poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trolley_pole) or [bow collectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bow_collector). [Ground-level power supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground-level_power_supply) has become a more recent innovation. Another technology uses [supercapacitors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercapacitor); when an [insulator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insulator_\(electricity\)) at a track switch cuts off power from the tram for a short distance along the line, the tram can use energy stored in a large [capacitor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capacitor) to drive the tram past the gap in the power feed.[^cite_note-88] The old tram systems in London, [Manhattan](45470.md) (New York City), and Washington, D.C., used live rails, like those on third-rail electrified railways, but in a conduit underneath the road, from which they drew power through a [plough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plough). It was called [conduit current collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conduit_current_collection). Washington's was the last of these to close, in 1962. No commercial tramway uses this system anymore. More recently, an equivalent to these systems has been developed which allows for the safe installation of a [third rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_rail) on city streets, known as surface current collection or ground-level power supply; the main example of this is the new tramway in [Bordeaux](4097.md).

#### Ground-level power supply

Main article: [Ground-level power supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground-level_power_supply)



A section of [APS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground-level_power_supply) track in [Bordeaux](4097.md) with powered and neutral sections.

A ground-level power supply system, also called surface current collection or *alimentation par le sol* (APS), is an updated version of the original stud type system. APS uses a third rail placed between the running rails, divided electrically into eight-metre powered segments with three-metre neutral sections between. Each tram has two power collection skates, next to which are antennas that send radio signals to energize the power rail segments as the tram passes over them.

Older systems required mechanical switching systems which were susceptible to environmental problems. At any one time no more than two consecutive segments under the tram should be live. Wireless and solid state switching eliminate mechanical problems.

[Alstom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alstom) developed the system primarily to avoid intrusive power supply cables in the sensitive area of the old city of old [Bordeaux](4097.md).[^cite_note-89]

### Routes



A map of the tram routes of Paris.

Route patterns vary greatly among the world's tram systems, leading to different [network topologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_topology).&#x20;

- Most systems start by building up a strongly nucleated radial pattern of routes linking the city centre with residential suburbs and traffic hubs such as railway stations and hospitals, usually following main roads. Some of these, such as those in [Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong_Tramways), [Blackpool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackpool_tramway) and [Bergen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bergen_Light_Rail), still essentially comprise a single route. Some suburbs may be served by loop lines connecting two adjacent radial roads. Some modern systems have started by reusing existing radial railway tracks, as in [Nottingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nottingham_Express_Transit) and [Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Midlands_Metro), sometimes [joining them together](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-city_route) by a section of street track through the city centre, as in [Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Metrolink). Later developments often include tangential routes linking adjacent suburbs directly, or multiple routes through the town centre to avoid congestion (as in Manchester's [Second City Crossing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Metrolink#Phase_2CC_%E2%80%93_Second_City_Crossing)).

- Other new systems, particularly those in large cities which already have well-developed metro and suburban railway systems, such as [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tramlink) and [Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tramways_in_%C3%8Ele-de-France), have started by building isolated suburban lines feeding into railway or metro stations. In Paris these have then been linked by [ring lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_route).

- A third, weakly nucleated, route pattern may grow up where a number of nearby small settlements are linked, such as in the coal-mining areas served by [BOGESTRA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BOGESTRA) or the [Silesian Interurbans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silesian_Interurbans).

- A fourth starting point may be a loop in the city centre, sometimes called a [downtown circulator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downtown_circulator), as in [Portland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portland_Streetcar) or [El Paso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Paso_Streetcar).

- Occasionally a modern tramway system may grow from a preserved heritage line, as in [Stockholm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Stockholm).

The resulting route patterns are very different. Some have a rational structure, covering their catchment area as efficiently as possible, with new suburbs being planned with tramlines integral to their layout – such is the case in [Amsterdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Amsterdam). [Bordeaux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bordeaux_Tramway) and [Montpellier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montpellier_tramway) have built comprehensive networks, based on radial routes with numerous interconnections, within the last two decades. Some systems serve only parts of their cities, with [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Berlin) being the prime example, as trams survived the city's political division only in the Eastern part. Other systems have ended up with a rather random route map, for instance when some previous operating companies have ceased operation (as with the *tramways vicinaux/buurtspoorwegen* in [Brussels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Brussels)) or where isolated outlying lines have been preserved (as on the eastern fringe of Berlin). In [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Rome), the remnant of the system comprises three isolated radial routes, not connecting in the ancient city centre, but linked by a ring route. Some apparently anomalous lines continue in operation where a new line would not on rational grounds be built, because it is much more costly to build a new line than to continue operating an existing one.

In some places, the opportunity is taken when roads are being repaved to lay tramlines (though without erecting overhead cables) even though no service is immediately planned: such is the case in [Leipzigerstraße](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipziger_Stra%C3%9Fe) in Berlin, the Haarlemmer Houttuinen in Amsterdam, and Botermarkt in Ghent.

#### Cross-border routes

Tram systems operate across national borders in [Basel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Basel#Cross-border_routes) (from Switzerland into France and Germany), [Geneva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Geneva) (from Switzerland into France) and [Strasbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strasbourg_tramway#Future_extensions) (from France into Germany).

In 2012, plans were made to connect the Polish town of [Słubice](656704.md) to the tram network of [Frankfurt an der Oder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Frankfurt_\(Oder\)). These plans were cancelled when voters in Frankfurt voted down funding for the project and replaced the tram line with a bus.[^cite_note-90] Another cross-border tramway that was planned [linking Hasselt (Belgium) with Maastricht (Netherlands)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasselt_%E2%80%93_Maastricht_tramway) was cancelled in June 2022.

## Track



[Cross section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_profile#Grooved_rail) of a grooved tram rail

Tramway track can have different [rail profiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_profile) to accommodate the various operating environments of the vehicle. They may be embedded into [concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concrete) for street-running operation, or use standard [ballasted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Track_ballast) track with [railroad ties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railroad_tie) on high-speed sections. A more ecological solution is to embed tracks into [grass turf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sod), an approach known as [green track](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_track).

Tramway tracks use a [grooved rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grooved_rail) with a [groove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groove_\(engineering\)) designed for tramway or railway track in [pavement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Road_surface) or grassed surfaces, also called grassed track or track in a lawn. The rail has the railhead on one side and the guard on the other. The guard provides accommodation for the flange. The guard carries no weight, but may act as a checkrail. Grooved rail was invented in 1852 by [Alphonse Loubat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphonse_Loubat), a French inventor who developed improvements in tram and rail equipment, and helped develop tram lines in New York City and Paris. The invention of grooved rail enabled tramways to be laid without causing a nuisance to other road users, except unsuspecting cyclists, who could get their wheels caught in the groove. The grooves may become filled with gravel and dirt (particularly if infrequently used or after a period of idleness) and need clearing from time to time, this being done by a "scrubber" tram. Failure to clear the grooves can lead to a bumpy ride for the passengers, damage to either wheel or rail and possibly derailing.

In narrow situations double-track tram lines sometimes reduce to single track, or, to avoid [switches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railroad_switch), have the tracks interlaced.



A 3-way point-setting system in [Amsterdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amsterdam_Centraal_station). The indicator on the right displays "Lijn 24, Spoor 4". The points are set to automatically send the next tram (route 24 to *De Boelelaan/VU*) to platform 4 on the left.

### Switches

On many tram systems where tracks diverge, the driver chooses the route, usually either by flicking a switch on the dashboard or by use of the power pedal – generally if power is applied the tram goes straight on, whereas if no power is applied the tram turns. Some systems use automatic point-setting systems, where the route for each journey is downloaded from a central computer, and an onboard computer actuates each point as it comes to it via an [induction loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Induction_loop). Such is the case at [Manchester Metrolink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Metrolink).[^cite_note-91] If the powered system breaks down, most points may be operated manually, by inserting a metal lever ('point iron') into the [point machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_machine).

### Track gauge

Main article: [List of tram systems by gauge and electrification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tram_systems_by_gauge_and_electrification)

Historically, the [track gauge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Track_gauge) has had considerable variations, with [narrow gauge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narrow_gauge_railways) common in many early systems. However, most light rail systems are now [standard gauge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_gauge). An important advantage of standard gauge is that standard railway maintenance equipment can be used on it, rather than custom-built machinery. Using standard gauge also allows light rail vehicles to be delivered and relocated conveniently using freight railways and locomotives.

Another factor favoring standard gauge is that low-floor vehicles are becoming popular, and there is generally insufficient space for wheelchairs to move between the wheels in a narrow gauge layout. Standard gauge also enables – at least in theory – a larger choice of manufacturers and thus lower procurement costs for new vehicles. However, other factors such as electrification or [loading gauge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loading_gauge) for which there is more variation may require costly custom-built units regardless.





Tram stops can range from purpose-built, tram-exclusive facilities (left), to simple stops within a public road (right).

### Tram stop

Main article: [Tram stop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tram_stop)

Tram stops may be similar to [bus stops](240496.md) in design and use, particularly in street-running sections, where in some cases other vehicles are legally required to stop clear of the tram doors. Some stops may have [railway platforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railway_platform), particularly in private right-of-way sections and where trams are boarded at standard [railway platform height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railway_platform_height), as opposed to using steps at the doorway or [low-floor trams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-floor_tram).

### Advantages



Produced in 1923–24, 900 Series trams are still use in [New Orleans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streetcars_in_New_Orleans). Trams typically have longer service life than internal combustion buses.



A tram running down [tracks embedded in grass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_track) in [Nantes](67453.md).



[Tramway system in Messina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Messina), opened in 2003

- Trams (and road public transport in general) can be much more efficient in terms of road usage than cars – one vehicle replaces about 40 cars (which take up a far larger area of road space).

  [^cite_note-94]

  [^cite_note-95]

- Vehicles run more [efficiently](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_efficiency_in_transportation) compared to similar vehicles that use rubber tyres, since the [rolling resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolling_resistance) of steel on steel is lower than rubber on asphalt.

  [^cite_note-96]

- Trams and light rail transit use sustainable technologies like electric propulsion and support limiting [urban sprawl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_sprawl) which in return lowers the carbon footprint.

  [^cite_note-97]

- There is a well studied effect that the installation of a tram service – even if service frequency, speed and price all remain constant – leads to higher ridership and mode shift away from cars compared to buses.

  [^cite_note-98]

  &#x20;Conversely, the abandonment of tram service leads to measurable declines in ridership.

- Being guided by rails means that even very long tram units can navigate tight, winding city streets that are inaccessible to long buses.

- Tram vehicles are very durable, with some being in continuous revenue service for more than fifty years. This is especially true compared to internal combustion buses, which tend to require high amounts of maintenance and break down after less than 20 years, mostly due to the vibrations of the engine.

- In many cases tram networks have a higher capacity than similar buses. This has been cited as a reason for the replacement of one of Europe's busiest bus lines (with three-minute headways in peak times) with a tram by [Dresdner Verkehrsbetriebe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dresdner_Verkehrsbetriebe).

- Due to the above-mentioned capacity advantage, labor costs (which form the biggest share of operating costs of many public transit systems) per passenger can be significantly lower compared to buses.

- Trams and light rail systems can be cheaper to install than subways or other forms of [heavy rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_rail). In Berlin the commonly cited figure is that one kilometer of subway costs as much as ten kilometers of tramway.

- Tramways can take advantage of old [heavy rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_rail) alignments. Some examples include the [Manchester Metrolink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Metrolink) of which the [Bury Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bury_Line) was part of the [East Lancashire Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Lancashire_Railway), the [Altrincham Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altrincham_Line) was part of the [Manchester South Junction and Altrincham Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_South_Junction_and_Altrincham_Railway), and the [Oldham and Rochdale Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oldham_and_Rochdale_Line) was the [Oldham Loop Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oldham_Loop_Line). Other examples can be found in [Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Tramway_Line_2), [London](17867.md), [Boston](24437894.md), [Melbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Melbourne) and [Sydney](27862.md). They hence sometimes take advantage of high speed track while on train tracks.

- As tram lines are permanent this allows local authorities to redevelop and revitalise their towns and cities provided suitable planning changes are made.

  [^cite_note-99]

  &#x20;Melbourne will allow higher buildings (5 to 6 story) along tram routes leaving the existing suburbs behind unchanged whilst doubling the cities density.

  [^cite_note-100]

- Trams produce less air pollution than rubber tyred transport which produce tyre, asphalt and brake based pollutants. The use of regenerative electric motor braking in trams lowers mechanical brake use. Steel wheel and rail particulates are produced but regular wheel alignment and flexible track mounting can reduce emissions.

- Tram networks can link to other operational heavy rail and rapid transit systems, allowing vehicles to move directly from one to the other without passengers needing to alight. Trams that are compatible with heavy rail systems are called [tram-trains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tram-trains), while those that can use subway tunnels are called [pre-metro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-metro) or [U-Stadtbahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stadtbahn).

- Trams can integrate more effectively with pedestrian heavy environments than other forms of transport due to compactness and predictable movement. Passengers can reach surface stations quicker than underground stations. Subjective safety at surface stations is often seen to be higher.

- Trams can be tourist attractions in ways buses usually are not.

- Many modern tram systems plant low growing vegetation – mostly grasses – between the tracks which has a psychological effect on perceived noise levels and the benefits of [greenspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_open_space). This is not possible for buses as they deviate too much from an "ideal" track in daily operations.

### Disadvantages



Tram tracks pose a hazard for cyclists, as their wheels may get caught in the track.

- Installing rails for tram tracks and overhead lines for power means a higher up-front cost than using buses, which require no modifications to streets to begin operations.

- Tram tracks can be hazardous for cyclists, as bikes, particularly those with narrow tyres, may get their wheels caught in the track grooves.

  [^cite_note-bv.com.au-101]

  &#x20;It is possible to close the grooves of the tracks on critical sections by rubber profiles that are pressed down by the wheelflanges of the passing tram but that cannot be lowered by the weight of a cyclist.

  [^cite_note-102]

  &#x20;If not well-maintained, however, these lose their effectiveness over time.

- When wet, tram tracks tend to become slippery and thus dangerous for bicycles and motorcycles, especially in traffic.

  [^cite_note-bv.com.au-101]

  [^cite_note-103]

  &#x20;In some cases, even cars can be affected.

  [^cite_note-104]

- The opening of new tram and light rail systems has sometimes been accompanied by a marked increase in car accidents, as a result of drivers' unfamiliarity with the physics and [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Track_geometry) of trams.

  [^cite_note-105]

  &#x20;Though such increases may be temporary, long-term conflicts between motorists and light rail operations can be alleviated by segregating their respective rights-of-way and installing appropriate signage and warning systems.

  [^cite_note-106]

- Rail transport can expose neighbouring populations to moderate levels of low-frequency noise. However, transportation planners use [noise mitigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noise_mitigation) strategies to minimise these effects.

  [^cite_note-107]

  &#x20;Most of all, the potential for decreased private motor vehicle operations along the tram's service line because of the service provision could result in lower [ambient noise levels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambient_noise_level) than without.

- The overhead power lines and supporting poles utilized by trams (except for those using a third rail) can be unsightly and contribute to [visual pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_pollution).

  [^cite_note-Shodorf_1973-108]

Trams are in a period of growth, with about 400 tram systems operating around the world, several new systems being opened each year, and many being gradually extended.[^cite_note-109] Some of these systems date from the late 19th or early 20th centuries. In the past 20 years their numbers have been augmented by modern tramway or light rail systems in cities that had abandoned this form of transport. There have also been some new tram systems in cities that never previously had them.

*Tramways with trams* ([British English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_English)) or *street railways with streetcars* ([North American English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_English)) were common throughout the industrialised world in the late 19th and early 20th centuries but they had disappeared from most British, Canadian, French and US cities by the mid-20th century.[^cite_note-110] After World War II most Australian cities also began to replace their trams with buses, but [Melbourne](17306237.md) defied the trend, opening new tram lines even in the mid-1950s. By the 1970s Melbourne was the only Australian city with a major tram network.[^cite_note-111]

By contrast, [trams in parts of continental Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Europe) continued to be used by many cities, although there were declines in some countries, including the Netherlands.[^cite_note-112]

Since 1980 trams have returned to favour in many places, partly because their tendency to dominate the roadway, formerly seen as a disadvantage, is considered to be a merit since it raises the visibility of public transport (encouraging car users to change their mode of travel), and enables streets to be reconfigured to give more space to pedestrians, making cites more pleasant places to live. New systems have been built in the United States, United Kingdom, Ireland, Italy, France, Australia and many other countries.

#### Historical



At its peak, the [Paris tram system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tramways_in_%C3%8Ele-de-France) was the world's largest, with over 1,111 kilometres (690 mi) of track in 1925.

Historically, the [Paris Tram System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tramways_in_%C3%8Ele-de-France) was, at its peak, the world's largest system, with 1,111 km (690 mi) of track in 1925[^cite_note-141] (according to other sources, c. 640 km (400 mi) of route length in 1930). However it was completely closed in 1938.[^cite_note-142] The next largest system appears to have been 857 km (533 mi), in [Buenos Aires](#South_America) before 19 February 1963. The third largest was Chicago, with over 850 km (530 mi) of track,[^cite_note-143] but it was all converted to [trolleybus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trolleybus) and bus services by 21 June 1958. Before its decline, the [BVG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berliner_Verkehrsbetriebe) in Berlin operated a very large network with 634 km (394 mi) of route. Before its system was converted to trolleybus (and later bus) services in the 1930s (last tramway closed 6 July 1952), the first-generation London network had 555 km (345 mi) of route in 1931.[^cite_note-144] In 1958 trams in Rio de Janeiro were employed on (433 km; 269 mi) of track. The final line, the [Santa Teresa route](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Teresa_Tram) was closed in 1968.[^cite_note-145] During a period in the 1980s, the world's largest tram system was in [Leningrad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Saint_Petersburg) (St. Petersburg) with 350 km (220 mi), USSR, and was included as such in the [Guinness World Records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinness_World_Records); however Saint Petersburg's tram system has declined in size since the fall of the Soviet Union. [Vienna](55866.md) in 1960 had 340 km (211 mi), before the expansion of bus services and the opening of a subway (1976). Substituting subway services for tram routes continues. 320 km (199 mi) was in [Minneapolis–Saint Paul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minneapolis%E2%80%93Saint_Paul) in 1947: There streetcars ended 31 October 1953 in Minneapolis and 19 June 1954 in St. Paul.[^cite_note-146] The [Sydney tram network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Sydney), before it was closed on 25 February 1961, had 291 km (181 mi) of route, and was thus the largest in Australia. Since 1961, the Melbourne system (recognised as the world's largest) has assumed Sydney's title as the largest network in Australia.

## Tram modelling

See also: [Rail transport modelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_transport_modelling)



A model of a town with a [tram model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_transport_modelling) built into it

Model trams are popular in [HO scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HO_scale) (1\:87) and [O scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O_scale) (1\:48 in the US and generally 1\:43.5 and 1\:45 in Europe and Asia). They are typically powered and will accept plastic figures inside. Common manufacturers are [Roco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roco_\(model_railroads\)) and [Lima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lima_\(models\)), with many custom models being made as well. The German firm Hödl[^cite_note-147] and the Austrian Halling[^cite_note-148] specialise in 1\:87 scale.[^cite_note-149]

In the US, [Bachmann Industries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachmann_Industries) is a mass supplier of HO streetcars and kits. [Bowser Manufacturing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowser_Manufacturing) has produced white metal models for over 50 years.[^cite_note-150] There are many boutique vendors offering limited run epoxy and wood models. At the high end are highly detailed brass models which are usually imported from Japan or Korea and can cost in excess of \$500. Many of these run on 16.5 mm (0.65 in) gauge track, which is correct for the representation of 4 ft 8+1⁄2 in (1,435 mm) (standard gauge) in HO scale as in US and Japan, but incorrect in 4 mm (1\:76.2) scale, as it represents 4 ft 8+1⁄2 in (1,435 mm). This scale/gauge hybrid is called OO scale. O scale trams are also very popular among tram modellers because the increased size allows for more detail and easier crafting of overhead wiring. In the US these models are usually purchased in epoxy or wood kits and some as brass models. The Saint Petersburg Tram Company[^cite_note-151] produces highly detailed polyurethane non-powered O Scale models from around the world which can easily be powered by trucks from vendors like Q-Car.[^cite_note-152]

## In popular culture

- *[A Streetcar Named Desire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Streetcar_Named_Desire_\(play\))* was written by [Tennessee Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tennessee_Williams) in 1947.

- The [Rev W. Awdry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._V._Awdry) wrote about [GER Class C53](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GER_Class_C53) called *[Toby the Tram Engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_The_Railway_Series_characters)*, which starred in his *[The Railway Series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Railway_Series)* with his faithful coach, Henrietta.

- "[The Trolley Song](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Trolley_Song)" in the film *[Meet Me in St. Louis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meet_Me_in_St._Louis)* received an Academy Award nomination.

- Trams feature in the opening titles of the world's longest running TV soap opera [Coronation Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronation_Street), set in a fictional suburb of [Greater Manchester](79819.md), and produced by [Granada Television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granada_Television). A [Blackpool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackpool) tram killed one of the main characters in 1989 and the most recent faked accident involved a tram (modelled on the [Manchester Metrolink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Metrolink)) careering off a viaduct into the set in 2009.

- A model electric trolley prominently featured in the American children's television program *[Mister Rogers' Neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mister_Rogers'_Neighborhood)* as a way to transition viewers between different segments of the show.

- The 1986 Australian film *[Malcolm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malcolm_\(film\))* is centred on an autistic tram enthusiast who builds his own tram and becomes involved with a pair of bank robbers.

- *[Toonerville Folks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toonerville_Folks)* comic strip (1908–55) by [Fontaine Fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fontaine_Fox) featured the "Toonerville Trolley that met all the trains".

- The Swiss author [Robert Walser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Walser) wrote prose pieces about the electric tram: "In the Electric Tram" (1908), which is included in the 2012 English-language collection *[Berlin Stories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Stories_\(Walser_short_story_collection\))*, and "In the Tram" (1921), included in the 2016 English-language collection *[Girlfriends, Ghosts, and Other Stories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girlfriends,_Ghosts,_and_Other_Stories).*

- Pedestrians in the [New York City](645042.md) borough of [Brooklyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooklyn) were nicknamed "trolley dodgers" because they had to avoid being hit by the borough's fast-moving trolleys while crossing their lines. This nickname was used by the borough's [Major League Baseball](38776.md) team, the Brooklyn Trolley Dodgers (later the [Brooklyn Dodgers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooklyn_Dodgers), now the [Los Angeles Dodgers](18213.md)).

- The *[Red Car Trolley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Car_Trolley)* is a transportation attraction at [Disney California Adventure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disney_California_Adventure) at the [Disneyland Resort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disneyland_Resort) in [Anaheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaheim,_California), [California](5407.md).

- The [trolley problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trolley_problem) is a [thought experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thought_experiment) revolving around choosing whether or not to divert a runaway trolley towards one person to save a larger group on the initial track.

- Since 2012 the [World Tramdriver Championship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Tramdriver_Championship) (Tram-WΜ; formerly, the European Tram Driver Championships (Tram EM)) have taken place annually in various cities and has also been streamed live for multiple years.
