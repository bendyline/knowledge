Traditional rank amongst European [imperiality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor), [royalty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarch), [peers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peerage), and [nobility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobility) is rooted in [Late Antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Antiquity) and the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages). Although they vary over time and among [geographic regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographic_region) (for example, one region's prince might be equal to another's [grand duke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_duke)), the following is a reasonably comprehensive list that provides information on both general ranks and specific differences. Distinction should be made between reigning (or formerly reigning) families and the nobility – the latter being a social class subject to and created by the former.

## Sovereign

Main articles: [Monarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarch) and [Sovereign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovereign)

- The word *monarch* is derived from the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) μονάρχης, *monárkhēs*, "sole ruler" (from μόνος, *mónos*, "single" or "sole", and ἄρχων, *árkhōn*, "[archon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archon)", "leader", "ruler", "chief", the word being the present participle of the verb ἄρχειν, *árkhein*, "to rule", "to lead", this from the noun ὰρχή, *arkhē*, "beginning", "authority", "principle") through the [Latinized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) form *monarcha*.

- The word *sovereign* is derived from the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) *super* ("above").

- *[Autocrat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocracy)* is derived from the Greek αὐτοκράτωρ: αὐτός ("self") and κρατείν ("to hold power"), and may be translated as "one who rules by himself."

Many titles listed may also be used by lesser nobles – non-sovereigns – depending on the historical period and state. The sovereign titles listed below are grouped together into categories roughly according to their degree of dignity; these being: imperial (Emperor/Empress, etc.), royal (King/Queen, Grand Duke, etc.), others (sovereign Prince, sovereign Duke, etc.), and religious.

### Imperial titles

- *[Emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor)* (in [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language)), *_Imperador_* (in [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_language)), *_Emperador_* (in [Spanish](26825.md)), *_Imperatore_* (in [Italian](14708.md)), *Împărat* (in [Romanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_language)), and *_Empereur_* (in [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language)), from the Latin *_[Imperator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperator)_*, was originally a military title.

  [^cite_note-1]

  &#x20;Soldiers would salute the leader of a victorious army as '*imperator*'. In English, the feminine form is *Empress* (the Latin is *imperatrix*). The realm of an emperor or empress is termed an *Empire*. Other words meaning Emperor include:&#x20;

  - *[Augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus_\(title\))*, a Roman honorific title which means "venerable" or "majestic", used by Roman Emperors from the beginning of the Empire onwards. The feminine form is [*Augusta*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Augustae).

  - *[Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caesar_\(title\))*, the appellation of [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire) emperors derived from the Roman [dictator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_dictator) [Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar), whose great-nephew and adopted son [Gaius Julius Caesar Augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaius_Julius_Caesar_Augustus) became the first emperor of Rome. Augustus' four successors were each made the adoptive son of his predecessor, and were therefore legally entitled to use "Caesar" as a constituent of their names; after [Nero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nero), however, the familial link of the [Julio-Claudian dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julio-Claudian_dynasty) was disrupted and use of the word *Caesar* continued as a title only.&#x20;

    - *[Kaiser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaiser)*, derived from Caesar, primarily used in Germanic countries. The feminine form in German is *Kaiserin.*

    - *[Tsar / Tzar / Csar / Czar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsar)*, derived as shortened variant of the Slavic pronunciation of Caesar (*tsyasar*), the feminine form is *[Tsaritsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsaritsa)*, primarily used in Bulgaria, and after that in Russia and other Slavic countries, although in English *Tsarina* was also sometimes used.

### Royal titles

- *[King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King)*, from the Germanic *\*kuningaz*, roughly meaning "son of the people." (See: [Germanic kingship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_kingship))

  [^cite_note-sov-21]

  &#x20;The realm of a king is termed a kingdom (sovereign kings are ranked above vassal kings). The female equivalent is *queen*, with a distinction between *[queen regnant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_regnant)* and *[queen consort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_consort)*, from the Germanic *\*kwoeniz*, or *\*kwenon*, "wife"; cognate of [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) γυνή, *gynē*, "woman"; from [PIE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-European_language) *\*gʷḗn*, "woman". Regardless of a ruler's sex, their realm is known as a *kingdom*.&#x20;

### Princely, ducal, and other sovereign titles

- *[Grand Vizier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_vizier)* was the title of the effective head of government ([prime-minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime-minister)) of many sovereign states in the [Muslim world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_world). The office of Grand Vizier was first held by officials in the later [Abbasid Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid_Caliphate). It was then held in the [Ottoman Empire](22278.md), the [Mughal Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire), the [Sokoto Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sokoto_Caliphate), the [Safavid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safavid_Empire) and [Morocco](19291.md). In the Ottoman Empire, the Grand Vizier held the imperial seal and could convene all other viziers (ministers) to attend to affairs of the state; the viziers in conference were called "Kubbealtı viziers" in reference to their meeting place, the Kubbealtı ('under the dome') in [Topkapı Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topkap%C4%B1_Palace). His offices were located at the [Sublime Porte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sublime_Porte). Today, the [Prime Minister of Pakistan](461896.md) is referred to in Urdu as Wazir-e-azam, which translates literally to Grand Vizier

## Other sovereigns, royalty, peers, and major nobility

Main articles: [Royal family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_family), [Peerage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peerage), [Nobility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobility), and [Imperial immediacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_immediacy)

Several ranks were widely used (for more than a thousand years in Europe alone) for both sovereign rulers and non-sovereigns. Additional knowledge about the territory and historic period is required to know whether the rank holder was a sovereign or non-sovereign. However, joint precedence among rank holders often greatly depended on whether a rank holder was sovereign, whether of the same rank or not. This situation was most widely exemplified by the [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire) (HRE) in Europe. Several of the following ranks were commonly both sovereign and non-sovereign within the HRE. Outside of the HRE, the most common sovereign rank of these below was that of Prince. Within the HRE, those holding the following ranks who were also sovereigns had (enjoyed) what was known as an *[immediate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_immediacy)* relationship with the Emperor. Those holding non-sovereign ranks held only a *mediate* relationship (meaning that the civil hierarchy upwards was mediated by one or more intermediaries between the rank holder and the Emperor).

### Titles

- *[Prince](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince)* (*Prinz* in German), junior members of a royal, grand ducal, ruling ducal or princely, or mediatised family. The title of *[Fürst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F%C3%BCrst)* was usually reserved, from the 19th century, for rulers of principalities—the smallest sovereign entities (e.g., Liechtenstein, Lippe, Schwarzburg, Waldeck-and-Pyrmont)—and for heads of high-ranking, noble but non-ruling families (Bismarck, Clary und Aldringen, Dietrichstein, Henckel von Donnersmarck, Kinsky, Paar, Pless, Thun und Hohenstein, etc.). Cadets of these latter families were generally not allowed to use *Prinz*, being accorded only the style of count (*[Graf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graf)*) or, occasionally, that of *Fürst* (Wrede, [Urach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Urach)) even though it was also a ruling title. Exceptional use of *Prinz* was permitted for some [morganatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morganatic_marriage) families (e.g., [Battenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battenberg_family), Montenuovo) and a few others (Carolath-Beuthen, Biron von Kurland). Prince is also used as the highest, non-royal title of nobility in instances such as that of [Prince Bernadotte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Bernadotte) where Swedish royal princes lost those titles due to unapproved marriages. ## Minor nobility, landed gentry, and other aristocracy

Main articles: [Aristocracy (class)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristocracy_\(class\)) and [Landed gentry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landed_gentry)

During the Middle Ages, in England, as in most of Europe, the [feudal system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feudal_system) was the dominant social and economic system. Under the feudal system, the monarch would grant land to the monarch's loyal subjects in exchange for the subject's loyalty and military service when called by the monarch. Besides grants of land, these subjects were usually given titles that implied nobility and rank, such as duke, earl, baron, etc., which were passed down through the holder's [male line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Male_line). Barons were the lowest rank of nobility and were granted small parcels of land. Earls were the next highest rank with larger land holdings. Dukes were the highest rank and held the largest holdings, known as duchies. The monarch was the ultimate authority and was able to grant and revoke titles.

An English peerage began to emerge as a separate entity from the feudal system in the 14th century. The peers held titles granted by the monarch, but did not necessarily hold any land or have any feudal obligations. The peerage was divided into five ranks; from highest to lowest: duke, marquess, earl, viscount, and baron.

The peerage system became more formalized over time. By the 18th century, peerages were no longer granted as a reward for military service, but instead were granted as a way to recognize social status and political influence.

Today, there are two types of peerages in England: hereditary and life peerages. Hereditary peerages are those that are passed down through the male line of the family. In contrast, life peerages are granted to an individual for their lifetime only, without any succession to heirs. Life peerages were relatively rare before 1958, and were held to entitle the bearer to sit in the [House of Lords](13658.md). Since the *[Life Peerages Act 1958](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_Peerages_Act_1958)*, nearly all new peerages are life baronies.

In addition to peerages, there are also a number of other titles in England, such as "knight" and "dame", granted by the monarch without hereditary succession.

### In German-speaking states

The actual rank of a title-holder in German-speaking states depended not only on the nominal rank of the title, but also the degree of [sovereignty](28791.md) exercised, the rank of the title-holder's [suzerain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzerain), and the length of time the family possessed its status within the nobility (*Uradel, Briefadel, altfürstliche, neufürstliche*, see: [German nobility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_nobility)). Thus, any reigning sovereign ranks higher than any [deposed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolished_monarchy) or [mediatized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Mediatisation) sovereign (e.g., the *[Fürst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F%C3%BCrst)* of [Waldeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waldeck_\(state\)), sovereign until 1918, was higher than the [Duke of Arenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duke_of_Arenberg), head of a mediatized family, although *[Herzog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herzog)* is nominally a higher title than *Fürst*). However, former holders of higher titles in extant monarchies retained their relative rank, i.e., a queen dowager of Belgium outranks the reigning Prince of Liechtenstein. Members of a formerly sovereign or mediatized house rank higher than the nobility. Among the nobility, those whose titles derive from the [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire) rank higher than the holder of an equivalent title granted by one of the German monarchs after 1806.

The constitution of the [Weimar Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weimar_Republic) in 1919 ceased to accord privileges to members of dynastic and noble families. Their titles henceforth became legal parts of the family name, and traditional forms of address (e.g., "Hoheit" or "[Durchlaucht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durchlaucht)") ceased to be accorded to them by governmental entities.[^cite_note-Art._109_WC-62] The last title was conferred on 12 November 1918 to [Kurt von Kleefeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurt_von_Kleefeld).[^cite_note-63]

In Austria, nobility titles may no longer be used since 1918.[^cite_note-aut-64]
