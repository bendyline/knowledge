A **solar flare** is a relatively intense, localized emission of [electromagnetic radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_radiation) in the [Sun's atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_atmosphere). Flares occur in [active regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_region) and are often, but not always, accompanied by [coronal mass ejections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronal_mass_ejection), [solar particle events](38180478.md), and other eruptive [solar phenomena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_phenomena).[^cite_note-1] The occurrence of solar flares varies with the 11-year [solar cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_cycle).[^cite_note-Pietrow2026-2]

Solar flares are thought to occur when stored [magnetic energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_energy) in the Sun's atmosphere accelerates [charged particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charged_particle) in the surrounding [plasma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_\(physics\)). This results in the emission of electromagnetic radiation across the [electromagnetic spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_spectrum). The typical time profile of these emissions features three identifiable phases: a *precursor phase*, an *impulsive phase* when particle acceleration dominates, a *gradual phase* in which hot plasma injected into the [corona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_corona) by the flare cools by a combination of radiation and conduction of energy back down to the lower atmosphere, and a currently unexplained *extreme ultraviolet (EUV) late phase* [^cite_note-3] that occurs in some flares.

The [extreme ultraviolet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_ultraviolet) and [X-ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray) radiation from solar flares is absorbed by the [daylight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daylight) side of Earth's upper atmosphere, in particular the [ionosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionosphere), and does not reach the surface. This absorption can temporarily increase the ionization of the ionosphere which may interfere with [short-wave radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short-wave_radio) communication.[^cite_note-4] The prediction of solar flares is an active area of research.

Flares also occur on other [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star), where the term *[stellar flare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_flare)* applies.[^cite_note-5]

## Physical description



An X3.2-class solar flare observed in different wavelengths. Clockwise from top left: 304, 335, 131, and 193 Å

Solar flares are eruptions of [electromagnetic radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_radiation) originating in the Sun's atmosphere.[^cite_note-NOAAflare-6] They affect all layers of the solar atmosphere ([photosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosphere#The_Sun), [chromosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromosphere), and [corona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_corona)).[^cite_note-woods09-7] The [plasma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_\(physics\)) medium is heated to >10<sup>7</sup> [kelvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelvin), while [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron), [protons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton), and heavier [ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion) are accelerated to near the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light).[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9] Flares emit electromagnetic radiation across the [electromagnetic spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_spectrum), from [radio waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_frequency) to [gamma rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_ray).[^cite_note-woods09-7]

Flares occur in [active regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_regions), often around [sunspots](27616.md), where intense [magnetic fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field) penetrate the [photosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosphere) to link the corona to the solar interior. Flares are [powered by](#Cause) the sudden (timescales of minutes to tens of minutes) release of magnetic energy stored in the corona. The same energy releases may also produce [coronal mass ejections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronal_mass_ejections) (CMEs), although the relationship between CMEs and flares is not well understood.[^cite_note-10]

Associated with solar flares are flare sprays.[^cite_note-Moromoto-11] They involve faster ejections of material than eruptive [prominences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_prominence),[^cite_note-12] and reach velocities of 20 to 2,000 kilometres per second.[^cite_note-13]

### Cause

Flares occur when accelerated charged particles, mainly electrons, interact with the [plasma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_\(physics\)) medium. Evidence suggests that the phenomenon of [magnetic reconnection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_reconnection) leads to this extreme acceleration of charged particles.[^cite_note-14] On the Sun, magnetic reconnection may happen on solar arcades—a type of [prominence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_prominence) consisting of a series of closely occurring [coronal loops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronal_loop) following magnetic lines of force.[^cite_note-15] These lines of force quickly reconnect into a lower arcade of loops leaving a helix of magnetic field unconnected to the rest of the arcade. The sudden release of energy in this reconnection is the origin of the particle acceleration. The unconnected magnetic helical field and the material that it contains may violently expand outwards forming a CME.[^cite_note-sciam1-16] This also explains why solar flares typically erupt from active regions on the Sun where magnetic fields are much stronger.

Although there is a general agreement on the source of a flare's energy, the mechanisms involved are not well understood. It is not clear how the [magnetic energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_energy) is transformed into the [kinetic energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_energy) of the particles, nor is it known how some particles can be accelerated to the GeV range (10<sup>9</sup> [electron volt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronvolt)) and beyond. There are also some inconsistencies regarding the total number of accelerated particles, which sometimes seems to be greater than the total number in the coronal loop.[^cite_note-17]

### Post-eruption loops and arcades

See also: [Coronal loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronal_loop)



A post-eruption arcade present after an X5.7-class solar flare during the [Bastille Day solar storm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bastille_Day_solar_storm)

[^cite_note-18]

After the eruption of a solar flare, **post-eruption loops** made of hot plasma begin to form across the neutral line separating regions of opposite magnetic polarity near the flare's source. These loops extend from the photosphere up into the corona and form along the neutral line at increasingly greater distances from the source as time progresses.[^cite_note-Livshits11-19] The existence of these hot loops is thought to be continued by prolonged heating present after the eruption and during the flare's decay stage.[^cite_note-20]

In sufficiently powerful flares, typically of [C-class](#Classification) or higher, the loops may combine to form an elongated arch-like structure known as a **post-eruption arcade**. These structures may last anywhere from multiple hours to multiple days after the initial flare.[^cite_note-Livshits11-19] In some cases, dark sunward-traveling [plasma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_\(physics\)) voids known as [supra-arcade downflows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supra-arcade_downflows) may form above these arcades.[^cite_note-21]

## Frequency



Solar flare occurrence and active region complexity as a function of solar cycle phase. Left panel shows simple (light) vs. complex (dark) magnetic regions peak at different phases. Right panel shows GOES flare class distribution (A–X), with more energetic flares concentrated around solar maximum. Data from solar cycles 23–25.

[^cite_note-Pietrow2026-2]

The frequency of occurrence of solar flares varies with the 11-year [solar cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_cycle). It can typically range from several per day during [solar maxima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_maximum) to less than one every week during [solar minima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_minimum). Additionally, more powerful flares are less frequent than weaker ones. For example, [X10-class](#Classification) (severe) flares occur on average about 2–3 times per cycle, whereas M-class flares occur on average about 1,200 times per cycle.[^cite_note-SWPCscales-22][^cite_note-Pietrow2026-2]

In 1984, [Erich Rieger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_Rieger) and coworkers discovered an approximately 154-day period in the occurrence of [gamma-ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma-ray) emitting solar flares at least since the [solar cycle 19](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_cycle_19).[^cite_note-23] The period has since been confirmed in most heliophysics data and the [interplanetary magnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interplanetary_magnetic_field) and is commonly known as the [*Rieger period*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_Rieger#Rieger_period). The period's [resonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resonance) [harmonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonics) also have been reported from most data types in the [heliosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliosphere).

The [frequency distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_distribution) of various flare phenomena can be characterized by [power-law distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power-law_distribution). For example, the peak fluxes of radio, extreme ultraviolet, and hard and soft [X-ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray) emissions; total energies; and flare durations (see [§ Duration](#Duration)) have been found to follow power-law distributions.[^cite_note-24][^cite_note-25][^cite_note-26][^cite_note-27]<sup>: 23–28 </sup> The flare frequency distribution follows approximately a power-law relationship with a consistent index of α ≈ 2.0, with more complex active regions producing a disproportionate share of energetic flares despite comprising only a small fraction of all sunspots.[^cite_note-Pietrow2026-2]

The modern classification system for solar flares uses the letters A, B, C, M, or X,[^cite_note-xclass-28][^cite_note-29] according to the peak [flux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flux) in watts per square metre (W/m<sup>2</sup>) of [soft X-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soft_X-ray) with [wavelengths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelength) 0.1 to 0.8 [nanometres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanometre) (1 to 8 [ångströms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%85ngstr%C3%B6m)), as measured by [GOES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GOES) satellites in [geosynchronous orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geosynchronous_orbit).[^cite_note-Baker1970-30]

| Classification | Peak flux range (W/m<sup>2</sup>) |
| --- | --- |
| A | < 10<sup>−7</sup> |
| B | 10<sup>−7</sup> – 10<sup>−6</sup> |
| C | 10<sup>−6</sup> – 10<sup>−5</sup> |
| M | 10<sup>−5</sup> – 10<sup>−4</sup> |
| X | > 10<sup>−4</sup> |

The strength of an event within a class is noted by a numerical suffix ranging from 1 to 9. Hence, an X2 flare is twice the strength of an X1 flare, an X3 flare is three times as powerful as an X1. M-class flares are a tenth the size of X-class flares with the same numeric suffix.[^cite_note-31] An X2 is four times more powerful than an M5 flare.[^cite_note-32] X-class flares with a peak flux that exceeds 10<sup>−3</sup> W/m<sup>2</sup> may be noted with a numerical suffix equal to or greater than 10.[^cite_note-xclass-28]

This system was originally devised in 1970 by Baker[^cite_note-Baker1970-30] and included only the letters C, M, and X. These letters were chosen to avoid confusion with other optical classification systems. The A and B classes were added in the 1990s as instruments became more sensitive to weaker flares. Around the same time, the [backronym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backronym) *moderate* for M-class flares and *extreme* for X-class flares began to be used.[^cite_note-pietrow22-33]

### Importance

An earlier classification system, sometimes referred to as the *flare importance*, was based on [H-alpha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H-alpha) spectral observations. The scheme uses both the intensity and emitting surface. The classification in intensity is qualitative, referring to the flares as: faint (f), normal (n), or brilliant (b). The emitting surface is measured in terms of millionths of the hemisphere and is described below (the total hemisphere area *A<sub>H</sub>* = 15.5 × 10<sup>12</sup> km<sup>2</sup>).

| Classification | Corrected area (millionths of hemisphere) |
| --- | --- |
| S | < 100 |
| 1 | 100–250 |
| 2 | 250–600 |
| 3 | 600–1200 |
| 4 | > 1200 |

A flare is then classified taking S or a number that represents its size and a letter that represents its peak intensity, e.g., Sn is a normal sunflare.[^cite_note-34]

### Duration

A common measure of flare duration is the [full width at half maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_width_at_half_maximum) (FWHM) time of flux in the soft X-ray bands 0.05 to 0.4 and 0.1 to 0.8 nm measured by GOES. The FWHM time spans from when a flare's flux first reaches halfway between its maximum flux and the background flux and when it again reaches this value as the flare decays. Using this measure, the duration of a flare ranges from approximately tens of seconds to several hours with a median duration of approximately 6 and 11 minutes in the 0.05 to 0.4 and 0.1 to 0.8 nm bands, respectively.[^cite_note-35][^cite_note-36]

Flares can also be classified based on their duration as either **impulsive** or **long duration events** (**LDE**). The time threshold separating the two is not well defined. The SWPC regards events requiring 30 minutes or more to decay to half maximum as LDEs, whereas Belgium's Solar-Terrestrial Centre of Excellence regards events with duration greater than 60 minutes as LDEs.[^cite_note-37][^cite_note-38]

## Effects

See also: [Space weather § Effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_weather#Effects)

The electromagnetic radiation emitted during a solar flare propagates away from the Sun at the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light) with [intensity inversely proportional to the square of the distance from its source region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse-square_law). The excess [ionizing radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionizing_radiation), namely X-ray and extreme ultraviolet (XUV) radiation, is known to affect [planetary atmospheres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_atmosphere) and is of relevance to human space exploration and the search for [extraterrestrial life](9588.md).

Solar flares also affect other objects in the Solar System. Research into these effects has primarily focused on the [atmosphere of Mars](4548379.md) and, to a lesser extent, [that of Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_Venus).[^cite_note-yan22-39] The impacts on other planets in the Solar System are little studied in comparison. As of 2024, research on their effects on [Mercury](19694.md) have been limited to modeling of the response of ions in [the planet's magnetosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury's_magnetic_field),[^cite_note-40] and their impact on [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter) and [Saturn](44474.md) have only been studied in the context of X-ray radiation [back scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Back_scatter) off of the planets' upper atmospheres.[^cite_note-41][^cite_note-42]

### Ionosphere

Further information: [Sudden ionospheric disturbance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudden_ionospheric_disturbance)



Structure of Earth's nightside (left) and dayside (right) ionospheric sub-layers under normal conditions

Enhanced [XUV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XUV_\(radiation\)) irradiance during solar flares can result in increased [ionization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionization), [dissociation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissociation_\(chemistry\)), and [heating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heating) in the [ionospheres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionosphere) of Earth and Earth-like planets. On Earth, these changes to the upper atmosphere, collectively referred to as *sudden ionospheric disturbances*, can interfere with [short-wave radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short-wave_radio) communication and [global navigation satellite systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_navigation_satellite_system) (GNSS) such as [GPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPS),[^cite_note-mitra74-43] and subsequent expansion of the upper atmosphere can increase drag on satellites in [low Earth orbit](47568.md) leading to [orbital decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_decay) over time.[^cite_note-44][^cite_note-hayes21-45]

Flare-associated XUV photons interact with and ionize neutral constituents of planetary atmospheres via the process of [photoionization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photoionization). The electrons that are freed in this process, referred to as *photoelectrons* to distinguish them from the ambient ionospheric electrons, are left with kinetic energies equal to the photon energy in excess of the [ionization threshold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionization_energy). In the lower ionosphere where flare impacts are greatest and [transport phenomena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_phenomena) are less important, the newly liberated photoelectrons lose energy primarily via [thermalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermalization) with the ambient electrons and neutral species and via secondary ionization due to collisions with the latter, or so-called *photoelectron [impact ionization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_ionization)*. In the process of thermalization, photoelectrons transfer energy to neutral species, resulting in heating and expansion of the neutral atmosphere.[^cite_note-46] The greatest increases in [ionization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionization) occur in the lower ionosphere where wavelengths with the greatest relative increase in irradiance—the highly penetrative X-ray wavelengths—are absorbed, corresponding to Earth's E and D layers and Mars's M<sub>1</sub> layer.[^cite_note-yan22-39][^cite_note-mitra74-43][^cite_note-47][^cite_note-48][^cite_note-lollo12-49]

#### Radio blackouts

See also: [Communications blackout § Space weather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communications_blackout#Space_weather)

The temporary increase in ionization of the daylight side of Earth's atmosphere, in particular the D layer of the [ionosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionosphere), can interfere with short-wave radio communications that rely on its level of ionization for [skywave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skywave) propagation. Skywave, or skip, refers to the propagation of radio waves reflected or refracted off of the ionized ionosphere. When ionization is higher than normal, radio waves get degraded or completely absorbed by losing energy from the more frequent collisions with free electrons.[^cite_note-NOAAflare-6][^cite_note-mitra74-43]

The level of ionization of the atmosphere correlates with the strength of the associated solar flare in soft X-ray radiation. The [Space Weather Prediction Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Weather_Prediction_Center), a part of the United States [National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration](37876.md), classifies radio blackouts by the peak soft X-ray intensity of the associated flare.[^cite_note-NOAAflare-6]

| --- | --- | --- |
| R1 | M1 | Minor radio blackout |
| R2 | M5 | Moderate radio blackout |
| R3 | X1 | Strong radio blackout |
| R4 | X10 | Severe radio blackout |
| R5 | X20 | Extreme radio blackout |

#### Solar flare effect

See also: [Ionospheric dynamo region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionospheric_dynamo_region)



Electric currents in Earth's dayside ionosphere can be strengthened during a large solar flare.

During non-flaring or solar quiet conditions, [electric currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current) flow through the ionosphere's dayside E layer [inducing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_induction) small-amplitude diurnal variations in the [geomagnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geomagnetic_field). These ionospheric currents can be strengthened during large solar flares due to increases in [electrical conductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_conductivity) associated with enhanced ionization of the E and D layers. The subsequent increase in the induced geomagnetic field variation is referred to as a **solar flare effect** (**sfe**) or historically as a **magnetic crochet**. The latter term derives from the French word *[crochet](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/crochet#French)* meaning *hook* reflecting the hook-like disturbances in magnetic field strength observed by ground-based [magnetometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetometer). These disturbances are on the order of a few [nanoteslas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesla_\(unit\)) and last for a few minutes, which is relatively minor compared to those induced during [geomagnetic storms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geomagnetic_storm).[^cite_note-50][^cite_note-51]

### Health

#### Low Earth orbit

For astronauts in [low Earth orbit](47568.md), an expected radiation dose from the electromagnetic radiation emitted during a solar flare is about 0.05 [gray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gray_\(unit\)), which is not immediately lethal on its own. Of much more concern for astronauts is the [particle radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_radiation) associated with solar particle events.[^cite_note-52]

#### Mars

The impacts of solar flare radiation on Mars are relevant to [exploration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exploration_of_Mars) and the search for [life on the planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_on_Mars). Models of its atmosphere indicate that the most energetic solar flares previously recorded may have provided acute doses of radiation that would have been harmful or almost lethal to mammals and other [higher organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_organism) on Mars's surface. Furthermore, flares energetic enough to provide lethal doses, while not yet observed on the Sun, are thought to occur and have been observed on other [Sun-like stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun-like_star).[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55]

## Observational history

Further information: [Solar observation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_observation)

Flares produce radiation across the electromagnetic spectrum, although with different intensity. They are not intense in visible light, but they can be bright at particular [spectral lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_line). They normally produce [bremsstrahlung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bremsstrahlung) in X-rays and [synchrotron radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synchrotron_radiation) in radio.[^cite_note-56]

### Optical observations



[Richard Carrington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Christopher_Carrington)'s sketch of the first recorded solar flare (A and B mark the initial bright points which moved over the course of five minutes to C and D before disappearing.)

[^cite_note-Carrington1859-57]

Solar flares were first observed by [Richard Carrington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Christopher_Carrington) and [Richard Hodgson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Hodgson_\(publisher\)) independently on [1 September 1859](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_storm_of_1859) by projecting the image of the solar disk produced by an optical telescope through a broad-band filter.[^cite_note-\:0-58][^cite_note-59] It was an extraordinarily intense *white light flare*, a flare emitting a high amount of light in the [visual spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_spectrum).[^cite_note-\:0-58]

Since flares produce copious amounts of radiation at [H-alpha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H-alpha),[^cite_note-60] adding a narrow (≈1 Å) passband filter centered at this wavelength to the optical telescope allows the observation of not bright flares with small telescopes. For years Hα was the main, if not the only, source of information about solar flares. Other passband filters are also used.

### Radio observations

Further information: [Solar radio emission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_radio_emission)

During [World War II](32927.md), on February 25 and 26, 1942, British radar operators observed radiation that [Stanley Hey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Stanley_Hey) interpreted as solar emission.[^cite_note-61] Their discovery did not go public until the end of the conflict. The same year, [Southworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Clark_Southworth) also observed the Sun in radio, but as with Hey, his observations were only known after 1945. In 1943, [Grote Reber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grote_Reber) was the first to report radioastronomical observations of the Sun at 160 MHz.[^cite_note-62] The fast development of [radioastronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioastronomy) revealed new peculiarities of the solar activity like *storms* and *bursts* related to the flares. Today, ground-based radiotelescopes observe the Sun from c. 15 MHz up to 400 GHz.

### Space telescopes

Observations of a solar flare by different instruments aboard the [Solar Dynamics Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_Dynamics_Observatory)

Because the Earth's atmosphere [absorbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absorption_\(electromagnetic_radiation\)) much of the electromagnetic radiation emitted by the Sun with wavelengths shorter than 300 nm, space-based telescopes allowed for the observation of solar flares in previously unobserved high-energy spectral lines.[^cite_note-63] Since the 1970s, the GOES series of satellites[^cite_note-64] have been continuously observing the Sun in soft X-rays, and their observations have become the standard measure of flares, diminishing the importance of the H-alpha classification. Additionally, space-based telescopes[^cite_note-65] allow for the observation of extremely long wavelengths—as long as a few kilometres—which cannot propagate through the ionosphere.[^cite_note-66]

The most powerful flare ever observed is thought to be the flare associated with the 1859 [Carrington Event](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrington_Event).[^cite_note-68] While no soft X-ray measurements were made at the time, the magnetic crochet associated with the flare was recorded by ground-based magnetometers allowing the flare's strength to be estimated after the event. Using these magnetometer readings, its soft X-ray class has been estimated to be greater than X10[^cite_note-69] and around X45 (±5).[^cite_note-70][^cite_note-71]

In modern times, the largest solar flare measured with instruments occurred on [4 November 2003](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halloween_solar_storms,_2003). This event saturated the GOES detectors, and because of this, its classification is only approximate. Initially, extrapolating the GOES curve, it was estimated to be X28.[^cite_note-72] Later analysis of the ionospheric effects suggested increasing this estimate to X45.[^cite_note-73][^cite_note-74] This event produced the first clear evidence of a new spectral component above 100 GHz.[^cite_note-75]

## Prediction

Current methods of flare prediction are problematic, and there is no certain indication that an active region on the Sun will produce a flare. However, many properties of active regions and their sunspots correlate with flaring. For example, magnetically complex regions (based on line-of-sight magnetic field) referred to as [*delta spots*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_region#Mount_Wilson_magnetic_classification) frequently produce the largest flares. A simple scheme of sunspot classification based on the McIntosh system for sunspot groups, or related to a region's fractal complexity[^cite_note-76] is commonly used as a starting point for flare prediction.[^cite_note-77] Predictions are usually stated in terms of probabilities for occurrence of flares above M- or X-class within 24 or 48 hours. The U.S. [National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration](37876.md) (NOAA) issues forecasts of this kind.[^cite_note-78] MAG4 was developed at the University of Alabama in Huntsville with support from the Space Radiation Analysis Group at Johnson Space Flight Center (NASA/SRAG) for forecasting M- and X-class flares, CMEs, fast CME, and solar energetic particle events.[^cite_note-79] A physics-based method that can predict imminent large solar flares was proposed by the Institute for Space-Earth Environmental Research (ISEE), [Nagoya University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagoya_University), Japan.[^cite_note-80]
