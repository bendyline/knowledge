**Analytical chemistry** (or **chemical analysis**) is the branch of [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry) concerned with the development and application of methods to identify the chemical composition of materials and quantify the amounts of components in [mixtures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixtures). It focuses on methods to identify unknown compounds, possibly in a mixture or solution, and quantify a compound's presence in terms of [amount of substance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amount_of_substance) (in any phase), [concentration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentration) (in aqueous or [solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solution_\(chemistry\)) phase), percentage by mass or number of [moles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_\(unit\)) in a mixture of compounds (or partial pressure in the case of gas phase).

It encompasses both classical techniques (e.g. [titration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titration), [gravimetric analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravimetric_analysis)) and modern instrumental approaches (e.g. [spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectroscopy), [chromatography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatography), [mass spectrometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_spectrometry), [electrochemical methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemistry)). Modern analytical chemistry is deeply intertwined with [data analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_analysis) and [chemometrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemometrics), and is increasingly shaped by trends such as automation, [miniaturization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miniaturization), and real-time [sensing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensors), with applications across fields as diverse as [biochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemistry), [medicinal chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicinal_chemistry), [forensic science](45710.md), [archaeology](18951655.md), [nutritional science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutritional_science), [agricultural chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_chemistry), [chemical synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_synthesis), [metallurgy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metallurgy), [chemical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_engineering) and [materials science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materials_science).

In the age of "[big data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_data)", analytical chemistry, along with [chemometrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemometrics) and [bioinformatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioinformatics), has become central to interpreting complex results from high-throughput techniques like [gas chromatography-mass spectrometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_chromatography-mass_spectrometry) (GCMS), [high-performance liquid chromatography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-performance_liquid_chromatography), [inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductively_coupled_plasma_mass_spectrometry), and high-resolution [mass spectrometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_spectrometry).[^cite_note-1] There is also a strong trend towards [miniaturization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miniaturization), automation, and the development of real-time, point-of-care diagnostic sensors.[^cite_note-2]

## History



[Gustav Kirchhoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Kirchhoff) (left) and [Robert Bunsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Bunsen) (right)

Analytical chemistry has been important since the early days of chemistry, providing methods for determining which elements and chemicals are present in the object in question. During this period, significant contributions to analytical chemistry included the development of systematic [elemental analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elemental_analysis) by [Justus von Liebig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justus_von_Liebig) and systematized organic analysis based on the specific reactions of functional groups.[^cite_note-3]

The first instrumental analysis was [flame emissive spectrometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flame_emissive_spectrometry), developed by [Robert Bunsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Bunsen) and [Gustav Kirchhoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Kirchhoff), who discovered [rubidium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubidium) (Rb) and [caesium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caesium) (Cs) in 1860.[^cite_note-4]

Most of the major developments in analytical chemistry took place after 1900. During this period, instrumental analysis became progressively dominant in the field. In particular, many of the basic spectroscopic and spectrometric techniques were discovered in the early 20th century and refined in the late 20th century.[^cite_note-5]

The [separation sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separation_process) follow a similar timeline of development and have also became increasingly transformed into high-performance instruments.[^cite_note-6] In the 1970s many of these techniques began to be used together as hybrid techniques to achieve a complete characterization of samples.

Starting in the 1970s, analytical chemistry became progressively more inclusive of biological questions ([bioanalytical chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioanalytical_chemistry)), whereas it had previously been largely focused on inorganic or [small organic molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_molecule). Lasers have been increasingly used as probes and even to initiate and influence a wide variety of reactions. The late 20th century also saw an expansion of the application of analytical chemistry from somewhat academic chemical questions to [forensic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forensic_chemistry), [environmental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_chemistry), [industrial](58721.md) and [medical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinical_chemistry) questions, such as in [histology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Histology).[^cite_note-7]

Modern analytical chemistry is dominated by instrumental analysis. Many analytical chemists focus on a single type of instrument. Academics tend to either focus on new applications and discoveries or on new methods of analysis. The discovery of a chemical present in blood that increases the risk of cancer would be a discovery that an analytical chemist might be involved in. An effort to develop a new method might involve the use of a [tunable laser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunable_laser) to increase the specificity and sensitivity of a spectrometric method. Many methods, once developed, are kept purposely static so that data can be compared over long periods of time. This is particularly true in industrial [quality assurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quality_Assurance) (QA), forensic, and environmental applications. Analytical chemistry plays an increasingly important role in the pharmaceutical industry where, aside from QA, it is used in the discovery of new drug candidates and in clinical applications where understanding the interactions between the drug and the patient are critical.[^cite_note-8]

The 21st century has been defined by the digitalization of analytical chemistry. The handling of large datasets ("big data") from instruments like Orbitrap mass spectrometers has made advanced data analysis, including [machine learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_learning), an essential skill.[^cite_note-9] This era also focuses strongly on [sustainability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainability), leading to the [green chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_chemistry) subfield of Green Analytical Chemistry, which aims to minimize the environmental impact of chemical analyses.[^cite_note-10][^cite_note-11]

## Classical methods



The presence of [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper) in this qualitative analysis is indicated by the bluish-green color of the flame.

Although modern analytical chemistry is dominated by sophisticated instrumentation, the roots of analytical chemistry and some of the principles used in modern instruments are from traditional techniques, many of which are still used today. These techniques also tend to form the backbone of most undergraduate analytical chemistry educational labs.

### Qualitative analysis

#### Chemical tests

Further information: [Chemical test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_test)

There are numerous qualitative chemical tests; examples include the [acid test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_test_\(gold\)) for [gold](12240.md) and the [Kastle-Meyer test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kastle-Meyer_test) for the presence of [blood](3997.md).[^cite_note-12][^cite_note-13]

#### Flame test

Further information: [Flame test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flame_test)

Inorganic qualitative analysis generally refers to a systematic scheme to confirm the presence of certain aqueous ions or elements by performing a series of reactions that eliminate a range of possibilities and then confirm suspected ions with a confirming test. Sometimes small carbon-containing ions are included in such schemes. With modern instrumentation, these tests are rarely used but can be useful for educational purposes and in fieldwork or other situations where access to state-of-the-art instruments is not available or expedient.

### Quantitative analysis

Further information: [Quantitative analysis (chemistry)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantitative_analysis_\(chemistry\))

&#x20;Quantitative analysis is the measurement of the quantities of particular chemical constituents present in a substance. Quantities can be measured by mass (gravimetric analysis) or volume (volumetric analysis).[^cite_note-14]

#### Gravimetric analysis

Further information: [Gravimetric analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravimetric_analysis)

&#x20;Gravimetric analysis involves determining the amount of material present by weighing the sample before and/or after some transformation. A common example used in undergraduate education is the determination of the amount of water in a [hydrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrate) by heating the sample to remove the water such that the difference in weight is due to the loss of water.[^cite_note-15]

#### Volumetric analysis

Further information: [Titration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titration)

&#x20;Titration involves the gradual addition of a measurable reactant to an exact volume of a solution being analyzed until some [equivalence point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivalence_point) is reached. Titration is a family of techniques used to determine the concentration of an [analyte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analyte).[^cite_note-\:0-16] Titrating accurately to either the [half-equivalence point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-equivalence_point) or the endpoint of a titration allows the chemist to determine the amount of moles used, which can then be used to determine a concentration or composition of the titrant. Most familiar to those who have taken chemistry during secondary education is the acid-base titration involving a color-changing [pH indicator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH_indicator), such as [phenolphthalein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenolphthalein). There are many other types of titrations, including [potentiometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potentiometric) titrations and precipitation titrations. Chemists might also create [titration curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titration_curves) by systematically testing the pH after every added drop in order to understand different properties of the titrant.[^cite_note-\:0-16]

## Instrumental methods

Main article: [Instrumental analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instrumental_analysis)



Block diagram of an analytical instrument showing the stimulus and measurement of response

### Spectroscopy

Further information: [Spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectroscopy)

Spectroscopy measures the interaction of the molecules with [electromagnetic radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_spectrum). Spectroscopy consists of many different applications such as [time-resolved raman spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time-resolved_spectroscopy), [atomic absorption spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_absorption_spectroscopy), [atomic emission spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emission_spectroscopy), [ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraviolet-visible_spectroscopy), [X-ray spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_spectroscopy), [fluorescence spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorescence_spectroscopy), [infrared spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared_spectroscopy), [Raman spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raman_spectroscopy), [dual polarization interferometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_polarization_interferometry), [nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_magnetic_resonance_spectroscopy), [photoemission spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photoemission_spectroscopy), [Mössbauer spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%B6ssbauer_spectroscopy) and so on.

### Mass spectrometry

Further information: [Mass spectrometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_spectrometry)



An [accelerator mass spectrometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accelerator_mass_spectrometry) used for [radiocarbon dating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiocarbon_dating) and other analysis

Mass spectrometry measures [mass-to-charge ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass-to-charge_ratio) of molecules using [electric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_field) and [magnetic fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field). In a mass spectrometer, a small amount of sample is ionized and converted to gaseous ions, where they are separated and analyzed according to their [mass-to-charge ratios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass-to-charge_ratio).[^cite_note-\:0-16]

There are several ionization methods: [electron ionization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_ionization), [chemical ionization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_ionization), [electrospray ionization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrospray_ionization), fast atom bombardment, [matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix-assisted_laser_desorption/ionization), and others. Also, mass spectrometry is categorized by approaches of mass analyzers: [magnetic-sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic-sector), [quadrupole mass analyzer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrupole_mass_analyzer), [quadrupole ion trap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrupole_ion_trap), [time-of-flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time-of-flight_mass_spectrometry), [Fourier transform ion cyclotron resonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_transform_ion_cyclotron_resonance), and so on.[^cite_note-17]

### Electrochemical analysis

Further information: [Electroanalytical method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroanalytical_method)

Electroanalytical methods measure the [potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_potential) ([volts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volts)) and/or [current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current) ([amps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampere)) in an [electrochemical cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemical_cell) containing the analyte.[^cite_note-18] These methods can be categorized according to which aspects of the cell are controlled and which are measured. The four main categories are [potentiometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion_selective_electrode) (the difference in electrode potentials is measured), [coulometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulometry) (the transferred charge is measured over time), [amperometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amperometry) (the cell's current is measured over time), and [voltammetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltammetry) (the cell's current is measured while actively altering the cell's potential).[^cite_note-19][^cite_note-20][^cite_note-21]

### Thermal analysis

Further information: [Calorimetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calorimetry) and [Thermal analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_analysis)

Calorimetry and thermogravimetric analysis measure the interaction of a material and [heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat).[^cite_note-22][^cite_note-VyazovkinThermalReview-23]

### Separation



Separation of black ink on a [thin-layer chromatography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thin-layer_chromatography) plate

Further information: [Separation process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separation_process), [Chromatography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatography), and [Electrophoresis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrophoresis)

Separation processes are used to decrease the complexity of material mixtures. [Chromatography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatography), [electrophoresis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrophoresis) and [field flow fractionation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_Flow_Fractionation) are representative of this field.[^cite_note-24]

#### Chromatographic assays

Further information: [Chromatography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatography)

Chromatography can be used to determine the presence of substances in a sample, as different components in a mixture have different tendencies to adsorb onto the stationary phase or dissolve in the mobile phase. Thus, different components of the mixture move at different speeds. Different components of a mixture can therefore be identified by their respective [R<sub>*ƒ*</sub> values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retardation_factor), which is the ratio between the migration distance of the substance and the migration distance of the solvent front during chromatography.

In combination with the instrumental methods, chromatography can be used in the quantitative determination of substances. There are different types of chromatography that differ from the media they use to separate the analyte and the sample. In [thin-layer chromatography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thin-layer_chromatography), the analyte mixture moves up and separates along the coated sheet under the volatile mobile phase. In [gas chromatography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_chromatography), the gas phase separates the volatile analytes. A common method of chromatography using liquid as a mobile phase is [high-performance liquid chromatography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-performance_liquid_chromatography).[^cite_note-25]

### Hybrid techniques

Combinations of the above techniques produce a "hybrid" or "hyphenated" technique.[^cite_note-pmid6353577-26][^cite_note-pmid9253184-27][^cite_note-pmid12462614-28][^cite_note-pmid12462615-29] Several examples are in popular use today and new hybrid techniques are under development. For example, [gas chromatography-mass spectrometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_chromatography-mass_spectrometry), gas chromatography-[infrared spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared_spectroscopy), [liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_chromatography-mass_spectrometry), liquid chromatography-[NMR spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NMR_spectroscopy), liquid chromatography-infrared spectroscopy, and capillary electrophoresis-mass spectrometry.[^cite_note-Patel2010-30]

Hyphenated separation techniques refer to a combination of two (or more) techniques to detect and separate chemicals from solutions. Most often the other technique is some form of [chromatography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatography). Hyphenated techniques are widely used in [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry) and [biochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemistry). A [slash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slash_\(punctuation\)) is sometimes used instead of [hyphen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyphen), especially if the name of one of the methods contains a hyphen itself.[^cite_note-Patel2010-30]

## Data analysis and chemometrics

The vast amount of data produced by modern analytical instruments has made computational data analysis an integral part of the field. The field of chemometrics uses statistical and mathematical methods to design optimal experimental procedures and to extract meaningful information from chemical data.[^cite_note-34]

Key areas include:

- Multivariate calibration: Used to develop models that correlate instrument responses (e.g., spectra) to analyte concentrations, essential in techniques like near-infrared spectroscopy.

  [^cite_note-35]

- Pattern recognition: Employed to classify samples based on their analytical profile, with applications in food authenticity and medical diagnostics.

- [Machine learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_learning) and [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence_in_chemistry): These techniques are increasingly used for predictive modeling, optimizing analytical methods, and automating data interpretation.

  [^cite_note-36]

## Standards

See also: [Analytical quality control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_quality_control)

### Standard curve



A calibration curve plot showing [limit of detection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detection_limit) (LOD), [limit of quantification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detection_limit) (LOQ), dynamic range, and limit of [linearity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearity) (LOL)

A general method for analysis of concentration involves the creation of a [calibration curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calibration_curve). This allows for the determination of the amount of a chemical in a material by comparing the results of an unknown sample to those of a series of known standards. If the concentration of an element or compound in a sample exceeds the detection range of the technique, it can simply be diluted in a pure solvent. If the amount in the sample is below an instrument's range of measurement, the method of addition can be used. In this method, a known quantity of the element or compound under study is added, and the difference between the concentration added and the concentration observed is the amount actually in the sample.[^cite_note-39]

### Internal standards

Sometimes an [internal standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_standard) is added at a known concentration directly to an analytical sample to aid in quantitation. The amount of analyte present is then determined relative to the internal standard as a calibrant. An ideal internal standard is an isotopically enriched analyte which gives rise to the method of [isotope dilution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotope_dilution).[^cite_note-40]

### Standard addition

The method of [standard addition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_addition) is used in instrumental analysis to determine the concentration of a substance ([analyte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analyte)) in an unknown sample by comparison to a set of samples of known concentration, similar to using a [calibration curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calibration_curve). Standard addition can be applied to most analytical techniques and is used instead of a calibration curve to solve the [matrix effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_effect) problem.[^cite_note-41]

## Signals and noise

One of the most important components of analytical chemistry is maximizing the desired signal while minimizing the associated [noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noise_\(electronics\)).[^cite_note-isbn0-495-01201-7-42] The analytical figure of merit is known as the [signal-to-noise ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal-to-noise_ratio) (S/N or SNR).

Noise can arise from environmental factors as well as from fundamental physical processes.

### Thermal noise

Main article: [Johnson–Nyquist noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnson%E2%80%93Nyquist_noise)

Thermal noise results from the motion of charge carriers (usually electrons) in an electrical circuit generated by their thermal motion. Thermal noise is [white noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_noise), meaning that the power [spectral density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_density) is constant throughout the [frequency spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_spectrum).

The [root mean square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_mean_square) value of the thermal noise in a resistor is given by[^cite_note-isbn0-495-01201-7-42]

&#x20;    v   R M S    =   4  k   B    T R Δ f   ,   {\displaystyle v\_{\rm {RMS}}={\sqrt {4k\_{\rm {B}}TR\Delta f}},}  {\displaystyle v\_{\rm {RMS}}={\sqrt {4k\_{\rm {B}}TR\Delta f}},}

where *k*<sub>B</sub> is the [Boltzmann constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_constant), *T* is the [temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature), *R* is the resistance, and     Δ f   {\displaystyle \Delta f}  {\displaystyle \Delta f} is the [bandwidth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandwidth_\(signal_processing\)) of the frequency     f   {\displaystyle f}  {\displaystyle f}.

### Shot noise

Main article: [Shot noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shot_noise)

Shot noise is a type of [electronic noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_noise) that occurs when the finite number of particles (such as [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) in an electronic circuit or [photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon) in an optical device) is small enough to give rise to statistical fluctuations in a signal.

Shot noise is a [Poisson process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_process), and the charge carriers that make up the current follow a [Poisson distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_distribution). The root mean square current fluctuation is given by[^cite_note-isbn0-495-01201-7-42]

&#x20;    i   R M S    =   2 e I Δ f     {\displaystyle i\_{\rm {RMS}}={\sqrt {2eI\Delta f}}}  {\displaystyle i\_{\rm {RMS}}={\sqrt {2eI\Delta f}}}

where *e* is the [elementary charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_charge) and *I* is the average current. Shot noise is white noise.

### Flicker noise

Main article: [Flicker noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flicker_noise)

Flicker noise is electronic noise with a 1/*ƒ* frequency spectrum; as *f* increases, the noise decreases. Flicker noise arises from a variety of sources, such as impurities in a conductive channel, generation, and [recombination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrier_generation_and_recombination) noise in a [transistor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transistor) due to base current, and so on. This noise can be avoided by [modulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulation) of the signal at a higher frequency, for example, through the use of a [lock-in amplifier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lock-in_amplifier).[^cite_note-43][^cite_note-44]

### Environmental noise



Noise in a [thermogravimetric analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermogravimetric_analysis); lower noise in the middle of the plot results from less human activity (and environmental noise) at night

[Environmental noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_noise) arises from the surroundings of the analytical instrument. Sources of electromagnetic noise are [power lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_lines), radio and television stations, [wireless devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless_device), [compact fluorescent lamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_fluorescent_lamp)[^cite_note-45] and [electric motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_motor). Many of these noise sources are narrow bandwidth and, therefore, can be avoided. Temperature and [vibration isolation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vibration_isolation) may be required for some instruments.[^cite_note-Skoog2018-46]

### Noise reduction

Noise reduction can be accomplished either in [computer hardware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_hardware) or [software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software). Examples of hardware noise reduction are the use of [shielded cable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shielded_cable), [analog filtering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analog_filter), and signal modulation. Examples of software noise reduction are [digital filtering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_filter), [ensemble average](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ensemble_average), [boxcar average](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boxcar_averager), and [correlation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correlation) methods.[^cite_note-isbn0-495-01201-7-42]

## Applications



A U.S. [Food and Drug Administration](11632.md) scientist uses a portable near-infrared spectroscopy device to inspect [lactose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactose) for adulteration with [melamine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melamine)

Analytical chemistry has applications across science and industry. It is fundamental to [forensic science](45710.md) (e.g., DNA fingerprinting and toxicology), [bioanalysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioanalysis) (e.g., measuring drug concentrations in pharmacokinetic studies), [clinical analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinical_analysis?action=edit\&redlink=1) (e.g., [blood glucose monitoring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_glucose_monitoring) and [COVID-19 PCR testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PCR_testing_procedure)), [environmental monitoring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_monitoring) (e.g., testing for pollutants in water and air), and [materials science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materials_science) (e.g., quality control of semiconductors and nanomaterials).[^cite_note-Szymanska2018Review-47]

Great effort is being put into shrinking the analysis techniques to [chip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_circuit) size. Although few examples of such systems compete with traditional analysis techniques, potential advantages include size/portability, speed, and cost. Micro [total analysis system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_analysis_system) (μTAS) or [lab-on-a-chip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lab-on-a-chip). [Microscale chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microscale_chemistry) reduces the amount of chemicals used.[^cite_note-Jayamohan2017-48]

Many developments improve the analysis of biological systems. Examples of rapidly expanding fields in this area are [genomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genomics), [DNA sequencing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA_sequencing) and related research in [genetic fingerprinting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_fingerprinting) and [DNA microarray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA_microarray); [proteomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proteomics), the analysis of protein concentrations and modifications, especially in response to various stressors, at various developmental stages, or in various parts of the body; [metabolomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolomics), which deals with metabolites; [transcriptomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcriptomics), including mRNA and associated fields; [lipidomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipidomics), dealing with lipids and its related fields; peptidomics, dealing with peptides and its related fields; and metallomics, dealing with metal concentrations and especially with their binding to proteins and other molecules.[^cite_note-Aebersold2016-49]

Analytical chemistry has played a critical role in the understanding of basic science to a variety of practical applications, such as biomedical applications, [environmental monitoring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_monitoring), quality control of industrial manufacturing, and forensic science.[^cite_note-50]

The recent developments in computer automation and information technologies have extended analytical chemistry into several new biological fields. For example, automated [DNA sequencing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA_sequencing) machines were the basis for completing human genome projects, leading to the birth of [genomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genomics). Protein identification and peptide sequencing by mass spectrometry opened a new field of [proteomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proteomics). In addition to automating specific processes, there is effort to automate larger sections of lab testing, such as in companies like [Emerald Cloud Lab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emerald_Cloud_Lab) and Transcriptic.[^cite_note-51]

Analytical chemistry has been an indispensable area in the development of [nanotechnology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanotechnology). Surface characterization instruments, [electron microscopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_microscopes) and scanning probe microscopes enable scientists to visualize atomic structures with chemical characterizations.[^cite_note-Eustis2006-52]
