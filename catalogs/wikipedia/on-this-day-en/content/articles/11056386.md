**Internet censorship** is the legal [control or suppression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship) of what can be accessed, published, or viewed on the [Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet). [Censorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship) is most often applied to specific [internet domains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_domain) (such as *Wikipedia.org*, for example) but exceptionally may extend to all Internet resources located outside the [jurisdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisdiction) of the censoring state. Internet censorship may also put restrictions on what information can be made internet accessible.[^cite_note-1] Organizations providing internet access – such as schools and libraries – may choose to preclude access to material that they consider undesirable, offensive, [age-inappropriate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age-inappropriate) or even illegal, and regard this as ethical behavior rather than censorship. Individuals and organizations may engage in [self-censorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-censorship) of material they publish, for moral, religious, or business reasons, to conform to [societal norms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Societal_norms), political views, due to intimidation, or out of fear of legal or other consequences.[^cite_note-NYT-20181015-2][^cite_note-NYT-20140311-3]

The extent of Internet censorship varies on a country-to-country basis. While some countries have moderate Internet censorship, other countries go as far as to limit the access of information such as news and suppress and silence discussion among citizens.[^cite_note-NYT-20140311-3] Internet censorship also occurs in response to or in anticipation of events such as elections, protests, and riots. An example is the increased censorship due to the events of the [Arab Spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Spring). Other types of censorship include the [use of copyrights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship_by_copyright), [defamation](28661.md), [harassment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harassment), and various obscene material claims as a way to deliberately suppress content.

Support for and opposition to Internet censorship also varies. In a [2012 Internet Society survey](#Internet_Society's_Global_Internet_User_Survey), 71% of respondents agreed that "censorship should exist in some form on the Internet". In the same survey, 83% agreed that "access to the Internet should be considered a basic human right" and 86% agreed that "[freedom of expression](21401843.md) should be guaranteed on the Internet". According to [GlobalWebIndex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GWI_\(company\)), over 400 million people use [virtual private networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_private_network) to [circumvent censorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_circumvention) or for increased user [privacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privacy).[^cite_note-Guardian_5_December_2014-4]

[Age verification](2636394.md) as a form of internet censorship to protect children is [expanding to more jurisdictions](80840255.md), particularly since [Australia](4689264.md) passed its [Online Safety Amendment](78404606.md) on 27 November 2024, which came into force on 10 December 2025.[^cite_note-5]

## Overview

Many of the challenges associated with Internet censorship are similar to those for offline censorship of more traditional media such as newspapers, magazines, books, music, radio, television, and film. One difference is that national borders are more permeable online: residents of a country that bans certain information can find it on websites hosted outside the country. Thus censors must work to prevent [access to information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Access_to_information) even though they lack physical or legal control over the websites themselves. This in turn requires the use of technical censorship methods that are unique to the Internet, such as site blocking and content filtering.[^cite_note-UNESCO-2011-6]

Views about the feasibility and effectiveness of Internet censorship have evolved in parallel with the development of the Internet and censorship technologies:

- A 1993 *[Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_\(magazine\))* magazine article quotes computer scientist [John Gilmore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Gilmore_\(activist\)), one of the founders of the [Electronic Frontier Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Frontier_Foundation), as saying "The Net interprets censorship as damage and routes around it".

  [^cite_note-7]

- In November 2007, "Father of the Internet" [Vint Cerf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vint_Cerf) stated that he sees government control of the Internet failing because the Web is almost entirely privately owned.

  [^cite_note-8]

- A report of research conducted in 2007 and published in 2009 by the [Berkman Klein Center for Internet & Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berkman_Klein_Center_for_Internet_&_Society) at Harvard University stated that: "We are confident that the \[[censorship circumvention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship_circumvention)] tool developers will for the most part keep ahead of the governments' blocking efforts", but also that "...we believe that less than two percent of all filtered Internet users use circumvention tools".

  [^cite_note-9]

- In contrast, a 2011 report by researchers at the [Oxford Internet Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_Internet_Institute) published by [UNESCO](21786641.md) concludes "... the control of information on the Internet and Web is certainly feasible, and technological advances do not therefore guarantee greater freedom of speech".

  [^cite_note-UNESCO-2011-6]

Blocking and filtering can be based on relatively static [blacklists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blacklist) or be determined more dynamically based on a real-time examination of the information being exchanged. Blacklists may be produced manually or automatically and are often not available to non-customers of the blocking software. Blocking or filtering can be done at a centralized national level, at a decentralized sub-national level, or at an institutional level, e.g., in libraries, universities or [Internet cafés](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_caf%C3%A9).[^cite_note-NYT-20140311-3] Blocking and filtering may also vary within a country across different ISPs.[^cite_note-Chadwick-2009-10] Countries may filter sensitive content on an ongoing basis and/or introduce temporary filtering during key time periods such as elections. In some cases, the censoring authorities may surreptitiously block content to mislead the public into believing that censorship has not been applied. This is achieved by returning a fake ["Not Found"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTTP_404) error message when an attempt is made to access a blocked website.[^cite_note-Faris-2008-11]

Unless the censor has total control over all Internet-connected computers, such as in [North Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship_in_North_Korea#Internet_policies) (who employs an [intranet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intranet) that only privileged citizens can access), or [Cuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_cuba), total censorship of information is very difficult or impossible to achieve due to the underlying distributed technology of the Internet. [Pseudonymity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudonymity) and [data havens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_haven) (such as [Hyphanet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyphanet)) protect [free speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_speech) using technologies that guarantee material cannot be removed and prevents the identification of authors. Technologically savvy users can often find ways to access blocked content. Nevertheless, blocking remains an effective means of limiting access to sensitive information for most users when censors, such as those in [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_China), are able to devote significant resources to building and maintaining a comprehensive censorship system.[^cite_note-UNESCO-2011-6]

The term "[splinternet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Splinternet)" is sometimes used to describe the effects of national [firewalls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firewall_\(computing\)). The verb "[rivercrab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphemisms_for_Internet_censorship_in_China)" colloquially refers to censorship of the Internet, particularly in Asia.[^cite_note-RC-12]

## Content suppression methods

### Technical censorship

Various parties are using different technical methods of preventing public access to undesirable resources, with varying levels of effectiveness, costs and side effects.

#### Blacklist

Entities mandating and implementing the censorship usually identify them by one of the following items: keywords, [domain names](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domain_name) and [IP addresses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_address). Lists are populated from different sources, ranging from private supplier through courts to specialized government agencies ([Ministry of Industry and Information Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Industry_and_Information_Technology) of China, Islamic Guidance in Iran).[^cite_note-Jones-2019-13]

As per Hoffmann, different methods are used to block certain websites or pages including [DNS spoofing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNS_spoofing), blocking access to [IPs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_address), analyzing and [filtering URLs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/URL), [inspecting filter packets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_packet_inspection) and [resetting connections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TCP_reset_attack).[^cite_note-howto-14]

#### Points of control

Enforcement of the censor-nominated technologies can be applied at various levels of countries and Internet infrastructure:[^cite_note-Jones-2019-13]

- [Internet backbone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_backbone), including [Internet exchange points (IXP)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_exchange_point) with international networks ([Autonomous Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_system_\(Internet\))), operators of [submarine communications cables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_communications_cable), [satellite Internet access](769088.md) points, international [optical fibre links](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiber-optic_communication) etc. In addition to facing huge performance challenges due to large bandwidths involved, these do not give censors access to information exchanged within the country.

- [Internet service providers](100245.md), which involves installation of voluntary (such as in the [UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_the_United_Kingdom)) or mandatory (such as in [Russia](37529240.md)) Internet surveillance and blocking equipment.

- Individual institutions, which in most cases implement some form of Internet access controls to enforce their own policies, but, especially in case of public or educational institutions, may be requested or coerced to do this on the request from the government.

- Personal devices, whose manufacturers or vendors may be required by law to install censorship software.

- Application service providers (e.g. social media companies), who may be legally required to remove particular content. Foreign providers with business presence in given country may be also coerced into restricting access to specific contents for visitors from the requesting country.

- [Certificate authorities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Certificate_authorities) may be required to issue counterfeit [X.509](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X.509) certificates controlled by the government, allowing [man-in-the-middle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Man-in-the-middle) surveillance of [TLS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_Layer_Security) encrypted connections.

- [Content Delivery Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Content_Delivery_Network) providers who tend to aggregate large amounts of content (e.g. images) may be also an attractive target for censorship authorities.

#### Approaches

Internet content is subject to technical censorship methods, including:[^cite_note-NYT-20140311-3][^cite_note-UNESCO-2011-6]

- **Internet Protocol (IP) address blocking**: Access to a certain [IP address](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_address) is denied. If the target Web site is hosted in a [shared hosting server](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shared_web_hosting_service), all websites on the same server will be blocked. This affects IP-based protocols such as [HTTP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTTP), [FTP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FTP) and [POP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post_Office_Protocol). A typical circumvention method is to find [proxies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proxy_server) that have access to the target websites, but proxies may be jammed or blocked, and some Web sites, such as Wikipedia (when editing), also block proxies. Some large websites such as [Google](1092923.md) have allocated additional IP addresses to circumvent the block, but later the block was extended to cover the new addresses. Due to challenges with [geopositioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geopositioning), [geo-blocking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geo-blocking) is normally implemented via IP address blocking.

- **Domain name system (DNS) filtering and redirection**: Connections to blocked [domain names](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domain_name) are not resolved, or an incorrect IP address is returned via [DNS hijacking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNS_hijacking) or other means. This affects all IP-based protocols such as HTTP, FTP and POP. A typical circumvention method is to find an alternative [DNS resolver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domain_Name_System) that resolves domain names correctly, but domain name servers are subject to blockage as well, especially IP address blocking. Another workaround is to bypass DNS if the IP address is obtainable from other sources and is not itself blocked. Examples are modifying the [Hosts file](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hosts_file) or typing the IP address instead of the domain name as part of a URL given to a [Web browser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_browser).

- **Uniform Resource Locator (URL) filtering**: [URL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/URL) strings are scanned for target keywords regardless of the domain name specified in the URL. This affects the [HTTP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTTP) protocol. Typical circumvention methods are to use [escaped characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percent-encoding) in the URL, or to use encrypted protocols such as [VPN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VPN) and [TLS/SSL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TLS/SSL).

  [^cite_note-15]

- **Packet filtering**: Terminate [TCP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmission_Control_Protocol) [packet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_packet) transmissions when a certain number of controversial keywords are detected. This affects all TCP-based protocols such as HTTP, FTP and POP, but [Search engine results pages](4555947.md) are more likely to be censored. Typical circumvention methods are to use encrypted connections – such as VPN and TLS/SSL – to escape the HTML content, or by reducing the [TCP/IP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TCP/IP) [stack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protocol_stack)'s [MTU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_transmission_unit)/[MSS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_segment_size) to reduce the amount of text contained in a given packet.

- **Connection reset**: If a previous [TCP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmission_Control_Protocol) connection is blocked by the filter, future connection attempts from both sides can also be blocked for some variable amount of time. Depending on the location of the block, other users or websites may also be blocked, if the communication is [routed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Routed) through the blocking location. A circumvention method is to ignore the reset packet sent by the firewall.

  [^cite_note-16]

- **Network disconnection**: A technically simpler method of Internet censorship is to completely cut off all routers, either by software or by hardware (turning off machines, pulling out cables). A circumvention method could be to use a satellite ISP to access Internet.

  [^cite_note-17]

- **Portal censorship** and **search result removal**: Major portals, including [search engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Search_engine), may exclude web sites that they would ordinarily include. This renders a site invisible to people who do not know where to find it. When a major portal does this, it has a similar effect to censorship. Sometimes this exclusion is done to satisfy a legal or other requirement, other times it is purely at the discretion of the portal. For example, Google.de and Google.fr remove [Neo-Nazi](54361.md) and other listings in compliance with German and French law.

  [^cite_note-18]

- **Computer network attacks**: [Denial-of-service attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denial-of-service_attack) and attacks that deface opposition websites can produce the same result as other blocking techniques, preventing or limiting access to certain websites or other online services, although only for a limited period of time. This technique might be used during the lead up to an election or some other sensitive period. It is more frequently used by non-state actors seeking to disrupt services.

  [^cite_note-19]

See also: [Internet censorship circumvention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_circumvention), [Internet forum § Word censor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_forum#Word_censor), and [Anti-spam techniques § Detecting spam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-spam_techniques#Detecting_spam)

#### Over and under blocking

Technical censorship techniques are subject to both over- and under-blocking since it is often impossible to always block exactly the targeted content without blocking other permissible material or allowing some access to targeted material and so providing more or less protection than desired.[^cite_note-UNESCO-2011-6] An example is blocking an IP-address of a server that hosts multiple websites, which prevents access to all of the websites rather than just those that contain content deemed offensive.[^cite_note-20]

#### Use of commercial filtering software



Screenshot of [Websense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forcepoint#Websense) blocking Facebook in an organization where it has been configured to block a category named "Personals and Dating"

Main article: [Content-control software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Content-control_software)

Writing in 2009, [Ronald Deibert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Deibert), professor of political science at the [University of Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Toronto) and co-founder and one of the principal investigators of the [OpenNet Initiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenNet_Initiative), and, writing in 2011, Evgeny Morzov, a visiting scholar at [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University) and an op-ed contributor to *[The New York Times](30680.md)*, explain that companies in the United States, Finland, France, Germany, Britain, Canada, and South Africa are in part responsible for the increasing sophistication of online content filtering worldwide. While the off-the-shelf [filtering software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filtering_software) sold by Internet security companies are primarily marketed to businesses and individuals seeking to protect themselves and their employees and families, they are also used by governments to block what they consider sensitive content.[^cite_note-Chadwick2009-1-21][^cite_note-22]

Among the most popular filtering software programs is [SmartFilter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SmartFilter) by [Secure Computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secure_Computing) in California, which was bought by [McAfee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McAfee) in 2008. SmartFilter has been used by [Tunisia](30188.md), Saudi Arabia, [Sudan](27421.md), the [UAE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UAE), Kuwait, [Bahrain](18933277.md), Iran, and [Oman](22316.md), as well as the United States and the UK.[^cite_note-guardian.co.uk-23] [Myanmar](19457.md) and [Yemen](350939.md) have used filtering software from [Websense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forcepoint#Websense). The [Canadian-made](5042916.md) commercial filter Netsweeper[^cite_note-24] is used in [Qatar](8238258.md), the [UAE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UAE), and [Yemen](350939.md).[^cite_note-Noman-2011-25] The Canadian organization CitizenLab has reported that [Sandvine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandvine) and [Procera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procera_Networks) products are used in Turkey and Egypt.[^cite_note-26]

On 12 March 2013, in a *Special Report on Internet Surveillance*, Reporters Without Borders named five "Corporate Enemies of the Internet": [Amesys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groupe_Bull#Amesys_controversy) (France), [Blue Coat Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Coat_Systems) (U.S.), [Gamma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FinFisher) (UK and Germany), [HackingTeam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HackingTeam) (Italy), and Trovicor (Germany). The companies sell products that are liable to be used by governments to violate human rights and freedom of information. RWB said that the list is not exhaustive and will be expanded in the coming months.[^cite_note-RSFSRS-March2013-27]

In a U.S. lawsuit filed in May 2011, [Cisco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cisco) is accused of helping the [Chinese government](23239.md) build a firewall, known widely as the [Golden Shield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Shield), to censor the Internet and keep tabs on dissidents.[^cite_note-28] Cisco said it had made nothing special for China. Cisco is also accused of aiding the Chinese government in monitoring and apprehending members of the banned [Falun Gong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falun_Gong) group.[^cite_note-29]

Many filtering programs allow blocking to be configured based on dozens of categories and subcategories. For example, Websense uses over 90 categories, including "abortion", "adult material", "advocacy groups", "drugs", "games", "job search", and "religion".[^cite_note-Noman-2011-25] However, these blocking categories can contain errors that lead to the unintended blocking of websites.[^cite_note-Chadwick2009-1-21] According to the [OpenNet Initiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenNet_Initiative), the 2007 blocking of [Dailymotion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dailymotion) in Tunisia resulted from Secure Computing wrongly categorizing Dailymotion as "pornography" in its SmartFilter software. While initial reports suggested the block was due to satirical videos about human rights violations in Tunisia, access was gradually restored after Secure Computing corrected the categorization error.[^cite_note-30]

Organizations such as the [Global Network Initiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Network_Initiative), the [Electronic Frontier Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Frontier_Foundation), [Amnesty International](18947898.md), and the [American Civil Liberties Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_Liberties_Union) have successfully lobbied some vendors such as [Websense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forcepoint#Websense) to make changes to their software, to refrain from doing business with repressive governments, and to educate schools who have inadvertently reconfigured their filtering software too strictly.[^cite_note-riaclu-31][^cite_note-32][^cite_note-amnesty_china-33] Nevertheless, regulations and accountability related to the use of commercial filters and services are often non-existent, and there is relatively little oversight from civil society or other independent groups. Vendors often consider information about what sites and content is blocked valuable intellectual property that is not made available outside the company, sometimes not even to the organizations purchasing the filters. Thus by relying upon out-of-the-box filtering systems, the detailed task of deciding what is or is not acceptable speech may be outsourced to the commercial vendors.[^cite_note-Noman-2011-25]

## Circumvention

Main article: [Internet censorship circumvention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_circumvention)

Internet censorship circumvention is one of the processes used by technologically savvy Internet users to bypass the technical aspects of Internet filtering and gain access to the otherwise censored material. Circumvention is an inherent problem for those wishing to censor the Internet because filtering and blocking do not remove content from the Internet, but instead block access to it. Therefore, as long as there is at least one publicly accessible uncensored system, it will often be possible to gain access to the otherwise censored material. However circumvention may not be possible by non-tech-savvy users, so blocking and filtering remain effective means of censoring the Internet access of large numbers of users.[^cite_note-UNESCO-2011-6]

Different techniques and resources are used to bypass Internet censorship, including [proxy websites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proxy_server), [virtual private networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_private_network), [sneakernets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sneakernet), the [dark web](40712897.md) and circumvention software tools. Solutions have differing ease of use, speed, security, and risks. Most, however, rely on gaining access to an Internet connection that is not subject to filtering, often in a different jurisdiction not subject to the same censorship laws. According to [GlobalWebIndex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GlobalWebIndex), over 400 million people use virtual private networks to circumvent censorship or for an increased level of privacy.[^cite_note-Guardian_5_December_2014-4] The majority of circumvention techniques are not suitable for day to day use.[^cite_note-38]

There are risks to using circumvention software or other methods to bypass Internet censorship. In some countries, individuals that gain access to otherwise restricted content may be violating the law and if caught can be expelled, fired, jailed, or subject to other punishments and loss of access.[^cite_note-NYT-20140311-3][^cite_note-39]

In June 2011, *The New York Times* reported that the U.S. is engaged in a "global effort to deploy 'shadow' Internet and mobile phone systems that dissidents can use to undermine repressive governments that seek to silence them by censoring or shutting down telecommunications networks."[^cite_note-40]

Another way to circumvent Internet censorship is to physically go to an area where the Internet is not censored. In 2017, a so-called "Internet refugee camp" was established by IT workers in the village of Bonako, just outside an area of Cameroon where the Internet is regularly blocked.[^cite_note-41][^cite_note-42]

### Increased use of HTTPS

The use of [HTTPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTTPS) versus what originally was [HTTP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTTP) in web searches created greater accessibility to most sites originally blocked or heavily monitored. Many social media sites including, Facebook, Google, and Twitter have added an automatic redirection to HTTPS as of 2017.[^cite_note-Connolly-43] With the added adoption of HTTPS use, "censors" are left with limited options of either completely blocking all content or none of it.[^cite_note-44]

The use of [HTTPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTTPS) does not inherently prevent the censorship of an entire domain, as the domain name is left unencrypted in the ClientHello of the [TLS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_Layer_Security) handshake. The [Encrypted Client Hello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encrypted_Client_Hello) TLS extension expands on HTTPS and encrypts the entire ClientHello but this depends on both client and server support.[^cite_note-45][^cite_note-46]

## Common targets

There are several motives or rationales for Internet filtering: politics and power, social norms and morals, and security concerns. Protecting existing economic interests is an additional emergent motive for Internet filtering. In addition, networking tools and applications that allow the sharing of information related to these motives are themselves subjected to filtering and blocking. And while there is considerable variation from country to country, the blocking of web sites in a local language is roughly twice that of web sites available only in English or other international languages.[^cite_note-Faris-2008-11]

### Information about individuals

Main article: [Right to be forgotten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_to_be_forgotten)

The *right to be forgotten* is a concept that has been discussed and put into practice in the [European Union](9317.md). In May 2014, the [European Court of Justice](87187.md) ruled against [Google](1092923.md) in *[Costeja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Costeja)*, a case brought by a Spanish man who requested the removal of a link to a digitized 1998 article in *La Vanguardia* newspaper about an auction for his foreclosed home, for a debt that he had subsequently paid.[^cite_note-66] He initially attempted to have the article removed by complaining to Spain's data protection agency—*Agencia Española de Protección de Datos*—which rejected the claim on the grounds that it was lawful and accurate, but accepted a complaint against Google and asked Google to remove the results.[^cite_note-67] Google sued in Spain and the lawsuit was transferred to the European Court of Justice. The court ruled in *Costeja* that search engines are responsible for the content they point to and thus, Google was required to comply with EU [data privacy](237536.md) laws.[^cite_note-68][^cite_note-69] It began compliance on 30 May 2014 during which it received 12,000 requests to have personal details removed from its search engine.[^cite_note-GoogleEU-70]

[Index on Censorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_on_Censorship) claimed that "*[Costeja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Costeja)* ruling ... allows individuals to complain to search engines about information they do not like with no legal oversight. This is akin to marching into a library and forcing it to pulp books. Although the ruling is intended for private individuals it opens the door to anyone who wants to whitewash their personal history....The Court's decision is a retrograde move that misunderstands the role and responsibility of search engines and the wider internet. It should send chills down the spine of everyone in the European Union who believes in the crucial importance of free expression and freedom of information."[^cite_note-71]

## Resilience

Various contexts influence whether or not an internet user will be resilient to censorship attempts. Users are more resilient to censorship if they are aware that information is being manipulated. This awareness of censorship leads to users finding ways to circumvent it. Awareness of censorship also allows users to factor this manipulation into their belief systems. Knowledge of censorship also offers some citizens incentive to try to discover information that is being concealed. In contrast, those that lack awareness of censorship cannot easily compensate for information manipulation.[^cite_note-Roberts-72]

Other important factors for censorship resiliency are the demand for the information being concealed, and the ability to pay the costs to circumvent censorship. Entertainment content is more resilient to online censorship than political content, and users with more education, technology access, and wider, more diverse social networks are more resilient to censorship attempts.[^cite_note-Roberts-72]

From 1995 to 2002, the government of [South Korea](27019.md) passed the Telecommunications Business Act (TBA), the first internet censorship law in the world.[^cite_note-Peng-73] As more people in more places begin using the Internet for important activities, there is an increase in online censorship, using increasingly sophisticated techniques. The motives, scope, and effectiveness of Internet censorship vary widely from country to country. The countries engaged in state-mandated filtering are clustered in three main regions of the world: east Asia, central Asia, and the [Middle East/North Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East_and_North_Africa).

Countries in other regions also practice certain forms of filtering. In the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_the_United_States), state-mandated Internet filtering occurs on some computers in libraries and [K–12 schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%E2%80%9312_schools). Content related to [Nazism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazism) or [Holocaust denial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocaust_denial) is blocked in [France](31630837.md) and [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_Germany). [Child pornography](25211885.md) and [hate speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hate_speech) are blocked in many countries throughout the world.[^cite_note-Zittrain-2008-74] In fact, many countries throughout the world, including some democracies with long traditions of strong support for [freedom of expression](21401843.md) and [freedom of the press](105323.md), are engaged in some amount of online censorship, often with substantial public support.[^cite_note-75]

Internet censorship in [China](5405.md) is among the most stringent in the world. The government blocks Web sites that discuss the [Dalai Lama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalai_Lama), the 1989 crackdown on [Tiananmen Square protesters](44534.md), the banned spiritual practice [Falun Gong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falun_Gong), as well as many general Internet sites.[^cite_note-76] The government requires Internet search firms and state media to censor issues deemed officially "sensitive," and blocks access to foreign websites including [Facebook](7529378.md), [Twitter](9988187.md), and [YouTube](3524766.md).[^cite_note-77] According to a study in 2014,[^cite_note-78] censorship in China is used to muzzle those outside government who attempt to spur the creation of crowds for any reason—in opposition to, in support of, or unrelated to the government.

There are international bodies that oppose internet censorship, for example "Internet censorship is open to challenge at the World Trade Organization (WTO) as it can restrict trade in online services, a forthcoming study argues".[^cite_note-79]

On 28 November 2024, [Australia](4689264.md) passed a [social media ban](78404606.md) for users under 16,[^cite_note-SAProposal-80] this, along with [The Anxious Generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Anxious_Generation) by [Johnathan Haidt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Haidt) and the [UK](31717.md)'s [Online Safety Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_Safety_Act_2023) have led to many other countries and subdivisions to enact or propose similar social media bans, with similar actions following in [Brazil](3383.md) and [Indonesia](14579.md) in March 2026, [Malaysia](3607937.md) in June 2026 and [Florida](18933066.md) by mid-June 2026. As a result of these proposals, some online platforms such as [Roblox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roblox) have made their age verification policies global in order to close loopholes around these policies because if they fail to comply, they could face heavy fines.[^cite_note-TickTok2026-81][^cite_note-82]

### Age verification

Main article: [Online age verification laws by country](80840255.md)



Online age verification laws by country as of September 2026

Since 2023, many countries and subdivisions across the globe have passed or proposed laws to require [age verification](2636394.md) for parts of the internet, including [social media](5897742.md) services, [social networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_network), devices, and other digital services with the intention of protecting young people from harmful and illegal content. Most of the passed laws or bills have the age verification process done through handing over a government-issued ID, a [facial scan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facial_age_estimation) or [credit card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credit_card), in order to prove that they are not underage, with the process often done with third-party age verification providers.

These laws and proposals vary greatly, with some of them restricting access only to certain features or distinguishing between different users online, which could lead to companies requiring age verification to comply with such restrictions as the [Kids Online Safety Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kids_Online_Safety_Act), or banning users under a certain age, which is the approach of the [Online Safety Amendment](78404606.md) in Australia which fully prohibits anyone under 16 years old from holding social media accounts. A few nations, such as [Pakistan](23235.md), have proposed punishing those who allow minors to access these services.[^cite_note-83][^cite_note-84][^cite_note-85][^cite_note-86]

These laws have received criticism from some,[^cite_note-87] including organizations that focus on digital and civil rights (such as the [Electronic Frontier Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Frontier_Foundation), [Access Now](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Access_Now), the [V-Dem Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V-Dem_Institute) and the [American Civil Liberties Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_Liberties_Union) in the [United States](3434750.md)) for normalizing surveillance and restricting freedom of speech through the elimination of anonymous accounts. The New Zealand Council for Civil Liberties, [UNICEF](19698110.md), and the [Council of Europe Commissioner for Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Europe_Commissioner_for_Human_Rights) have also opposed such laws on the basis that they constitute violations of the rights to [freedom of speech](21401843.md) and [privacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_to_privacy), that such laws may backfire, and that there are better ways of protecting children online.[^cite_note-88][^cite_note-89][^cite_note-90][^cite_note-91][^cite_note-92]

These laws can also be bypassed by loopholes, such as [VPNs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_private_network), which have seen a significant increase in users in countries where such laws are enforced. While these laws typically only apply in their respective countries, some online platforms (such as [Roblox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roblox)) made their age verification policies global,[^cite_note-93] and [Discord](49271581.md),[^cite_note-94] which require age verification for certain features to close said loopholes to avoid receiving a lawsuit.[^cite_note-95]

Despite these laws being mainly used for requiring social media services to verify the age of their users, critics even believe that these laws would silently expand beyond the internet and create a realm of digital censorship, with devices, apps, games, and websites being required to age verify their users with intrusive methods such as with a facial scan or the presentation of a [government ID](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_ID) as a result of [mission creep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mission_creep).[^cite_note-96][^cite_note-97]

Despite recent online protests over Internet censorship, there have been no physical protests related to age verification despite recent protest over internet censorship. Critics worry that media outlets such as [BBC](19344654.md) and [CNN](62028.md) will silence these views. On 6 August 2026, a [Texas](29810.md) man, Joseph Roy Ozment became the first person to be arrested for using voicemail to threaten the Texas Governor's Office over age verification on [Crunchyroll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crunchyroll).[^cite_note-98]

### International concerns

Generally, national laws affecting content within a country only apply to services that operate within that country and do not affect international services, but this has not been established clearly by international case law. There are concerns that due to the vast differences in freedom of speech between countries, that the ability for one country to affect speech across the global Internet could have chilling effects.

For example, [Google](1092923.md) had won a case at the [European Court of Justice](87187.md) in September 2019 that ruled that the EU's right to be forgotten only applied to services within the EU, and not globally.[^cite_note-99] But in a contrary decision in October 2019, the same court ruled that [Facebook](7529378.md) was required to globally comply with a takedown request made in relationship to defamatory material that was posted to Facebook by an Austrian that was libelous of another, which had been determined to be illegal under Austrian laws. The case created a problematic precedent that the Internet may become subject to regulation under the strictest national defamation laws, and would limit free speech that may be acceptable in other countries.[^cite_note-100]

A 2026 Guardian report revealed that Saudi officials had asked major U.S. social media companies to block the accounts of Saudi dissidents. In response, Meta restricted access to 108 Instagram accounts and Facebook pages that April, including those of activists Abdullah Alaoudh and Omar Abdulaziz, making them unavailable within the kingdom. Snapchat also deleted accounts without alerting the account holders.[^cite_note-101]

### Internet shutdowns

Several governments have resorted to shutting down most or all Internet connections in all or part of the country.&#x20;

This appears to have been the case on 27 and 28 January 2011 during the [2011 Egyptian revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Egyptian_revolution), in what has been widely described as an "unprecedented" internet block.[^cite_note-jamescowie_techdetails_Egyptfullblock-102][^cite_note-IDGnews_Egyptfullblock-103] About 3500 [Border Gateway Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Border_Gateway_Protocol) (BGP) routes to Egyptian networks were shut down from about 22\:10 to 22\:35 UTC 27 January.[^cite_note-jamescowie_techdetails_Egyptfullblock-102] This full block was implemented without cutting off major intercontinental [fibre-optic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibre-optic) links, with Renesys stating on 27 January, "Critical European-Asian fiber-optic routes through Egypt appear to be unaffected for now."[^cite_note-jamescowie_techdetails_Egyptfullblock-102] Full blocks also occurred in [Myanmar/Burma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_in_Myanmar#Censorship) in 2007,[^cite_note-104] [Libya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_speech_in_the_media_during_the_Libyan_civil_war_\(2011\)) in 2011,[^cite_note-Libya-RWB-2011-105] [Iran](14653.md) in 2019,[^cite_note-106] and [Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_Syria) during the [Syrian civil war](30741795.md).

Almost all Internet connections in [Sudan](27421.md) were disconnected from 3 June to 9 July 2019, in response to a political opposition sit-in seeking civilian rule.[^cite_note-107][^cite_note-108] and since the beginning of the civil war in April 2023 between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF), there have been series of [Internet shutdowns in Sudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_shutdowns_in_Sudan).[^cite_note-109][^cite_note-110] A near-complete shutdown in [Ethiopia](187749.md) lasted for a week after the [Amhara Region coup attempt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amhara_Region_coup_attempt).[^cite_note-111] A week-long shutdown in [Mauritania](18778516.md) followed disputes over the [2019 Mauritanian presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Mauritanian_presidential_election).[^cite_note-112] Other country-wide shutdowns in 2019 include [Zimbabwe](34399.md) after a gasoline price protests triggered police violence, [Gabon](12027.md) during the [2019 Gabonese coup attempt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Gabonese_coup_attempt), and during or after elections in [Democratic Republic of the Congo](76762.md), [Benin](3459.md), [Malawi](19088.md), and [Kazakhstan](16642.md).[^cite_note-proton-113]

Local shutdowns are frequently ordered in India during times of unrest and security concerns.[^cite_note-114][^cite_note-115] Some countries have used localized Internet shutdowns to combat cheating during exams, including [Iraq](7515928.md),[^cite_note-116] [Ethiopia](187749.md), [India](14533.md), [Algeria](358.md), and [Uzbekistan](31853.md).[^cite_note-proton-113]

The Iranian government imposed a [total internet shutdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Internet_blackout_in_Iran) from 16 to 23 November 2019, in response to [the fuel protests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019%E2%80%932020_Iranian_protests).[^cite_note-117] [Doug Madory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doug_Madory), the director of Internet analysis at [Oracle](22591.md), has described the operation as "unusual in its scale" and way more advanced.[^cite_note-Kottasová_and_Mazloumsaki-2019-118] Beginning Saturday afternoon on 16 November 2019, the government of [Iran](14653.md) ordered the disconnection of much of the country's internet connectivity as a response to widespread protests against the government's decision to raise gas prices. While [Iran](14653.md) is no stranger to government-directed interference in its citizens’ access to the [internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet), this outage is notable in how it differs from past events. Unlike previous efforts at censorship and bandwidth throttling, the internet of Iran is presently experiencing a multi-day wholesale disconnection for much of its population – arguably the largest such event ever for Iran.[^cite_note-Kottasová_and_Mazloumsaki-2019-118][^cite_note-119][^cite_note-120]

#### OpenNet Initiative reports

Through 2010, the OpenNet Initiative had documented Internet filtering by governments in over forty countries worldwide.[^cite_note-Noman-2011-25] The level of filtering in 26 countries in 2007 and in 25 countries in 2009 was classified in the political, social, and security areas. Of the 41 separate countries classified, seven were found to show no evidence of filtering in all three areas ([Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_in_Egypt#Censorship), [France](31630837.md), [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_Germany), [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_India), [Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights_in_Ukraine#Media_freedom_and_freedom_of_information), [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_the_United_Kingdom), and [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_the_United_States)), while one was found to engage in pervasive filtering in all three areas ([China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_China)), 13 were found to engage in pervasive filtering in one or more areas, and 34 were found to engage in some level of filtering in one or more areas. Of the 10 countries classified in both 2007 and 2009, one reduced its level of filtering ([Pakistan](4285830.md)), five increased their level of filtering ([Azerbaijan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_in_Azerbaijan#Filtering,_surveillance,_and_harassment), [Belarus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship_in_Belarus), [Kazakhstan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_Kazakhstan), [South Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_South_Korea), and [Uzbekistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_Uzbekistan)), and four maintained the same level of filtering ([China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_the_People's_Republic_of_China), [Iran](12831280.md), [Myanmar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_in_Myanmar#Censorship), and [Tajikistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights_in_Tajikistan)).[^cite_note-UNESCO-2011-6][^cite_note-ONIChildPornLegal-122]

#### Freedom on the Net reports

The *Freedom on the Net* reports from [Freedom House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_House) provide analytical reports and numerical ratings regarding the state of [Internet freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_freedom) for countries worldwide.[^cite_note-FOTN-2018-123] The countries surveyed represent a sample with a broad range of geographical diversity and levels of economic development, as well as varying levels of political and media freedom. The surveys ask a set of questions designed to measure each country's level of Internet and digital media freedom, as well as the access and openness of other digital means of transmitting information, particularly mobile phones and text messaging services. Results are presented for three areas: Obstacles to Access, Limits on Content, and Violations of User Rights. The results from the three areas are combined into a total score for a country (from 0 for best to 100 for worst) and countries are rated as "Free" (0 to 30), "Partly Free" (31 to 60), or "Not Free" (61 to 100) based on the totals.

Starting in 2009 Freedom House has produced nine editions of the report.[^cite_note-FOTN-2009-124][^cite_note-FOTN-2011-125][^cite_note-FOTN-2012-126][^cite_note-FOTN-2013-127][^cite_note-FOTN-2014-128][^cite_note-FOTN-2015-129][^cite_note-FOTN-2016-130][^cite_note-FOTN-2017-131][^cite_note-FOTN-2018-123] There was no report in 2010. The reports generally cover the period from June through May.

##### RWB *Special report on Internet Surveillance*

On 12 March 2013, [Reporters Without Borders](526054.md) published a *Special report on Internet Surveillance*.[^cite_note-RSFSRS-March2013-27] The report includes two new lists:

- a list of "State Enemies of the Internet", countries whose governments are involved in active, intrusive surveillance of news providers, resulting in grave violations of freedom of information and human rights; and

- a list of "Corporate Enemies of the Internet", companies that sell products that are liable to be used by governments to violate human rights and freedom of information.

The five "State Enemies of the Internet" named in March 2013 are: [Bahrain](18933277.md), [China](5405.md), [Iran](14653.md), [Syria](7515849.md), and [Vietnam](202354.md).[^cite_note-RSFSRS-March2013-27]

#### V-Dem Digital Societies Project

The V-Dem Digital Societies Project measures a range of questions related to internet censorship, misinformation online, and internet shutdowns.[^cite_note-137] This annual report includes 35 indicators assessing five areas: disinformation, digital media freedom, state regulation of digital media, polarization of online media, and online social cleavages.[^cite_note-Mechkova-2021-138] The data set uses V-Dem's methodology of aggregating surveys of experts from around the world.[^cite_note-Mechkova-2021-138] It has been updated each year starting in 2019, with data covering from 2000 to 2021.[^cite_note-Mechkova-2021-138] These ratings are more similar to other expert analyses like Freedom House than remotely sensed data from Access Now.[^cite_note-Fletcher-2020-139]

#### Access Now #KeepItOn

Access Now maintains an annual list of internet shutdowns, throttling, and blockages as part of the #KeepItOn project.[^cite_note-Fletcher-2020-139][^cite_note-140][^cite_note-141] These data track several features of shutdowns including their location, their duration, the particular services impacted, the government's justification for the shutdown, and actual reasons for the shutdown as reported by independent media.[^cite_note-Access_Now-2021-142] Unlike Freedom House or V-Dem, Access Now detects shutdowns using [remote sensing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remote_sensing) and then confirms these instances with reports from civil society, government, in-country volunteers, or ISPs.[^cite_note-Access_Now-2021-142][^cite_note-Fletcher-2020-139] These methods have been found to be less prone to false positives.[^cite_note-Fletcher-2020-139]

#### BBC World Service global public opinion poll

A poll of 27,973 adults in 26 countries, including 14,306 Internet users,[^cite_note-143] was conducted for the [BBC World Service](37627.md) by the international polling firm [GlobeScan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GlobeScan) using telephone and in-person interviews between 30 November 2009 and 7 February 2010. GlobeScan Chairman Doug Miller felt, overall, that the poll showed that:

Despite worries about privacy and fraud, people around the world see access to the internet as their fundamental right. They think the web is a force for good, and most don't want governments to regulate it.

[^cite_note-BBCPoll-2010-144]

Findings from the poll include:[^cite_note-BBCPoll-2010-144]

- Nearly four in five (78%) Internet users felt that the Internet had brought them greater freedom.

- Most Internet users (53%) felt that "the internet should never be regulated by any level of government anywhere".

- Opinion was evenly split between Internet users who felt that "the internet is a safe place to express my opinions" (48%) and those who disagreed (49%). Users in [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_Germany) and [France](31630837.md) agreed the least, followed by users in a highly filtered country such as [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_China), while users in [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_Egypt), [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_India) and [Kenya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunications_in_Kenya) agreed more strongly.

  [^cite_note-UNESCO-2011-6]

- The aspects of the Internet that cause the most concern include: fraud (32%), violent and explicit content (27%), threats to [privacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_privacy) (20%), state censorship of content (6%), and the extent of corporate presence (3%).

### Transparency of filtering or blocking activities

Among the countries that filter or block online content, few openly admit to or fully disclose their filtering and blocking activities. States are frequently opaque and/or deceptive about the blocking of access to political information.[^cite_note-Chadwick-2009-10] For example:

- [Saudi Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship_in_Saudi_Arabia#Censorship_on_various_platforms) and the [United Arab Emirates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunications_in_the_United_Arab_Emirates#Internet_censorship) (UAE) are among the few states that publish detailed information about their filtering practices and display a notification to the user when attempting to access a blocked website. The websites that are blocked are mostly [pornographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pornographic) or considered un-Islamic.

- In contrast, countries such as [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_China) and [Tunisia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_Tunisia) send users a false error indication. China blocks requests by users for a banned website at the [router level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_layer) and a connection error is returned, effectively preventing the user's [IP address](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IP_address) from making further [HTTP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTTP) requests for a varying time, which appears to the user as "time-out" error with no explanation. Tunisia has altered the block page functionality of [SmartFilter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SmartFilter), the commercial filtering software it uses, so that users attempting to access blocked websites receive a fake "File not found" error page.

- In [Uzbekistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_Uzbekistan), users are frequently sent block pages stating that the website is blocked because of pornography, even when the page contains no pornography. Uzbeki ISPs may also redirect users' request for blocked websites to unrelated websites, or sites similar to the banned websites, but with different information.

### Arab Spring

See also: [Internet censorship in the Arab Spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_the_Arab_Spring), [Internet in Egypt § 2011 Internet shutdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_in_Egypt#2011_Internet_shutdown), and [Free speech in the media during the Libyan civil war (2011)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_speech_in_the_media_during_the_Libyan_civil_war_\(2011\))

During the [Arab Spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Spring) of 2011, media [jihad](16203.md) (media struggle) was extensive. Internet and mobile technologies, particularly social networks such as Facebook and Twitter, played and are playing important new and unique roles in organizing and spreading the protests and making them visible to the rest of the world. An activist in Egypt tweeted, "we use Facebook to schedule the protests, Twitter to coordinate, and YouTube to tell the world".[^cite_note-149]

This successful use of digital media in turn led to increased censorship including the complete loss of Internet access for periods of time in Egypt[^cite_note-jamescowie_techdetails_Egyptfullblock-102][^cite_note-IDGnews_Egyptfullblock-103][^cite_note-150] and [Libya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_speech_in_the_media_during_the_Libyan_civil_war_\(2011\)) in 2011.[^cite_note-Libya-RWB-2011-105][^cite_note-Analysis_of_Egyptian_and_Libyan_outages-151] In Syria, the Syrian Electronic Army (SEA), an organization that operates with at least tacit support of the government, claims responsibility for defacing or otherwise compromising scores of websites that it contends spread news hostile to the Syrian government. SEA disseminates denial of service (DoS) software designed to target media websites including those of [Al Jazeera](30204052.md), [BBC News](1139893.md), Syrian satellite broadcaster [Orient TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orient_News), and Dubai-based [Al Arabiya](321645.md) TV.[^cite_note-152]

In response to the greater freedom of expression brought about by the Arab Spring revolutions in countries that were previously subject to very strict censorship, in March 2011, Reporters Without Borders moved [Tunisia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_censorship_in_Tunisia) and [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_in_Egypt#Censorship) from its "Internet enemies" list to its list of countries "under surveillance"[^cite_note-153] and in 2012 dropped [Libya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_speech_in_the_media_during_the_Libyan_civil_war) from the list entirely.[^cite_note-RWBEnemies2012-135] At the same time, there were warnings that Internet censorship might increase in other countries following the events of the Arab Spring.[^cite_note-154][^cite_note-155] However, in 2013, Libyan communication company [LTT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libya_Telecom_&_Technology) blocked the pornographic websites.[^cite_note-156] It even blocked the family-filtered videos of ordinary websites like [Dailymotion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dailymotion).[^cite_note-157]

### Russian invasion of Ukraine

During the [Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine) in 2022, Russia was reported to have blocked the internet websites [Twitter](9988187.md) and [Facebook](7529378.md). Facebook was noted as being suspended due to an objection to its policy of reviewing news stories for authenticity where they were produced by Russian state-backed media before allowing them to be published on its platform. It was subject to a total ban whereas Twitter was suspended regionally. Reports have identified that [VPN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VPN) use has enabled people to circumvent the restrictions by installing software.[^cite_note-158]

It been reported that the [European Union](9317.md) would seek to censor Russian media outlets regarded as producing propaganda.
