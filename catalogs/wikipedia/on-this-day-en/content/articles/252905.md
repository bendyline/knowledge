**Classical antiquity**, also known as the **classical era**, **classical period**, **classical age**, or simply **antiquity**,[^cite_note-1] is the period of cultural [European history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Europe) between the 8th century BC and the 5th century AD,[^cite_note-Note_1-2] following the [Greek Dark Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Dark_Ages) and being succeeded by [late antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_antiquity). It comprises the interwoven civilizations of [ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece) and [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome), known together as the [Greco-Roman world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Roman_world), which played a major role in shaping the culture of the [Mediterranean basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_basin).[^cite_note-3] It is the period during which ancient Greece and Rome flourished and had major influence throughout much of [Europe](9239.md), [North Africa](21714.md), and [West Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Asia).[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5]

Conventionally, it is often considered to begin with the earliest recorded [Epic Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeric_Greek) poetry of [Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer) (8th–7th centuries BC) and end with the [fall of the Western Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_Western_Roman_Empire) in 476 AD. Such a wide span of history and territory covers many disparate cultures and periods. *Classical antiquity* may also refer to an idealized vision among later people of what was, in [Edgar Allan Poe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Allan_Poe)'s words, "the glory that was Greece, and the grandeur that was Rome".[^cite_note-6]

The [culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture) of the [ancient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_history) [Greeks](42056.md), together with some influences from the [ancient Near East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Near_East), was the basis of art,[^cite_note-7] philosophy, society, and education in the Mediterranean and Near East until the [Roman imperial period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire). The Romans preserved, imitated, and [spread](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campaign_history_of_the_Roman_military) this culture throughout Europe, until they were able to compete with it.[^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9] This Greco-Roman cultural foundation has been immensely influential on the language, politics, law, educational systems, [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy), science, warfare, literature, historiography, ethics, rhetoric, art and architecture of both the [Western](21208200.md), and through it, the modern world.[^cite_note-10]

Surviving fragments of classical culture helped produce a revival beginning during the 14th century, which later came to be known as the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance), and various [neo-classical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoclassicism) revivals occurred during the 18th and 19th centuries.[^cite_note-11][^cite_note-12]

### Archaic period (c. 8th to c. 6th centuries BC)

Main article: [Iron Age Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age_Europe)

The earliest period of classical antiquity occurs during a time of gradual resurgence of [historical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical) sources after the [Late Bronze Age collapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Bronze_Age_collapse). The 8th and 7th centuries BC are still largely [protohistorical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protohistorical), with the earliest [Greek alphabetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Greek_alphabet) inscriptions appearing during the first half of the 8th century. The legendary poet [Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer) is usually assumed to have lived during the 8th or 7th century BC, and his lifetime is often considered as the beginning of classical antiquity. During the same period is the [traditional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional) date for the establishment of the [Ancient Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Olympic_Games), in 776 BC.

#### Phoenicians, Carthaginians and Assyrians

Main articles: [Phoenicia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenicia), [Ancient Carthage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Carthage), and [Ancient history of Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_history_of_Cyprus)



Map of [Phoenician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenicia) (in yellow) and [Greek colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Greek_colonisation) (in red) about 8th to 6th century BC.

The Phoenicians originally expanded from [ports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port) in [Canaan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canaan), by the 8th century dominating trade in the [Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean). [Carthage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carthage) was founded in 814 BC, and the Carthaginians by 700 BC had established strongholds in [Sicily](27619.md), Italy and [Sardinia](29376.md), which created conflicts of interest with [Etruria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etruria). A [stele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stele) found in [Kition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kition), [Cyprus](5593.md), commemorates the victory of King [Sargon II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sargon_II) in 709 BC over the seven kings of the island, marking an important part of the transfer of Cyprus from [Tyrian](30796.md) rule to the [Neo-Assyrian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Assyrian_Empire).[^cite_note-13][^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15][^cite_note-16]

#### Greece

Main article: [Archaic Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaic_Greece)

The Archaic period followed the [Greek Dark Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Dark_Ages), and saw significant advancements in [political theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_theory), and the beginnings of [democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy), [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy), [theatre](20913771.md), [poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetry), as well as the revitalization of the written language (which had been lost during the Dark Ages).

In pottery, the Archaic period sees the development of the [Orientalizing style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientalizing_Period), which signals a shift from the [geometric style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_style) of the later Dark Ages and the accumulation of influences derived from Egypt, [Phoenicia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenicia) and [Syria](7515849.md). Pottery styles associated with the later part of the Archaic age are the [black-figure pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black-figure_pottery), which originated in [Corinth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Corinth) during the 7th century BC and its successor, the [red-figure style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red-figure_pottery), developed by the [Andokides Painter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andokides_Painter) in about 530 BC.

#### Greek colonies

This section is an excerpt from [Greek colonisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_colonisation).\[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Greek_colonisation\&action=edit)]



Greek territories and colonies (Archaic period: 750–550 BC)

The [Greek colonisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_colonisation) was the expansion of [Archaic Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaic_Greece) across the [Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean) and the [Black Sea](3386.md), particularly during the 8th–6th centuries [BCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BCE).

The Archaic expansion differed from the [Iron Age migrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_Age_Greek_migrations) of the [Greek Dark Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Dark_Ages), in that it consisted of organised direction by *[oikistes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oikistes)* away from the originating *[metropolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolis#Etymology)* rather than the simplistic movement of tribes, which characterised the aforementioned earlier migrations. Many [colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colony), or *apoikiai* ([Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek): ἀποικία, transl. "home away from home"), that were founded during this period eventually evolved into strong [Greek city-states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polis), functioning independently of their *metropolis*.

#### Iron Age Italy



[Etruscan civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etruscan_civilization) in north of Italy, 800 BC.

The [Etruscans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etruscan_civilization) had established political control in the region by the late 7th-century BC, forming the aristocratic and monarchial elite. The Etruscans apparently lost power in the area by the late 6th-century BC, and at this time, the [Italic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italic_peoples) tribes reinvented their government by creating [republics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic), with greater restraints on the ability of individual rulers to exercise power.[^cite_note-17]

#### Roman kingdom

Main article: [Roman Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Kingdom)

According to legend, [Rome was founded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Founding_of_Rome) on 21 April 753 BC by twin descendants of the [Trojan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troy) prince [Aeneas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeneas), [Romulus and Remus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romulus_and_Remus).[^cite_note-18] As the city was bereft of women, legend says that the Latins invited the [Sabines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabines) to a festival and stole their unmarried maidens, resulting in the integration of Latins and Sabines.[^cite_note-19]

Archaeological evidence indeed shows first traces of settlement at the [Roman Forum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Forum) in the mid-8th century BC, though settlements on the [Palatine Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palatine_Hill) may date back to the 10th century BC.[^cite_note-20][^cite_note-21]

According to legend, the seventh and final king of Rome was [Tarquinius Superbus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarquinius_Superbus). As the son of [Tarquinius Priscus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarquinius_Priscus) and the son-in-law of [Servius Tullius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Servius_Tullius), Superbus was of Etruscan birth. It was during his reign that the Etruscans reached their apex of power. Superbus removed and destroyed all the Sabine shrines and altars from the [Tarpeian Rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarpeian_Rock), enraging the people of Rome. The people came to object to his rule when he failed to recognize the rape of [Lucretia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucretia), a patrician Roman, by his own son. Lucretia's kinsman, [Lucius Junius Brutus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucius_Junius_Brutus) (ancestor to [Marcus Brutus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Brutus)), summoned the Senate and had Superbus and the monarchy expelled from Rome in 510 BC. After Superbus' expulsion, the Senate in 509 BC voted to never again allow the rule of a king and reformed Rome into a [republican government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republic).

### Classical Greece (5th to 4th centuries BC)

Main article: [Classical Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Greece)



Delian League ("Athenian Empire"), just before the [Peloponnesian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peloponnesian_War) in 431 BC.

The classical period of ancient Greece corresponds to most of the 5th and 4th centuries BC, in particular, from the end of the [Athenian tyranny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyrant#Athens) in 510 BC to the [death of Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_of_Alexander_the_Great) in 323 BC. In 510, Spartan troops helped the Athenians overthrow the tyrant [Hippias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippias_\(son_of_Pisistratus\)), son of [Peisistratos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peisistratos_\(Athens\)). [Cleomenes I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleomenes_I), king of Sparta, established a pro-Spartan oligarchy conducted by [Isagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isagoras).

The [Greco-Persian Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Persian_Wars) (499–449 BC), concluded by the [Peace of Callias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_of_Callias) ended with not only the liberation of Greece, [Macedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonia_\(ancient_kingdom\)), [Thrace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrace), and [Ionia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionia) from [Persian rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire), but also with the dominance of [Athens](1216.md) in the [Delian League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delian_League), which resulted in conflict with [Sparta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparta) and the [Peloponnesian League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peloponnesian_League), resulting in the [Peloponnesian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peloponnesian_War) (431–404 BC), ending with a Spartan victory.

Greece began the 4th century with [Spartan hegemony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spartan_hegemony), but by 395 BC the Spartan rulers dismissed [Lysander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lysander) from office, and Sparta lost its naval supremacy. [Athens](1216.md), [Argos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argos,_Peloponnese), [Thebes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thebes,_Greece) and [Corinth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Corinth), the latter two of which were formerly Spartan allies, challenged Spartan dominance in the [Corinthian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corinthian_War), which ended inconclusively in 387 BC. Later, in 371 BC, the Theban generals [Epaminondas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epaminondas) and [Pelopidas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelopidas) won a victory at the [Battle of Leuctra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Leuctra). The result of this battle was the end of Spartan supremacy and the establishment of [Theban hegemony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theban_hegemony). Thebes sought to maintain its dominance until it was finally ended by the increasing power of [Macedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedon) in 346 BC.

During the reign of [Philip II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_II_of_Macedon) (359–336 BC), Macedon expanded into the territory of the [Paeonians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paeonians), the [Thracians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thracians) and the [Illyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illyrians). Philip's son, [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great), (356–323 BC) managed to briefly extend [Macedonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonia_\(ancient_kingdom\)) power not only over the central Greek city-states but also to the [Persian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_Empire), including [Egypt](8087628.md) and lands as far east as the fringes of [India](14533.md). The classical Greek period conventionally ends at the death of Alexander in 323 BC and the fragmentation of his empire, which was at this time divided among the [Diadochi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diadochi).

### Hellenistic period (323–146 BC)

Main article: [Hellenistic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period)

Greece began the Hellenistic period with the increasing power of [Macedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedon) and the conquests of [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great). [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koine_Greek) became the *[lingua franca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lingua_franca)* far beyond Greece itself, and Hellenistic culture interacted with the cultures of [Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucid_Empire), the [Kingdom of Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Israel_\(Samaria\)) and [Kingdom of Judah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Judah), [Central Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Bactrian_Kingdom) and [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_Egypt). Significant advances were made in the sciences ([geography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_geography), [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_astronomy), [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics), etc.), notably with the [followers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peripatetic_school) of [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle) ([Aristotelianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotelianism)).

The Hellenistic period ended with the increase of the [Roman Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republic) to a super-regional power during the 2nd century BC and the Roman conquest of Greece in 146 BC.

### Roman Republic (5th to 1st centuries BC)

Main article: [Roman Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republic)



The extent of the Roman Republic and Roman Empire in 218 BC (dark red), 133 BC (light red), 44 BC (orange), 14 AD (yellow), after 14 AD (green), and maximum extension under Trajan 117 (light green).

The [Republican period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republic) of Ancient Rome began with the overthrow of the [Monarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Kingdom) circa 509 BC and lasted more than 450 years until its [subversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subversion_\(politics\)) through a series of [civil wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republican_civil_wars), into the [Principate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principate) form of government and the Imperial period. During the half millennium of the Republic, Rome increased from a regional power of the [Latium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latium) to the dominant force in Italy and beyond. The unification of Italy by the Romans was a gradual process, brought about by a series of conflicts of the 4th and 3rd centuries, the [Samnite Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samnite_Wars), [Latin War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_War), and [Pyrrhic War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrrhic_War). Roman victory in the [Punic Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punic_Wars) and [Macedonian Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonian_Wars) established Rome as a super-regional power by the 2nd century BC, followed by the acquisition of [Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Greece) and [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_\(Roman_province\)). This tremendous increase of power was accompanied by economic instability and social unrest, resulting in the [Catiline conspiracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Catilinarian_conspiracy), the [Social War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_War_\(91%E2%80%9388_BC\)) and the [First Triumvirate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Triumvirate), and finally the transformation to the Roman Empire during the latter half of the 1st century BC.

### Roman Empire (1st century BC to 5th century AD)

Main article: [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire)



The extent of the Roman Empire under Trajan, AD 117.

The precise end of the Republic is disputed by modern historians;[^cite_note-22] Roman citizens of the time did not recognize that the Republic had ceased to exist. The early [Julio-Claudian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julio-Claudian_dynasty) [Emperors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Emperor) maintained that the *[res publica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Res_publica)* still existed, albeit protected by their extraordinary powers, and would eventually return to its earlier Republican form. The Roman state continued to term itself a *res publica* as long as it continued to use Latin as its official language.

Rome acquired [imperial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperialism) character *de facto* from the 130s BC with the acquisition of [Cisalpine Gaul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cisalpine_Gaul), [Illyria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illyria), [Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Greece) and [Hispania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispania), and definitely with the addition of [Iudaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iudaea_Province), [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_\(Roman_province\)) and [Gaul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Gaul) during the 1st century BC. At the time of the empire's maximal extension during the reign of [Trajan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajan) (AD 117), Rome controlled the entire [Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean) as well as Gaul, parts of [Germania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germania) and [Britannia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Britain), the [Balkans](4829.md), [Dacia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dacia), Asia Minor, the [Caucasus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caucasus), and [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia).

Culturally, the Roman Empire was significantly [Hellenized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenization), but also incorporated syncretic "eastern" traditions, such as [Mithraism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mithraism), [Gnosticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnosticism), and most notably [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origins_of_Christianity).

Classical Rome had vast differences within their family life compared to the Greeks. Fathers had great power over their children, and husbands over their wives. In fact, the word family, *familia* in Latin, actually referred to those who were subject to the authority of a male head of household. This included non-related members such as slaves and servants. By marriage, both men and women shared property. Divorce was allowed first during the first century BC and could be done by either man or woman.[^cite_note-23]

### Late antiquity (4th to 6th centuries AD)

Main articles: [Late antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_antiquity), [Migration period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Migration_period), and [Fall of the Western Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_Western_Roman_Empire)



The [Western](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Roman_Empire) and [Eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Roman_Empire) [Roman Empires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire) by 476



The Byzantine Empire in 650 after the [Arabs conquered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Muslim_conquests) the provinces of Syria and Egypt. At the same time [early Slavs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Slavs) settled in the Balkans.

The Roman Empire began to weaken as a result of the [crisis of the third century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crisis_of_the_third_century). During [Late antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_antiquity) [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Christianity) became increasingly popular, finally ousting the [Roman imperial cult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_imperial_cult) with the [Theodosian decrees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodosian_decrees) of 393. Successive invasions of [Germanic tribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_tribes) finalized the [weakening of the Western Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decline_of_the_Roman_Empire) during the 5th century, while the [Eastern Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Roman_Empire) persisted throughout the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), in a state called Romania by its citizens, and designated the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire) by later historians. Hellenistic philosophy was succeeded by the continued development of [Platonism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonism) and [Epicureanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicureanism), with Neoplatonism in due course influencing the [theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_theology) of the Christian [Church Fathers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_Fathers).

Many writers have attempted to name a specific date for the symbolic "end" of antiquity, with the most prominent dates being the deposing of the last [Western Roman Emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Roman_Emperor) in 476,[^cite_note-24][^cite_note-25] the closing of the last [Platonic Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_Academy) in Athens by the [Eastern Roman emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire) [Justinian I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justinian_I) in 529,[^cite_note-26] and the [conquest of much of the Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_conquest_of_the_Maghreb) by the new [Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim) faith from 634 to 718.[^cite_note-Pirenne-27] These Muslim conquests, of Syria (637), Egypt (639), Cyprus (654), North Africa (665), Hispania (718), Southern Gaul (720), Crete (820), Sicily (827), Malta (870), as well as the sieges of the Eastern Roman capital ([first in 674–78](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Constantinople_\(674%E2%80%9378\)) and then in [717–718](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Constantinople_\(717%E2%80%93718\))) severed the economic, cultural, and political links that had traditionally united the classical cultures around the Mediterranean, ending antiquity (see [Pirenne Thesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pirenne_Thesis)).[^cite_note-Pirenne-27]

The original Roman Senate continued to express decrees into the late 6th century, and the last Eastern Roman emperor to use [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) as the language of his court in Constantinople was emperor [Maurice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_\(emperor\)), who reigned until 602. The overthrow of Maurice by his mutinying Danube army commanded by [Phocas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phocas) resulted in the Slavic invasion of the Balkans and the weakening of Balkan and Greek urban culture (resulting in the flight of Balkan Latin speakers to the mountains, see [Origin of the Romanians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origin_of_the_Romanians)), and also provoked the [Byzantine–Sasanian War of 602–628](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine%E2%80%93Sasanian_War_of_602%E2%80%93628) in which all the great eastern cities except Constantinople were lost. The resulting turmoil did not end until the [Muslim conquests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Muslim_conquests) of the 7th century finalized the irreversible loss of all the largest Eastern Roman imperial cities besides the capital itself. The emperor [Heraclius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraclius) in [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople), who reigned during this period, conducted his court in Greek, not Latin, though Greek had always been an administrative language of the eastern Roman regions. Eastern-Western associations weakened with the ending of the [Byzantine Papacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Papacy).

The Eastern Roman Empire's capital city [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople) remained the only unconquered large urban site of the original Roman Empire, as well as being the largest city in Europe. Yet many classical books, sculptures, and technologies survived there along with classical Roman cuisine and scholarly traditions, well into the Middle Ages, when much of it was "rediscovered" by visiting Western crusaders. Indeed, the inhabitants of Constantinople continued to refer to themselves as Romans, as did their eventual conquerors in 1453, the [Ottomans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Turks) (see [Romaioi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romaioi) and [Rûm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%BBm).) The classical scholarship and culture that was still preserved in Constantinople were brought by refugees fleeing its conquest in 1453 and helped to begin the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance) (see [Greek scholars in the Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_scholars_in_the_Renaissance)).

Ultimately, it was a slow, complex, and graduated change of the socio-economic structure in [European history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_history) that resulted in the changeover between classical antiquity and medieval society and no specific date can truly exemplify that.

## Political revivalism

Main articles: [Carolingian Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carolingian_Renaissance), [Ottonian Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottonian_Renaissance), [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance), [Classicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classicism), and [Legacy of the Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legacy_of_the_Roman_Empire)

In politics, the late Roman conception of the Empire as a universal state, commanded by one supreme divinely appointed ruler, united with Christianity as a universal religion likewise headed by a supreme [patriarch](175409.md), proved very influential, even after the disappearance of imperial authority in the West. This tendency reached its maximum when [Charlemagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlemagne) was [crowned](226191.md) "Roman Emperor" in the year 800, an act which resulted in the formation of the [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire). The notion that an [emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor) is a [monarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarch) who outranks a king dates from this period. In this political ideal, there would always be a Roman Empire, a state the jurisdiction of which extended through the entire civilized western world.

That model continued to exist in Constantinople for the entirety of the Middle Ages, where the [Byzantine Emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Emperor) was considered the sovereign of the entire Christian world. The [Patriarch of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecumenical_Patriarch_of_Constantinople) was the Empire's highest-ranked cleric, but even he was subordinate to the emperor, who was "God's Viceregent on Earth". The Greek-speaking Byzantines and their descendants continued to call themselves "[Romioi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romioi)" until the creation of a new Greek state in 1832.

After the [capture of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Constantinople) in 1453, the Russian [Czars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czar_of_Russia) (a title derived from *Caesar)* claimed the Byzantine legacy as the champion of [Orthodoxy](10186.md); [Moscow](19004.md) was described as the "[Third Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Rome)", and the Czars ruled as divinely appointed Emperors into the 20th century.

Despite the fact that the Western Roman secular authority disappeared entirely in Europe, it still left traces. The [Papacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papacy) and the [Catholic Church](606848.md) in particular maintained Latin language, culture, and literacy for centuries; to this day the popes are termed *[Pontifex Maximus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontifex_Maximus)* which during the classical period was a title belonging to the emperor, and the ideal of [Christendom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christendom) continued the legacy of a united European civilization even after its political unity had ended.

The political idea of an Emperor in the West to match the Emperor in the East continued after the Western Roman Empire's collapse; it was revived by the coronation of [Charlemagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlemagne) in 800; the self-described [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire) ruled central Europe until 1806.

The [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance) idea that the classical Roman virtues had been lost as a result of medievalism was especially powerful in European politics of the 18th and 19th centuries. Reverence for Roman republicanism was strong among the [Founding Fathers of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Founding_Fathers_of_the_United_States) and the Latin American revolutionaries; the Americans described their new government as a *republic* (from *[res publica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Res_publica)*) and gave it a *Senate* and a *President* (another Latin term), rather than use available English terms like *commonwealth* or *parliament*.

Similarly in [Revolutionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution) and [Napoleonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_French_Empire) France, republicanism and Roman martial virtues were promoted by the state, as can be seen in the architecture of the [Panthéon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panth%C3%A9on,_Paris), the [Arc de Triomphe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_de_Triomphe), and the paintings of [Jacques-Louis David](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques-Louis_David). During the revolution, France transitioned from kingdom to republic to dictatorship to Empire (complete with Imperial Eagles), a process that the Romans had experienced centuries earlier.

## Cultural legacy

Main articles: [Classicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classicism) and [Classical studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_studies)



[Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato) and [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle) walking and disputing. Detail from [Raphael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raphael)'s *[The School of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_School_of_Athens)* (1509–1511).

*Classical antiquity* is a general term for a long period of cultural [history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History). Such a wide sampling of history and territory covers many rather disparate cultures and periods. "Classical antiquity" often refers to an idealized vision of later people, of what was, in [Edgar Allan Poe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Allan_Poe)'s words, "the glory that was [Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece), the grandeur that was [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome)!" During the 18th and 19th centuries AD, reverence for classical antiquity was much greater in [Europe](9239.md) and the [United States](3434750.md) than it is now. Respect for the ancient people of Greece and Rome affected [politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics), [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy), [sculpture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sculpture), [literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literature), [theatre](20913771.md), [education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education), [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture), and [sexuality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_sex).

[Epic poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_poetry) in [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) continued to be written and circulated well into the 19th century. [John Milton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Milton) and even [Arthur Rimbaud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Rimbaud) received their first poetic educations in Latin. Genres like epic poetry, [pastoral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastoral) verse, and the frequent use of characters and themes from [Greek mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mythology) affected Western literature greatly. In architecture, there have been several [Greek Revivals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Revival), which seem more inspired in retrospect by Roman architecture than Greek. [Washington, DC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington,_DC) has many large [marble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marble) buildings with façades made to look like [Greek temples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_temple), with columns constructed in the [classical orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_orders) of architecture.

The philosophy of St. [Thomas Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas) was derived largely from that of [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle), despite the intervening change in [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion) from [Hellenic Polytheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_religion) to [Christianity](5211.md).[^cite_note-28] Greek and Roman authorities such as [Hippocrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocrates) and [Galen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galen) formed the basis of the practice of [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine) even longer than Greek thought prevailed in philosophy. In the [French](5843419.md) [theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theater), playwrights such as [Molière](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moli%C3%A8re) and [Racine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Racine) wrote plays on mythological or classical historical subjects and subjected them to the strict rules of the [classical unities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_unities) derived from Aristotle's *[Poetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetics_\(Aristotle\))*. The desire to [dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dance) in a manner allegedly similar to the manner of the ancient Greeks caused [Isadora Duncan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isadora_Duncan) to create her brand of [ballet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballet).
