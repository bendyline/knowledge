**Anti-terrorism legislation** are [laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law) aimed at fighting [terrorism](30636.md). They usually, if not always, follow specific [bombings](47628.md) or [assassinations](2963.md). [Anti-terrorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-terrorism) legislation usually includes specific amendments allowing the state to bypass its own legislation when fighting terrorism-related crimes, under alleged grounds of necessity.

Because of this suspension of regular procedure, such legislation is sometimes criticized as a form of *[lois scélérates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lois_sc%C3%A9l%C3%A9rates)* which may unjustly repress all kinds of popular protests. Critics often allege that anti-terrorism legislation endangers democracy by creating a [state of exception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_of_exception) that allows [authoritarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authoritarian) style of government.

Although some legal scholars have long expressed concern that the wording of anti-terrorism laws would allow them to be used against non-violent [direct action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_action) protestors,[^cite_note-Mead-1][^cite_note-2][^cite_note-3][^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5] the proscription of UK-based direct action group [Palestine Action](67777543.md) was described by one expert as "unprecedented".[^cite_note-Mead-1] In some countries, direct action is expressly excluded from the definition of terrorism.[^cite_note-6]

## International conventions related to terrorism and counter-terrorism cases

Terrorism has been on the international agenda since 1934, when the [League of Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Nations), predecessor of the [United Nations](31769.md), began the elaboration of a convention for the prevention and punishment of terrorism.[^cite_note-7] Although the convention was eventually adopted in 1937, it never came into force.

Today, there are 15 counter-terrorism international conventions in force. They were developed under the auspices of the United Nations and its specialized agencies and the [International Atomic Energy Agency](14984.md) (IAEA). Moreover, on 8 September 2006, the [UN General Assembly](31957.md) adopted a "Global Counter-Terrorism Strategy".[^cite_note-8]

### Italy

Further information: [History of the Italian Republic](1161302.md)

Italy passed various anti-terrorist laws during the "[Years of Lead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Years_of_Lead_\(Italy\))" (*anni di piombo*) in the 1970s.

The Reale Act was adopted on 22 May 1975. It allowed the police to carry out searches and arrest persons without being mandated by an investigative judge. Interrogation could take place without the presence of a lawyer. Critics underlined that this contradicted article 3 of the [Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Italy) on equality before the law.[^cite_note-Reale\&al-15]

Preventive detention was fixed before 1970 to two years, for a possible sentence going between 20 years to perpetuity, while it was limited to one year for charges of crimes leading to a sentence of less than 20 years. It passed to four years after 1970. A [decree](322949.md)-law of 11 April 1974 authorized a four-year detention until the first judgment, six years until the appeal, and eight years until the definitive judgment. In case of indictment for "acts of terrorism," the preventive detention was extended to twelve years.[^cite_note-Reale\&al-15]

The [Cossiga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Cossiga) decree-law was passed on 15 December 1979. It prolonged the length of [preventive detention](2896261.md) relative to terrorism suspicions and allowed [wiretaps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiretaps). Critics have pointed out that this violated articles 15 and 27 of the Constitution.[^cite_note-Reale\&al-15] The Cossiga decree-law also created the status of *[pentito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentito)* (officially "collaborators of justice"): those accused of terrorism crimes and who accepted of [confessing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confession_\(law\)) them and of informing the authorities about their accomplices could be liberated.

Law n°191 of May 21, 1978, called "[Moro law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldo_Moro)", and law n°15 of February 6, 1980, were ratifications by the assembly of decrees of emergency enacted by the executive power, respectively on March 28, 1978, and on December 15, 1979.[^cite_note-16]

## Anti-terrorism legislation in civil law countries (outside the European Union)

### China

China passed [Counterterrorism Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterterrorism_Law_of_the_People's_Republic_of_China) on December 27, 2015.[^cite_note-30]

The Anti-terrorism Law has 10 chapters and 97 articles, taking effect on January 1, 2016. Before the promulgation of Anti-terrorism Law, though anti-terrorism laws can be found in the Criminal Law or some other emergency action regulations, there was not a systematic legal structure or source for anti-terrorism actions.

The most controversial provisions of the Anti-terrorism Law are the numerous new restrictions on the operation of internet and technology based companies, among which Article 21 says that an internet operator or provider is obligated to verify the identity of each user and shall refuse to provide services to a user who refuses such verification or fails to provide a clear identity. Any company who fails to meet such obligation may face fines, orders of rectification and its management and executives may face fines and even detentions from 5 to 15 days. In addition, Article 18 says any telecommunication operator or internet provider shall provide technology access and source code or other de-encryption support and assistance for the purposes of preventing and investigating terrorism by Public Safety Department or National Security Department.

### Chile

Human Rights Watch has criticized the Chilean government for inappropriately using anti-terrorist legislation against [Mapuche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mapuche) groups involved in land conflicts. While the legislation in question was originally enacted by the Pinochet dictatorship, the democratic governments that have followed have actually increased its severity. Human Rights Watch has expressed special concern that the current version of the law lists arson as a "terrorist" offence. This has allowed the application of the law against Mapuche vandals. While recognizing that crimes have certainly been committed, the international organization believes that they are not comparable to terrorist acts.[^cite_note-31] In 2018, Amnesty International urged Chile to stop utilizing the [Anti-Terrorism Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Terrorist_Law_\(Chile\)?action=edit\&redlink=1) to prosecute Mapuche dissidents affirmed all people's [right to a fair trial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_to_a_fair_trial).[^cite_note-32]

### El Salvador

[El Salvador](9356.md), presided by [Antonio Saca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_Saca) of the right-wing [ARENA](542175.md) party, had adopted in September 2006 an anti-terrorist law. All major parties, including the [FMLN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FMLN), have criticized the law, claiming it could be used against social movements[^cite_note-33]

The government first attempted to use the law against illegal street vendors who violently resisted removal by the police. These charges did not result in convictions.

In July 2007, the Salvadoran government charged fourteen people with acts of terrorism for their participation and/or association with a demonstration against privatization of the nation's water system. Charges were dismissed against one of those arrested. The remainder, known as the Suchitito 13, were released, but continued to face charges under the Special Law Against Terrorist Acts.[^cite_note-34][^cite_note-35] The charges were reduced to "disorderly conduct" in early February 2008 and then completely dropped later in the month.

### Israel

Many years Israel has relied on mandatory regulations as a legal basis for fighting terrorism and for convicting terrorists both in civilian and military courts. In 2016, after a long and thorough work by the [Minister of Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Justice_\(Israel\)) [Ayelet Shaked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayelet_Shaked), the [Knesset](74656.md) passed a comprehensive law against terrorism, forbidding any kind of terrorism and support of terrorism, and setting severe punishments for terrorists. The law also regulates legal efforts against terrorism.[^cite_note-Israel-anti-terror-law-36]

### Peru

Peru adopted anti-terrorist laws in 1992, under [Alberto Fujimori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberto_Fujimori)'s presidency. The laws were criticized by Amnesty International, who declared in its 2002 report that "Detainees falsely charged with 'terrorism-related' offences in previous years remained held. 'Anti-terrorism' legislation which had resulted in unfair trials since its introduction in 1992 remained in force. Members of the security forces accused of human rights violations continued to have their cases transferred to military courts."[^cite_note-37] [Lori Berenson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lori_Berenson), a US citizen serving a 20-year prison term in Peru, has been condemned in virtue of these laws, on charges of collaboration with the [Túpac Amaru Revolutionary Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C3%BApac_Amaru_Revolutionary_Movement).

### Philippines

Main articles: [Human Security Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Security_Act) and [Anti-Terrorism Act of 2020](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Terrorism_Act_of_2020)

See also: [Press freedom in the Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Press_freedom_in_the_Philippines)

The [Human Security Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Security_Act) of 2007, signed into law by President [Gloria Macapagal Arroyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gloria_Macapagal_Arroyo) and effective since July 2007, officially aimed at tackling militants in the southern Philippines, including the [Abu Sayyaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Sayyaf) group, which has links to [al-Qaeda](1921.md) and has been blamed for bombings and kidnappings in the region.[^cite_note-bbc-38] Under the law, three days of warrantless detention are authorized,[^cite_note-bbc-38] although arresting officers are obliged to immediately inform a judge about the arrest.[^cite_note-bbc-38] Furthermore, detained suspects are entitled to see a lawyer, a priest, a doctor, or family members.[^cite_note-bbc-38] The law allows eavesdropping on suspects[^cite_note-ABSPhil-39] as well as access to bank accounts for authorities.[^cite_note-bbc-38] Convictions could result in 40-year prison sentences, but compensations are provided for in case of [miscarriage of justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miscarriage_of_justice).[^cite_note-bbc-38][^cite_note-ABSPhil-39] Terrorism was defined by Section 3 as "sowing and creating a condition of widespread and extraordinary fear and panic among the populace in order to coerce the government to give in to an unlawful demand",[^cite_note-ABSPhil-39] a formulation criticized by Wilson Fortaleza, national president and third nominee of the labor party-list group [Sanlakas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanlakas), who claimed the law could be used to crush political dissent.[^cite_note-ABSPhil-39]

Human rights group [Karapatan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karapatan) said that "Under Marcos Jr., the Anti-Terrorism Act of 2020 and the Terrorism Financing Prevention and Suppression Act of 2012 have been aggressively enforced not to protect the public, but to persecute critics and suppress dissent".[^cite_note-40] Human Rights Watch had previously criticized the Anti-Terrorism Act signed by President Rodrigo Duterte in 2020, describing the law as a "human rights disaster in the making" that opens the door to "arbitrary arrests and long prison sentences for people or representatives of organizations that have displeased the president".[^cite_note-\:7-41] In 2026, journalist Frenchie Mae Cumpio and activist Marielle Domequil were convicted of financing terrorism. Press freedom advocates described the conviction as a "travesty of justice".[^cite_note-\:4-42]

### Indonesia

Following the October [2002 Bali bombings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2002_Bali_bombings), [Indonesia](14579.md) adopted Government Regulation in Lieu of Law 1/2002. Under the Indonesian legal system, a Government Regulation in Lieu of Law has the same power as a parliament-enacted legislation, except that it can only be issued under emergency circumstances and is subject to review by the next parliamentary session. Nevertheless, Indonesian Parliament enacted this emergency regulation into Law 15/2003. As since, Indonesia has an anti-terror legislation with strong political support. The Anti-Terror Law cultivates many criticism, however. The Law contained provisions which can circumvent normal criminal proceeding such as quick and long detention. One of the main contentious provision of the Law is that it allows Intelligence Information to be used as a preliminary evidence that can be used for apprehending a suspect. The role of Intelligence Information as evidence has been a subject of hot debate in Indonesia.[^cite_note-indolawreport-43]

### Turkey

Further information: [Human rights in Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights_in_Turkey)

Article 8 of the [Anti-Terror Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Terror_Law_of_Turkey) (Law 3713; April 1991), slightly amended in 1995 and later repealed,[^cite_note-44] imposed three-year prison sentences for "separatist propaganda." Despite its name, the Anti-Terror Law punished many non-violent offences.[^cite_note-HRW02-45] Pacifists have been imprisoned under Article 8. For example, publisher Fatih Tas was prosecuted in 2002 under Article 8 at Istanbul [State Security Court](2963429.md) for translating and publishing writings by [Noam Chomsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noam_Chomsky), summarizing the history of the [human rights of Kurdish people in Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights_of_Kurdish_people_in_Turkey); he was acquitted, however, in February 2002.[^cite_note-HRW02-45]

State Security Courts were transformed into Heavy Penal Courts following June 2004 reforms to the 1982 [Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_of_Turkey), enacted following the [1980 military coup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1980_Turkish_coup_d'%C3%A9tat).

As of 2008, detainees arrested under the Anti-Terror Law have access to lawyers at the very beginning of their detention.[^cite_note-46]
