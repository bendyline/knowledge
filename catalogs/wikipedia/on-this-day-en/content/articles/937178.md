The **Bombardier Challenger 600 series** is a family of [business jets](247003.md) developed by [Canadair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadair) after a [Bill Lear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Lear) concept, and then produced from 1986 by its new owner, [Bombardier Aerospace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombardier_Aerospace).  At the end of 1975, Canadair began funding the development of LearStar 600, and then bought the design for a wide-cabin business jet in April 1976. On 29 October, the programme was launched, backed by the [Canadian federal government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_federal_government), and designed to comply with new [FAR part 25](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Aviation_Regulations#Part_25) standards.

In March 1977, it was renamed the Challenger 600 after Bill Lear was phased out, and the original [conventional tail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conventional_tail) was changed for a [T-tail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-tail) among other developments. The first prototype was rolled out on 25 May 1978, and performed its maiden flight on 8 November. The [flight test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_test) program saw a deadly crash on 3 April 1980, but [Transport Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Transport_\(Canada\)) approved the CL-600 [type certification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_certification) on 10 August 1980.

In 1986, Canadair was close to [bankruptcy](4695.md) and was bought by Bombardier. The jet was later stretched into the [Bombardier CRJ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombardier_CRJ100/200) regional airliner, introduced on 19 October 1992, and the longer range [Global Express](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombardier_Global_Express), introduced in July 1999. The 500th Challenger was rolled out in May 2000, and the 1000th was delivered to [NetJets](2299551.md) in December 2015. By October 2018, 1,066 aircraft had been built.

The Challenger is a low-wing jet powered by two turbofans mounted in aft fuselage pods, with a [supercritical wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercritical_wing) and a stand-up cabin with two seating sections.  The original [Lycoming ALF 502](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycoming_ALF_502) turbofans were replaced by a pair of [General Electric CF34s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric_CF34) on the CL-601, which also gained [winglets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winglets), and first flew on 10 April 1982. Subsequent variants have updated systems, avionics, and higher weights.

## Development

### Origins



Initial LearStar 600 concept, with conventional tail

Around 1974, American aviation inventor [Bill Lear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Lear) conceptualised the LearStar 600, a low-wing, long-distance [business jet](247003.md), which was powered by a pair of [Garrett TFE731](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garrett_TFE731)-1 [geared turbofan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geared_turbofan) engines and equipped with a [supercritical wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercritical_wing).[^cite_note-Upton2007-2][^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3] Lear lacked the capabilities to launch such an aircraft, thus sought out other agencies to collaborate with to both produce and sell it, including the [Canadian](5042916.md) aerospace manufacturer [Canadair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadair). According to authors Ron Picklet and Larry Milberry, Canadair's top management were of the opinion that Lear's concept was sketchy at best.[^cite_note-Pickler_and_Milberry_p._263-4] Lear did not have an expert grasp of aeronautical engineering; so far, he had only been able to pay an American aeronautical consultant to undertake very preliminary design explorations.[^cite_note-5]

Following a study, contrasting the proposed Learstar against rivals such as the [Lockheed Jetstar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_Jetstar), [Dassault Falcon 50](3098320.md), and [Grumman Gulfstream II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grumman_Gulfstream_II), Canadair decided to give its backing to the idea near the end of 1975.[^cite_note-Upton2007-2][^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3] According to aerospace industry publication *[Flight International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_International)*, the programme was viewed by many Canadians as a step towards developing a privately driven high-technology aviation industry that would compete at a global level.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3] Perhaps more importantly, the Canadian government had issued a demand that Canadair become self-sufficient, thus the company wanted to depend less upon [subcontracting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subcontractor) arrangements with other firms, such as France's [Dassault Aviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dassault_Aviation) and America's [Boeing](18933266.md), or providing support packages for existing aircraft for which they had already ended production, such as the [CF-5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadair_CF-5) fighter. Canadair felt a need to prove its ability to independently develop original high-tech projects at this time.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3]

Canadair planned to use Lear's name and skills at self-promotion to secure extensive financial guarantees for a business-jet project from the Canadian federal government.[^cite_note-Pickler_and_Milberry_p._263-4] This proved an effective choice: Future Prime Minister [Jean Chrétien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Chr%C3%A9tien) specifically refers to the effect of personal contact with Lear on his decision to direct financial support to Canadair's program.[^cite_note-6] At the time of these events, Chrétien was successively president of the Treasury Board, minister of Industry, Trade, and Commerce, and minister of Finance, in the Canadian government. Due to the use of [letters of comfort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letter_of_comfort_\(contract_law\)), the extent of the ministry's financial commitments for Canadair could be kept from parliament and the public for several years.[^cite_note-7] These financial guarantees were later used as an academic example of insufficient monitoring and lax controls in government support of industry.[^cite_note-8]

In April 1976, Canadair acquired the LearStar 600 concept. By then it was 63 ft (19 m) long, and 53.3 ft (16.2 m) wide, and capable of a maximum speed of Mach 0.85 and a range of 7,240 km (3,910 nmi). As an executive jet, it had sufficient capacity for 14 passengers. In a [freighter](2375692.md) configuration, it had a 3,400 kg (7,500 lb) payload capacity, loaded and unloaded through a forward door. As a [commuter airliner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commuter_airliner), it could seat up to 30 passengers in a 2–1 seating configuration.[^cite_note-Upton2007-2] Canadair developed the design into a large airframe, furnished with a new supercritical wing design, new avionics and engines, as well as for compliance with new [FAR part 25](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Aviation_Regulations#Part_25) standards. The configuration was frozen in August and a 1/25 model was tested in the [National Aeronautical Establishment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Research_Council_of_Canada) transonic [wind tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_tunnel).[^cite_note-Upton2007-2] Reportedly, in excess of 1,800 hours of wind tunnel testing were performed upon the supercritical wing alone.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3]

### Launch

Backed by the federal government, the programme was launched on 29 October 1976 with firm orders and deposits for 53 aircraft.[^cite_note-Upton2007-2] Within the next two years, roughly 2,500 employees were involved in designing the aircraft.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3] Changes to the original Learstar configuration had been made on the run up to the launch, such as the [conventional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conventional_tail) tailplane being substituted for a [T-tail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-tail) counterpart after the former was found to be in the path of the engine's exhaust flow, the relocation of fuel storage to the wings, and multiple increases of the aircraft's gross weight.[^cite_note-Upton2007-2][^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3] Following disagreements over the direction of the programme, Bill Lear was phased out of involvement. In March 1977, the aircraft was renamed the Challenger 600.[^cite_note-Upton2007-2] Reportedly, following his disassociation with the venture, Lear referred to Canadair's revised design as *Fat Albert*.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3] Following Lear's death in May 1978, Canadair paid an estimated \$25 million to his estate for his contribution to the programme.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3]

Due to the expansion of the design, the original powerplant configuration became untenable. Engine manufacturer [Lycoming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycoming_Engines) proposed developing a new model, the [Lycoming ALF 502](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycoming_ALF_502)L, which Canadair's design team accepted for the enlarged Challenger, and around which drew up its general arrangement.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3] The type's wide cargo door had been designed in response to the needs of [FedEx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FedEx), the type's original launch customer, having placed an order for 25 aircraft.[^cite_note-Upton2007-2] FedEx had experienced problems with the [General Electric CF34](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric_CF34) engines, and favoured the Lycoming ALF 502D, instead. Those later had delivery troubles and lacked performance.[^cite_note-Upton2007-2] Reportedly, FedEx converted most of its orders into the Challenger's stretched version, intending to carry up to 12,500 lb of freight.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3] FedEx ultimately opted to cancel its orders due to the US [Airline Deregulation Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airline_Deregulation_Act), and the specific aircraft that were already in production were sold to other customers instead.[^cite_note-Upton2007-2]

By the spring of 1977, Canadair had received over 70 firm orders and had begun constructing three [prototypes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prototypes).[^cite_note-Upton2007-2] A \$70 million loan was borrowed from European sources to help finance the program, which lessened the financial burden on the Canadian government.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3] A full-scale fuselage [mockup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mockup) was displayed at the 1977 [Paris Air Show](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Air_Show) before a European and North American tour. 106 were sold by the end of 1977.[^cite_note-Upton2007-2] In late 1977, in the face of criticism that the project would not fulfil the performance guarantees made, Canadair stated that the first flight was to occur in 1978, and that initial deliveries were scheduled for September 1979. *Flight International* noted that even prior to the prototype's first flight, the type had already made a noticeable impact upon the competition, including the launch of the [Cessna Citation III](9374265.md) and [Grumman Gulfstream III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grumman_Gulfstream_III).[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3]

By early March 1978, the first prototype was almost finished and the assembly of the two other had debuted.[^cite_note-Upton2007-2] Destined to control [handling qualities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handling_qualities) and test flight performance, it was formally rolled out on 25 May 1978. 116 orders were confirmed 19 months after go-ahead.[^cite_note-Upton2007-2] By this point, production [jigs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jig_\(tool\)) allowing for a production rate of up to seven Challengers per month had been established, ready for quantity production to proceed.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3] Airframe [structural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural) testing began in February 1979. Operational test cycling started in December 1979, simulating 72,638 [flight hours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_hours) by February 1985, while its predicted lifetime was 30,000 hours.[^cite_note-Upton2007-2]

### Flight test phase



The third prototype was reused as the ACT [fly-by-wire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fly-by-wire) demonstrator.

[^cite_note-Upton2007-2]

On 8 November 1978, the prototype aircraft took off for its [maiden flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maiden_flight) from [Montreal, Quebec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montreal,_Quebec). The [flight test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_test) and certification program were conducted at [Mojave Kern County Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mojave_Air_and_Space_Port) instead of Canada due to better weather.[^cite_note-Upton2007-2] The second and third prototypes first flew during 1979. A test flight on 3 April 1980 in the Mojave Desert resulted in disaster. The aircraft crashed due to the failure of the release mechanism to detach the recovery chute after a [deep stall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_stall), killing one of the test pilots. The other test pilot and the flight test engineer [parachuted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parachute) to safety.[^cite_note-9]

The CL-600-1A11 [type certification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_certification) was approved by [Transport Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_Canada) in August 1980,[^cite_note-10] and by the US [Federal Aviation Administration](11186.md) in November 1980.[^cite_note-11]

The program cost was C\$1.5 billion (US\$1.1 billion at the time).[^cite_note-12]

### Further development

In 1980, the first production model of the Challenger series, the CL-600, entered service with end customers.[^cite_note-Flight25Nov2018-13] Early marketing of the type by Canadair typically contrasted the more spacious cabin offered by the Challenger against its competitors, which typically had more narrow fuselages, and therefore cramped conditions, as well as the type's fuel economy. By 1982, while only 10 aircraft had entered service, the company had begun publicising a new model of the aircraft, the CL-601, which was to be powered by a pair of [General Electric CF34](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric_CF34) [turbofan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbofan) engines in place of the original models' Lycoming units. On 10 April 1982, the CL-601 performed its maiden flight.[^cite_note-Flight29May1982-14] According to *Flight International*, the decision to adopt the CF34 engine for the new model was responsible for generating a substantial boom in Challenger sales.[^cite_note-Flight10oct2018-15]

According to *Flight International*, the slow initial sales of the Challenger heavily contributed to the near-[bankruptcy](4695.md) of Canadair, which was only avoided by the purchase of the company by Bombardier in 1986.[^cite_note-Flight25Nov2018-13] Bombardier elected not only to continue production of the type, but also to finance the development of new models and derivatives. This choice was aided by Canadair's design decision to enable the Challenger to be readily stretched from the onset.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3] As of October 2018, the best-selling variant of the Challenger series has been the CL-604, which was launched in 1995. The 500th aircraft was rolled out in May 2000.[^cite_note-Flight10oct2018-15][^cite_note-16] The 1000th, a 650, was delivered to [NetJets](2299551.md) in December 2015.[^cite_note-17]

According to *Flight International*, the Challenger 600 has been a foothold in the market for Bombardier, allowing them to more easily develop further business jets, such as the [Bombardier Global Express](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombardier_Global_Express). Another direct derivative of the Challenger series has been the [Bombardier CRJ100 series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombardier_CRJ100/200), a larger [regional airliner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_airliner). The publication commented that the Challenger family "appears to have a solid future", observing a production rate of two aircraft per month throughout 2018.[^cite_note-Flight25Nov2018-13]

## Design



The Challenger stand-up, flat floor cabin



The Challenger (here a [Royal Canadian Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Canadian_Air_Force) CC-144) has a [swept wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swept_wing), a [T-tail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-tail) and two aft-mounted [turbofans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbofan)

The Challenger is a twin-engined business jet, described by *Flight International* as being a "miniaturised twinjet airliner in every respect".[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3] While the Challenger is generally similar in configuration to previous aircraft of its type, some of its features stand out; for example, a widened fuselage allows a "walk-about cabin". The Challenger was also one of the first business jets to be designed with a [supercritical wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercritical_airfoil). The wing was referred to by Canadair as being one of the aircraft's most advanced features.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3] It is also capable of performing short takeoffs while maintaining the speed and comfort levels normally associated with larger jetliners.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3]

The Challenger's wing has been referred to as being a modified [NACA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NACA) symmetrical aerofoil.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3] Akin to other supercritical wings, it features a rounded [leading edge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leading_edge), an inverted [camber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camber_\(aerodynamics\)), a blunt [trailing edge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trailing_edge) and scalloping of the underside. The twin-spar wing box structure spans the entire length of the wing and is compartmentalised to form five internal fuel tanks; these tanks can accommodate up to 14,661 lb of fuel, nearly half the aircraft's empty weight.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3]

The skins of the wings are produced using large milling machines, which in 1978 were claimed to be superior to anything else in North America. Many elements, such as the flaps, [ailerons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aileron), and leading edge, feature conventional construction. Several parts, including the wing/fuselage fairing, flap shrouds, and wingtips, are [moulded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molding_\(process\)) out of [Kevlar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevlar), as are other elements of the aircraft.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3]

The original CL-600 Challenger was powered by two Lycoming ALF 502L turbofan engines, which were developed specifically for the Challenger.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3] Subsequent models adopted other powerplants, including the General Electric CF34 engine. The engines are mounted on the rear fuselage close to the aircraft's tail, providing smooth airflow to the engines even when flown at high [angles of attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_of_attack), although this was in a lower position than the original LearStar concept had placed them to mitigate unfavourable pitch control characteristics.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3] The engines are fitted with [thrust reversers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrust_reverser) to decrease landing distances. An [auxiliary power unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auxiliary_power_unit) is also present for starting the engines and providing [air conditioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_conditioning) within the cabin while on the ground.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3]

The fuselage comprises three sections — the nose, centre, and tail — which are manufactured separately in their own jigs and joined later on in the production process.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3] It has been designed to be [pressurised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressurization) at a maximum differential of 9.3 lb/sq in. Various cutouts are present across the fuselage to accommodate features such as a large main door on the port side of the aircraft forward of the wing, multiple regulation-compliant [emergency exits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergency_exit), a baggage hatch on the port-side aft of the wing, and numerous windows.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3]

The fuselage diameter was designed to accommodate an unobstructed cabin floor, a cabin height of 6 ft 1 in in the centre section, and space for the [wing box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wing_box), underfloor integral fuel tanks, air ducts, and various control cabling. It was also designed to easily accommodate Canadair's early plans to stretch the fuselage, for which equal-length plugs are installed fore and aft of the centre section to greatly increase the Challenger's capacity.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3]

Various avionics are present in the Challenger. As standard, the CL-600 is furnished with a dual-channel [Sperry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sperry_Corporation) SPZ-600 [automatic flight control system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_control_system), incorporating a [flight director](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_director_\(aeronautics\)) and [air data computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_data_computer); more typical to larger commercial aircraft, this system is certifiable for conducting Category 3A automatic landings.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3] The flight control system features significant redundancy, including three individual [hydraulic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulics) systems; even with complete failure and the loss of one [actuator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actuator), a viable level of assisted control over the key flight surfaces remains present. [Weather radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weather_radar) and [Marconi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marconi)-built solid-state instrument displays are supplied as standard, as well as a [Collins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockwell_Collins)-built radio set; optional long-range, radio-based equipment, such as a [HF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_frequency) radio set and [VHF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Very_high_frequency) navigational aids can be installed.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3]

In a standard executive aircraft configuration, the cabin is divided between the forward [galley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galley_\(kitchen\)), and two seating sections, which are typically fitted with a four-chair club section followed by either a conference grouping area or divans, along with a lavatory at the aft end.[^cite_note-BCA25aug2016-18][^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3] The chairs are fully reclining and can swivel, while the divans can serve as sleeping accommodation. Early examples feature luxuries such as telephones, lighting controls, and stereo systems; foldaway tables attached to the cabin walls were also installed, along with a pair of wardrobes, one fore and one aft, for storing hand luggage and other small items.[^cite_note-Flight12aug1978-3]

Compared to the Challenger 604, the airframe was updated with larger cabin windows and a new tail cone, and the flight deck was updated with the Collins Pro Line 21 system with [electronic flight bag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_flight_bag) capability. The 200th Challenger 605 entered service in October 2012.

[^cite_note-24]

CL-605 MSA

A [maritime patrol aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maritime_patrol_aircraft) design under development by Boeing.

[^cite_note-25]

&#x20;Boeing has proposed a repackaging of some of the [Boeing P-8 Poseidon](731169.md) sensors but not weapons into a less expensive airframe, the Bombardier Challenger 605 business jet.

[^cite_note-26]

&#x20;This aircraft is named Maritime [Surveillance Aircraft](46818.md) (MSA) and has been depicted with the AN/APY-10 radar, an electro-optical sensor in a retractable turret, and a magnetic anomaly detector.

[^cite_note-CASR201307-27]

&#x20;In February 2014, a MSA demonstrator, which is a modified CL-604, made its first flight. The final aircraft will use the CL-605 airframe.

[^cite_note-28]

[^cite_note-29]

&#x20;The demonstrator currently has the external shapes for the sensors and communications systems which will be added later. The final MSA is expected to cost \$55 million to \$60 million per aircraft.

[^cite_note-30]

The 605 and 650 improve the avionics and cabin, but their performance figures are similar to the 604.[^cite_note-BCA25aug2016-18]



A Bombardier Challenger 650

### Challenger 650

Challenger 650

Following a first flight in 2015, the Challenger 650 was certified and introduced in 2015.

[^cite_note-31]

&#x20;Compared to the Challenger 605, it has a redesigned interior cabin, Rockwell Collins Pro Line 21 Advanced avionics, similar to the Challenger 350, [Synthetic vision system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_vision_system) (SVS) and a 5% increase in takeoff thrust.

[^cite_note-32]

&#x20;In 2023, its equipped price was \$33M.

Planform view

**General characteristics**

- **Crew:** Two (captain and first officer)

- **Capacity:** 2,200 kg (4,850 lb) and up to 19 passengers

- **Length:** 20.85 m (68 ft 5 in)

- **Wingspan:** 19.61 m (64 ft 4 in)

- **Height:** 6.30 m (20 ft 8 in)

- **Wing area:** 45.4 m

  2

  &#x20;(489 sq ft)

- **[Aspect ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspect_ratio_\(aeronautics\)):** 8.46

- **Empty weight:** 12,315 kg (27,150 lb)

- **Max takeoff weight:** 21,863 kg (48,200 lb)

- **Fuel capacity:** 9,072 kg (20,000 lb)

- **Cabin:** 1.83 m (6 ft) high, 2.41 m (7 ft 11 in) wide

- **Powerplant:** 2 × [General Electric CF34](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric_CF34)-3B [turbofans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbofan), 41.0 kN (9,220 lbf) thrust each

- **Range:** 7,400 km (4,600 mi, 4,000 nmi)

- **Service ceiling:** 12,000 m (41,000 ft)

- **[Thrust/weight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrust-to-weight_ratio):** 0.38

- **Takeoff:** 1,720 m (5,640 ft) (SL, ISA, MTOW)

- **Landing:** 732 m (2,402 ft) (SL, ISA, typical)
