The **Beechcraft Model 18** (or "**Twin Beech**", as it is also known) is a 6- to 11-seat,[^cite_note-TC710-1] twin-engined, low-wing, [tailwheel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tailwheel) [light aircraft](2643987.md) designed and manufactured by the [Beech Aircraft Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beechcraft) of [Wichita, Kansas](114932.md). Continuously produced from 1937 to November 1969 (over 32 years, a world record at the time), over 9,000 aircraft were built, making the Model 18 one of the world's most widely used light aircraft. It was sold worldwide as a civilian executive, utility, cargo aircraft, and passenger [airliner](51215.md) on tailwheels, nosewheels, skis, or floats, and also saw use as a [military aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_aircraft).[^cite_note-beech_nasm-2][^cite_note-c45h_ex-3][^cite_note-twin_magz-4]

The Model 18 was developed during the mid-to-late 1930s, primarily as a compact [business aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_aircraft) and [regional airliner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_airliner). The [prototype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prototype) made its [maiden flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maiden_flight) on 15 January 1937 and gained [type certification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_certificate) only seven weeks later. During and after [World War II](32927.md), over 4,500 Beech 18s were used for various military purposes, including as a light transport, light bomber (for China), aircrew trainer (for bombing, navigation, and gunnery), [photo reconnaissance](20338498.md), and "mother ship" for target [drones](58900.md)—including [United States Army Air Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army_Air_Forces) (USAAF) **C-45 Expeditor**, **AT-7 Navigator**, and **AT-11 Kansan**; and [United States Navy](20518076.md) (USN) **UC-45J Navigator**, **SNB-1 Kansan**, and others. In World War II, over 90 percent of USAAF bombardiers and navigators were trained in these aircraft.[^cite_note-beech_nasm-2][^cite_note-c45h_ex-3][^cite_note-beech_at11_kansan-5]

Production of the type for military purposes ended shortly after the end of World War II, the company transitioning entirely onto civil production by October 1945. During the early [post-war era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aftermath_of_World_War_II), the Beech 18 was the pre-eminent "business aircraft" and "feeder airliner". Besides carrying passengers, its civilian applications included [aerial spraying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_aircraft), [sterile insect release](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sterile_insect_technique), [fish stocking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish_stocking), [dry-ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dry-ice) [cloud seeding](449660.md), [aerial firefighting](1143974.md), [airmail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airmail) delivery, ambulance service, numerous movie productions, [skydiving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skydiving), freight, weapon- and drug-[smuggling](28645.md), engine testbed, [skywriting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skywriting), [banner towing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banner_towing), and stunt aircraft. Many Model 18s are privately owned, around the world, with 240 aircraft in the U.S. still on the [Federal Aviation Administration](11186.md) Aircraft Registry in August 2017.[^cite_note-beech_nasm-2][^cite_note-twin_magz-4][^cite_note-techy_ga-6][^cite_note-faa_air-7]

## Design and development



Beech 18 on floats in [Manitoba](18926.md), 1986



Beechcraft AT-11 over the West Texas prairies, around 1944

During the mid 1930s, Beechcraft management speculated that sufficient demand would exist for a new design, referred to as the *Model 18*. It was envisioned as a twin-engine transport aircraft, capable of seating between six and eight passengers dependent upon configuration, that would be suitable both as a [business aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_aircraft) and a small [regional airliner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_airliner), the latter being viewed as a relatively small niche of the market due to few feeder lines existing at that time.[^cite_note-beech_nasm-2] Furthermore, the Model 18 would be designed so that it could be readily converted to be used for several military applications as well as to haul cargo.[^cite_note-key_summary2018-8] The decisions to proceed was issued in late 1935 and preliminary works commenced thereafter, which was headed by aeronautical engineer Theodore Wells.[^cite_note-tedtwin_one-9] To accommodate the anticipated volume, the company's main production facilities were expanded around this time.

The design team investigated several different airfoils, which included a series of [wind tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_tunnel) tests performed at [Wichita State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wichita_State_University), before selecting the 23000 series airfoil developed by the [National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Advisory_Committee_for_Aeronautics), which possessed acceptable lift-[drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drag_\(physics\)#Aerodynamics) profile when flying at low airspeeds and produced relatively safe [stall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stall_\(fluid_dynamics\)#Fixed-wing_aircraft) characteristics.[^cite_note-tedtwin_one-9] Furthermore, the [wingroot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wingroot) featured an angle of incidence that progressively lessened toward the wingtips in combination with a gentle twist to the [chord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chord_\(aeronautics\)) between the root and the wingtip that produced a smooth airflow over the [ailerons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aileron) (thereby retaining [roll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_principal_axes#Longitudinal_axis_\(roll\)) control during stalls), achieving acceptable aerodynamic performance during both landings and take-offs.[^cite_note-tedtwin_one-9] The wings were furnished with electrically actuated [flaps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flap_\(aeronautics\)); the retraction mechanism for the [undercarriage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landing_gear) was also electrically actuated.[^cite_note-tedtwin_one-9]

The resulting aircraft was fairly conventional for the era, including twin [radial engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radial_engines) and all-metal [semimonocoque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-monocoque) construction. The flight control surfaces, such as the ailerons, [elevator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevator_\(aeronautics\)) and [rudders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudder), were composed of [aluminum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminum) [alloy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alloy) but covered in [cotton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton) [fabric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fabric).[^cite_note-tedtwin_one-9] One of the less conventional features of the aircraft was its [twin-tailfin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin-tail) configuration. While typically outfitted with a tailwheel undercarriage, it could be interchangeably equipped with either [skis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skis) or [float-landing gear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Float_\(nautical\)).[^cite_note-beech_nasm-2] The Model 18 can be mistaken for the larger [Lockheed Electra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_Model_10_Electra) series of airliners, which closely resemble it.

On 5 January 1937, the Model 18 [prototype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prototype) made its [maiden flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maiden_flight) from the company's facility in Wichita, Kansas;[^cite_note-beech_nasm-2] its construction had begun 13 months earlier.[^cite_note-tedtwin_one-9] For the subsequent flight test programme, experienced personnel were borrowed from the airline [Transcontinental and Western Air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans_World_Airlines).[^cite_note-tedtwin_one-9] On 4 March of that same year, it received [type certification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_certificate), clearing the aircraft to enter civil service,[^cite_note-pellp66-10] although only six production standard aircraft would be produced that year and deliveries would not commence until 1938.[^cite_note-tedtwin_one-9][^cite_note-tedtwin_two-11] Following the completion of flight testing, [Walter Beech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Beech) personally flew the prototype on numerous demonstration flights; it performed a tour across both the United States and Canada as part of publicity efforts with the goal of driving sales of the type.[^cite_note-tedtwin_one-9]

Early orders for the type typically came from [charter airlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charter_airline), including those based overseas, as well as a few corporations seeking executive aircraft.[^cite_note-beech_nasm-2] Furthermore, as early as January 1939, the US government took an interest in the type, initially to perform [photo reconnaissance](20338498.md).[^cite_note-beech_nasm-2] Throughout [World War II](32927.md), military orders for the type took precedence over the company's commercial activities. Shortly after the [Surrender of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrender_of_Japan), which ended the conflict, military production ceased. Beechcraft transitioned back to full-rate commercial aircraft production by October 1945.[^cite_note-beech_nasm-2] Numerous refinements and improvements were incorporated around this time, including revised landing gear, brakes, and tires that, in conjunction with other structural modifications, increased the aircraft's maximum weight capacity.[^cite_note-beech_nasm-2][^cite_note-key_summary2018-8]

Early production aircraft were powered either by a pair of 330-hp (250-kW) Jacobs L-6s or 350-hp (260-kW) [Wright R-760](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wright_R-760)Es; at the time, these were the only engines available that satisfied the designer's requirements.[^cite_note-tedtwin_one-9][^cite_note-key_summary2018-8] Many aircraft, such as the *D18S* variant, were powered by two 450-hp (336-kW) [Pratt & Whitney R-985](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_&_Whitney_R-985) "Wasp Junior" nine-cylinder [radial engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radial_engine#Aircraft), paired with [Hamilton Standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton_Standard) [constant speed propellers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constant_speed_propeller).[^cite_note-beech_nasm-2][^cite_note-tedtwin_two-11] The Model 18 has used a variety of engines and has been subject to a number of airframe modifications to increase both gross weight and speed. At least one aircraft was modified to use 600-hp (447-kW) [Pratt & Whitney R-1340](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_&_Whitney_R-1340) "Wasp" powerplants. With the added weight of about 200 lb (91 kg) per engine, the concept of a Model 18 fitted with R-1340 engines was deemed unsatisfactory due to the weakest structural area of the aircraft being the engine mounts.&#x20;

On 10 December 1953, the prototype of the improved *Super 18* made its first flight.[^cite_note-beech_nasm-2] In 1955, deliveries of the Model E18S commenced; the E18S featured a [fuselage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuselage) that was extended 6 in (150 mm) higher for more headroom in the passenger cabin. All later Beech 18s (sometimes called Super 18s) featured this taller fuselage, and some earlier models (including one AT-11) have had this modification. The Model H18, introduced in 1963, featured optional [tricycle undercarriage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tricycle_undercarriage). Unusually, the undercarriage was developed for earlier-model aircraft under an [supplemental type certificate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supplemental_type_certificate) by [Volpar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volpar?action=edit\&redlink=1), and installed in H18s at the factory during manufacture. A total of 109 H18s was built with tricycle undercarriage, and another 240 earlier-model aircraft were modified with this.[^cite_note-BHM18S-12][^cite_note-13]

In November 1969, production of the Model 18 came to an end;[^cite_note-beech_nasm-2] the final aircraft, a Model H18, was delivered to Miyazaki Aviation College, [Japan](15573.md), that same year.[^cite_note-BA-765-14] Through the years, 32 variations of the basic design had flown, over 200 improvement modification kits were developed, and almost 8,000 aircraft were built. In one case, the aircraft was modified to a triple tail, trigear, humpbacked configuration and appeared similar to a miniature [Lockheed Constellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_Constellation).[^cite_note-jawa67_p250-15] Another distinctive conversion was carried out by Pacific Airmotive to become the *PacAero Tradewind*, which featured a lengthened nose to accommodate the tricycle [nosewheel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landing_gear), and replaced the Model 18's twin tailfins with a single fin arrangement.[^cite_note-BCAM-16]

## Operational history



Beechcraft 18 on floats

Production got an early boost when [Nationalist China](16903.md) paid the company US\$750,000 for six M18R light bombers,[^cite_note-AFB-17] but by the time of the U.S. entry into World War II, only 39 Model 18s had been sold, of which 29 were for civilian customers.[^cite_note-BHM18S-12][^cite_note-BHM-18] Work began in earnest on a variant specifically for training [United States Army Air Forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army_Air_Forces) (USAAF) military pilots, bombardiers, and navigators. The effort resulted in the Army AT-7. Further development led to the AT-11 navigation trainer, C-45 military transport, and F-2 (the "F" standing for "Fotorecon", short for ["photographic reconnaissance"](159746.md)). The [United States Navy](20518076.md) (USN) first adopted the Beech 18 as the JRB-1, equivalent to the F-2, followed by the JRB-2 transport; the JRB was initially named the **Voyager**, but this name did not enter common use, and JRBs were generally called Expeditors like their USAAF counterparts.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESwanboroughBowers197641-19] The first JRB-1 obtained by the USN, [bureau number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_number) (BuNo) *09771*, was converted from the last civilian Model 18 built before production was earmarked solely for the military for the duration of the war.[^cite_note-Baugher_1-20] The USN subsequently obtained more Model 18s as the JRB-3 (C-45B), JRB-4 (UC-45F), SNB-1 Kansan (AT-11), SNB-2 (AT-7), and SNB-2C (AT-7C).[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESwanboroughBowers197641–42-21] Existing naval Twin Beeches were subsequently modified into the SNB-2H air ambulance, SNB-2P reconnaissance trainer, and SNB-3Q [electronic countermeasures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_countermeasures) trainer.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESwanboroughBowers197642-22] The [United States Coast Guard](32223.md) acquired seven JRB-4 and JRB-5 aircraft from the USN between 1943 and 1947; they were primarily used as utility transports, with one aircraft later converted for aerial mapping and another used for proficiency flying.[^cite_note-uscg-23][^cite_note-Baugher_cg-24]

After the conflict, the USAAF was reorganised into the [United States Air Force](32090.md) (USAF), and its [Strategic Air Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_Air_Command) had Model 18 variants (AT-11 Kansans, C-45 Expeditors, F-2 Expeditors, and UC-45 Expeditors) from 1946 until 1951. In 1950, the USN still had around 1,200 JRB and SNB aircraft in inventory.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESwanboroughBowers197642-22] From 1951 to 1955, the USAF had many of its aircraft remanufactured with new fuselages, wing center sections, and undercarriages to take advantage of the improvements to the civilian models since the end of World War II. Eventually, 900 aircraft were remanufactured to be similar to the then-current Model D18S and given new designations, constructor's numbers, and USAF serial numbers.[^cite_note-BHM45-25] The USN had many of its surviving aircraft remanufactured, as well, resulting in the JRB-6, SNB-5, and SNB-5P.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESwanboroughBowers197642–43-26] The Coast Guard retired its JRBs in 1956 and sold most of them as surplus in 1959, but one was retained by the [United States Coast Guard Reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Coast_Guard_Reserve) until at least 1972.[^cite_note-uscg-23][^cite_note-Baugher_cg-24] With the adoption of the [1962 United States Tri-Service aircraft designation system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1962_United_States_Tri-Service_aircraft_designation_system), the USN's SNB-5 and SNB-5P became the TC-45J and RC-45J, respectively, later becoming the UC-45J as their primary mission shifted from aircrew training to utility transport work.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTESwanboroughBowers197643-27] The C-45 flew in USAF service until 1963, the USN retired its last UC-45J in 1972, while the U.S. Army flew its C-45s until 1976. In later years, the military called these aircraft "bug smashers" in reference to their extensive use supplying mandatory flight hours for desk-bound aviators in [the Pentagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pentagon).[^cite_note-Proceedings-28]

Beech 18s were used extensively by [Air America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_America_\(airline\)) during the [Vietnam War](32611.md); initially more-or-less standard ex-military C-45 examples were used, but then the airline had 12 aircraft modified by Conrad Conversions in 1963 and 1964 to increase performance and payload. The modified aircraft were known as Conrad Ten-Twos, as the maximum takeoff weight (MTOW) was increased to 10,200 lb (4,600 kg).[^cite_note-AA-29][^cite_note-30] The increase was achieved by several airframe modifications, including increased horizontal stabilizer angle-of-incidence, redesigned undercarriage doors, and aerodynamically improved wingtips. Air America then had Volpar convert 14 aircraft to [turboprop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turboprop) power, fitted with [Garrett AiResearch TPE-331](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garrett_AiResearch_TPE-331) engines; modified aircraft were called Volpar Turbo Beeches, and also had a further increase in MTOW to 10,286 lb (4,666 kg).[^cite_note-AA-29]

### Spar problems

The [wing spar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wing_spar) of the Model 18 was fabricated by welding an assembly of tubular steel. The configuration of the tubes in combination with drilled holes from aftermarket supplemental type certificate (STC) modifications on some of these aircraft have allowed the spar to become susceptible to corrosion and cracking while in service.[^cite_note-31] This prompted the FAA to issue an [Airworthiness Directive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airworthiness_Directive) in 1975, mandating the fitting of a spar strap to some Model 18s. This led, in turn, to the retirement of a large number of STC-modified Model 18s when owners determined the aircraft were worth less than the cost of the modifications. The corrosion on unmodified spars was not a problem; it occurred due to the additional exposed surface area created through the STC hole-drilling process. Further requirements have been mandated by the FAA and other national airworthiness authorities, including regular removal of the spar strap to allow the strap to be checked for cracks and corrosion and the spar to be [X-rayed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_radiography). In Australia, the airworthiness authority has placed a life limit on the airframe, beyond which aircraft are not allowed to fly.[^cite_note-32][^cite_note-33][^cite_note-34]

## Accidents and incidents

The Beechcraft Model 18 family has been involved in the following notable accidents and incidents:

- 25 April 1951: [Cubana de Aviación Flight 493](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubana_de_Aviaci%C3%B3n_Flight_493), a [Douglas DC-4](518813.md) bound from Miami to Havana, registration *CU-T188*, collided with a U.S. Navy SNB-1, [bureau number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_number) *39939*, which was performing a practice [instrument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instrument_flight_rules) approach to [Naval Air Station Key West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_Air_Station_Key_West). The collision and ensuing crashes killed all 34 passengers and five crew aboard the DC-4 and all five crew aboard the SNB. The accident occurred at midday, weather was clear with unlimited visibility, and both flight crews had been cleared to fly under [visual flight rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_flight_rules), being expected to "see and avoid" other aircraft; the student flying the SNB was wearing view-limiting goggles, but the other SNB crew were not, and were expected to keep watch. Ground witnesses said that neither aircraft took evasive action prior to the collision, and the [Civil Aeronautics Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Aeronautics_Board) attributed the accident to the failure of both flight crews to see and avoid conflicting air traffic.

  [^cite_note-CAB_493-103]

- 19 September 1965: During the [India–Pakistan war of 1965](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India%E2%80%93Pakistan_war_of_1965), eight people including Chief Minister [Balwantrai Mehta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balwantrai_Mehta) of Gujarat, India, were killed when their civilian-registered Beechcraft 18 was [shot down](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gujarat_Beechcraft_incident) southwest of [Bhuj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhuj) by an [F-86 Sabre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-86_Sabre) of the [Pakistan Air Force](377433.md), which had concluded that the [Indian Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Army) had been conducting reconnaissance of a nearby conflict zone using civil aircraft.

  [^cite_note-104]

- 1967: [Mohammed bin Awad bin Laden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohammed_bin_Awad_bin_Laden) was killed in the crash of a Beechcraft 18 in Saudi Arabia.

  [^cite_note-105]

- 10 December 1967: American [soul music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_music) singer [Otis Redding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otis_Redding), four members of his backing band the [Bar-Kays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bar-Kays), the pilot, and another member of Redding's entourage were killed in the crash of Redding's H18, registration *N390R*, into [Lake Monona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Monona) on approach to [Truax Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dane_County_Regional_Airport) in Wisconsin. The [National Transportation Safety Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Transportation_Safety_Board) (NTSB) was unable to determine the cause of the crash, noting that the left engine and propeller were not recovered.

  [^cite_note-ntsb_redding-106]

  [^cite_note-107]

  &#x20;Trumpet player [Ben Cauley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Cauley), the sole survivor of the crash, subsequently revived the Bar-Kays together with another band member who was aboard a different aircraft.

  [^cite_note-108]

- 20 September 1973: American [folk rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_rock) singer-songwriter [Jim Croce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Croce), four members of his entourage, and the pilot were killed when their chartered E18S, registration *N50JR*, crashed into a tree shortly after takeoff from [Natchitoches Regional Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natchitoches_Regional_Airport) in Louisiana.

  [^cite_note-rstrap-109]

  [^cite_note-AP-110]

  [^cite_note-ntsb_croce-111]

  &#x20;The NTSB attributed the accident to reduced visibility due to fog, and to physical impairment of the pilot, who had severe [coronary artery disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronary_artery_disease) and had run 3 mi (4.8 km) to the airport.

  [^cite_note-ntsb_croce-111]

  &#x20;An investigation conducted for a lawsuit against the charter company attributed the accident solely to [pilot error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilot_error), citing his downwind takeoff into a "black hole" of severe darkness, causing him to experience [spatial disorientation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spatial_disorientation).

  [^cite_note-112]

- 26 September 1978: [Air Caribbean Flight 309](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Caribbean_Flight_309), an [air taxi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_taxi) flight by a D18S, registration *N500L*, crashed on approach to [Isla Verde International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isla_Verde_International_Airport) in Puerto Rico, killing the pilot and the five passengers aboard the aircraft and causing substantial property damage and injuries to bystanders on the ground. The pilot could not communicate with [approach control](48563.md#Approach_and_terminal_control) and was following directions relayed by local tower controllers, who told the pilot to make a turn and maintain separation from a [Lockheed L-1011](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_L-1011) that was overtaking the flight, but the pilot did not turn, and the D18S passed underneath and very close to the L-1011. Both the NTSB and a U.S. District Court ruling attributed the crash to the D18S pilot's failure to correctly follow visual flight rules and air traffic control instructions to maintain separation from the much larger L-1011, causing a loss of aircraft control due to [wake turbulence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wake_turbulence).

  [^cite_note-NTSB_309-113]

  [^cite_note-114]

  &#x20;A contributing factor was the pilot's difficulties in communication with controllers.

  [^cite_note-NTSB_309-113]

- 4 July 1987: Ten people, including all then-current members of [The Montana Band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Montana_Band), were killed when the pilot of their chartered D18S, *N132E*,

  [^cite_note-ntsb_montana-115]

  &#x20;failed to clear a hillside near [Lakeside, Montana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakeside,_Montana), while performing a [flypast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flypast) of the venue where the band had performed earlier.

  [^cite_note-116]

  [^cite_note-117]

  &#x20;The pilot performed an "abrupt" climb and performed a "[hammerhead stall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stall_turn)" maneuver, reversing direction and entering a dive. The accident was attributed to the pilot's poor judgment and failure to maintain altitude during unauthorized attempted [aerobatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerobatics).

  [^cite_note-ntsb_montana-115]

## Specifications (UC-45 Expeditor)

3-view line drawing of the Beechcraft SNB-2 Navigator

3-view line drawing of the Beechcraft SNB-2 Navigator

*Data from* *Jane's Fighting Aircraft of World War II.*[^cite_note-jane-162]

**General characteristics**

- **Crew:** 2 pilots

- **Capacity:** 6 passengers

- **Length:** 34 ft 3 in (10.44 m)

- **Wingspan:** 47 ft 8 in (14.53 m)

- **Height:** 9 ft 9 in (2.97 m)

- **Wing area:** 349 sq ft (32.4 m

  2

  )

- **Empty weight:** 5,420 lb (2,458 kg)

- **Gross weight:** 7,500 lb (3,402 kg)

- **Powerplant:** 2 × [Pratt & Whitney R-985-AN-1 "Wasp Junior"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_&_Whitney_R-985_Wasp_Junior) [radial engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radial_engine), 450 hp (340 kW) each

**Performance**

- **Maximum speed:** 225 mph (362 km/h, 196 kn)

- **Range:** 1,200 mi (1,900 km, 1,000 nmi) at 160 mph (260 km/h; 140 kn) and 5,000 ft (1,500 m)

- **Service ceiling:** 26,000 ft (7,900 m)

- **Rate of climb:** 1,850 ft/min (9.4 m/s)
