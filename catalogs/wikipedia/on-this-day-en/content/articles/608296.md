The **arms industry**, also known as the **defense** (or **defence**) **industry** or **military industry** is a global [industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industry_classification) which [manufactures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing) and sells [weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weapon) and other [military technology](90815.md) to a variety of customers, including the [armed forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armed_forces) of [states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_\(polity\)) and civilian individuals and organizations. Products of the arms industry include [weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weapon), [munitions](18940621.md), [weapons platforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weapons_platform), [communications systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_communications), and other electronics, and related equipment. The arms industry also provides defense-related services, such as logistical and operational support. As a matter of policy, many governments of [industrialized countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrialized_countries) maintain or support a network of organizations, facilities, and resources to produce weapons and equipment for their military forces (and sometimes those of other countries). This is often referred to as a [defense industrial base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defense_industrial_base). Entities involved in arms production for military purposes vary widely, and include [private sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_sector) [commercial firms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Company), [state-owned enterprises](204768.md) and [public sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_sector) organizations, and scientific and academic institutions.[^cite_note-1] Such entities perform a wide variety of functions, including [research and development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_and_development), [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering), production, and servicing of [military](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military) material, equipment, and facilities. The weapons they produce are often made, maintained, and stored in [arsenals.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arsenal)

In some regions of the world, there is a substantial legal trade in [firearms](11966.md) for use by individuals (commonly cited purposes include self-defense and hunting/sporting). Illegal [small arms trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_arms_trade) occurs in many countries and regions affected by [political instability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_instability).

## History



[Coventry Ordnance Works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coventry_Ordnance_Works) producing [naval artillery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_artillery) during World War I

Military technology evolved slowly until the 19th century when a quartet of factors suddenly globalized the arms trade. First, accelerating technical innovation created a shifting frontier of desirable weapons. Second, the development of mass production allowed technically advanced weapons to be exported at scale. Third, private firms, rather than government arsenals, became leaders in the manufacture of arms. And finally, global communications and transport networks facilitated long-distance trade. Early leading arms makers included [Schneider-Creusot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schneider-Creusot), [Krupp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krupp), [Vickers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vickers), and [Armstrong Whitworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armstrong_Whitworth). These firms were encouraged both by market conditions and by their home nations to export far outside of Europe to areas such as South America, Asia, the [Near East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_East), and Africa as part of the 19th century vogue for [imperialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperialism).[^cite_note-held-2]<sup>: 108 </sup>

During the [early modern period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_modern_period), England, France, Sweden, and the Netherlands became self-sufficient in arms production, with diffusion and migration of skilled workers to more peripheral countries such as Portugal and Russia.

The modern arms industry emerged in the second half of the nineteenth century as a product of the creation and expansion of the first large [military–industrial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military%E2%80%93industrial_complex) companies. As smaller countries and even newly industrializing countries like Russia and Japan could no longer produce cutting-edge military equipment with their indigenous capacity-based resources, they increasingly began to [outsource](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outsource) the manufacture of military equipment, such as [battleships](4054.md), [artillery pieces](2508.md) and [rifles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rifle) to foreign government military entities. In 1854, the [British government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_government) awarded a contract to the [Elswick Ordnance Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elswick_Ordnance_Company) to supply the latest loading artillery pieces. This galvanized the private sector into weapons production, with the surplus increasingly exported to foreign countries. [William Armstrong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Armstrong,_1st_Baron_Armstrong) became one of the first international arms dealers, selling his systems to governments across the world from Brazil to Japan.[^cite_note-3] In 1884, he opened a shipyard at [Elswick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elswick_Ordnance_Company) to specialize in warship production – at the time, it was the only factory in the world that could build a battleship and arm it completely.[^cite_note-4] The factory produced warships for foreign naval forces, including the [Imperial Japanese Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Japanese_Navy). Several Armstrong cruisers played an important role in defeating the Russian fleet at the [Battle of Tsushima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Tsushima) in 1905. In the American Civil War in 1861 [the North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_\(American_Civil_War\)) had about ten times the manufacturing capacity of the [economy of the Confederate States of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_the_Confederate_States_of_America). This advantage over [the South](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederacy_\(American_Civil_War\)) included the ability to produce (in relatively small numbers) [breech-loading rifles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breechloader) for use against the [muzzle-loading rifles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muzzle-loading_rifle) of the South. This began the transition to industrially produced mechanized weapons such as the Gatling gun.[^cite_note-5]

This industrial innovation in the defense industry was adopted by [Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussia) in its 1864, 1866, and 1870–71 defeats of Denmark, Austria, and, France respectively. By this time the machine gun had begun entering arsenals. The first examples of its effectiveness were in 1899 during the [Boer War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boer_war) and in 1905 during the [Russo-Japanese War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Japanese_War). However, Germany led the innovation of weapons and this advantage in the [weapons of World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weapons_of_World_War_I) nearly defeated the allies.

In 1885, France decided to capitalize on this increasingly lucrative trade and repealed its ban on weapon exports. The regulatory framework for the period up to the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War) was characterized by a [laissez-faire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laissez-faire) policy that placed little obstruction in the way of weapons exports. Due to the carnage of World War I, arms traders began to be regarded with odium as [merchants of death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merchants_of_death) and were accused of having instigated and perpetuated the war for earning their profits from weapons sales. An inquiry into these allegations in Britain failed to find evidence to support them. However, the sea change in attitude about war more generally meant that governments began to control and regulate the trade themselves.



Stacks of shells in the [National Shell Filling Factory, Chilwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Shell_Filling_Factory,_Chilwell) during World War I

For much of the time after the First World War, a national defence industry was treated by many countries as an element of their [national sovereignty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_sovereignty), and the emergence of collaborative international projects did not occur until the latter part of the [Cold War](325329.md).[^cite_note-6]

The volume of the arms trade greatly increased during the later 20th century, and it began to be used as a political tool, especially during the Cold War when the United States and the USSR supplied weapons to their proxies across the world, particularly [third world countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_World) (see [Nixon Doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nixon_Doctrine)).[^cite_note-7]

## Sectors

### Land-based weapons

British Mark V Tank

British [Mark V tank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_V_tank)

This category includes everything from [light arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SALW) to [heavy artillery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_artillery), and the majority of producers are small. Many are located in third-world countries. International trade in [handguns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handgun), [machine guns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_gun), [tanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tank), [armored personnel carriers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armored_personnel_carrier), and other relatively inexpensive weapons is substantial. There is relatively little regulation at the international level, and as a result, many weapons fall into the hands of organized crime, rebel forces, terrorists, or regimes under sanctions.[^cite_note-autogenerated1-8]

#### Small arms

Main articles: [Small arms trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_arms_trade), [Small arms and light weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_arms_and_light_weapons), and [List of most-produced firearms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_most-produced_firearms)



The [AK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AK-47) [series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalashnikov_rifle) of weapons have been produced in greater numbers than any other [firearm](11966.md) and have been used in conflicts all over the world.

One billion firearms were in global circulation in 2017; of those, 857 million (85%) were possessed by civilians, 133 million (13%) were possessed by national militaries, and 23 million (2%) belonged to law enforcement agencies.[^cite_note-9] 1,135 companies based in more than 98 countries manufactured small arms as well as their various components and ammunition as of 2003.[^cite_note-10]

### Aerospace systems

Main article: [Aerospace manufacturer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerospace_manufacturer)

Encompassing military aircraft (both land-based and [naval aviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_aviation)), conventional missiles, and [military satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_satellites), this is the most technologically advanced sector of the market. It is also the least competitive from an economic standpoint, with a handful of companies dominating the entire market. The top clients and major producers are virtually all located in the [western world](21208200.md) and Russia, with the United States easily in the first place. Prominent aerospace firms include [Rolls-Royce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolls-Royce_Holdings), [BAE Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BAE_Systems), [Saab AB](26968.md), [Dassault Aviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dassault_Aviation), [Sukhoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sukhoi), [Mikoyan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikoyan), [EADS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EADS), [Leonardo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_S.p.A.), [Thales Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thales_Group), [Lockheed Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lockheed_Martin), [Northrop Grumman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northrop_Grumman), [RTX Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RTX_Corporation), and [Boeing](18933266.md). There are also several [multinational](214491.md) consortia mostly involved in the manufacturing of [fighter jets](10929.md), such as the [Eurofighter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurofighter). The largest military contract in history, signed in October 2001, involved the development of the [Joint Strike Fighter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_Strike_Fighter).[^cite_note-autogenerated1-8]

### Naval systems

Several of the world's [great powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_power) maintain substantial [naval forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_force) to provide a global presence, with the largest nations possessing [aircraft carriers](2219.md), [nuclear submarines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_submarine) and advanced [anti-air defense systems](146640.md). The vast majority of military ships are conventionally powered, but some are [nuclear-powered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_marine_propulsion). There is also a large global market in second-hand naval vessels, generally purchased by [developing countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developing_countries) from [Western](21208200.md) governments.[^cite_note-autogenerated1-8]

## International arms transfers

### Over time

#### 2010–2014



Share of arms sales by country in 2013. Source is provided by [SIPRI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SIPRI).

[^cite_note-13]

According to research institute [SIPRI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SIPRI), the volume of international transfers of major weapons in 2010–14 was 16 percent higher than in 2005–2009. The five biggest exporters in 2010–2014 were the United States, Russia, China, Germany, and France, and the five biggest importers were India, Saudi Arabia, China, the United Arab Emirates, and Pakistan. The flow of arms to the Middle East increased by 87 percent between 2009–13 and 2014–18, while there was a decrease in flows to all other regions: Africa, the Americas, Asia and Oceania, and Europe.[^cite_note-sipri.org-14]

#### 2014–2018

SIPRI has identified 67 countries as exporters of major weapons in 2014–18. The top 5 exporters during the period were responsible for 75 percent of all arms exports. The composition of the five largest exporters of arms changed between 2014 and 2018 and remained unchanged compared to 2009–13, although their combined total exports of major arms were 10 percent higher. In 2014–18, significant increases in arms exports from the US, France and Germany were seen, while Chinese exports rose marginally and Russian exports decreased.[^cite_note-sipri.org-14]

In 2014–18, 155 countries (about three-quarters of all countries) imported major weapons. The top 5 recipients accounted for 33 percent of the total arms imports during the period. The top five arms importers – Saudi Arabia, India, Egypt, Australia, and Algeria – accounted for 35 percent of total arms imports in 2014–18. Of these, Saudi Arabia and India were among the top five importers in both 2009–13 and 2014–18.

In 2014–18, the volume of major arms international transfers was 7.8 percent higher than in 2009–13 and 23 percent higher than that in 2004–08. The largest arms importer was Saudi Arabia, importing arms primarily from the United States, United Kingdom, and France. Between 2009–13 and 2014–18, the flow of arms to the Middle East increased by 87 percent. Also including India, Egypt, Australia, and Algeria, the top five importers received 35 percent of the total arms imports, during 2014–18. The five largest exporters were the United States, Russia, France, Germany and China.[^cite_note-sipri.org-14]

#### Post-2021

The global volume of major arms transferred between states in 2021-2025 was 9.2 per cent higher than in the previous five-year period (2016–20). This was the biggest increase since 2011-15. Arm imports by states in Europe more than trebled between the two periods (+210 per cent). The five largest arms importers in 2021–25 were Ukraine, India, Saudi Arabia, Qatar and Pakistan, while the five largest arms exporters were the United States, France, Russia, Germany and China.[^cite_note-15]

## Economics of the arms industry

### [Market failures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_failures)

> ... in all countries, markets for military goods work poorly. This is to a large extent independent of the constitution of the state and the social and economic system. In all countries, whether ownership is private or collective, and whether rulers are democratic or authoritarian, the agents on each side of the defense market are powerful and well connected. On one side a senior minister manages a government [monopsony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monopsony): there is only one significant customer for such items as heavy artillery, aircraft, and battleships. On the other side is a charmed circle of big defense contractors. A few large-scale corporations supply such weapons; their ability to squeeze money out of government is augmented by the fact that they are too important for production, employment, and national security for the government to let them fail. As a direct result, defense markets everywhere are notorious for cost overruns, delayed deliveries, quality shortfalls, subsidies, and kickbacks. It would be a mistake, however, to conclude that defense markets everywhere are uniformly the same.

— Harrison & Markevich[^cite_note-harrison-harrison-markevich-23]<sup>: 156 </sup>

A [free market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_market) for weapons cannot exist within a [state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_\(polity\)) because the market is necessarily a [monopsony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monopsony) where there is a single buyer and a small number of suppliers.[^cite_note-gansler_industry-24]<sup>: 5, 30–31, 69 </sup> The high cost of weapons together with the lack of a free market makes pricing controversial and allegations of corruption and inefficiency common.[^cite_note-gansler_industry-24]<sup>: 1 </sup> Furthermore, the complexity and specialization involved in weapons together with [barriers to entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barriers_to_entry) created by the [government procurement](8488190.md) process frequently result in monopolistic situations where suppliers are able to charge high prices and dictate long delivery times.[^cite_note-gansler_industry-24]<sup>: 6 </sup> The cyclic nature of the business has driven consolidation which further impedes pricing.[^cite_note-gansler_industry-24]<sup>: 11 </sup>

While [profiteering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Profiteering) by the arms industry is frequently blamed for the costs of defense procurement, a comparison between commercial and defense companies found little difference in profitability. Rather, cost overruns appear to be caused by a variety of complicated factors inherent in the structure of military procurement including needless levels of technological sophistication, a bidding process that rewards underbidding, a profit formula that rewards inefficiency by paying contractors a percentage of the total cost, procurement organization structure that hampers decision-making, and [concurrent engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concurrent_engineering) that requires rework of already produced equipment.[^cite_note-rosen-rosen-25]<sup>: 18–19 </sup> [Technological determinism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_determinism) may arise where competition between weapons systems drives relentless development of new weapons, not because they are needed, but because they are possible.[^cite_note-roland-26]<sup>: 32–33 </sup> Pressure on the US government has resulted in an inefficient procurement system where the government negotiates with the objective of low contractor profit rather than low overall cost.[^cite_note-gansler_industry-24]

Poorly managed procurement during the [American Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War) resulted in generals and states competing against each other when buying arms which resulted in a seller’s market where prices were ten times higher than before the war and the goods were sometimes unusable.[^cite_note-cooling-molander-27]<sup>: 178 </sup> Similar problems occurred at the start of WWII before procurement was centralized to the [War Production Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_Production_Board) to prevent useless competition.[^cite_note-cooling-beaumont-28]<sup>: 119 </sup>

Bids for government contracts may involve collusion among the bidders to extract exorbitant profits.[^cite_note-cooling-trotter-29]<sup>: 94 </sup> High profitability of companies involved in the manufacture of armor plate for ships resulting from their anti-competitive tactics around the year 1900 lead to much public controversy. This culminated in the [ Budget and Accounting Act of 1921](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budget_and_Accounting_Act) to limit the sort of abuses perpetrated by the nickel-steel cartel.[^cite_note-cooling-lischka-30]<sup>: 56 </sup> The period leading up to WWI saw Navy Department funds being used as a source of federal [patronage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoils_system).[^cite_note-cooling-ferrell-31]<sup>: 60 </sup>

A continuing community of interests between the military and industry creates the potential for an [old boy network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_boy_network) in control of weapons procurement that threatens the public interest.[^cite_note-pursell-proxmire-32]<sup>: 256–257 </sup> This may involve a [revolving door](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolving_door_\(politics\)) dynamic were personnel frequently change their employment between government and private industry, thus making their allegiance unclear.[^cite_note-cooling-molander-27]<sup>: 179 </sup> [Elizabeth Warren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Warren) said that there is a widespread practice of American defense contractors hiring former US Government officials. She said that hundreds of such cases exist and that they constitute a revolving door that creates "at minimum, the appearance of corruption and favoritism".[^cite_note-33]

### International trade

[Multinational corporations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multinational_corporations) form a global network stitched together by reciprocal agreements and interlocking ownership that may pursue objectives contrary to that of the nations whose resources they employ.[^cite_note-cooling-trotter-29]<sup>: 94 </sup>[^cite_note-gansler_conversion-34]<sup>: 43 </sup> For example, the English arms maker [Vickers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vickers) supplied field guns to Germany prior to 1914. These guns were then used against British troops during WWI.[^cite_note-cooling-trotter-29]<sup>: 95 </sup>

Furthermore, a tradeoff exists between procuring the best specialized parts and materials from international businesses, or attempting to achieve [autarky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autarky) by developing purely domestic substitutes. During the [Gulf War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_War), a shortage of advanced ceramic components for [Tomahawk missiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomahawk_\(missile_family\)) occurred. This was caused by a ceramics manufacturer located in the US being pressured by its Japanese parent company, which was in turn pressured by [Socialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Socialist_Party) members of the [National Diet](163847.md) to withhold support for the war.[^cite_note-gansler_conversion-34]<sup>: 43–44 </sup>

## Arms control

Main article: [Arms control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arms_control)

Arms control refers to international restrictions upon the development, production, stockpiling, proliferation, and usage of small arms, conventional weapons, and [weapons of mass destruction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weapon_of_mass_destruction).[^cite_note-35] It is typically exercised through the use of [diplomacy](28165911.md), which seeks to persuade [governments](12229.md) to accept such limitations through agreements and [treaties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty), although it may also be forced upon non-consenting governments.

### Notable international arms control treaties

- [Arms Trade Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arms_Trade_Treaty), concluded in 2013, entered into force on 24 December 2014.

  [^cite_note-36]

- [Biological Weapons Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_Weapons_Convention), signed in 1972, entered into force during 1975

- [Chemical Weapons Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_Weapons_Convention), signed in 1993, entered into force during 1997

- [Geneva Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geneva_Protocol) on biological and chemical weapons during 1925

- [Missile Technology Control Regime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missile_Technology_Control_Regime) (MTCR), 1987

- [Ottawa Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottawa_Treaty) on anti-personnel land mines, signed in 1997, entered into force during 1999

- [Outer Space Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_Space_Treaty), signed and entered into force during 1967

- [New START Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/START_treaty_\(2010\)), signed by Russia and the United States in April 2010, entered into force in February 2011
