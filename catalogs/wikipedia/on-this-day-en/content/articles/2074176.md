# Overvoltage

This article is about a concept in electrical engineering. For use in electrochemistry, see [overpotential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overpotential).



Voltage spike.

In [electrical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering), **overvoltage** is the raising of [voltage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltage) beyond the design limit of a [circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_network) or [circuit element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circuit_element). The conditions may be hazardous. Depending on its duration, the overvoltage event can be [transient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transient_\(oscillation\))—a short (milliseconds) [voltage spike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltage_spike)—or *sustained* (also known as *power frequency overvoltage*) with a stable value on multiple terminals.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTELi201814-1] In an [electrical grid](20344155.md), [voltage control and reactive power management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltage_control_and_reactive_power_management) are inseparable facets of a single activity.[^cite_note-FOOTNOTEKirbyHirst19971-2]

## Explanation



A 3-phase, star-connected electric system with a broken neutral wire. A fault caused the neutral voltage to be no longer clamped to ground and the wire can float as high as the [line voltage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_voltage_\(three-phase\)). This can create overvoltage conditions for small appliances connected to a single phase (as the line voltage is much higher than the [phase voltage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_voltage))

Electronic and electrical devices are designed to operate at a certain maximum supply voltage, and considerable damage can be caused by voltage that is higher than that for which the devices are rated.

For example, an electric [light bulb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incandescent_light_bulb) has a [wire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_filament) in it that at the given rated voltage will carry a [current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current) just large enough for the wire to get very hot (giving off [light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light) and [heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat)), but not hot enough for it to [melt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melting). The amount of current in a circuit depends on the voltage supplied: if the voltage is too high, then the wire may melt and the light bulb burn out. Similarly other electrical devices may stop working, or may even burst into [flames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flame) if an overvoltage is delivered to the circuit.

## Sources

### Natural

A typical natural source of transient overvoltage events is [lightning](61344.md). Bursts of [solar wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_wind) following [solar flares](54648.md) are also known to cause overvoltage in electrical circuits, especially onboard space [satellites](27683.md).

### Man-made

&#x20;Man-made sources of spikes are usually caused by [electromagnetic induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_induction) when switching on or off inductive loads (such as [electric motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_motor) or [electromagnets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnet)), or by switching heavy resistive AC loads when [zero-crossing circuitry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_cross_circuit) is not used - anywhere a large change of current takes place. One of the purposes of [electromagnetic compatibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_compatibility) compliance is to eliminate such sources.

An important potential source of dangerous overvoltage is [electronic warfare](82272.md). There is intensive [military](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military) research in this field, whose goal is to produce various [transient electromagnetic devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transient_electromagnetic_device) designed to generate [electromagnetic pulses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_pulse) that will disable an enemy's electronic equipment. A recent military development is that of the exploding [capacitor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capacitor) designed to radiate a high voltage electromagnetic pulse. Another intense source of an electromagnetic pulse is a [nuclear explosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_explosion#Electromagnetic_pulse).

[Diesel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diesel_generator) or petrol powered generators can also be a source of overvoltage. If the generator speed increases suddenly, such as due to inadvertent throttle movement or unstable operation of the [prime mover](41228673.md), this can cause brief but damaging overvoltage to any connected equipment. A faulty [voltage regulator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltage_regulator) can also cause overvoltage.[^cite_note-3]

An example of an overvoltage event occurred on August 25, 2011, in [East Palo Alto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Palo_Alto,_California), California. The line voltage supplied to 232 customers increased from 110 volts to around 400 volts for 80 minutes. The event damaged electronic equipment, light bulbs, and other electrical equipment including [smart meters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smart_meter).[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5] The opposite of such an event is a [brownout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brownout_\(electricity\)).

## Conduction path

The transient pulses can get into the equipment either by power or data lines, or directly through space from a strong [electromagnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field) change - an [electromagnetic pulse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_pulse) (EMP). [Filters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_filter) are used to prevent spikes entering or leaving the equipment through wires, and the devices coupled electromagnetically to space (such as [radio-frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_frequency) pick-up coils in [MRI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MRI) scanners) are protected by [shielding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_shielding).
