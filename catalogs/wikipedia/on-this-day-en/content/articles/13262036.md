# Death threat

For other uses, see [Death threat (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_threat_\(disambiguation\)).



A threatening note that was left in the mailbox of [Holyoke, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holyoke,_Massachusetts) Mayor [Edwin A. Seibel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_A._Seibel) after the murder of H. Noel in 1955.

A **death threat** is a [threat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threat), often made [anonymously](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anonymity), by a person or group to kill one or more people. These threats are often designed to [intimidate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intimidate) victims in order to [manipulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_manipulation) their behaviour, in which case a death threat could be a form of [coercion](6512.md). For example, a death threat could be used to dissuade a public figure from pursuing a criminal investigation or an advocacy campaign.

## Legality

In most jurisdictions, death threats are a serious type of [criminal offence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_offence). Death threats are often covered by coercion statutes. For instance, the coercion statute in [Alaska](624.md) says:

> A person commits the crime of coercion if the person compels another to engage in conduct from which there is a legal right to abstain or abstain from conduct in which there is a legal right to engage, by means of instilling in the person who is compelled a fear that, if the demand is not complied with, the person who makes the demand or another may inflict physical injury on anyone....[^cite_note-1]

In the United States, a [judge](44719.md) during a [legal proceeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_proceeding) lost his temper and said that he hoped the defendant would die in prison, and "If this was a death penalty state, you'd be getting the [\[electric\] chair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_chair)".[^cite_note-2] Another judge on a US state supreme court was banned from ever being a state justice after saying at a social party about a group of teenaged children "when they trespass you can shoot them on the property, I'll shoot them on the property".[^cite_note-3]

## Methods

A death threat can be communicated via a wide range of media, among these letters, [newspaper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newspaper) publications, [telephone calls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephone_call), [internet blogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blog),[^cite_note-4] [e-mail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E-mail), and [social media](5897742.md). If the threat is made against a political figure, it can also be considered [treason](31292.md). If a threat targets a location that is frequented by people (e.g. a building), it could be a terrorist threat. Sometimes, death threats are part of a wider campaign of abuse targeting a person or a group of people (see [terrorism](30636.md), [mass murder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_murder)).

## Against a head of state

In many governments, including [monarchies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarchy) and [republics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic) of all levels of political freedom, threatening to kill the [head of state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_of_state) or [head of government](37570.md) (such as the [sovereign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovereign), [president](26156113.md), or [prime minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_minister)) is considered a crime. Punishments for such threats vary. United States law provides for up to five years in prison for threatening any [government official](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threatening_government_officials_of_the_United_States), especially the president.[^cite_note-5] In the [United Kingdom](31717.md), under the [Treason Felony Act 1848](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treason_Felony_Act_1848), it is illegal to attempt to kill or deprive the [monarch](47063.md) of their throne; this offense was originally punished with [penal transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penal_transportation), and then was changed to the [death penalty](5902.md), and currently the penalty is [life imprisonment](148681.md).

## Osman warning

Named after a high-profile case, *[Osman v United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osman_v_United_Kingdom)*, Osman warnings (also called letters or notices) are warnings of a high risk of murder issued by British police or legal authorities to the possible victim. They are used when there is intelligence of the threat, but there is not enough evidence to justify the police arresting the potential murderer.[^cite_note-6]
