A **geocentric orbit**, **Earth-centered orbit**, or **Earth orbit** involves any object [orbiting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit) [Earth](9228.md), such as the [Moon](19331.md) or [artificial satellites](27683.md). In 1997, [NASA](18426568.md) estimated there were approximately 2,465 artificial satellite payloads orbiting Earth and 6,216 pieces of [space debris](266344.md) as tracked by the [Goddard Space Flight Center](337031.md).[^cite_note-Goddard-1997-1] More than 16,291 objects previously launched have undergone [orbital decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_decay) and [entered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_entry) [Earth's atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_Earth).[^cite_note-Goddard-1997-1]

A spacecraft enters orbit when its [centripetal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centripetal_force) [acceleration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acceleration) due to [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity) is less than or equal to the [centrifugal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centrifugal_force) acceleration due to the horizontal component of its velocity. For a [low Earth orbit](47568.md), this velocity is about 7.8 km/s (28,100 km/h; 17,400 mph);[^cite_note-hill1999-2] by contrast, the fastest crewed airplane speed ever achieved (excluding speeds achieved by deorbiting spacecraft) was 2.2 km/s (7,900 km/h; 4,900 mph) in 1967 by the [North American X-15](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_X-15).[^cite_note-shiner20071101-3] The energy required to reach Earth orbital velocity at an [altitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altitude) of 600 km (370 mi) is about 36 [MJ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megajoule)/kg, which is six times the energy needed merely to climb to the corresponding altitude.[^cite_note-dimotakis1999-4]

Spacecraft with a [perigee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apsis) below about 2,000 km (1,200 mi) are subject to drag from the Earth's atmosphere,[^cite_note-5] which decreases the orbital altitude. The rate of orbital decay depends on the satellite's cross-sectional area and mass, as well as variations in the air density of the upper atmosphere. Below about 300 km (190 mi), decay becomes more rapid with lifetimes measured in days. Once a satellite descends to 180 km (110 mi), it has only hours before it vaporizes in the atmosphere.[^cite_note-slsa-6] The [escape velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escape_velocity) required to pull free of Earth's gravitational field altogether and move into interplanetary space is about 11.2 km/s (40,300 km/h; 25,100 mph).[^cite_note-williams2010-7]

## List of terms and concepts

The following words may have more than one definition or other non-Earth specific definition(s).\
In the spirit of brevity some of the definitions have been altered or [truncated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truncation) to reflect only their usage on this page.

[Altitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altitude)

as used here, the height of an object above the average surface of the Earth's oceans ([mean sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean_sea_level)).

[Analemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analemma)

a term in [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy) used to describe the plot of the positions of the Sun on the [celestial sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_sphere) throughout one year. Closely resembles a figure-eight.

[Apogee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apogee)

is the farthest point that a satellite or [celestial body](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_object) can go from Earth, at which the orbital velocity will be at its minimum.

[Eccentricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eccentricity_\(mathematics\))

a measure of how much an orbit deviates from a perfect circle. Eccentricity is strictly defined for all [circular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_orbit) and [elliptical orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptical_orbit), and [parabolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabolic_trajectory) and [hyperbolic trajectories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_trajectory).

[Equatorial plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equatorial_plane)

as used here, an imaginary [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_\(mathematics\)) extending from the equator on the Earth to the [celestial sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_sphere).

[Escape velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escape_velocity)

as used here, the minimum [velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velocity) an object without [propulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacecraft_propulsion) needs to have to move away indefinitely from the Earth. An object at this velocity will enter a [parabolic trajectory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabolic_trajectory); above this velocity it will enter a [hyperbolic trajectory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_trajectory).

[Impulse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impulse_\(physics\))

the [integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral) of a [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force) over the time during which it acts. Measured in ([N](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton_\(unit\))·[sec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second) or [lb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound_\(force\)) \* sec).

[Inclination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclination)

the [angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle) between a [reference plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_plane) and another [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_\(mathematics\)) or [axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_axis). In the sense discussed here the [reference plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_plane) is the Earth's [equatorial plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equatorial_plane).

[Orbital arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_arc)

an imaginary arc in the sky as seen from any given location on the surface of the Earth.

[Orbital characteristics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_parameters)

the six parameters of the [Keplerian elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keplerian_elements) needed to specify that orbit uniquely.

[Orbital period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_period)

as defined here, time it takes a satellite to make one full orbit around the Earth.

[Perigee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perigee)

is the nearest approach point of a satellite or celestial body from Earth, at which the orbital velocity will be at its maximum.

[Sidereal day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidereal_day)

the time it takes for a [celestial object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_object) to rotate 360°. For the Earth this is: 23 hours, 56 minutes, 4.091 seconds.

[Solar time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_time)

as used here, the local time as measured by a [sundial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sundial).

[Velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velocity)

an object's speed in a particular direction. Since velocity is defined as a [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_\(geometric\)), both speed and direction are required to define it.

## Types

The following is a list of different geocentric orbit classifications.

### Altitude classifications



Low (cyan) and Medium (yellow) Earth orbit regions to scale. The black dashed line is the geosynchronous orbit. The green dashed line is the 20,230 km orbit used for [GPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Positioning_System) satellites.

[Transatmospheric orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transatmospheric_orbit) (TAO)

Geocentric orbits with altitudes at [apogee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apogee) higher than 100 km (62 mi) and [perigee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perigee) that intersects with the [defined atmosphere](738149.md).

[^cite_note-8]

[Low Earth orbit](47568.md) (LEO)

Geocentric orbits ranging in altitude from 160 km (100 mi) to 2,000 km (1,200 mi) above [mean sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean_sea_level). At 160 km, one revolution takes approximately 90 minutes, and the circular orbital speed is 8 km/s (26,000 ft/s).

[Medium Earth orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medium_Earth_orbit) (MEO)

Geocentric orbits with altitudes at apogee ranging between 2,000 km (1,200 mi) and that of the [geosynchronous orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geosynchronous_orbit) at 35,786 km (22,236 mi).

[Geosynchronous orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geosynchronous_orbit) (GSO)

Geocentric circular orbit with an altitude of 35,786 km (22,236 mi). The period of the orbit equals one [sidereal day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidereal_day), coinciding with the rotation period of the Earth. The speed is approximately 3 km/s (9,800 ft/s).

[High Earth orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Earth_orbit) (HEO)

Geocentric orbits with altitudes at apogee higher than that of the geosynchronous orbit. A special case of high Earth orbit is the [highly elliptical orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highly_elliptical_orbit), where altitude at perigee is less than 2,000 km (1,200 mi).

[^cite_note-nasa-9]

### Inclination classifications

[Inclined orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclined_orbit)

An orbit whose [inclination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclination) in reference to the [equatorial plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equatorial_plane) is not 0.&#x20;

[Polar orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_orbit)

A satellite that passes above or nearly above both poles of the planet on each revolution. Therefore it has an inclination of (or very close to) 90 [degrees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_\(angle\)).

[Polar Sun synchronous orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun-synchronous_orbit)

A nearly [polar orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_orbit) that passes the [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator) at the same local time on every [pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pass_\(spaceflight\)). Useful for image-taking satellites because shadows will be the same on every pass.

### Eccentricity classifications

[Circular orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_orbit)

An orbit that has an [eccentricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_eccentricity) of 0 and whose path traces a circle.

[Elliptic orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_orbit)

An orbit with an [eccentricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_eccentricity) greater than 0 and less than 1 whose orbit traces the path of an [ellipse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellipse).&#x20;

[Hohmann transfer orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hohmann_transfer_orbit)

An orbital maneuver that moves a spacecraft from one [circular orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_orbit) to another using two engine [impulses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impulse_\(physics\)). This maneuver was named after [Walter Hohmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Hohmann).

[Geosynchronous transfer orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geosynchronous_transfer_orbit) (GTO)

A geocentric-[elliptic orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_orbit) where the [perigee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perigee) is at the altitude of a [low Earth Orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_Earth_Orbit) (LEO) and the [apogee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apogee) at the altitude of a [geosynchronous orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geosynchronous_orbit).

[Highly elliptical orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highly_elliptical_orbit) (HEO)

Geocentric orbit with apogee above 35,786 km and low perigee (about 1,000 km) that result in long dwell times near apogee.&#x20;

[Molniya orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molniya_orbit)

A [highly elliptical orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highly_elliptical_orbit) with [inclination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclination) of 63.4° and [orbital period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_period) of ½ of a [sidereal day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidereal_day) (roughly 12 hours). Such a satellite spends most of its time over a designated area of the Earth.

[Tundra orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tundra_orbit)

A [highly elliptical orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highly_elliptical_orbit) with [inclination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclination) of 63.4° and [orbital period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_period) of one [sidereal day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidereal_day) (roughly 24 hours). Such a satellite spends most of its time over a designated area of the Earth.

[Hyperbolic trajectory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_trajectory)

An "orbit" with eccentricity greater than 1. The object's [velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velocity) reaches some value in excess of the [escape velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escape_velocity), therefore it will escape the gravitational pull of the Earth and continue to travel [infinitely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitely) with a velocity (relative to Earth) decelerating to some finite value, known as the [hyperbolic excess velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_excess_velocity).&#x20;

Escape Trajectory

This trajectory must be used to launch an interplanetary probe away from Earth, because the excess over escape velocity is what changes its [heliocentric orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliocentric_orbit) from that of Earth.

Capture Trajectory

This is the mirror image of the escape trajectory; an object traveling with sufficient speed, not aimed directly at Earth, will move toward it and accelerate. In the absence of a decelerating engine impulse to put it into orbit, it will follow the escape trajectory after periapsis.

[Parabolic trajectory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabolic_trajectory)

An "orbit" with eccentricity exactly equal to 1. The object's [velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velocity) equals the [escape velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escape_velocity), therefore it will escape the gravitational pull of the Earth and continue to travel with a velocity (relative to Earth) decelerating to 0. A spacecraft launched from Earth with this velocity would travel some distance away from it, but follow it around the Sun in the same [heliocentric orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliocentric_orbit). It is possible, but not likely that an object approaching Earth could follow a parabolic capture trajectory, but speed and direction would have to be precise.

### Directional classifications

[Prograde orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prograde_orbit)

an orbit in which the projection of the object onto the equatorial plane revolves about the Earth in the same direction as the rotation of the Earth.

[Retrograde orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrograde_orbit)

an orbit in which the projection of the object onto the equatorial plane revolves about the Earth in the direction opposite that of the rotation of the Earth.

### Geosynchronous classifications

[Semi-synchronous orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-synchronous_orbit) (SSO)

An orbit with an altitude of approximately 20,200 km (12,600 mi) and an [orbital period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_period) of approximately 12 hours.

[Geosynchronous orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geosynchronous_orbit) (GEO)

Orbits with an altitude of approximately 35,786 km (22,236 mi). Such a satellite would trace an [analemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analemma) (figure 8) in the sky.&#x20;

[Geostationary orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geostationary_orbit) (GSO)

A [geosynchronous orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geosynchronous_orbit) with an [inclination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclination) of zero. To an observer on the ground this satellite would appear as a fixed point in the sky.

[Clarke orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarke_orbit)

Another name for a geostationary orbit. Named after the writer [Arthur C. Clarke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_C._Clarke).&#x20;

Earth orbital [libration points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libration_points)

The [libration points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libration_points) for objects orbiting Earth are at 105 degrees west and 75 degrees east. More than 160 satellites are gathered at these two points.

[^cite_note-10]

[Supersynchronous orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supersynchronous_orbit)

A disposal / storage orbit above GSO/GEO. Satellites will drift west.

[Subsynchronous orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsynchronous_orbit)

A drift orbit close to but below GSO/GEO. Satellites will drift east.

[Graveyard orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graveyard_orbit), disposal orbit, junk orbit

An orbit a few hundred kilometers above [geosynchronous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geosynchronous) that satellites are moved into at the end of their operation.

### Special classifications

[Sun-synchronous orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun-synchronous_orbit)

An orbit which combines altitude and [inclination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclination) in such a way that the satellite passes over any given point of the [planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet)'s surface at the same local [solar time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_time). Such an orbit can place a satellite in constant sunlight and is useful for imaging, [spy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spy_satellite), and [weather satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weather_satellite).

[Moon orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon_orbit)

The [orbital characteristics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_parameters) of Earth's Moon. Average altitude of 384,403 kilometres (238,857 mi), [elliptical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptical_orbit)–[inclined orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclined_orbit).

### Non-geocentric classifications

[Horseshoe orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horseshoe_orbit)

An orbit that appears to a ground observer to be orbiting a planet but is actually in [co-orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Co-orbital_satellite) with it. See asteroids [3753](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3753_Cruithne) (Cruithne) and [2002 AA<sub>29</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2002_AA29).

[ Sub-orbital flight](455295.md)

A launch where a [spacecraft](37910.md) approaches the height of orbit but lacks the [velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velocity) to sustain it.
