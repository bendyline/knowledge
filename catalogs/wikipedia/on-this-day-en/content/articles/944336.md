**Sepang International Circuit** (pronounced [\[ˈsəpaŋ\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/Malay) [*SUH-pung*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:Pronunciation_respelling_key); abbr.  **SIC**, [Malay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malay_language): *Litar Antarabangsa Sepang*), known as **Petronas Sepang International Circuit** for commercial reasons, is a 5.543 km (3.444 mi) [motorsport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motorsport) [race track](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Race_track) in [Sepang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sepang), [Selangor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selangor), Malaysia. It is located approximately 45 km (28 mi) south of [Kuala Lumpur](16854.md), close to [Kuala Lumpur International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuala_Lumpur_International_Airport). It hosted the [Formula One](10854.md) [Malaysian Grand Prix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysian_Grand_Prix) between 1999 and 2017, and is also the venue for the [Malaysian Motorcycle Grand Prix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysian_Motorcycle_Grand_Prix), the [Malaysia Merdeka Endurance Race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysia_Merdeka_Endurance_Race), and other major motorsport events.&#x20;

Previously known as the Sepang F1 Circuit, it was renamed the Sepang International Circuit. On 31 October 2023, it was announced that [Petronas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petronas) has acquired the naming rights to the circuit for three years in an undisclosed fee.[^cite_note-Malay_Mail-2] On 26 July 2026, it was announced that Sepang would return to the Formula One calendar as the host of the [2026 Bahrain Grand Prix](80201648.md), to be named the "[Gulf Air](377403.md) Bahrain Grand Prix in Malaysia 2026", after the [conflict in the Middle East](82533582.md) rendered Bahrain unsafe to host the race. Bahrain will retain all ticket pricing rights and race proceeds.[^cite_note-Formula_One-3]

## History



Sepang International Circuit [grandstand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grandstand), and its iconic umbrella shade.

The circuit was designed by German designer [Hermann Tilke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Tilke), who would subsequently design circuits including in [Shanghai](952143.md), [Sakhir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain_International_Circuit), [Istanbul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istanbul_Park), [Marina Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marina_Bay_Street_Circuit), and [Yas Marina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yas_Marina_Circuit). As part of a series of major infrastructure projects in the 1990s under [Mahathir Mohamad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahathir_Mohamad)'s government, the Sepang International Circuit was constructed between 1997 and 1999 close to [Putrajaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Putrajaya), the then-newly founded administrative capital of the country, with the intent of hosting the Malaysian Grand Prix. The Malaysian climate means the circuit is known for its unpredictable humid tropical weather, varying from clear furnace-hot days to tropical rain-storms.



[Petronas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petronas) sponsored the Formula One Malaysian Grand Prix as the title sponsor since its inaugural race in 1999.

The circuit was officially inaugurated by the 4th [Prime Minister of Malaysia](496731.md) [Mahathir Mohamad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahathir_Mohamad) on 7 March 1999 at 20\:30 [MST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_in_Malaysia) ([UTC+08\:00](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UTC+08\:00)).[^cite_note-4] He subsequently went on to inaugurate the first [MotoGP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Prix_motorcycle_racing) [Malaysian Grand Prix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysian_motorcycle_Grand_Prix) on 20 April 1999 (see [1999 Malaysian motorcycle Grand Prix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1999_Malaysian_motorcycle_Grand_Prix)) and the first [Formula One](10854.md) [Petronas Malaysian Grand Prix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysian_Grand_Prix) on 17 October 1999 (see [1999 Malaysian Grand Prix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1999_Malaysian_Grand_Prix)).

On 23 October 2011, on the second lap of the [MotoGP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Prix_motorcycle_racing) [Shell Advance Malaysian Grand Prix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Malaysian_motorcycle_Grand_Prix), the Italian [motorcycle racer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motorcycle_racing) [Marco Simoncelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marco_Simoncelli) died following a crash in turn 11 on Lap 2, resulting in an abandonment of the race.

The track was completely resurfaced in 2016 with the support of Italian designers Dromo, with several corners reprofiled to emphasize mechanical, rather than aerodynamic grip. Notably, the final corner was raised by approximately 1 meter,[^cite_note-5] which officials claimed would force drivers to take a later apex and explore different racing lines through the hairpin. In October 2016, it was rumoured that the Sepang circuit may be dropped from the Formula One calendar due to dwindling ticket sales, and held its nineteenth and last World Championship Grand Prix in 2017.[^cite_note-bbc.com/sport-6] The race's contract was due to expire in 2018, but its future had been under threat due to rising hosting fees and declining ticket sales.

In July 2026, Prime Minister [Anwar Ibrahim](19264815.md) announced that SIC would host the [2026 Bahrain Grand Prix](80201648.md), officially titled the "Formula 1 [Gulf Air](377403.md) Bahrain Grand Prix in Malaysia", from 2 to 4 October. Reports indicated that Bahrain would cover most of the rescheduling costs. The Grand Prix had originally been scheduled to take place at the [Bahrain International Circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahrain_International_Circuit) in April, but its original date was called off because of the [2026 Iran war](82533582.md) before the event was relocated to Sepang. The [2026 Saudi Arabian Grand Prix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Saudi_Arabian_Grand_Prix), which had been called off at the same time, remained cancelled.[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8][^cite_note-9]

## Layout



View from Mall Area, Main Grandstand North, Lower Tier.

The main circuit, normally raced in a clockwise direction, is 5.543 km (3.444 mi) long, and is noted for its sweeping corners and wide straights from 16–22 m (52–72 ft).[^cite_note-10] The layout is quite unusual, with a 0.927 km (0.576 mi) long back straight separated from the pit straight by just one very tight [hairpin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hairpin_turn).

Other configurations of the Sepang circuit can also be used. The north circuit is also raced in a clockwise direction. It is basically the first half of the main circuit. The course turns back towards the pit straight after turn 6 and is 2.706 km (1.681 mi) long in total.[^cite_note-sepang_rcircuits-11]

The south circuit is the other half of the racecourse. The back straight of the main circuit becomes the pit straight when the south circuit is in use, and joins onto turn 8 of the main circuit to form a [hairpin turn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hairpin_turn). Also run clockwise, this circuit is 2.609 km (1.621 mi) in length.[^cite_note-sepang_rcircuits-11]

Sepang International Circuit also features [kart racing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kart_racing) and [motocross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motocross) facilities.

## A lap in a Formula One car



[Lewis Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Hamilton) during the 2017 Malaysian Grand Prix.

Sepang starts with a long pit straight where the [DRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drag_reduction_system) zone exists – crucial for drivers to get a good exit out of the last corner to gain as much speed as possible. Turn 1 is a very long, slow corner taken in second gear. Most drivers brake incredibly late and lose speed gradually as they file round the corner, similar to [Shanghai's](952143.md) first turn but slower. Turn 1 leads straight into Turn 2, a tight left hairpin which goes downhill quite significantly. The first two corners are quite bumpy, making it hard to put power onto the track.[^cite_note-Malaysia-12] Turn 3 is a long flat out right hander which leads into Turn 4 – known locally as the [Langkawi](422472.md) Curve[^cite_note-jol-13] – a second gear, right-angle right-hander. Turns 5 and 6 make up an incredibly high-speed, long chicane that hurts tyres and puts a lot of stress on drivers due to high G-Force. It is locally known as the [Genting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genting_Highlands) Curve.[^cite_note-jol-13] Turns 7 and 8 (the KLIA curve) make up a long, medium-speed, double-apex right hander, and a bump can cause the car to lose balance here.[^cite_note-Malaysia-12] Turn 9 is a very slow left-hand hairpin (the Berjaya [Tioman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulau_Tioman) Corner[^cite_note-jol-13]), similar to turn two but uphill. Turn 10 leads into a challenging, medium-speed right hander at turn 11, requiring braking and turning simultaneously. Turn 12 is a flat-out, bumpy left which immediately leads into the flat right at turn 13, then the challenging '[Sunway Lagoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunway_Lagoon)'[^cite_note-jol-13] curve at turn 14. Similar to turn 11, it requires hard-braking and steering at the same time. It is taken in second gear. The long back straight can be a good place for drivers to overtake as they brake hard into turn 15, a left-handed, second-geared hairpin but drivers are advised by experts to be careful not to get re-overtaken as they come into turn 1.
