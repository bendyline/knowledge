**Petroleum production in Canada** is a [major industry](5195.md) which is important to the overall [economy of North America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_North_America). [Canada](5042916.md) has the third largest [oil reserves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_proven_oil_reserves) in the world and is the world's fourth largest [oil producer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_oil_production) and fourth largest [oil exporter](17808102.md). In 2019 it produced an average of 750,000 cubic metres per day (4.7 Mbbl/d) of [crude oil](23195.md) and equivalent. Of that amount, 64% was upgraded from [unconventional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unconventional_\(oil_&_gas\)_reservoir) [oil sands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_sands), and the remainder [light crude oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_crude_oil), [heavy crude oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_crude_oil) and [natural-gas condensate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural-gas_condensate).[^cite_note-crudeoilfacts-1] Most of the Canadian petroleum production is exported, approximately 600,000 cubic metres per day (3.8 Mbbl/d) in 2019, with 98% of the exports going to the United States.[^cite_note-crudeoilfacts-1] Canada is by far the largest single source of oil imports to the United States, providing 43% of US crude oil imports in 2015.[^cite_note-2]

The [petroleum industry](994887.md) in Canada is also referred to as the "Canadian Oil Patch"; the term refers especially to [upstream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upstream_\(oil_industry\)) operations (exploration and production of oil and gas), and to a lesser degree to [downstream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downstream_\(oil_industry\)) operations (refining, distribution, and selling of oil and gas products). In 2005, almost 25,000 new [oil wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_well) were spudded (drilled) in Canada. Daily, over 100 new wells are spudded in the province of [Alberta](717.md) alone.[^cite_note-3] Although Canada is one of the largest oil producers and exporters in the world, it also imports significant amounts of oil into its eastern provinces since its oil pipelines do not extend all the way across the country and many of its oil refineries cannot handle the types of oil its oil fields produce. In 2017 Canada imported 405,700 bbl/day (barrels per day) and exported 1,115,000 bbl/day of refined petroleum products.[^cite_note-4][^cite_note-5]

## History

Main article: [History of the petroleum industry in Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_petroleum_industry_in_Canada)

The Canadian petroleum industry developed in parallel with that of the United States. The first oil well in Canada was dug by hand (rather than drilled) in 1858 by [James Miller Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Miller_Williams) near his asphalt plant at [Oil Springs, Ontario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_Springs,_Ontario). At a depth of 4.26 metres (14.0 ft)[^cite_note-6] he struck oil, one year before "Colonel" [Edwin Drake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Drake) drilled the first oil well in the United States.[^cite_note-7] Williams later went on to found the Canadian Oil Company which qualified as the world’s first [integrated oil company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_integration).

Petroleum production in Ontario expanded rapidly, and practically every significant producer became his own [refiner](195137.md). By 1864, 20 refineries were operating in Oil Springs and seven in [Petrolia, Ontario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrolia,_Ontario). Oil was also found by the nearby town of [Bothwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bothwell,_Ontario), resulting in a short-lived oil boom there in the mid-1860s.[^cite_note-\:0-8] However, Ontario's status as an important oil producer did not last long. By 1880 Canada was a net importer of oil from the United States.



**Western Canadian Sedimentary Basin** Most of Canada's oil and gas production occurs in the [Western Canadian Sedimentary Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Canadian_Sedimentary_Basin) which stretches from southwestern [Manitoba](18926.md) to northeastern [BC](3392.md). The basin also covers most of Alberta, the southern half of [Saskatchewan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saskatchewan) and the southwest corner of the [Northwest Territories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Territories).

Canada's unique [geography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography), [geology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology), resources and patterns of settlement have been key factors in the [history of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Canada). The development of the [petroleum](23195.md) sector helps illustrate how they have helped make the nation quite distinct from the United States. Unlike the United States, which has a number of different major oil producing regions, the vast majority of Canada's petroleum resources are concentrated in the enormous [Western Canadian Sedimentary Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Canadian_Sedimentary_Basin) (WCSB), one of the largest petroleum-containing formations in the world. It underlies 1,400,000 square kilometres (540,000 sq mi) of [Western Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Canada) including most or part of four western provinces and one northern territory. Consisting of a massive wedge of [sedimentary rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedimentary_rock) up to 6 kilometres (3.7 mi) thick extending from the [Rocky Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocky_Mountains) in the west to the [Canadian Shield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Shield) in the east, it is far distant from Canada's [east and west coast ports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transportation_in_Canada#Ports_and_harbours) as well as its [historical industrial centres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_and_industrial_history_of_Canada). It is also far from [American industrial centres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_and_industrial_history_of_the_United_States). Because of its geographic isolation, the area was settled relatively late in the history of Canada, and its true resource potential was not discovered until after World War II. As a result, Canada built its major [manufacturing centres](5195.md#Manufacturing) near its historic [hydroelectric power sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroelectricity_in_Canada) in Ontario and Quebec, rather than its petroleum resources in Alberta and Saskatchewan. Not knowing about its own potential, Canada began to import the vast majority of its petroleum from other countries as it developed into a modern industrial economy.

The province of Alberta lies at the centre of the WCSB and the formation underlies most of the province. The potential of Alberta as an oil-producing province long went unrecognized because it was geologically quite different from American oil producing regions. The [first oil well in western Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Oil_Well_in_Western_Canada) was drilled in southern Alberta in 1902, but did not produce for long and served to mislead geologists about the true nature of Alberta's subsurface geology. The [Turner Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turner_Valley) oil field was discovered in 1914, and for a time was the biggest oil field in the [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire), but again it misled geologists about the nature of Alberta's geology. In Turner Valley, the mistakes oil companies made led to billions of dollars in damage to the oil field by [gas flaring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_flaring) which not only burned billions of dollars' worth of gas with no immediate market, but destroyed the field's gas drive that enabled the oil to be produced. The gas flares in Turner Valley were visible in the sky from Calgary, 75 km (50 mi) away. As a result of the highly visible wastage, the Alberta government launched vigorous political and legal attacks on the Canadian Government and the oil companies that continued until 1938 when the province set up the Alberta Petroleum and Natural Gas Conservation Board and imposed strict conservation legislation.

The status of Canada as an oil importer from the US suddenly changed in 1947 when the [Leduc No. 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leduc_No._1) well was drilled a short distance south of Edmonton. Geologists realized that they had completely misunderstood the geology of Alberta, and the highly prolific Leduc oil field, which has since produced over 50,000,000 m<sup>3</sup> (310,000,000 bbl) of oil was not a unique formation. There were hundreds more [Devonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devonian) [reef](240844.md) formations like it underneath Alberta, many of them full of oil. There was no surface indication of their presence, so they had to be found using [reflection seismology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_seismology). The main problem for oil companies became how to sell all the oil they had found rather than buying oil for their refineries. Pipelines were built from Alberta through the Midwestern United States to Ontario and to the west coast of British Columbia. Exports to the U.S. increased dramatically.

Most of the oil companies exploring for oil in Alberta were of U.S. origin, and at its peak in 1973, over 78 per cent of Canadian oil and gas production was under foreign ownership and over 90 per cent of oil and gas production companies were under foreign control, mostly American. This foreign ownership spurred the [National Energy Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Energy_Program) under the [Trudeau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Trudeau) government.[^cite_note-9]

## Major players

Main article: [Canadian petroleum companies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_petroleum_companies)

Although around a dozen companies operate oil refineries in Canada, only three companies – [Imperial Oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Oil), [Shell Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shell_Canada) and [Suncor Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suncor_Energy) – operate more than one refinery and market products nationally. Other refiners generally operate a single refinery and market products in a particular region. Regional refiners include [North Atlantic Refining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Atlantic_Refining) in Newfoundland, [Irving Oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irving_Oil) in New Brunswick, [Valero Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valero_Energy) in Quebec, [Federated Co-operatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federated_Co-operatives) in Saskatchewan, [Parkland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parkland_Fuel_Corporation) in British Columbia, and [Cenovus Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Husky_Energy) in Alberta, BC, and Saskatchewan.[^cite_note-Canadian_Refineries-10] While [Petro Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petro_Canada) was once owned by the Canadian government, it was acquired by Suncor Energy, which continues to use the Petro Canada label for marketing purposes. In 2007 Canada's three biggest oil companies brought in record profits of \$11.75 billion, up 10 percent from \$10.72 billion in 2006. Revenues for the Big Three climbed to \$80 billion from about \$72 billion in 2006. The numbers exclude Shell Canada and ConocoPhillips Canada, two private subsidiaries that produced almost 500,000 barrels per day in 2006.[^cite_note-11]

## Divisions

Approximately 96% of Canadian oil production occurs in three provinces: [Alberta](717.md), [Saskatchewan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saskatchewan), and [Newfoundland and Labrador](21980.md). In 2015 Alberta produced 79.2% of Canada's oil, Saskatchewan 13.5%, and the province of Newfoundland and Labrador 4.4%. [British Columbia](3392.md) and [Manitoba](18926.md) produced about 1% apiece.[^cite_note-NEB_2015-12] The four [Western Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Canada) provinces of Alberta, British Columbia, Saskatchewan and Manitoba all produce their oil from the vast and oil rich [Western Canadian Sedimentary Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Canadian_Sedimentary_Basin), which is centered on Alberta but extends into the other three Western provinces and into the [Northwest Territories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Territories). The province of Newfoundland and Labrador produces its oil from [offshore drilling](9969688.md) on the [Grand Banks of Newfoundland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Banks_of_Newfoundland) in the western [Atlantic Ocean](698.md).[^cite_note-13]

### Alberta



Oil extraction near [Drayton Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drayton_Valley)

[Alberta](717.md) is Canada's largest oil producing province, providing 79.2% of Canadian oil production in 2015. This included [light crude oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_crude_oil), [heavy crude oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_crude_oil), [crude bitumen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crude_bitumen), [synthetic crude oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_crude_oil), and [natural-gas condensate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural-gas_condensate). In 2015 Alberta produced an average of 492,265 cubic metres per day (3.1 Mbbl/d) of Canada's 621,560 cubic metres per day (3.9 Mbbl/d) of oil and equivalent production.[^cite_note-NEB_2015-12] Most of its oil production came from its enormous [oil sands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_sands) deposits, whose production has been steadily rising in recent years. These [unconventional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unconventional_\(oil_&_gas\)_reservoir) deposits give Canada the world's third [largest oil reserves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_proven_oil_reserves), which are rivaled only by similar but even larger [oil reserves in Venezuela](19100139.md), and conventional [oil reserves in Saudi Arabia](19099890.md). Although Alberta has already produced over 90% of its conventional crude oil reserves, it has produced only 5% of its oil sands, and its remaining oil sands reserves represent 98% of Canada's established oil reserves.[^cite_note-ST98-14]

In addition to being the world's largest producer of oil sands bitumen in the world, Alberta is the largest producer of conventional [crude oil](23195.md), [synthetic crude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_crude), [natural gas](22131.md) and [natural gas liquids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas_liquids) products in Canada.

#### Oil sands



Drilling rig in northern [Alberta](717.md)

Alberta's oil sands underlie 142,200 square kilometres (54,900 sq mi) of land in the Athabasca, Cold Lake and Peace River areas in northern Alberta - a vast area of [boreal forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boreal_forest) which is larger than [England](9316.md). The [Athabasca oil sands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athabasca_oil_sands) is the only large oil field in the world suitable for [surface mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_mining), while the [Cold Lake oil sands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_Lake_oil_sands) and the [Peace River oil sands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_River_oil_sands) must be produced by drilling.[^cite_note-15] With the advancement of extraction methods, bitumen and economical synthetic crude are produced at costs nearing that of conventional crude. This technology grew and developed in Alberta. Many companies employ both conventional strip mining and non-conventional methods to extract the bitumen from the Athabasca deposit. About 24 billion cubic metres (150 Gbbl) of the remaining oil sands are considered recoverable at current prices with current technology.[^cite_note-ST98-14] The city of [Fort McMurray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_McMurray) developed nearby to service the oil sands operations, but its remote location in the otherwise uncleared [boreal forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boreal_forest) became a problem when the entire population of 80,000 had to be evacuated on short notice because of the [2016 Fort McMurray Wildfire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Fort_McMurray_Wildfire) which enveloped the city and destroyed over 2,400 homes.[^cite_note-16]

#### Oil fields

Major [oil fields](2910801.md) are found in southeast (Brooks, Medicine Hat, Lethbridge), northwest (Grande Prairie, High Level, Rainbow Lake, Zama), central (Caroline, Red Deer), and northeast (heavy crude oil found adjacent to the oil sands) [Alberta](717.md).

Structural regions include: Foothills, Greater Arch, Deep Basin.

#### Oil pipelines

Since it is Canada's largest oil producing province, Alberta is the hub of Canadian crude oil pipeline systems. About 415,000 kilometres (258,000 mi) of Canada’s oil and gas pipelines operate solely within Alberta’s boundaries and fall under the jurisdiction of the [Alberta Energy Regulator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberta_Energy_Regulator). Pipelines that cross provincial or international borders are regulated by the [National Energy Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Energy_Board).[^cite_note-18] Major pipelines carrying oil from Alberta to markets in other provinces and US states include:[^cite_note-19]

- The Interprovincial Pipeline System (now called the [Enbridge Pipeline System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enbridge_Pipeline_System)) was built in 1950 to transport crude oil from [Edmonton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmonton), [Alberta](717.md) to [Superior, Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superior,_Wisconsin) where it supplies the [Midwestern United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midwestern_United_States). In 1953 it was extended to [Sarnia, Ontario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarnia,_Ontario) to supply the Ontario market, and in 1976 to [Montreal, Quebec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montreal,_Quebec).

- The [Trans Mountain Pipeline System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans_Mountain_Pipeline_System) was built in 1953 to transport crude oil and refined products from Edmonton to [Vancouver](32706.md), BC. It also supplies feedstock to large US oil refineries in the [state of Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_of_Washington). Only crude oil and condensate are shipped to the United States.

  [^cite_note-20]

- The Norman Wells Pipeline (now owned by Enbridge) was built in 1985 to carry crude oil from [Norman Wells, NWT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Wells,_NWT) to [Zama City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zama_City), Alberta, where it connects with the Alberta pipeline network.

- The Express Pipeline was built in 1997 to carry oil from the Alberta pipeline hub at [Hardisty, Alberta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardisty,_Alberta) to the US states of [Montana](19978.md), [Utah](31716.md), [Wyoming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wyoming) and [Colorado](5399.md).

- The [Keystone Pipeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keystone_Pipeline) was built in 2011 to carry oil from Hardisty, Alberta to the major US pipeline hub at [Cushing, Oklahoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cushing,_Oklahoma), where it connects to pipelines to [Texas](29810.md), [Louisiana](18130.md), and many of the [Eastern United States](509486.md).

#### Other oil-related activities

Two of the largest producers of petrochemicals in [North America](21139.md) are located in central and north central Alberta. In both [Red Deer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Deer,_Alberta) and [Edmonton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmonton), world class [polyethylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyethylene) and [vinyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyvinyl_chloride) manufacturers produce products shipped all over the world, and Edmonton's [oil refineries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_refineries) provide the raw materials for a large [petrochemical industry](2293201.md) to the east of Edmonton. There are hundreds of small companies in Alberta dedicated to providing various services to this industry—from drilling to well maintenance, [pipeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipeline_transport) maintenance to [seismic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_seismology) exploration.

While [Edmonton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmonton) (population 972,223 in 2019[^cite_note-StatsCan_CMA-21]) is the provincial capital and is considered the pipeline, manufacturing, chemical processing, research and refining centre of the Canadian oil industry, its [rival city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Alberta) [Calgary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calgary,_Alberta) (population 1.26 million[^cite_note-StatsCan_CMA-21]) is the main oil company head office and financial centre, with more than 960 senior and junior oil company offices. Calgary also has regional offices of all six major Canadian banks, some 4,300 petroleum, energy and related service companies, and 1,300 financial service companies, helping make it the second largest head office city in Canada after Toronto.[^cite_note-22]

### Saskatchewan

[Saskatchewan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saskatchewan) is Canada's second-largest oil-producing province after Alberta, producing about 13.5% of Canada's petroleum in 2015. This included [light crude oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_crude_oil), [heavy crude oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_crude_oil), and [natural-gas condensate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural-gas_condensate). Most of its production is heavy oil but, unlike Alberta, none of Saskatchewan's heavy oil deposits are officially classified as [bituminous sands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bituminous_sands). In 2015 Saskatchewan produced an average of 83,814 cubic metres per day (527,000 bbl/d) oil and equivalent production.[^cite_note-NEB_2015-12]

#### Oil fields

All of Saskatchewan's oil is produced from the vast [Western Canadian Sedimentary Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Canadian_Sedimentary_Basin), about 25% of which underlies the province. Lying toward the shallower eastern end of the later the sedimentary basin, Saskatchewan tends to produce more oil and less natural gas than other parts. It has four major oil-producing regions:[^cite_note-24]

- The [Lloydminster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lloydminster) area in west-central Saskatchewan has very large reserves of very [heavy crude oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_crude_oil). (The oil field crosses the Alberta/Saskatchewan border, as do the production facilities.)

  [^cite_note-Lloyd-25]

- The [Kindersley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kindersley) area in south-central Saskatchewan produces [light crude oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_crude_oil) using [hydraulic fracturing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulic_fracturing) from Saskatchewan's portion of the [Bakken Formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bakken_Formation), which also produces most of [North Dakota](21651.md)'s oil.

- The [Swift Current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swift_Current) area in southwest Saskatchewan produces mostly conventional oil.

### Newfoundland and Labrador

[Newfoundland and Labrador](21980.md) is Canada's third largest oil producing province, producing about 4.4% of Canada's petroleum in 2015. This consisted almost exclusively of [light crude oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_crude_oil) produced by offshore oil facilities on the [Grand Banks of Newfoundland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Banks_of_Newfoundland). In 2015 these offshore fields produced an average of 27,373 cubic metres per day (172,000 bbl/d) of light crude oil.[^cite_note-NEB_2015-12]

#### Oil fields

- The [Hibernia oil field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hibernia_oil_field) is located approximately 315 kilometres (196 mi) east-southeast of [St. John's, Newfoundland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._John's,_Newfoundland). The field was discovered in 1979 and has been producing since 1997. The [Hibernia Gravity Base Structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hibernia_Gravity_Base_Structure) is the world's largest oil platform by weight since it has to withstand collisions by icebergs.

- The [Terra Nova oil field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terra_Nova_oil_field) is located 350 kilometres (220 mi) off the east coast of Newfoundland. The field was discovered in 1984 and has been producing since 2002. It uses a [Floating Production Storage and Offloading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating_Production_Storage_and_Offloading) (FPSO) vessel rather than a fixed platform to produce oil.

- The [White Rose oil field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Rose_oil_field) is located 350 kilometres (220 mi) off the east coast of Newfoundland. The field was discovered in 1984 and has been producing since 2005. It uses a FPSO vessel to produce oil.

#### Oil refinery

Newfoundland has one oil refinery, the [Come By Chance Refinery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Come_By_Chance_Refinery), which has a capacity of 115,000 barrels per day (18,300 m<sup>3</sup>/d). The refinery was built before the discovery of oil offshore Newfoundland to process cheap imported oil and sell the products mainly in the United States. Unfortunately the startup of the refinery in 1973 coincided with the [1973 oil crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_oil_crisis) which quadrupled the price of the refinery's crude oil supply. This and technical problems caused the refinery to go bankrupt in 1976. It was restarted under new owners in 1986 and has gone through a series of owners until now, when it is operated by North Atlantic Refining Limited.[^cite_note-30] However, despite the fact that major oil fields were subsequently discovered offshore of Newfoundland, the refinery was not designed to process the type of oil they produced, and it did not process any Newfoundland oil at all until 2014. Until then all of Newfoundland's production went to refineries in the United States and elsewhere in Canada, while the refinery imported all its oil from other countries.[^cite_note-31]

### Eastern Canada (onshore)

#### Oil fields

Ontario was the centre of the Canadian oil industry in the 19th century. #### Oil pipelines

Canada had one of the world’s first oil pipelines in 1862 when a pipeline was built to deliver oil from [Petrolia, Ontario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrolia,_Ontario) to refineries at [Sarnia, Ontario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarnia,_Ontario). However, Ontario's oil fields began to decline toward the end of the 19th century, and by World War II Canada was importing 90% of its oil. By 1947, only three Canadian crude oil pipelines existed. One was built to handle only Alberta production. A second moved imported crude from coastal [Maine](19977.md) to [Montreal](7954681.md), while the third brought American oil into Ontario.[^cite_note-42] However, in 1947 the first big oil discovery was made in Alberta when [Leduc No. 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leduc_No._1) struck oil 40 kilometres (25 mi) southwest of central [Edmonton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmonton), [Alberta](717.md). It was followed by many even larger discoveries in Alberta, so pipelines were built to take the newly discovered oil to refineries in the [American Midwest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Midwest) and from there to refineries in Ontario.[^cite_note-43]

- The Interprovincial Pipeline (now known as [Enbridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enbridge)) was built in 1950 to take Alberta oil to US refineries. In 1953 it was extended through the US to [Sarnia, Ontario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarnia,_Ontario) and in 1956 to [Toronto](64646.md). This made it the longest crude oil pipeline in the world.

- The Interprovincial Pipeline was extended to [Montreal](7954681.md) in 1976 after the [1973 oil crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_oil_crisis) interrupted foreign oil supplies to Eastern Canada.

- The [Portland–Montreal Pipe Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portland%E2%80%93Montreal_Pipe_Line) was built during [World War II](32927.md) to bring imported oil from the marine terminal at [South Portland, Maine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Portland,_Maine) through the United States to [Montreal](7954681.md). As of 2016, the pipeline is no longer operational since the only remaining [Montreal Refinery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montreal_Refinery), is now owned by [Suncor Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suncor_Energy), which produces enough oil to meet its needs from the Canadian oil sands.

Broadly speaking Canadian conventional oil production (via standard deep drilling) peaked in the mid-1970s, but [East Coast offshore basins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeanne_d'Arc_Basin) being exploited in Atlantic Canada did not peak until 2007 and are still producing at relatively high rates.[^cite_note-46]

Production from the Alberta [oil sands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_sands) is still in its early stages and the province's established bitumen resources will last for generations into the future. The [Alberta Energy Regulator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberta_Energy_Regulator) estimates that the province has 50 [billion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1000000000_\(number\)) [cubic metres](30125638.md) (310 billion [barrels](594043.md#Oil_barrel)) of ultimately recoverable bitumen resources. The AER projects that bitumen production will increase to 641,800 m<sup>3</sup>/d (4.0 million bbl/d) by 2024, but at that rate they would still last for about 213 years.[^cite_note-ST98-2015-47]<sup>: 3-10–3-26 </sup> Because of the enormous size of the known oil sands deposits, economic, labor, environmental, and government policy considerations are the constraints on production rather than finding new deposits.

In addition, the Alberta Energy Regulator has recently identified over 67 billion cubic metres (420 Gbbl) of unconventional shale oil resources in the province.[^cite_note-ST98-2015-47]<sup>: 4–3 </sup> This volume is larger than the province's oil sands resources, and if developed would give Canada the largest crude oil reserves in the world. However, due to the recent nature of the discoveries there are not yet any plans to develop them.

## Upstream, midstream and downstream components of Canadian petroleum industry

There are three components of the Canadian petroleum industry: upstream, midstream and downstream.

### Upstream

The upstream oil sector is also commonly known as the *exploration and production (E\&P) sector*.[^cite_note-Petroleum_industry-48][^cite_note-Upstream,_midstream_&_downstream-49][^cite_note-Industry_Overview-50]

The upstream sector includes the searching for potential underground or underwater [crude oil](23195.md) and [natural gas](22131.md) fields, drilling of exploratory wells, and subsequently drilling and operating the wells that recover and bring the crude oil and/or raw natural gas to the surface. With the development of methods for extracting [methane](18582230.md) from [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal) seams,[^cite_note-51] there has been a significant shift toward including [unconventional gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unconventional_gas) as a part of the upstream sector, and corresponding developments in [liquified natural gas (LNG)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquified_natural_gas) processing and transport. The upstream sector of the petroleum industry includes [Extraction of petroleum](8082027.md), [Oil production plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_production_plant), [Oil refinery](195137.md) and [Oil well](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_well).



Overview of an oil pipeline system from the wellhead to downstream consumers

### Midstream

The midstream sector involves the transportation, storage, and wholesale marketing of crude or refined petroleum products. Canada has a large network of [pipelines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipeline_transport) - over 840,000 km - that transport crude oil and natural gas across the country.[^cite_note-52] There are four main pipeline groups: gathering, feeder, transmission, and distribution pipelines. Gathering pipelines transport crude oil and natural gas from wells drilled in the subsurface to oil batteries or natural gas processing facilities. The majority of these pipelines are found in petroleum producing areas in Western Canada.[^cite_note-\:0-8] Feeder pipelines move crude oil, natural gas, and natural gas liquids (NGLs) from the batteries, processing facilities, and storage tanks to the long-distance portion of the transportation system: transmission pipelines. These are the major carriers of crude oil, natural gas, and NGLs within provinces and across provincial or international borders, where the products are either sent to refineries or exported to other markets.[^cite_note-\:0-8] Finally, distribution pipelines are the conduit for delivering natural gas to downstream customers, such as local utilities, and then further distributed to homes and businesses. If pipelines are near capacity or non-existent in certain areas, crude oil is then transported over land by [rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_transport#Rail_cars) or [truck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_transport#Trucks), or over water by [marine vessels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_transport#Marine_Vessels).

The midstream operations are often taken to include some elements of the upstream and downstream sectors. For example, the midstream sector may include [natural gas processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas_processing) plants which purify the raw natural gas as well as removing and producing [elemental sulfur](27127.md) and [natural gas liquids (NGL)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas_liquids) as finished end-products. Midstream service providers in Canada refer to [Barge](4177.md) companies, [Railroad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railroad) companies, [Trucking and hauling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Road_transport) companies, [Pipeline transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipeline_transport) companies, [Logistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logistics) and [technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technology) companies, [Transloading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transloading) companies and Terminal developers and operators. Development of the massive oil sand reserves in Alberta would be facilitated by enhancing the [North American pipeline network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_oil_pipelines#North_America) which would transport [dilbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dilbit) to refineries or export facilities.[^cite_note-NYT82513-53]

### Downstream

The downstream sector commonly refers to the [refining of petroleum crude oil](195137.md) and the [processing and purifying of raw natural gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas_processing),[^cite_note-Petroleum_industry-48][^cite_note-Upstream,_midstream_&_downstream-49][^cite_note-Industry_Overview-50] as well as the marketing and distribution of [products derived from crude oil](23195.md) and [natural gas](22131.md). The downstream sector touches consumers through products such as [gasoline or petrol](23639.md), [kerosene](16992.md), [jet fuel](1205435.md), [diesel oil](81761.md), [heating oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heating_oil), [fuel oils](392547.md), [lubricants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lubricants), [waxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waxes), [asphalt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitumen), [natural gas](22131.md), and [liquified petroleum gas (LPG)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquified_petroleum_gas) as well as hundreds of [petrochemicals](67728.md). Midstream operations are often included in the downstream category and considered to be a part of the downstream sector.

#### Crude oil

Crude oil, for example, [Western Canadian Select](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Canadian_Select) (WCS) is a mixture of many varieties of [hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbons) and most usually has many [sulfur-containing compounds](27127.md). The refining process converts most of that sulfur into gaseous [hydrogen sulfide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_sulfide). Raw natural gas also may contain gaseous hydrogen sulfide and sulfur-containing [mercaptans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercaptans), which are removed in [natural gas processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gas_processing) plants before the gas is distributed to consumers. The [hydrogen sulfide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_sulfide) removed in the refining and processing of crude oil and natural gas is subsequently converted into byproduct elemental sulfur. In fact, the vast majority of the 64,000,000 metric tons of sulfur produced worldwide in 2005 was byproduct sulfur from refineries and natural gas processing plants.[^cite_note-54][^cite_note-55]

## Export capacity

Total Canadian crude oil production, most of which is coming from the [Western Canada Sedimentary Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Canadian_Sedimentary_Basin) (WCSB), is forecast to increase from 3.85 million barrels per day (b/d) in 2016 to 5.12 million b/d by 2030.[^cite_note-\:1-56] Supply from the Alberta oil sands accounts for most of the growth and is expected to increase from 1.3 million b/d in 2016 to 3.7 million b/d in 2030.[^cite_note-\:1-56] Bitumen from the oil sands requires blending with a [diluent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diluent) in order to decrease its viscosity and density so that it can easily flow through pipelines. The addition of diluent will add an estimated 200,000 b/d to the total volumes of crude oil in Canada, for a total of 1.5 million extra barrels per day requiring the creation of additional transport capacity to markets.[^cite_note-\:1-56] The current takeaway capacity in Western Canada is tight, as oil producers are beginning to outpace the movement of their products.

Pipeline capacity measurements are complex and subject to variability. They depend on a number of factors, such as the type of product being transported, the products it is mixed with, pressure reductions, maintenance, and pipeline configurations.[^cite_note-57] The major oil pipelines exiting Western Canada have a design transport capacity of 4.0 million b/d.[^cite_note-\:1-56] In 2016, however, the pipeline capacity was estimated at 3.9 million b/d,[^cite_note-crudeoilfacts-1] and in 2017 the [Canadian Association of Petroleum Producers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Association_of_Petroleum_Producers) (CAPP) estimated the pipeline capacity to be 3.3 million b/d.[^cite_note-\:1-56] The lack of available pipeline capacity for petroleum forces oil producers to look to alternative transport methods, such as rail.

Crude-by-rail shipments are expected to increase as existing pipelines reach capacity and proposed pipelines experience approval delays.[^cite_note-\:63-58] The rail loading capacity for crude in Western Canada is close to 1.2 million b/d, although this varies depending on several factors including the length of the unit trains, size and type of railcars used, and the types of crude oil loaded.[^cite_note-Board-59] Other studies, however, estimate the current rail loading capacity in Western Canada to be 754,000 b/d.[^cite_note-\:1-56] The [International Energy Agency](285292.md) (IEA) forecasts that crude-by-rail exports will increase from 150,000 b/d in late 2017 to 390,000 b/d in 2019, which is much greater than the record high of 179,000 b/d in 2014.[^cite_note-\:2-60] The IEA also warns that rail shipments could reach as high as 590,000 b/d in 2019 unless producers store their produced crude during peak months.[^cite_note-\:2-60] The oil industry in the WCSB may need to continue to rely on rail in the forecastable future, as no major new pipeline capacity is expected to be available before 2019.[^cite_note-Board-59] The capacity - to a certain extent - is there, but producers must be willing to pay a premium to move crude by rail. &#x20;

### Getting to tidewater

Canada has had access to western tide water since 1953, with a capacity of roughly 890,000 bpd <https://www.transmountain.com/history> via the Trans Mountain (TMX) Pipeline. Following the 2024 expansion of TMX, producers in Alberta are able to reach the Port of Vancouver for shipping to Asia, significantly diversifying exports. China is the largest Asian buyer, representing about 61% of exports to the Indo-Pacific region with over 200,000 barrels per day (bpd) average being shipped to China as of 2025.

With regard to the claim that Canada does not have access to “international prices”, many economists decry the concept that Canada does have access to the globalized economy as ridiculous and attribute the price differential to the costs of shipping heavy, sour crude thousands of kilometres, compounded by over supply in the destinations able to process aforementioned oil.[^cite_note-61] Due to a doubling of a “production and export” model bet on by the biggest players in the oil sands, producers in 2018 encountered an over supply problem. Preferred access ports include the US Gulf ports via the [Keystone XL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keystone_XL) pipeline to the south, the British Columbia Pacific coast in [Kitimat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitimat) via the [Enbridge Northern Gateway Pipelines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enbridge_Northern_Gateway_Pipelines), and the [Trans Mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans_Mountain_Pipeline) line to Vancouver, BC. Under Prime Minister [Stephen Harper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Harper), the Canadian government spent \$9 million by May 2012, and \$16.5 million by May 2013, to promote Keystone XL.[^cite_note-CP22may20132-62] In the United States, Democrats are concerned that Keystone XL would simply facilitate getting Alberta oil sands products to tidewater for export to China and other countries via the American Gulf Coast of Mexico.[^cite_note-CP22may20132-62]

In 2013, Generating for Seven Generations (G7G) and [AECOM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AECOM) received \$1.8 million in funding from [Alberta Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberta_Energy) to study the feasibility of building a railway from northern Alberta to the Port of [Valdez, Alaska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valdez,_Alaska).[^cite_note-63] The proposed 2,440-km railway would be capable of transporting 1 million to 1.5 million b/d of bitumen and petroleum products, as well as other commodities, to tidewater[^cite_note-\:4-64] (avoiding the [tanker ban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enbridge_Northern_Gateway_Pipelines#Tanker_moratorium_in_British_Columbia) along British Columbia's northern coast). The last leg of the route - [Delta Junction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_Junction,_Alaska) through the coastal mountain range to Valdez - was not deemed economically feasible by rail; an alternative, however, may be the transfer of products to the underutilized [Trans Alaska Pipeline System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-Alaska_Pipeline_System) (TAPS) to Valdez.[^cite_note-\:4-64]

Port Metro Vancouver has a number of petroleum terminals, including Suncor Burrard Terminal in Port Moody, Imperial Oil Ioco Terminal in Burrard Inlet East, and Kinder Morgan Westridge, Shell Canada Shellburn, and Chevron Canada Stanovan terminals in Burnaby.[^cite_note-65]

### Pipeline versus rail debate

The public debate surrounding the trade-offs between pipeline and rail transportation has been developing over the past decade as the amount of crude oil transported by rail has increased.[^cite_note-66][^cite_note-\:63-58] It was invigorated in 2013 after the deadly [Lac-Mégantic disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lac-M%C3%A9gantic_rail_disaster) in Quebec when a freight train derailed and spilled 5.56 million litres[^cite_note-67] of crude oil, which resulted in explosions and fires that destroyed much of the town's core. That same year, a train carrying propane and crude derailed near Gainford, Alberta, resulting in two explosions but no injuries or fatalities.[^cite_note-68] These [rail accidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_rail_accidents_\(2010%E2%80%93present\)), among other examples, have raised concerns that the regulation of rail transport is inadequate for large-scale crude oil shipments. Pipeline failures also occur, for instance, in 2015 a [Nexen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nexen_Energy) pipeline ruptured and leaked 5 million litres of crude oil over approximately 16,000 m<sup>2</sup> at the company's [Long Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Lake_\(oil_sands\)#2015_oil_spill) oilsands facility south of Fort McMurray.[^cite_note-69] Although both pipeline and rail transportation are generally quite safe, neither mode is without risk. Numerous studies, however, indicate that pipelines are safer, based on the number of occurrences (accidents and incidents) weighed against the quantity of product transported.[^cite_note-70][^cite_note-71] Between 2004 and 2015, the likelihood of rail accidents in Canada was 2.6 times greater than for pipelines per thousand barrels of oil equivalents (Mboe).[^cite_note-\:32-72] Natural gas products were 4.8 times more likely to have a rail occurrence when compared to similar commodities transported by pipelines.[^cite_note-\:32-72] Critics question if pipelines carrying diluted bitumen from Alberta's oil sands are more likely to corrode and cause incidents, but evidence shows the risk of corrosion being no different from that of other crude oils.[^cite_note-73]

#### Costs

A 2017 study by the [National Bureau of Economic Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bureau_of_Economic_Research) found that contrary to popular belief, the sum of air pollution and [greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions](9528025.md) costs is substantially larger than accidents and spill costs for both pipelines and rail.[^cite_note-74] For crude oil transported from the North Dakota [Bakken Formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bakken_Formation), air pollution and [greenhouse gas emission](9528025.md) costs are substantially larger for rail compared to pipeline. For pipelines and rail, the [Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipeline_and_Hazardous_Materials_Safety_Administration)'s (PHMSA) central estimate of spill and accident costs is US\$62 and US\$381 per million-barrel miles transported, respectively.[^cite_note-\:5-75] Total GHG and air pollution costs are 8 times higher than accident and spills costs for pipelines (US\$531 vs US\$62) and 3 times higher for rail (US\$1015 vs US\$381).[^cite_note-\:5-75]

Finally, transporting oil and gas by rail is generally more expensive for producers than transporting it by pipeline. On average, it costs between US\$10-\$15 per barrel to transport oil and gas by rail compared to \$5 a barrel for pipeline.[^cite_note-76][^cite_note-77] In 2012,16 million barrels of oil were exported to USA by rail. By 2014, that number increased to 59 million barrels.[^cite_note-\:7-78] Although quantities decreased to 48 million in 2017, the competitive advantages offered by rail, particularly its access to remote regions as well as lack of regulatory and social challenges compared with building new pipelines, will likely make it a viable transportation method for years to come.[^cite_note-\:7-78] Both forms of transportation play a role in moving oil efficiently, but each has its unique trade-offs in terms of the benefits it offers.

## Regulatory agencies in Canada

See also: [Energy policy of Canada](10469240.md)

The jurisdiction over the petroleum industry in Canada, which includes energy policies regulating the petroleum industry, is shared between the [federal](616255.md) and [provincial and territorial governments](75763.md). Provincial governments have jurisdiction over the exploration, development, conservation, and management of [non-renewable resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-renewable_resources) such as petroleum products. Federal jurisdiction in energy is primarily concerned with regulation of inter-provincial and [international trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_trade) (which included pipelines) and commerce, and the management of non-renewable resources such as petroleum products on [federal lands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_lands).[^cite_note-NRCAN-79]

### Natural Resources Canada (NRCan)

Oil and Gas Policy and Regulatory Affairs Division (Oil and Gas Division) of Natural Resources Canada (NRCan) provides an annual review of and summaries of trending of crude oil, natural gas and petroleum product industry in Canada and the United States (US)[^cite_note-80]

### National Energy Board

Until February 2018, the petroleum industry was also regulated by the [National Energy Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Energy_Board) (NEB), an independent federal [regulatory agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulatory_agency). The NEB regulated inter-provincial and international oil and gas [pipeline transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipeline_transport) and [power lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_lines); the export and import of natural gas under long-term licenses and short-term orders, oil exports under long-term licenses and short-term orders (no applications for long-term exports have been filed in recent years), and [frontier lands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frontier_lands) and [offshore areas](9969688.md) not covered by provincial/federal management agreements.

In 1985, the federal government and the provincial governments in [Alberta](717.md), [British Columbia](3392.md) and [Saskatchewan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saskatchewan) agreed to [deregulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deregulate) the prices of crude oil and natural gas. Offshore oil [Atlantic Canada](71095.md) is administered under joint federal and provincial responsibility in [Nova Scotia](21184.md) and [Newfoundland and Labrador](21980.md).[^cite_note-NRCAN-79]

### Provincial regulatory agencies

There were few regulations in the early years of the petroleum industry. In [Turner Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_petroleum_industry_in_Canada,_part_one#Turner_Valley), Alberta for example, where the first significant field of petroleum was found in 1914, it was common to extract a small amount of petroleum liquids by [flaring off](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_petroleum_industry_in_Canada,_part_one#Waste_and_conservation) about 90% of the natural gas. According to a 2001 report that amount of gas that would have been worth billions. In 1938 the Alberta provincial government responded to the conspicuous and wasteful burning of natural gas. By the time crude oil was discovered in the Turner Valley field, in 1930, most of the free gas cap had been flared off.[^cite_note-81] The Alberta Petroleum and Natural Gas Conservation Board (today known as the [Energy Resources Conservation Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_Resources_Conservation_Board)) was established in 1931 to initiate conservation measures but by that time the Depression caused a waning of interest in petroleum production in Turner Valley which was revived from 1939 to 1945.[^cite_note-82]
