**Bulawayo** ([/bʊləˈwɑːjoʊ/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English), [/-ˈweɪoʊ/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English);[^cite_note-3] [Northern Ndebele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ndebele_language): *Bulawayo*) is the second largest city in [Zimbabwe](34399.md), and the largest city in the country's [Matabeleland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matabeleland) region.[^cite_note-4] The city's population is disputed; the 2022 census listed it at 665,940,[^cite_note-5] while the Bulawayo City Council claimed it to be about 1.2 million. Bulawayo covers an area of 546 square kilometres (211 square miles) in the western part of the country, along the Matsheumhlope River. Along with the capital [Harare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harare), Bulawayo is one of two cities in Zimbabwe that are also [provinces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provinces_of_Zimbabwe).

Bulawayo was founded by a group led by [Gundwane Ndiweni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gundwane_Ndiweni) around 1840 as the [kraal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kraal) of [Mzilikazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mzilikazi), the Ndebele king and was known as Gibixhegu.[^cite_note-6] His son, [Lobengula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lobengula), succeeded him in the 1860s, and changed the name to koBulawayo and ruled from Bulawayo until 1893, when the settlement was captured by [British South Africa Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_South_Africa_Company) soldiers during the [First Matabele War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Matabele_War).[^cite_note-7] That year, the first white settlers arrived and rebuilt the town. The town was besieged by Ndebele warriors during the [Second Matabele War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Matabele_War). Bulawayo attained municipality status in 1897, and city status in 1943.[^cite_note-8]

Historically, Bulawayo has been the principal industrial centre of Zimbabwe; its factories produce cars and car products, building materials, electronic products, textiles, furniture, and food products. Bulawayo is also the hub of Zimbabwe's rail network and the headquarters of the [National Railways of Zimbabwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Railways_of_Zimbabwe).[^cite_note-9]

Bulawayo's central business district (CBD) covers 5.4 km<sup>2</sup> (2.1 sq mi) in the heart of the city and is surrounded by numerous suburbs. The majority of the city's population belongs to the [Ndebele people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ndebele_people), with minorities of [Shona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shona_people) and other groups, including [Coloured](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coloureds) and [White Zimbabweans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Zimbabweans). Bulawayo is home to over a dozen colleges and universities, most notably the [National University of Science and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_University_of_Science_and_Technology,_Zimbabwe), Bulawayo Polytechnic College,[^cite_note-tech-10] [Zimbabwe School of Mines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zimbabwe_School_of_Mines), Hillside Teachers College,[^cite_note-11] and the United College of Education.[^cite_note-12][^cite_note-13] The [Natural History Museum of Zimbabwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_History_Museum_of_Zimbabwe), formerly the National Museum, is located in Bulawayo. The city is close to tourist sites such as [Matobo National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matobo_National_Park) and the [Khami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khami) [World Heritage Site](44940.md).[^cite_note-14]

## History

The city was founded by the Ndebele king [Lobengula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lobengula), the son of King Mzilikazi, born of Matshobana,[^cite_note-\:1-15] who settled in modern-day Zimbabwe around the 1840s.[^cite_note-16] This followed the Ndebele people's great trek from northern [Kwazulu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kwazulu). The name *Bulawayo* comes from the [Ndebele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ndebele_language) word *bulala* and it translates to "the one to be killed". It is thought that at the time of the formation of the city there was a civil war. Mbiko ka Madlenya Masuku, a trusted confidant of [King Mzilikazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Mzilikazi) and leader of the Zwangendaba regiment, fought Prince Lobhengula as he did not believe that he was the legitimate heir to the throne. This was because Lobhengula was born to a [Swazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swazi_people) mother, and Masuku felt that she was of a lesser class.

At the time Lobengula was a prince fighting to ascend his father's throne. It was common at the time for people to refer to Bulawayo as *Bulawayo UmntwaneNkosi*, "a place where they are fighting or rising against the prince". The city of Bulawayo coincidentally has a similar name to the capital of the great [Zulu](14754726.md) warrior king [Shaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaka) ka Senzangakhona in [Kwazulu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kwazulu), where Mzilikazi and his [Khumalo clan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khumalo_clan) and other [Nguni people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguni_people) came from.[^cite_note-17]

In the 1860s, the city was influenced by European intrigue. Many colonial powers cast covetous eyes on Bulawayo and the land surrounding it because of its strategic location. Britain made skilful use of private initiative in the shape of [Cecil Rhodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecil_Rhodes) and the Chartered Company to disarm the suspicion of her rivals. Lobengula once described Britain as a chameleon and himself as the fly.[^cite_note-\:1-15]

During the 1893 [First Matabele War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Matabele_War), [British South Africa Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_South_Africa_Company) (BSAC) troops invaded and forced King Lobengula to evacuate his followers, after first detonating munitions and setting fire to the town.[^cite_note-18] BSAC troops and white settlers occupied the ruins. On 4 November 1893, [Leander Starr Jameson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leander_Starr_Jameson) declared Bulawayo a settlement under the rule of the BSAC. Cecil Rhodes ordered the new settlement to be founded on the ruins of Lobengula's royal kraal, a typical action by a conquering power. This is where the [State House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_House_\(Bulawayo\)) stands today.[^cite_note-DSO-19]

In 1897, the new town of Bulawayo acquired the status of municipality in the British colonial system, and Lt. Col. Harry White was appointed as one of the first mayors.[^cite_note-DSO-19][^cite_note-20]

### Siege

At the outbreak of the [Second Matabele War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Matabele_War), in March 1896, Bulawayo was besieged by Ndebele forces. The settlers established a [laager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laager) here for defensive purposes. The Ndebele had experienced the brutal effectiveness of the [Maxim guns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxim_gun) employed by BSAC troops in the First Matabele War, so they never mounted a significant attack against Bulawayo, although over 10,000 Ndebele warriors gathered to surround the town.[^cite_note-21] Rather than wait passively for attack, the settlers mounted patrols, called the Bulawayo Field Force, under [Frederick Selous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Selous) and [Frederick Russell Burnham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Russell_Burnham). These patrols rode out to rescue any surviving settlers in the countryside and attacked the Ndebele. In the first week of fighting, 20 men of the Bulawayo Field Force were killed and 50 were wounded. An unknown number of Ndebele were killed and wounded.[^cite_note-22]

During the siege, conditions in Bulawayo quickly deteriorated. By day, settlers could go to homes and buildings in the town, but at night they were forced to seek shelter in the much smaller laager. Nearly 1,000 women and children were crowded into the small area and false alarms of attacks were common.[^cite_note-23] The Ndebele neglected to cut the telegraph lines connecting Bulawayo to [Mafikeng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mafikeng). The settlers and forces appealed for relief, and the BSAC sent additional troops from [Salisbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harare) and [Fort Victoria](447930.md) (now Harare and Masvingo respectively) 500 kilometres (300 miles) to the north, and from [Kimberley](313628.md) and Mafeking 1,000 km (600 mi) to the south. Once the relief forces arrived in late May 1896, the siege was broken. An estimated 50,000 Ndebele retreated into their stronghold of the [Matobo Hills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matobo_Hills) near Bulawayo. Not until October 1896 did the Ndebele finally surrender their arms to the BSAC.[^cite_note-24]

### Modern era

By the late 1930s, Bulawayo was no longer the country's biggest city. Influence and activity moved eastwards to the other cities, especially Salisbury, a trend which continues up to the present day. Despite this, after the [Second World War](32927.md), prosperity and population growth revived, as the city became an industrial powerhouse, peaking during the Federal years as new markets opened in [Malawi](19088.md) and [Zambia](34415.md). However, Bulawayo trailed the development of other cities notably, [Johannesburg](58893.md), [Harare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harare) and [Cape Town](6653.md) during the same period.[^cite_note-ozy.com-25] In 1940, the [Royal Air Force](25679.md) (RAF) launched the [Empire Air Training Scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_Air_Training_Scheme), and facilitated the construction of multiple bases of the [Rhodesian Air Training Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodesian_Air_Training_Group). Bulawayo had ideal conditions, which included having overall flat terrain and being 1,200 meters high in elevation, which made it malaria-free. Three RAF Stations were subsequently established, which included [RAF Heany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAF_Heany), [RAF Kumalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAF_Kumalo), and [RAF Induna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAF_Induna), and were all equipped with a hangar and landing ground. Additionally, two bombing ranges were established in the outskirts of Bulawayo, and were named Miasi and Myelbo.[^cite_note-26] In 1943, Bulawayo received city status.

By 1992, [population decline](720599.md) and slow growth were beginning to occur which disproportionately affected heavy industry. In response, Bulawayo sought to re-invent itself as a 'heritage city', with its wide main streets refurbished and its [Victorian architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_architecture) and industrial heritage preserved. Institutions such as the [Bulawayo Railway Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulawayo_Railway_Museum) and [Nesbitt Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nesbitt_Castle) were restored. The city was also recognised as a centre of excellence in tertiary education and research, as the [National University of Science and Technology, Zimbabwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_University_of_Science_and_Technology,_Zimbabwe) was formed and expanded and other colleges growth also accelerated.[^cite_note-ozy.com-25]

Since the late 20th century, Bulawayo has suffered a sharp fall in living standards coinciding with the protracted [economic crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Zimbabwe#2000%E2%80%932009) affecting the country. The main challenges the city faces include underinvestment, declining [infrastructure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrastructure), [de-industrialisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De-industrialisation) and the effects of corruption and nepotism. Much of the city's educated workers have migrated south to neighbouring South Africa or further afield to the [United Kingdom](31717.md), [Canada](5042916.md) and [Australia](4689264.md). Public service concerns have become steadily more acute, with particular concern in the health sector from a growing shortage of experienced doctors and nurses.[^cite_note-27] As a result, the city faced an avoidable [cholera](7591.md) outbreak in 2008. Though the city is the centre of the southern population generally categorized as the Matebele, the population includes various ethnicities, as well as a small number of expats, mostly from neighbouring countries.[^cite_note-ozy.com-25]

The Central Business District has the widest roads. These were designed to accommodate the ox-drawn carts, and to allow them to make a turn in the street, that were used as a primary means of transport when the town was planned and erected.[^cite_note-28]

Bulawayo is nicknamed the "City of Kings" or "kontuthu ziyathunqa"—a [Ndebele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ndebele_language) phrase for "smoke arising". This name arose from the city's historically large industrial base. The large cooling towers of the coal-powered electricity generating plant situated in the city centre once used to exhaust steam and smoke over the city.[^cite_note-mg.co.za-29]

## Economy

Bulawayo was known as the industrial hub of Zimbabwe, leading to the Zimbabwe International Trade Fair being hosted in Bulawayo. It had a large manufacturing presence with large industries based here before Zimbabwe's economic decline.[^cite_note-\:2-37] However, some of these companies have either moved operations to [Harare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harare) or have closed down – which has crippled Bulawayo's economy. Most factories are deserted and the infrastructure has since been left to deteriorate. The reason for the de-industrialization has largely been political, with some factories like Goldstar Sugars removing machinery to open new factories in Harare. When the Zimbabwean government passed indigenisation laws, some successful businesses were taken over by [ZANU–PF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZANU%E2%80%93PF) supporters, only to close down a few years later.

Many locals argue that it is because of marginalisation they experience against the government due to political tensions with the ZANU-PF government in [Harare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harare) and the MDC-run Bulawayo council. For instance, the National Railways of Zimbabwe (headquarters in Bulawayo) is a government-owned entity and, as such, should have been thriving had it not been for embezzlement of funds by company executives who are believed to be Shona. The water issue is not new and had brought about the "help a thirsty Matabele" initiative of the 1970s and the [Matabeleland Zambezi Water Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matabeleland_Zambezi_Water_Project) which would put an end to the water issue in [Matabeleland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matabeleland) was drafted; however, this project was put on hold soon after independence.[^cite_note-state.gov-38]

These allegations have all been refuted by national authorities. The city still contains the bulk of Zimbabwe's heavy industry and food processing capability.

Like many parts of the country, Bulawayo seen a huge drop in service delivery and an increase in unemployment.[^cite_note-\:2-37] Many people resort to farming, mining, and the black market for sustenance, while others depended on the little foreign currency that is sent by family in other countries. However, with the inauguration of the Mnangagwa government, a new approach is seen by investors in the city who admire the already-available infrastructure; the huge workforce; and see Bulawayo as a potential business hub.[^cite_note-state.gov-38] It is set to once again contribute greatly to the economy of Zimbabwe.

## Geography

Bulawayo is located in the south west of Zimbabwe, in the middle of savanna country. It has four seasons, with rains starting in late October to about March. The coldest months are May and June with July being cold and windy.[^cite_note-48]

### Topography

The city sits on a plain that marks the Highveld of Zimbabwe and is close to the watershed between the [Zambezi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zambezi) and [Limpopo](365944.md) drainage basins. The land slopes gently downwards to the north and northwest. The southern side is hillier, and the land becomes more broken in the direction of the [Matobo Hills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matobo_Hills) to the south.

### Climate

Under the [Köppen climate classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%B6ppen_climate_classification), Bulawayo features a [hot semi-arid climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hot_semi-arid_climate) (*BSh*). Due to its relatively high altitude, the city has fairly moderate temperatures despite lying in the tropics. The mean annual temperature is 19.16 °C (66.49 °F),[^cite_note-49] similar to [Pretoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pretoria), which is a similar elevation but almost 600 kilometres (370 mi) further south. As with much of southern and eastern Zimbabwe, Bulawayo is cooled by a prevailing southeasterly airflow most of the year and experiences three broad seasons: a dry, cool winter season from May to August; a hot dry period in early summer from late August to early November; and a warm wet period for the rest of the summer, early November to April.

The hottest month is October, which is usually the height of the dry season. The average maximum temperature ranges from 21 °C (70 °F) in July to 30 °C (86 °F) in October. During the rainy season, daytime maxima are around 26 °C (79 °F). Nights are always cool, ranging from 8 °C (46 °F) in July to 16 °C (61 °F) in January.

The city's average annual rainfall is 594 mm (23.4 in), which supports open woodland vegetation, dominated by *[Combretum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combretum)* and *[Terminalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminalia_\(plant\))* trees. Most rain falls between December and February, while June to August is usually rainless. Being close to the [Kalahari Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalahari_Desert), Bulawayo is vulnerable to droughts and rainfall tends to vary sharply from year to year. In 1978, 888 mm (35.0 in) of rain fell in the three months up to February. February 1944 was the wettest month on record with 368 mm (14.5 in), while in the three months ending in February 1983, only 84 mm (3.3 in) fell.

### Water supply

Bulawayo has good-quality tap water meeting international standards. Bulawayo does not recycle waste water but uses treated waste water for irrigation.

Bulawayo experiences water shortages in drought seasons due to the overwhelming increase in population versus the static and sometimes decreasing capacity of dams. The geographical factors causing [water scarcity](15380061.md) are rising temperatures, the area's high elevation and the arid environment of [Matabeleland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matabeleland).

Environmental and sanitation circumstances have detrimental effects on water quality. Sources such as groundwater and tap water are subject to pollution due to waste from burst sewers contaminating them. Samples taken from well water from the Pumula and Robert Sinyoka suburbs show that well water has levels of [Coliform bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coliform_bacteria) exceeding levels recommended by the Standards Association of Zimbabwe and the [World Health Organization](33583.md).[^cite_note-53][^cite_note-54]

## Transport



14A class Engine no 515 on Ash Spur shunt, Bulawayo Station

The city has a total road network of about 2,100 kilometres; in 2017, 70 percent was in poor condition.[^cite_note-55] The [R2 road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R2_Road_\(Zimbabwe\)) links Bulawayo with Harare, and the [Cape to Cairo Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_to_Cairo_Road) links with [Gaborone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaborone) and [Lusaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lusaka).

The Bulawayo railway station is the central point of the railway line that connects the cities of [Lusaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lusaka) and [Gaborone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaborone) (part of the [Cape to Cairo Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_to_Cairo_Railway)), as well as being the terminal of the [Beira–Bulawayo railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beira%E2%80%93Bulawayo_railway), which connects with the cities of [Gweru](737324.md), [Harare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harare), [Mutare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutare) and [Beira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beira,_Mozambique).[^cite_note-56] Through the station on the outskirts of [Umzingwane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umzingwane), Bulawayo is connected to the [Beitbridge Bulawayo Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beitbridge_Bulawayo_Railway).

On 1 November 2013, a new terminal of [Joshua Mqabuko Nkomo International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joshua_Mqabuko_Nkomo_International_Airport), formerly known as Bulawayo Airport, was opened.[^cite_note-57]

## Healthcare

Bulawayo is home to many hospitals and other medical facilities. The United Bulawayo Hospitals, a public [hospital network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hospital_network), operates [Bulawayo Central Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulawayo_Central_Hospital), Richard Morris Hospital, Lady Rodwell Maternity Hospital, and Robbie Gibson Infectious Diseases Hospital.[^cite_note-58] [Mpilo Central Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mpilo_Hospital), is the largest hospital in Bulawayo, and the second-largest in Zimbabwe, and features a [nursing school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nursing_school) and [midwifery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midwifery) school on its campus. Bulawayo is also home to [Ingutsheni Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ingutsheni_Hospital), which at 700 beds is the largest [psychiatric hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychiatric_hospital) in Zimbabwe. Other hospitals in Bulawayo include All Saints Children's Hospital, Hillside Hospital, [Mater Dei Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mater_Dei_Hospital_\(Bulawayo\)), the Nervous Disorders Hospital, [St Francis Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Francis_Hospital_\(Bulawayo\)) and Thorngrove Isolation Hospital.

### Higher education

Bulawayo is home to a number of [colleges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College) and [universities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University). The National University of Science and Technology, Zimbabwe, ([NUST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_University_of_Science_and_Technology,_Zimbabwe)), the second largest university in Zimbabwe, was established in Bulawayo in 1991.[^cite_note-60] [Solusi University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solusi_University), a [Seventh-day Adventist](28632.md) institution established in Bulawayo in 1894, gained university status in 1994.

The [Bulawayo Polytechnic College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulawayo_Polytechnic_College) offers tertiary training for students who have completed GCE O Level and A Level education. It issues national certificates ([NC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Certificate)), diplomas and higher national diplomas ([HND](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_National_Diploma)) certificates. Bulawayo has two specialist teacher training colleges: Hillside Teachers College for secondary education and the United College of Education for primary education.

Bulawayo is home to a number of [institutes of technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_technology) and [vocational colleges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vocational_university), including [Zimbabwe School of Mines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zimbabwe_School_of_Mines), Westgate Industrial Training College, and the Zimbabwe Theological College. In addition, companies such as the National Railways of Zimbabwe ([NRZ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Railways_of_Zimbabwe)) and Zimbabwe Electricity and Supply Authority ([ZESA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZESA)) offer apprenticeship training for qualifying students who then become certified tradesworkers upon completion.

## Media

### Newspapers

[*The Chronicle*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Chronicle_\(Zimbabwe\)), a [state-owned](20803507.md) daily newspaper, and its Sunday edition, *The Sunday News*, are published in Bulawayo. *The Chronicle* is the second-oldest newspaper in Zimbabwe, and along with [*The Herald*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Herald_\(Zimbabwe\)), published in Harare, it is one of two major state-owned newspapers in the country. *UMthunywa*, a state-owned [Ndebele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ndebele_language)-language newspaper, is also published in Bulawayo, where the majority of the population belongs to the [Ndebele people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ndebele_people). Private online publications like Bulawayo24 News and B-Metro are also based in Bulawayo.

### Radio

The two radio stations, [Skyz Metro FM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skyz_Metro_FM), which is the first dedicated commercial radio station for the city and [Khulumani FM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khulumani_FM), owned by the [Zimbabwe Broadcasting Corporation](1346178.md) are based in the city and offer their programming mainly in [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language) and [Ndebele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ndebele_language) and other languages spoken in the [Matabeleland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matabeleland) region. The other six radio stations, only two of which are privately owned, are also accessible in the city via FM transmission.

### Television

The state owned [ZBC TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZBC_TV) is the only free TV channel in the city. The majority of households rely on the South African-based satellite television distributor, [DStv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DStv) and [OVHD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OVHD) for entertainment, news and sport across Africa and the world.

### Internet

There are a number of internet service providers in the city. Most people in the city access the internet through their mobile phones mainly for news, entertainment and communication.
