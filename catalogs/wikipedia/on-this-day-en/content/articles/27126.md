**Schleswig-Holstein** ([/ˈʃlɛswɪɡ ˈhoʊlstaɪn/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English))[^cite_note-6] is a coastal state that is the [northernmost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Germany) of the sixteen [states of Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_of_Germany), comprising most of the historical [Duchy of Holstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Holstein) and the southern part of the former [Duchy of Schleswig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Schleswig). It borders [Denmark](76972.md), [Mecklenburg-Vorpommern](38168.md), [Lower Saxony](18435.md) and the city-state of [Hamburg](13467.md). Its capital city is [Kiel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiel); other notable cities are [Lübeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%BCbeck) and [Flensburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flensburg). It covers an area of 15,763 km<sup>2</sup> (6,086 sq mi), making it the [5th smallest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_German_states_by_area) German state by area (including the city-states). Historically, the name can also refer to a larger region, containing both present-day Schleswig-Holstein and the former [South Jutland County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Jutland_County) (Northern Schleswig; now part of the [Region of Southern Denmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Region_of_Southern_Denmark)) in Denmark.&#x20;

Schleswig, known as South Jutland at the time, had been under Danish control since the fifth century. In the 12th century, however, it became a duchy within Denmark due to infighting in the Danish royal house. It bordered Holstein, which was a part of the [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire). Beginning in 1460, the King of Denmark ruled both Schleswig and Holstein as their duke. Schleswig was still part of Denmark, while Holstein remained part of the Holy Roman Empire. In the 19th century, both Danes and Germans believed they had a rightful claim to Schleswig-Holstein. While the population of Holstein and Lauenburg was entirely ethnically German, Schleswig had been predominantly Danish until the late 1700s and early 1800s, when extensive German influence led to the Germanization of [South Schleswig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Schleswig). The resulting long-term political and territorial dispute was known as the [Schleswig-Holstein Question](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schleswig-Holstein_Question). In 1848, Denmark tried to formally annex the area. Prussia responded by invading, thus beginning the [First Schleswig War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Schleswig_War), which ended in a victory for Denmark. But in the [Second Schleswig War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Schleswig_War) (1864), Prussia and Austria won, and the territory was absorbed into Prussia in 1867. After the German defeat in [World War I](4764461.md) the Allies required that the question of sovereignty over the territory be submitted to [plebiscites](51582.md) (the [1920 Schleswig plebiscites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1920_Schleswig_plebiscites)). They resulted in the return of the Danish-speaking North Schleswig to Denmark. The area was subsequently renamed South Jutland, restoring the historical name used before it was changed to Schleswig by [Gerhard III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerhard_III,_Count_of_Holstein-Rendsburg) of the [House of Schauenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Schaumburg). After [World War II](32927.md), Schleswig-Holstein [took in over a million refugees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refugees_in_Schleswig-Holstein_after_the_Second_World_War).

Today, Schleswig-Holstein's economy is known for its agriculture, such as its [Holstein cows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holstein_cow). Its position on the Atlantic Ocean makes it a major trade point and shipbuilding site; it is also the location of the [Kiel Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiel_Canal). Its offshore oil wells and wind farms produce significant amounts of energy. Fishing is a major industry and the basis of its distinctive, unique local cuisine. It is a popular tourist destination for Germans and visitors from across the globe.

The historic settlement areas in present-day Schleswig-Holstein

The term "Holstein" derives from [Old Saxon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Saxon) *Holseta Land* (*Holz* means wood in modern Standardized German; *[holt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodland)* is a now-archaic English word for woods.) Originally, the term referred to the central of the three [Saxon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saxons) tribes north of the [River Elbe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Elbe): *Tedmarsgoi* (Dithmarschen), [Holstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holstein), and *Sturmarii* ([Stormarn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stormarn_\(gau\))). The area inhabited by the tribe of the Holsts lay between the [Stör](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St%C3%B6r) River and [Hamburg](13467.md); after [Christianization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianization), their main church was in [Schenefeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schenefeld,_Steinburg). [Saxon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Saxony) Holstein became a part of the [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire) after [Charlemagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlemagne)'s [Saxon campaigns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saxon_Wars) in the late eighth century. Beginning in 811, the northern border of Holstein (and thus of the Empire) was the [River Eider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eider_\(river\)).

The term "Schleswig" originally referred to the city of [Schleswig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schleswig,_Schleswig-Holstein). The word Schleswig is a German transliteration of the Danish word Slesvig, which consists of two words: [Schlei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schlei) and vig. The Schlei refers to the river at which the city lies, and vig means "[inlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inlet)" or bay. Schleswig, therefore, means (in Danish): "The bay at the river Schlei". The Schlei is known as Slien in Danish and is believed to have been used only for the inner Slien (the Great and Little Bay near the city of Schleswig). The word is thought to be related to Slæ, which means reeds and aquatic plants found in this area.[^cite_note-7]

The [Duchy of Schleswig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Schleswig), or Southern Jutland, was originally an integral part of Denmark, but in [medieval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval) times was established as a [fief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fief) under the control of the Kingdom of Denmark, having the same relationship with the [Danish Crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_Royal_Family) as, for example, [Brandenburg](3765.md) or [Bavaria](3764.md) had with the [Holy Roman Emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Emperor). Around 1100, the [Duchy of Saxony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Saxony) gave Holstein to [Count](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Count) [Adolf I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_I_of_Holstein) of [Schauenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_of_Schauenburg).

### The Migration Period



The [Hærvejen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C3%A6rvejen), starting at its northern terminus in [Viborg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viborg,_Denmark) and reaching its southern terminus in [Hamburg](13467.md)

During the [Migration Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Migration_Period), the [Jutland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jutland) peninsula was home to several tribes. The [Jutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jutes) inhabited the most northern part of the peninsula from [Grenen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grenen) to [Olger's Dyke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olger's_Dyke?action=edit\&redlink=1). This dyke, dating back to around the first century, served as the boundary between the Jutes in the north and the [Angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angles_\(tribe\)) in the south.[^cite_note-8] This dyke lost its relevance in the 200s when the Angles expanded northward, leading to the establishment of a new dyke called the [Wendish Dyke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wendish_Dyke?action=edit\&redlink=1). The southern border of the Angles was marked by the [marshes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsh) surrounding the [Eider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eider_\(river\)), that combined with the dense [old-growth forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old-growth_forest) to the south of the river, formed a natural barrier.[^cite_note-9] South of the forest lay the region now known as Holstein, which was divided between the [Germanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_peoples) [Saxons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saxons), who inhabited the western part, and the [Slavic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavs) [Wagri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wagria), who lived in the eastern part. In the 8th century, the Wagri became part of the Slavic tribal confederation known as the [Obotrites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obotrites)[^cite_note-10] (also known as the [Wends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wends) by the Danes and Saxons).[^cite_note-11]

During the 4th and early 5th centuries, a significant migration saw the Jutes, Angles, and Saxons depart from their homelands to settle in the [British Isles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Isles).[^cite_note-12] This mass exodus left much of the Jutland Peninsula sparsely populated, allowing the Danes from southern Scandinavia and the islands of [Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zealand), [Funen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funen), and other smaller Danish isles to migrate into the peninsula. They gradually settled the region, integrating the remaining Jutes and Angles who had not left for Britain. By the mid-5th century, the Danes had established settlements from Grenen in the north to just north of the Eider River and its marshes. Their southernmost settlements being around [Schwansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwansen), [Hedeby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedeby) and [Husum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Husum), mirroring the same southern border as their Angle predecessors.[^cite_note-13] As raiding was a frequent practice among the Danes, Saxons, and Wagrians, the borderlands was a hostile and unsafe area to inhabit. In response to these threats, the Danes began constructing the [Danevirke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danevirke). Originally built as a dyke, it was gradually expanded into a 30-kilometer earthwork with a palisade fortification, forming a barrier between the Danes and their southern neighbors. The Danevirke was strategically positioned at the narrowest point of the peninsula, with its eastern end beginning at the [Treene River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treene_\(river\)) and extending to the [Schlei Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schlei).[^cite_note-14] This fortification served to deter Saxon and Wagrian raids while enabling the Danes to launch their own raids into southern territories. It would remain in use until 1864, being expanded and adapted to the changing military needs of the Danes multiple times.[^cite_note-15]

The establishment of the Danevirke not only helped to prevent Saxon and Wagrian raids into Danish territory but also served as a toll station. Danish chieftains would collect fees from traders, merchants, and peasants traveling along the [Hærvejen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C3%A6rvejen) (Heerweg/Armyway), the main trade route running through the peninsula. Saxons, Wagrians, and Danes alike used the Hærvejen to trade goods such as honey, furs, amber, glass, metalwork, and other commodities, such as livestock, with cattle and oxen being particularly important. Especially the Danes developed a thriving livestock breeding industry, driving large herds along the Hærvejen to sell on the continent. This trade gave the route its alternative names, such as "Studevejen" (Cattle Way) in Danish and "Ochsenweg" (Oxen Way) in German.[^cite_note-16] Therefore control of the Danevirke, through which the Hærvejen ran, was of immense financial significance. Whoever controlled the gates of Danevirke also controlled the trade along the Hærvejen, giving them access to substantial wealth.

### The Early Middle Ages

After approximately 350 to 400 years of Danes being the sole inhabitants north of the Eider, the [Frisians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frisians) arrived in two waves, the first of which occurred in the 800s. They came from [Frisia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frisia) and initially settled on the islands of [Heligoland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heligoland), [Sylt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylt), [Föhr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F%C3%B6hr), and [Amrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amrum) in the southwestern part of Jutland. Later, they expanded to [Eiderstedt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eiderstedt) and the Jutish coastline.[^cite_note-17] These areas eventually became known as [North Frisia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Frisia), though historically, the region was referred to as [Uthlande](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uthlande) (Outland).[^cite_note-18] In these settlements, the Frisians established fishing and trading stations. The local Danes soon became a minority and, over time, assimilated into the Frisian population.[^cite_note-19] The Frisians contributed to the broader Hærvejen trade network, with sea routes extending along the Frisian and Dutch coasts, supplying high-quality salt, fish, and other maritime goods. Moreover, they were particularly active in trade with [East Anglia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Anglia) in [England](9316.md), where pottery was exchanged in large quantities.

#### Saxon Wars

Main article: [Saxon Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saxon_Wars)



The *[Limes Saxoniae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limes_Saxoniae)* border between the [Saxons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saxons) and the [Obotrites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obotrites), established about 810 in present-day Schleswig-Holstein

Normalcy in the area vanished with the expansion of the [Frankish Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francia) into [Saxony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saxons) from 772 to 804, triggering a generational war on an unprecedented scale for the region. Before this, the Franks had spent nearly 20 years, from the late 600s to the early 700s, subjugating and converting the [Frisian Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frisian_Kingdom). Their primary opponent was the formidable Frisian king [Redbad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radbod_of_Frisia), who fiercely resisted the Franks until his death. Now, the Frisians' neighbors, the Saxons, faced Frankish expansion. The *casus belli* was a Saxon raid on the church in [Deventer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deventer) in January of 772. This conflict, fueled by [Charlemagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlemagne)'s desire to conquer the Saxons and convert them from their belief in the [Germanic pantheon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_mythology) to [Christianity](5211.md), used the Deventer raid as a pretext to wage a war that would ultimately reshape the political and cultural landscape of what would later become Holstein forever. Throughout 18 campaigns, carried out in three phases over 32 years, Charlemagne aimed to subdue the Saxons and forcibly convert them to Christianity, in what became known as the [Saxon Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saxon_Wars). In retaliation for the raid on the church in Deventer, Charlemagne ordered his troops to destroy the holy pillar [Irminsul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irminsul), near [Paderborn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paderborn) in either 772 or 773—a notorious act that sent shockwaves throughout the Germanic pagan world. It has been postulated that Irminsul symbolised [Yggdrasil Ash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yggdrasil) - the world tree. Charlemagne then destroyed all Saxon settlements up to the [Wesser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weser) river. After defeating the Saxons and securing [hostages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hostage_diplomacy), he turned his attention to northern [Italy](14532.md).

For centuries, the Danes and Saxons had regarded each other as kindred peoples, sharing the same belief in the Germanic pantheon and frequently intermarrying, especially among the elite. Thus, the defeated Saxon warleader [Widukind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Widukind) sought refuge with his father-in-law, Danish king [Sigfred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigfred). The *[Royal Frankish Annals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Frankish_Annals)* mentions that Widukind received substantial aid from Sigfred, though the exact nature of this aid is not explicitly stated. However, the chronicles do note that Sigfred and Charlemagne brokered a peace agreement some years later, indicating that Sigfred, upon hearing Widukind's plea, may have mustered his army and joined the war on the side of the Saxons. In response to the Danes' involvement in the war, Charlemagne seems to have recruited the [Obotrites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obotrites) by promising them the Saxon portion of Holstein. This alliance with Charlemagne shifted the balance of power in the region. In 782, after another defeat of the Saxons, Charlemagne ordered the mass execution of 4,500 Saxons, an atrocity that became known as the [Massacre of Verden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massacre_of_Verden). Following this brutal act, Charlemagne was nicknamed the "Butcher of Saxons" or "Saxonslaughterer."

In 796, despite Saxony being fully under Frankish rule, the Saxons rose once more, supported by the Danes. The rebellion was triggered by the forced conscription of Saxons for the Frankish [wars against the Avars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avar_Wars). Moreover, Charlemagne, in alliance with the Obotrites, planned to subjugate the Danes, now led by King [Gudfred Sigfredson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gudfred). However, Gudfred struck first. He expanded the Danevirke, assembled a fleet, mustered an army, and launched attacks on the Obotrites and later Frankish Frisia. But he was slain, either by one of his [huscarls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huscarls) or possibly his own illegitimate son, on the Frisian campaign. The new Danish king, [Hemming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemming_of_Denmark), Gudfred's nephew, initiated peace talks, which resulted in the [Treaty of Heiligen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Heiligen) in 810. The treaty established the Danish border at the Eider River. Charlemagne retained Saxony, including Holstein, and established the [Limes Saxoniae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limes_Saxoniae) as a border with the Obotrites. This agreement established firm boundaries between the Franks, Obotrites, and Danes, securing peace in the region.

### Duchies in the Danish realm

Between 500 and 1200, Schleswig was an integral part of Denmark, but during the 12th century, [Duke Abel of Schlewig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abel,_King_of_Denmark) came into conflict with his brother [King Eric IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_IV_of_Denmark). Abel managed to gain autonomy from his brother, making Schleswig an autonomous duchy. Later, Abel had Eric assassinated and seized the throne. Despite this, Schleswig remained an autonomous duchy within the Kingdom, setting the stage for future conflicts. Beginning in 1460, both the [Duchy of Schleswig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Schleswig) and [Duchy of Holstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Holstein) were ruled together by the Danish king, who acted as the duke of both regions. Holstein, being a duchy within the [Holy Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Roman_Empire), created a situation where the Danish king was sovereign of Denmark but also a duke within the Holy Roman Empire. Both were ruled for several centuries by the kings of Denmark. In 1721, all of Schleswig was united into a single duchy under the king of Denmark, and the great powers of Europe confirmed in an international treaty that all future kings of Denmark should automatically become dukes of Schleswig: consequently, Schleswig would always follow the order of succession that applied in the Kingdom of Denmark.

After the Protestant Reformation, German was established as the language of commerce, administration, education, and clergy in Schleswig despite the population being ethnically Danish. This was because Schleswig was managed by the German Chancellery in Kiel, which was later renamed the Schleswig-Holstein Chancellery in 1806. Therefore, Danes were sent to Kiel for their education instead of Copenhagen, where they received their education in German rather than their native Danish. As a result, Danish students, future administrators, clergy, and educators were taught in German and continued to use the language throughout their professional lives.

In 1814, mandatory schooling was instituted, and it was taught in German. This created generations of Danish children who learned German from an early age. Their schooling was conducted in German, they heard Sermons in German, and when they grew up, their interactions with the administration and business were conducted in German. Additionally, if Danes didn't learn German, they couldn't communicate with the administration, which often cared little if the citizens were able to understand them. Therefore, if the Danes weren't able to speak German, they were effectively frozen out of any official matters. As a result, a language shift slowly began forming in South Schleswig and gradually spread north, which alarmed Copenhagen. The Danish authorities started taking countermeasures to halt the language shift by banning German in all official matters in Schleswig, which only served to create tensions between Danes and Germans. This language strife significantly contributed to shaping the inhabitants' national sentiments during a time of national unrest in Europe. It is also during this period that we see surname changes, such as from Jørgensen to Jürgensen or Nielsen to Nilsen, in South Schleswig. By the time of the First Schleswig War, one-third of Schleswig and half of South Schleswig spoke German as their first language. By the time of the Second Schleswig War in 1864, half of Schleswig and the vast majority of South Schleswig spoke German as their first language.

### Schleswig-Holstein Question

Main article: [Schleswig–Holstein question](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schleswig%E2%80%93Holstein_question)

In the 19th century, fueled by [nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalism), both Danes and Germans claimed Schleswig-Holstein. The Germans wanted both Schleswig and Holstein to separate from Denmark and join the [German Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Confederation), invoking the [Treaty of Ribe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Ribe) stating that the two duchies should stay "Forever Undivided". The Danes, on the other hand, furthered the Eider Policy ([da\:Ejderpolitikken](https://da.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ejderpolitikken)), stating that the natural Danish border was the [Eider (river)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eider_\(river\)) as first recognised in the [Treaty of Heiligen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Heiligen). Therefore, the Danes sought to reintegrate Schleswig into the [Kingdom of Denmark](11746442.md), reversing the separation created by King Abel, while also granting Holstein independence to join the German Confederation as a sovereign entity. The resulting long-term political and territorial dispute was known as the [Schleswig-Holstein Question](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schleswig%E2%80%93Holstein_question). Holstein was entirely German-speaking, while Schleswig was predominantly Danish-speaking until the late 1700s and early 1800s. During this period, a linguistic shift began in southern Schleswig, transitioning from Danish to German. This meant that Schleswig was linguistically divided with a Danish-speaking north and a German-speaking south.

In 1848, King [Frederick VII of Denmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_VII_of_Denmark) declared that he would grant Denmark a liberal constitution and the immediate goal of the Danish national movement was to ensure that this constitution would give rights to all Danes, i.e. not only to those in the Kingdom of Denmark, but also to Danes (and Germans) living in Schleswig. Furthermore, they demanded protection for the Danish language in Schleswig (the dominant language in almost a quarter of Schleswig had changed from Danish to German since the beginning of the 19th century). A liberal constitution for Holstein was not seriously considered in [Copenhagen](5166.md), since it was well known that the political élite of Holstein were more conservative than Copenhagen's. Representatives of German-minded Schleswig-Holsteiners demanded that Schleswig and Holstein be unified and allowed its own constitution and that Schleswig join Holstein as a member of the [German Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Confederation). These demands were rejected by the Danish government in 1848, and the Germans of Holstein and Southern Schleswig rebelled.&#x20;

#### First Schleswig War

This began the [First Schleswig War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Schleswig_War) (1848–51). Against unbelievable odds, Denmark emerged victorious, managing to politically outmaneuver the German alliance by garnering support from the [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire), the [Russian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire) and the [Second French Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_French_Empire) while defeating the Germans at the [Battles of Bov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Bov), the [Dybbøl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dybb%C3%B8l_\(1848\)), the [Fredericia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Fredericia) and [Isted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Isted). However, under pressure from the [Great Powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Power), led by [Tsar Alexander II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_II_of_Russia), who had forced [Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Prussia) and [Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_Empire) out of Denmark, the Danes were not permitted to reintegrate Schleswig into Denmark. Alexander thereby sought to preserve the existing European order, per the principles established by the [Concert of Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concert_of_Europe). This led to the signing of the [1852 London Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Protocol_\(1852\)), which failed to provide a solution to the issue and merely upheld the status quo.

#### Second Schleswig War

In 1863, conflict broke out again when Frederick VII died without legitimate issue. According to the [order of succession](6970425.md) of Denmark and Schleswig, the crowns of both Denmark and Schleswig would pass to Duke Christian of [Glücksburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Gl%C3%BCcksburg), who became [Christian IX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_IX_of_Denmark). The transmission of the duchy of Holstein to the head of the (German-oriented) branch of the Danish royal family, the [House of Augustenborg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Schleswig-Holstein-Sonderburg-Augustenburg), was more controversial. The separation of the two duchies was challenged by the Augustenborg heir, who claimed, as in 1848, to be the rightful heir of both Schleswig and Holstein. A common constitution for Denmark and Schleswig was promulgated in November 1863, which was a breach of the 1852 London Protocol. This left Denmark politically isolated and led to the [Second Schleswig War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Schleswig_War), with Prussia and Austria invading once again. Denmark achieved some initial victories at the [Battles of Mysunde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Mysunde_\(1864\)), and [Sankelmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Sankelmark), but these successes were short-lived. The Austrians defeated the Danes at the [Königshügel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_for_K%C3%B6nigsh%C3%BCgel) and [Vejle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Vejle). However, it was the Prussians who decided the war by decisively winning the pivotal [Battles of Dybbøl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dybb%C3%B8l) and [Als](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Als).

British attempts to mediate in the [London Conference of 1864](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Conference_of_1864) failed. With the peace [Treaty of Vienna (1864)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Vienna_\(1864\)), Denmark was forced to cede Schleswig, Holstein, and [Lauenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saxe-Lauenburg) to Prussia and Austria. Prussia wished to annex the conquered territories, but Austria insisted they be held as [condominiums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condominium_\(international_law\)). This disagreement led to the 1865 [Gastein Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastein_Convention), which, despite its *[casus belli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casus_belli)* being the defence of the German-speaking Schleswig-Holsteiners' wish to remain unified, granted Schleswig and Lauenburg to Prussia and Holstein to Austria. However, this situation did not last long.

### Austro-Prussian War and Province of Prussia

In 1866, the [Austro-Prussian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Prussian_War) broke out, in which Prussia quickly defeated Austria and its allies. In the 1866 [Peace of Prague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_of_Prague_\(1866\)), Prussia gained Holstein in addition to Schleswig and Lauenburg. Contrary to the hopes of German Schleswig-Holsteiners, the area did not gain its independence, but was annexed to Prussia, becoming the [Province of Schleswig-Holstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Schleswig-Holstein) in 1868; Lauenburg remained a separate entity until it was incorporated as *[Kreis Herzogtum Lauenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kreis_Herzogtum_Lauenburg)* in 1876.&#x20;

The Peace of Prague also led to the dissolution of the loose ([confederal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederal)) Austrian-led [German Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Confederation) and the establishment of the far more integrated ([federal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federalism)) Prussian-led [North German Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_German_Confederation), of which Schleswig-Holstein was now part as a Prussian Province; the North German Confederation became the [German Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Empire) in 1871.

Section five of the Peace of Prague had also stipulated that the people of Northern Schleswig would be consulted in a [referendum](51582.md) on whether to remain under Prussian rule or return to Danish rule. This condition, however, was never fulfilled by Prussia. During the decades of Prussian rule within the [German Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Empire), authorities attempted a [Germanisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanisation) policy in the northern part of Schleswig, which remained predominantly Danish. The period also meant increased industrialisation of Schleswig-Holstein and the use of Kiel and Flensburg as important [Imperial German Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_German_Navy) locations. The northernmost part and the west coast of the province saw a wave of emigration to America, while some Danes of North Schleswig emigrated to Denmark.

### Plebiscite in 1920

Main article: [1920 Schleswig plebiscites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1920_Schleswig_plebiscites)



Results of the 1920 plebiscites in North and Central Schleswig

After the defeat of Germany in [World War I](4764461.md), the Allied powers arranged [a plebiscite in northern and central Schleswig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schleswig_Plebiscites). The plebiscite was conducted under the auspices of an international commission, which designated two voting zones to cover the northern and south-central parts of Schleswig. Steps were taken to also create a third zone covering a southern area, but zone III was cancelled again and never voted, as the Danish government asked the commission not to expand the plebiscite to this area.

In zone I covering Northern Schleswig (10 February 1920), 75% voted for [reunification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_union) with Denmark and 25% voted for Germany. In zone II covering central Schleswig (14 March 1920), the results were reversed; 80% voted for Germany and just 20% for Denmark. Only minor areas on the island of [Föhr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F%C3%B6hr) showed a Danish majority, and the rest of the Danish vote was primarily in the town of Flensburg.[^cite_note-20] On 15 June 1920, Northern Schleswig officially returned to Danish rule. The Danish/German border was the only one of the borders imposed on Germany by the [Treaty of Versailles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Versailles) after World War I that was never challenged by the Nazis.

In 1937, the Nazis passed the so-called [Greater Hamburg Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Hamburg_Act) (*Groß-Hamburg-Gesetz*), where the nearby Free and Hanseatic City of Hamburg was expanded, to encompass towns that had formerly belonged to the Prussian [province of Schleswig-Holstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Schleswig-Holstein). To compensate Prussia for these losses (and partly because Hitler had a personal dislike for [Lübeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%BCbeck)[^cite_note-21]), the 711-year-long independence of the Hansestadt Lübeck came to an end, and almost all its territory was incorporated into Schleswig-Holstein, along with the [Oldenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_State_of_Oldenburg) exclave centred on [Eutin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eutin) (for historical reasons known as *[Landesteil Lübeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principality_of_L%C3%BCbeck)*).

### State of Federal Germany

After [World War II](32927.md), the Prussian province of Schleswig-Holstein came under [British occupation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_occupation_zone_in_Germany). On 23 August 1946, the military government abolished the province and reconstituted it as a separate *Land*.[^cite_note-22]

On 9 September 1946, the British and Soviets offered Denmark South Schleswig, in the [Septembernote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Septembernote?action=edit\&redlink=1). In this note, they gave Denmark carte blanche on how to deal with the German-speaking South Schleswigers, whether to integrate them into the Danish state or to forcibly expel them from South Schleswig, as was [being done to Germans in Eastern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_and_expulsion_of_Germans_\(1944%E2%80%931950\)). Denmark's then Prime Minister, [the Liberal Party's](403109.md) [Knud Kristensen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knud_Kristensen), enthusiastically accepted the offer of South Schleswig reunification with Denmark. A survey showed that 75% of the Danish population supported the incorporation, 500,000 signatures had been collected in support of it, and the Danish South Schleswig Association had sent the government a formal request for incorporation. However, the dominating [Social Liberal Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_Social_Liberal_Party) feared that Denmark might again face destructive wars like the two Schleswig Wars once Germany recovered from World War II. Given that the Germans had conquered Denmark in six hours during the [German invasion of Denmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_invasion_of_Denmark_\(1940\)), they believed Denmark would not stand a chance in such a scenario. Therefore, they pressured the Prime Minister to call for [new elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1947_Danish_Folketing_election), where the [Liberal Party](403109.md) did not secure enough votes to form a government. The [Social Democrats](403131.md) won the election with a minority government and could not gather a strong enough mandate for the incorporation of South Schleswig. This outcome created outrage within the Danish population and was considered a scandal.

Due to the [forced migrations of Germans between 1944 and 1950](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_and_expulsion_of_Germans_\(1944%E2%80%931950\)), Schleswig-Holstein [took in almost a million refugees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refugees_in_Schleswig-Holstein_after_the_Second_World_War) after the war, increasing its population by 33%.[^cite_note-23] A pro-Danish political movement arose in Schleswig, with transfer of the area to Denmark as an ultimate goal. This was supported neither by the British occupation administration nor the Danish government. In 1955, the German and Danish governments issued the [Bonn-Copenhagen Declarations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonn-Copenhagen_declarations) confirming the rights of the ethnic minorities on both sides of the border.[^cite_note-Landesportal_Schleswig-Holstein-2-24] Conditions between the nationalities have since been stable and generally respectful.

## Geography

See also: [List of places in Schleswig-Holstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_places_in_Schleswig-Holstein)



Topographic map of Schleswig-Holstein

Schleswig-Holstein lies on the base of [Jutland Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jutland_Peninsula) between the [North Sea](21179.md) and the [Baltic Sea](3335.md). Strictly speaking, "Schleswig" refers to the German Southern Schleswig (German: *Südschleswig* or *Landesteil Schleswig*, [Danish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_language): *Sydslesvig*), whereas Northern Schleswig is in Denmark ([South Jutland County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Jutland_County), [Region of Southern Denmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Region_of_Southern_Denmark)). The state of Schleswig-Holstein further consists of Holstein, as well as [Lauenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lauenburg) and the formerly independent city of Lübeck.

Schleswig-Holstein borders Denmark ([Southern Denmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Region_of_Southern_Denmark)) to the north, the North Sea to the west, the Baltic Sea to the east, and the German states of [Lower Saxony](18435.md), Hamburg, and [Mecklenburg-Vorpommern](38168.md) to the south. In the western part of the state, the lowlands have virtually no hills. The [North Frisian Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Frisian_Islands), as well as almost all of Schleswig-Holstein's North Sea coast, form the [Schleswig-Holstein Wadden Sea National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schleswig-Holstein_Wadden_Sea_National_Park) (*Nationalpark Schleswig-Holsteinisches Wattenmeer*), which is the largest national park in Central Europe.

The Baltic Sea coast in the east of Schleswig-Holstein is marked by bays, [fjords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fjord), and cliff lines. Rolling hills (the highest elevation is the Bungsberg at 168 metres or 551 feet) and many lakes are found, especially in the eastern part of Holstein called the [Holstein Switzerland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holstein_Switzerland) and the former Duchy of Lauenburg (*Herzogtum Lauenburg*). The longest river besides the [Elbe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elbe) is the [Eider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eider_\(river\)). Among the states of Germany, Schleswig-Holstein has the least area covered by forest, 11%, which is less than in the city-states of [Hamburg](13467.md) and [Bremen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bremen_\(state\)). (The national average is 32%).[^cite_note-25]

Schleswig-Holstein combines Danish, Frisian, and German aspects of culture. The castles and manors in the countryside are the best example for this tradition; some dishes like [Rødgrød](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%B8dgr%C3%B8d) (German: *Rote Grütze*, literal English "red [grits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grits)" or "red [groats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groats)") are also shared, as well as surnames such as [Hansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hansen_\(surname\)).

&#x20;The most important festivals are the Kiel Week, [Schleswig-Holstein Musik Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schleswig-Holstein_Musik_Festival), an annual classic music festival all over the state, and the [Lübeck Nordic Film Days](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%BCbeck_Nordic_Film_Days), an annual film festival for movies from Scandinavian countries, held in Lübeck. The [Kiel Week](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiel_Week) is an annual event, except for 2020 and 2021 due to the [COVID19-Pandemic](62750956.md).[^cite_note-NDR-2-35] It took place again in June 2022.[^cite_note-NDR-2-35] The annual [Wacken Open Air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wacken_Open_Air) festival is considered to be the largest heavy metal rock festival in the world.

### Symbols

The [coat of arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coat_of_arms) shows the symbols of the two duchies united in Schleswig-Holstein, i.e., the two lions for Schleswig and the leaf of nettle for Holstein. Supposedly, [Otto von Bismarck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_von_Bismarck) decreed that the two lions were to face the nettle because of the discomfort to their bottoms, which would have resulted if the lions faced away from it.

Government agencies of Schleswig-Holstein use a logo showing a stylized version of the Schleswig Lions and the Holstein nettle combined with the abbreviation of Schleswig-Holstein, "SH". Written either below or to the right of the lion and the nettle is "Schleswig-Holstein" below which either the Name of the agency using the logo is shown or the motto "Der echte Norden" (Germany's true North).[^cite_note-36]



Schleswig-Holstein logo

The motto of Schleswig-Holstein is *"Up ewich ungedeelt"* ([Middle Low German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Low_German): "Forever undivided", modern High German: *"Auf ewig ungeteilt"*). It goes back to the [Treaty of Ribe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Ribe) (Danish: Ribe Håndfæstning German: Handfeste von Ripen) in 1460. Ripen ([Ribe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ribe)) is a historical small town in [Northern Schleswig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Schleswig), nowadays Denmark.[^cite_note-Landesportal_Schleswig-Holstein-2-24]

The anthem from 1844 is called "[Wanke nicht, mein Vaterland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wanke_nicht,_mein_Vaterland)" ("Don't falter, my fatherland"), but it is usually referred to with its first line *"Schleswig-Holstein meerumschlungen"* (i.e., "Schleswig-Holstein embraced by the seas") or "Schleswig-Holstein-Lied" (Schleswig-Holstein song). The old city of Lübeck is a UNESCO [World Heritage Site](44940.md).

### Food and drink

Main article: [Schleswig-Holstein cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schleswig-Holstein_cuisine)

The distinctive point of the cuisine is a combination of sweetness with a taste contrast, like sour or salty. These combinations are also described as "broken sweetness," which is especially present in sweet-sour dishes.

Typical dishes are:

- [Birnen, Bohnen und Speck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birnen,_Bohnen_und_Speck) consist of pears, [beans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phaseolus_vulgaris), [savory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summer_savory), [parsley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parsley), [bacon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacon) and potatoes

- Holsteiner Sauerfleisch is sour aspic

- Holsteiner Katenschinken is ham with a traditional cold-smoking method

- Different uses of [Nordseekrabben](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crangon_crangon) in soup, porrenpann, with toast or scrambled eggs

- Famous is smoked [Kieler Sprotten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_sprat)

- Other fish also is popular: [Flatfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flatfish) or [Herring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herring)

- [Grünkohl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kale). In Schleswig-Holstein, there is a real cult around this vegetable. In the autumn and winter months, groups of friends or colleagues go on a cabbage ride and choose their cabbage king, often combined with the typical regional sports of Boßeln and Klootschießen. The most popular dish is [Grünkohl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kale) with Mettenden, but also other combinations like Grünkohl with [Kassler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kassler) and 'Schweinebacke'. The [Dithmarsch marshland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dithmarschen) is particularly suitable for growing cabbage. The soils are fertile, so that a good yield can still be achieved even in bad years. Due to the constant sea wind, there are far fewer pests in the area.

- [Lübecker Marzipan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%BCbecker_Marzipan) is a sweet made from ground almonds, sugar, and added flavorings

- [Lakritz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquorice_\(confectionery\)) confection flavored with extract of the roots of the liquorice plant (sweet, salt, salmiak, and choco)

- Lübecker Rotspon, [Bordeaux wine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bordeaux_wine), which is delivered in oak barrels to Lübeck to be aged

- Flensburger Rum-Verschnitt, brown mix of imported rum, water, and neutral alcohol (typically 40–42%)

### Languages

The official language of Schleswig-Holstein is German.[^cite_note-37] In addition, [Low German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_German), [Danish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_language) and [North Frisian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Frisian_language) are recognized minority languages.[^cite_note-38] Historically, Low German (in Holstein and [Southern Schleswig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Schleswig)), Danish (in Schleswig), and North Frisian (in Western Schleswig) were widely spoken in Schleswig-Holstein. During the language change in the 19th century some Danish and North Frisian dialects in Southern Schleswig were replaced by [Standard German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_German).[^cite_note-39][^cite_note-40][^cite_note-41]

Low German is still used in many parts of the state. [Missingsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missingsch), a Low German dialect with heavy [High German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_German) (Standard German) influence, is commonly spoken informally throughout the state, while a [mixed language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_language) [Petuh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petuh) (mixture of High German and [Danish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_language)) is used in and around [Flensburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flensburg). Danish is used by the [Danish minority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_minority_of_Southern_Schleswig) in Southern Schleswig, and North Frisian is spoken by the [North Frisians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Frisians) of the [North Sea Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Sea_Coast) and the Northern Frisian Islands in Southern Schleswig. The North Frisian dialect called [Heligolandic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heligolandic_dialect) (*Halunder*) is spoken on the island of [Heligoland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heligoland).

As is the case throughout Germany, High German, introduced in the 16th century, has come to steadily replace local dialects for official purposes, and is today the predominant language of media, law, and legislature. It is spoken by virtually all inhabitants in formal situations. Since the end of World War II and the widespread adoption of TV, radio, and other mass media, it has gradually come to supplant local dialects in urban areas as well.

## Economy



Schleswig-Holstein's islands, beaches, and cities are popular tourist attractions. Shown here is the Isle of [Sylt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylt).

The Gross domestic product (GDP) of the state was 62.7 billion euros in 2018, accounting for 1.9% of German economic output. GDP per capita adjusted for purchasing power was 30,400 euros or 101% of the EU27 average in the same year. The GDP per employee was 95% of the EU average. The GDP per capita was the lowest of all states in West Germany.[^cite_note-42] In 2017, Schleswig-Holstein had an export surplus for the first time since 1989: export 22.6 billion euros/ import 20.8 billion euros.

### Energy

Schleswig-Holstein is a leader in the [country's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renewable_energy_in_Germany) growing [renewable energy](25784.md) industry.[^cite_note-43] In 2014, Schleswig-Holstein became the first [German state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_of_Germany) to cover 100% of its electric power demand with renewable energy sources (chiefly [wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_power) 70%, [solar](13690575.md) 3.8%, and biomass 8.3%).[^cite_note-44] By 2023, according to Schleswig-Holstein Netz, renewable energy sources were providing 204% of Schleswig-Holstein's electricity demand (the 104% surplus are exports).[^cite_note-45]

The largest German oil field [Mittelplate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mittelplate) is located in the North Sea off the [Dithmarsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dithmarschen) coast and connected with a refinery in [Hemmingstedt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemmingstedt) and chemical plants in [Brunsbüttel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brunsb%C3%BCttel) via pipeline. It produces ca. 1.4 million tonnes of oil annually.

#### Nuclear power

There were three nuclear power plants in Schleswig-Holstein: [Krümmel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kr%C3%BCmmel_Nuclear_Power_Plant), [Brunsbüttel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brunsb%C3%BCttel_Nuclear_Power_Plant), and [Brokdorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brokdorf_Nuclear_Power_Plant). The last operating plant in Schleswig-Holstein, the [Brokdorf-plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brokdorf_Nuclear_Power_Plant) was shut down on new-years eve 2021.[^cite_note-NDR-46] There is also a nuclear research center known "Helmholtz-Zentrum [Geesthacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geesthacht)" (rebranded as Hereon) with 2 research reactors, located right next to the Krümmel plant.[^cite_note-Reactors-47]

During the 1990s, ten more cases of leukemia among children than were expected were identified in [Elbmarsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elbmarsch), near the Krümmel plant. Anti-nuclear activists believed it was due to the nuclear plant, which led to several investigations. The reported discovery of small spherical beads of nuclear material in the area led to further concern, as well as the presence of minute amounts of plutonium in the [Elbe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elbe). The origins of the nuclear material were disputed, with one report determining them not to be that of the Krümmel plant. Another report claimed that they may have come from an undisclosed fire in 1986, however, this theory has been questioned as it would have required a substantial government cover-up. The Chernobyl disaster has also been suggested as a source, though it is considered unlikely. The probable source of the material, especially in the Elbe, is nuclear reprocessing plants in France. A 2010 report exonerated the nuclear power plants on the Elbe as the cause of contamination. Further doubt was cast on the nature of the supposed beads of nuclear material, with a Federal commission chastising the original commission that claimed to have discovered the beads. The exact cause of the increased leukemia cases remains unknown, and could be due to other environmental factors, or even by chance.[^cite_note-Leukemia1-48][^cite_note-49][^cite_note-An_almost_perfect_crime-50][^cite_note-51][^cite_note-52]

&#x20;The nuclear plants have further been questioned as a source of the cases due to comparison to the [Savannah River Site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savannah_River_Site) in the United States. Despite the release of radiation at the Savannah River Site, there is no increase in cases of leukemia around it. Alternative hypotheses for the cause of the cases have included [electromagnetic fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field), parental [radiation](25856.md) exposure before conception, other [carcinogens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carcinogen), and [benzene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzene) exposure; however, none have been supported by the existing evidence. Intriguingly, a larger case-control study in [Lower Saxony](18435.md) found a correlation between the "untrained immune system" (as judged as contact with other children, vaccinations, etc.) and leukemia risk, suggested that an immature immune system that has not been challenged is at greater risk for developing malignancy, possibly secondary to an undetermined environment factor.[^cite_note-Grosche_1999-53][^cite_note-HoffmannTerschueren2007-54]

### Animal husbandry



A Holstein heifer

The dairy and cattle farming in connection with fodder cultivation is mainly concentrated on the marshland and the bordering Geest areas. In 2020, around 1 million cattle, including 360,000 dairy cows, were counted in Schleswig-Holstein, ranking 4th among the German states. Livestock is continuously declining.[^cite_note-59]

Schleswig-Holstein is home to the most productive dairy cattle: [Holsteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holstein_Friesian_cattle), which produce an average of 8,125 L (2,146 US gal) per year of milk. It is now the main dairy cow around the world.

Pig breeding is mainly found in the [Schleswig-Holstein Uplands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schleswig-Holstein_Uplands). In principle, Schleswig-Holstein is one of the regions with relatively few pigs (a total of around 1.6 million; in comparison, [Lower Saxony](18435.md): over 8 million). Poultry and sheep are also of little importance in animal husbandry.[^cite_note-Schleswig-58] Schleswig-Holstein had Europe's largest snake farm in [Uetersen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uetersen) with over 600 venomous reptiles, but it closed in 2019.[^cite_note-60]

Total production from fishing in North and Baltic Seas was 40 780 tonnes in 2019, ca. 1/3 German production.[^cite_note-Fish-61] In the [Baltic Sea](3335.md) total production amounted to 10377 tonnes (2019), of which 5432 tonnes of [sprat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_sprat), 2568 tonnes of [flatfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flatfish) and 1190 tonnes of [cod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_cod).[^cite_note-Fish-61]

In the [North Sea](21179.md) the numbers were 19,487 tonnes of [mussels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mytilidae), 3560 tonnes of [North Sea shrimp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crangon_crangon), 1166 tonnes of [herring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herring) and 7062 other fishes.[^cite_note-Fish-61] One important aquaculture product is [mussels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mytilidae), 16864 tonnes.[^cite_note-Fish-61] Inland fishing and aquaculture are not significant, with 221 and 250 tonnes in 2019, respectively.[^cite_note-Fish-61]

### Ports

The state has 46 public ports and landing stages, four of which fulfill international transit functions: [Kiel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_of_Kiel), Lübeck / [Travemünde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travem%C3%BCnde) and [Puttgarden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puttgarden) on the [Baltic Sea](3335.md), Brunsbüttel on the [North Sea](21179.md). Kiel and Lübeck are also important for freight traffic to Scandinavia and Eastern Europe. Lübeck-Travemünde and Kiel are also important ferry and cruise ports. Puttgarden is the German port of the Vogelfluglinie to Denmark. Brunsbüttel is a significant port for bulk goods and also serves as a foundation for the offshore wind energy industry.

## Education

### General education

In Schleswig-Holstein, the [school education system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School_education) begins with a four-year primary school, called *[Grundschule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grundschule)*.[^cite_note-\:0-70] [Compulsory education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compulsory_education) applies to all children who turn six years old by June 30th of the current calendar year.[^cite_note-\:0-70] In addition to the four-year primary school, the secondary level (grades 5 to 10), equivalent to middle school, consists of a two-tier school system comprising *[Gemeinschaftsschulen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comprehensive_school)* and *[Gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnasium_\(Germany\))*.[^cite_note-\:1-71][^cite_note-\:2-72] At all *[Gemeinschaftsschulen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comprehensive_school)*, [Mittlere Reife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mittlere_Reife) can be obtained. Currently, 44 comprehensive schools also offer three additional years of [highschool](554992.md) education, where students can complete the [Abitur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abitur) (higher education entrance qualification) after a total of 13 school years.[^cite_note-\:1-71]

Most *[Gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnasium_\(Germany\))* in Schleswig-Holstein offer a nine-year educational track, with only one *[Gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnasium_\(Germany\))* leading to the [Abitur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abitur) in eight years. Three of the [Gymnasien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnasium_\(Germany\)) offer both three-year and two-year pathways for the [Abitur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abitur).[^cite_note-\:2-72]

The third option to obtain the [Abitur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abitur) is through the '*[Berufliches Gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnasium_\(Germany\))*' (vocational gymnasium).[^cite_note-\:3-73] At the 28 *[berufliches Gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnasium_\(Germany\))* in Schleswig-Holstein, students – unlike in the profile upper stage of regular *[Gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnasium_\(Germany\))* and *[Gemeinschaftsschulen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gesamtschule)* – have the opportunity to choose a specific subject area. The offered disciplines include [agricultural economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_economics), nutrition, technology, economics, as well as [health and social care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_science).[^cite_note-\:3-73] Admission requirements for the *[berufliches Gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnasium_\(Germany\))* include above-average completion of the *[Mittlere Reife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mittlere_Reife)*.Currently, around one-fifth of the high school graduates in Schleswig-Holstein graduate from *[berufliches Gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnasium_\(Germany\))*.[^cite_note-\:3-73]

There are three universities in [Kiel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Kiel) (classical, budget 167.1 M€), [Lübeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_L%C3%BCbeck) (medicine, budget 80.8 M€) and [Flensburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Flensburg) (pedagogical, 37.4 M€).[^cite_note-EducationSH2-74] Six public Universities of Applied Sciences exist in [Wedel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wedel), [Altenholz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altenholz), [Flensburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flensburg_University_of_Applied_Sciences), [Heide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heide), [Kiel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiel_University_of_Applied_Sciences), and [Lübeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_University_of_Applied_Sciences_L%C3%BCbeck).[^cite_note-75] There is the Conservatory in Lübeck and the Muthesius Academy of Fine Arts in Kiel.
