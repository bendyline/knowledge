A **skyscraper** is a very tall [building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Building) or otherwise permanently habitable structure, typically defined as reaching a minimum height of either 100 metres (328 ft)[^cite_note-1] or 150 metres (492 ft)[^cite_note-2] in height; however, there remains no universally accepted definition of a skyscraper, other than being a [high-rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_rise). Skyscrapers can host a variety of spaces, typically including [office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office), [commercial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_area), [hotel](14276.md), and [residential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Residential_area) space. Skyscrapers are a common feature in the [downtown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downtown) or [central business districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_business_district) (CBD) of major [cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City), especially in the [Americas](29833.md), [Asia](689.md), and [Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australia_\(continent\)), often due to a [high demand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_estate_economics) for space and limited availability of land.[^cite_note-Countries_by-3]

The majority of skyscrapers are designed with a [steel frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel_frame) and [shear walls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_wall) that support [curtain walls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtain_wall_\(architecture\)). These curtain walls either bear on the framework below or are suspended from the framework above, rather than resting on [load-bearing walls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Load-bearing_wall) of conventional construction. Some early skyscrapers have a steel frame that enables the construction of load-bearing walls taller than those made of [reinforced concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforced_concrete). Modern skyscraper walls are not load-bearing, and are characterized by large surface areas of windows made possible by steel frames and curtain walls. However, skyscrapers can have curtain walls that mimic conventional walls with a small surface area of windows. Modern skyscrapers often have a [tubular structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tube_\(structure\)), and are designed to act like a hollow [cylinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder) to resist wind, [seismic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seismic), and other lateral loads. To appear more slender, allow less wind exposure and transmit more [daylight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daylight) to the ground, many skyscrapers are built with [setbacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Setback_\(architecture\)), which in some cases is also structurally required.

Skyscrapers first emerged in the United States towards the end of the 19th century, especially in [New York City](645042.md) and [Chicago](6886.md). Early 20th‑century skyscraper construction expanded to several other countries, but slowed sharply during the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression) of the 1930s and did not fully resume until the 1950s. A new wave of skyscraper building occurred in many United States cities from the 1960s through the 1980s. From the late 20th century onward, a significant acceleration of skyscraper development took place, with a notable boom in [East Asia](19605700.md) and [Southeast Asia](28741.md), in the 1990s. China has since built more skyscrapers than any other country. Today, skyscrapers are a global phenomenon, present in over 70 countries.[^cite_note-Countries_by-3]

There are over seven thousand skyscrapers over 150 m (492 ft) in height worldwide, most of which were built in the 21st century.[^cite_note-Countries_by-3] Over three quarters of skyscrapers taller than 150 m (492 ft) are located in [Asia](689.md). Twenty cities in the world have at least a hundred skyscrapers that are taller than 150 m (492 ft), most recently [Singapore](27318.md), [Hangzhou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hangzhou), [Moscow](19004.md), and [Toronto](64646.md) in 2025. The [city with the most skyscrapers in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_cities_with_the_most_skyscrapers) is [Hong Kong](13404.md), with a total of 569 skyscrapers; followed by [Shenzhen](83368.md), China, with 465; [New York City](645042.md), United States, with 324; and [Dubai](211583.md), United Arab Emirates, with 269. The [tallest skyscraper in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings) is the [Burj Khalifa](794957.md) in [Dubai](211583.md), with a height of 828 m (2,717 ft).

## Definition



By some measures, what came to be known as a "skyscraper" first appeared in [Chicago](6886.md) with the 1885 completion of the world's first largely steel-frame structure, the [Home Insurance Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_Insurance_Building). It was demolished in 1931.

The term "skyscraper" was first applied to buildings of [steel-framed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel_frame) construction of at least 10 [stories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storey) in the late 19th century, a result of public amazement at the tall buildings being built in major United States cities like [New York City](645042.md), [Philadelphia](50585.md), [Boston](24437894.md), [Chicago](6886.md), [Detroit](8687.md), and [St. Louis](27687.md).[^cite_note-britannica-4][^cite_note-VisualDictionary-5]

The first [steel-frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel_frame) skyscraper was the [Home Insurance Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_Insurance_Building), originally 10 stories with a height of 42 m or 138 ft, in Chicago in 1885; two additional stories were added.[^cite_note-6] Some point to Philadelphia's 10-story [Jayne Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_L._Johnston) (1849–50) as a proto-skyscraper,[^cite_note-7] or to New York's seven-floor [Equitable Life Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equitable_Life_Building_\(Manhattan\)), built in 1870. Steel skeleton construction has allowed for today's [supertall skyscrapers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supertall_skyscraper) now being built worldwide.[^cite_note-8] The nomination of one structure versus another being the first skyscraper, and why, depends on what factors are stressed.[^cite_note-9]

The structural definition of the word *skyscraper* was refined later by architectural historians, based on engineering developments of the 1880s that had enabled construction of tall multi-story buildings. This definition was based on the [steel skeleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel_frame)—as opposed to constructions of load-bearing [masonry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masonry), which passed their practical limit in 1891 with Chicago's [Monadnock Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monadnock_Building).

> What is the chief characteristic of the tall office building? It is lofty. It must be tall. The force and power of altitude must be in it, the glory and pride of exaltation must be in it. It must be every inch a proud and soaring thing, rising in sheer exaltation that from bottom to top it is a unit without a single dissenting line.

— [Louis Sullivan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Sullivan)'s *The Tall Office Building Artistically Considered* (1896)

Some [structural engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_engineer) define a high-rise as any vertical construction for which wind is a more significant [load factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_load) than [earthquake](10106.md) or weight. This criterion fits not only high-rises but some other tall structures, such as [towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower).

Different organizations from the United States and Europe define skyscrapers as buildings at least 150 m (490 ft) in height or taller,[^cite_note-skyscrapernews.com-10][^cite_note-VisualDictionary-5][^cite_note-emporis.com-11] with "[supertall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supertall)" skyscrapers for buildings higher than 300 m (984 ft) and "[megatall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_megatall_skyscrapers)" skyscrapers for those taller than 600 m (1,969 ft).[^cite_note-CTBUH-12]

## History

### Precursors

The [tallest structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_freestanding_structures) in ancient times was the 146 m (479 ft) [Great Pyramid of Giza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Pyramid_of_Giza) in [ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt), built in the 26th century BC. It was not surpassed in height for thousands of years, the 160 m (520 ft) [Lincoln Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln_Cathedral) having exceeded it in 1311–1549, before its central spire collapsed.[^cite_note-13] The latter in turn was not surpassed until the 555-foot (169 m) [Washington Monument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_Monument) in 1884, which was surpassed by the [Eiffel Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eiffel_Tower) in 1889, the first ever supertall structure. In 1930, the [Chrysler Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chrysler_Building) surpassed the Eiffel Tower by pinnacle height, becoming the tallest structure built until then and the first supertall skyscraper by pinnacle height, only to be surpassed a year later in every regard by the [Empire State Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_State_Building) as the first supertall skyscraper also by roof height.

High-rise [apartments](265567.md) flourished in [classical antiquity](252905.md). [Ancient Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome) [insulae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insula_\(building\)) in [imperial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire) cities reached 10 and more stories.[^cite_note-Gregory_S._Aldrete-14] Beginning with [Augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus) (r. 30 BC-14 AD), several [emperors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_emperor) attempted to establish limits of 20–25 m (66–82 ft) for multi-stories buildings, but were met with only limited success.[^cite_note-15][^cite_note-16] Lower floors were typically occupied by shops or wealthy families, with the upper rented to the lower classes.[^cite_note-Gregory_S._Aldrete-14] Surviving [Oxyrhynchus Papyri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxyrhynchus_Papyri) indicate that seven-stories buildings existed in [provincial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_province) towns such as in 3rd century AD [Hermopolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermopolis) in [Roman Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Roman_Egypt).[^cite_note-17][^cite_note-18][^cite_note-19]

The skylines of many important [medieval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) cities had large numbers of high-rise urban towers, built by the wealthy for defense and status. The residential [Towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Towers_of_Bologna) of 12th century [Bologna](21069333.md) numbered between 80 and 100 at a time, the tallest of which is the 97.2 m (319 ft) high Asinelli Tower. A [Florentine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence) law of 1251 decreed that all urban buildings be immediately reduced to less than 26 m (85 ft).[^cite_note-Werner_Müller_345-20] Even medium-sized towns of the era are known to have proliferations of towers, such as the 72 towers that ranged up to 51 m (167 ft) height in [San Gimignano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Gimignano).[^cite_note-Werner_Müller_345-20]

The [medieval Egyptian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Arab_Egypt) city of [Fustat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fustat) housed many high-rise residential buildings, which [Al-Muqaddasi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Muqaddasi) in the 10th century described as resembling [minarets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minaret). [Nasir Khusraw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasir_Khusraw) in the early 11th century described some of them rising up to 14 stories, with [roof gardens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roof_garden) on the top floor complete with ox-drawn [water wheels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_wheel) for irrigating them.[^cite_note-21] [Cairo](6293.md) in the 16th century had high-rise [apartment buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apartment_building) where the two lower floors were for commercial and storage purposes and the multiple stories above them were [rented](342324.md) out to [tenants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leasehold_estate).[^cite_note-22] An early example of a city consisting entirely of high-rise housing is the 16th-century city of [Shibam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shibam) in Yemen. Shibam was made up of over 500 tower houses,[^cite_note-UNESCO-23] each one rising 5 to 11 stories high,[^cite_note-24] with each floor being an [apartment](265567.md) occupied by a single family. The city was built in this way in order to protect it from [Bedouin](61770.md) attacks.[^cite_note-UNESCO-23] Shibam still has the tallest [mudbrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mudbrick) buildings in the world, with many of them over 30 m (98 ft) high.[^cite_note-25]

An early modern example of high-rise housing was in [17th-century Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Town,_Edinburgh), Scotland, where a defensive city wall defined the boundaries of the city. Due to the restricted land area available for development, the houses increased in height instead. Buildings of 11 stories were common, and there are records of buildings as high as 14 stories. Many of the stone-built structures can still be seen today in the old town of Edinburgh. The oldest iron framed building in the world, although only partially iron framed, is [The Flaxmill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ditherington_Flax_Mill) in [Shrewsbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shrewsbury), England. Built in 1797, it is seen as the "grandfather of skyscrapers", since its fireproof combination of cast iron columns and cast iron beams developed into the modern steel frame that made modern skyscrapers possible. In 2013 funding was confirmed to convert the derelict building into offices.[^cite_note-26]

### Early skyscrapers

Main article: [Early skyscrapers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_skyscrapers)



Built in 1864, [Oriel Chambers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriel_Chambers) in [Liverpool](18081.md) is the world's first metal framed glass [curtain walled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtain_wall_\(architecture\)) building. The stone [mullions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mullion) are decorative.



At its completion in 1928, [Palacio Salvo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palacio_Salvo) in [Montevideo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montevideo), was the tallest [reinforced concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforced_concrete) structure at 100 m (330 ft) high.

[Wainwright Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wainwright_Building) (1891) in [St. Louis](27687.md)



[Flatiron Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flatiron_Building) (1902), in [New York City](645042.md)



[Royal Liver Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Liver_Building) (1911), in [Liverpool](18081.md)

In 1857, [Elisha Otis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elisha_Otis) introduced the [safety elevator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safety_elevator) at the [E. V. Haughwout Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._V._Haughwout_Building) in New York City, allowing convenient and safe transport to buildings' upper floors. Otis later introduced the first commercial passenger elevators to the [Equitable Life Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equitable_Life_Building_\(Manhattan\)) in 1870, considered by some architectural historians to be the first skyscraper. Another crucial development was the use of a steel frame instead of stone or brick, otherwise the walls on the lower floors on a tall building would be too thick to be practical. An early development in this area was [Oriel Chambers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriel_Chambers) in [Liverpool](18081.md), England, built in 1864. It was only five floors high.[^cite_note-27][^cite_note-28] The [Royal Academy of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Academy_of_Arts) states, "critics at the time were horrified by its 'large agglomerations of protruding plate glass bubbles'. In fact, it was a precursor to Modernist architecture, being the first building in the world to feature a metal-framed glass [curtain wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtain_wall_\(architecture\)), a design element which creates light, airy interiors and has since been used the world over as a defining feature of skyscrapers".[^cite_note-29]

Further developments led to what many individuals and organizations consider the world's first skyscraper, the ten-story [Home Insurance Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_Insurance_Building) in Chicago, built from 1884 to 1885.[^cite_note-Verbivore's_Feast,_p289-30] While its original height of 42.1 m (138 ft) does not qualify as a skyscraper today, it was record setting for the day. The building of tall buildings in the 1880s gave the skyscraper its first architectural movement, broadly termed the [Chicago School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_school_\(architecture\)), which developed what has been called the Commercial Style.[^cite_note-31]

The architect, Major [William Le Baron Jenney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Le_Baron_Jenney), created a load-bearing structural frame. In this building, a steel frame supported the entire weight of the walls, instead of load-bearing walls carrying the weight of the building. This was then draped with a stone curtain for aesthetic purposes. This development led to the "Chicago skeleton" form of construction. In addition to the steel frame, the Home Insurance Building also utilized fireproofing, elevators, and electrical wiring, key elements in most skyscrapers today.[^cite_note-32]

[Burnham and Root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burnham_and_Root)'s 45 m (148 ft) [Rand McNally Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rand_McNally_Building) in Chicago, 1889, was the first all-steel framed skyscraper,[^cite_note-33] while [Louis Sullivan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Sullivan)'s 41 m (135 ft) [Wainwright Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wainwright_Building) in St. Louis, Missouri, 1891, was the first [steel-framed building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel-framed_building) with soaring vertical bands to emphasize the height of the building and is therefore considered to be the first early skyscraper. In 1889, the [Mole Antonelliana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_Antonelliana) in Italy was 197 m (549 ft) tall.

Most early skyscrapers emerged in the land-strapped areas of New York City and Chicago toward the end of the 19th century. A land boom in [Melbourne](17306237.md), Australia between 1888 and 1891 spurred the creation of a significant number of early skyscrapers, though none of these were steel reinforced and few remain today. Height limits and fire restrictions were later introduced. In the late 1800s, [London](17867.md) builders found building heights limited due to issues with existing buildings. High-rise development in London is restricted at certain sites if it would obstruct [protected views](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protected_view) of [St Paul's Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Paul's_Cathedral) and other historic buildings.[^cite_note-34] This policy, 'St Paul's Heights', has officially been in operation since 1927.[^cite_note-35]

Interwar skyscrapers



[Empire State Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_State_Building) (1931), in [New York City](645042.md), global standard of reference for the height and length of other mega-structures



[Boerentoren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boerentoren) (1932), in [Antwerp](32149462.md)

Concerns about [aesthetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetics) and fire safety had likewise hampered the development of skyscrapers across continental Europe for the first half of the 20th century. By 1940, there were around 100 high-rise buildings in Europe ([List of early skyscrapers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_early_skyscrapers)). Some examples of these are the 43 m (141 ft) tall 1898 [Witte Huis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witte_Huis) *(White House)* in [Rotterdam](26049.md); the 51.5 m (169 ft) tall [PAST Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PAST_\(Poland\)) (1906–1908) in [Warsaw](32908.md); the [Royal Liver Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Liver_Building) in Liverpool, completed in 1911 and 90 m (300 ft) high;[^cite_note-36] the 57 m (187 ft) tall 1924 [Marx House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Marx_House) in [Düsseldorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C3%BCsseldorf), the 65 m (213 ft) tall [Borsigturm](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borsig%20\(Unternehmen\)#Borsigturm) in [Berlin](3354.md), built in 1924, the 65 m (213 ft) tall [Hansahochhaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hansahochhaus) in [Cologne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cologne), Germany, built in 1925; the 61 m (200 ft) [Kungstornen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kungstornen) *(Kings' Towers)* in [Stockholm](26741.md), Sweden, which were built 1924–25;[^cite_note-37] the 77 m (253 ft) [Ullsteinhaus](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ullsteinhaus) in Berlin, Germany, built in 1927; the 89 m (292 ft) [Edificio Telefónica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edificio_Telef%C3%B3nica) in [Madrid](41188263.md), Spain, built in 1929; the 87.5 m (287 ft) [Boerentoren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boerentoren) in Antwerp, Belgium, built in 1932; the 66 m (217 ft) [Prudential Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prudential,_Warsaw) in [Warsaw](32908.md), Poland, built in 1934; and the 108 m (354 ft) [Torre Piacentini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrazza_Martini_Tower) in [Genoa](47332321.md), Italy, built in 1940.

After an early competition between New York City and Chicago for the world's tallest building, New York took the lead by 1895 with the completion of the 103 m (338 ft) tall [American Surety Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Surety_Building), leaving New York with the title of the world's tallest building for many years. America by far produced the most skyscrapers in this period.

### Modern skyscrapers



Skyscrapers and buildings in [Abu Dhabi](18950756.md), Middle East

Modern skyscrapers are built with [steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel) or [reinforced concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforced_concrete) frameworks and [curtain walls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curtain_wall_\(architecture\)) of [glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glass) or [polished stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polished_stone). They use mechanical equipment such as [water pumps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_pump) and [elevators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevator). Since the 1960s, according to the CTBUH (Council on Tall Buildings and Urban Habitat) the skyscraper has been reoriented away from a symbol for [North American](21139.md) corporate power to instead communicate a city or nation's place in the world.[^cite_note-38]

Stalinist skyscrapers



[Main building of Moscow State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_building_of_Moscow_State_University) (1953), in [Moscow](19004.md)



[Palace of Culture and Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_Culture_and_Science) (1955), in [Warsaw](32908.md)



[Grand Hotel International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Hotel_International_Prague) (1956), in [Prague](23844.md)

The construction of very tall skyscrapers entered a three-decades-long era of stagnation in 1930 due to the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression) and then [World War II](32927.md). Shortly after the war ended, Russia began construction on a series of skyscrapers in [Moscow](19004.md). Seven, dubbed the "[Seven Sisters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Sisters_\(Moscow\))", were built between 1947 and 1953; and one, the [Main building of Moscow State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_building_of_Moscow_State_University), was the tallest building in Europe for nearly four decades (1953–1990). Other skyscrapers in the style of [Socialist Classicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stalinist_architecture) were erected in East Germany ([Frankfurter Tor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankfurter_Tor)), Poland ([PKiN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_Culture_and_Science)), Ukraine ([Hotel Moscow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotel_Ukrayina)), Latvia ([Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latvian_Academy_of_Sciences)), and other [Eastern Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Bloc) countries. [Western European](33800.md) countries also began to permit taller skyscrapers during the years immediately following World War II. Early examples include [Edificio España](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edificio_Espa%C3%B1a) (Spain) and [Torre Breda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torre_Breda) (Italy).

From the 1930s onward, skyscrapers began to appear in various cities in [East](19605700.md) and [Southeast Asia](28741.md) as well as in [Latin America](18524.md). Finally, they also began to be constructed in cities in Africa, the Middle East, [South Asia](21566765.md), and Oceania from the late 1950s.

Skyscraper projects after World War II typically rejected the classical designs of the [early skyscrapers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_skyscrapers), instead embracing the uniform [international style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Style_\(architecture\)); many older skyscrapers were redesigned to suit contemporary tastes or even demolished—such as New York's [Singer Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singer_Building), once the world's tallest skyscraper.

German-American architect [Ludwig Mies van der Rohe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Mies_van_der_Rohe) became one of the world's most renowned architects in the second half of the 20th century. He conceived the glass façade skyscraper[^cite_note-39] and, along with Norwegian [Fred Severud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Severud),[^cite_note-40] designed the [Seagram Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seagram_Building) in 1958, a skyscraper that is often regarded as the pinnacle of modernist high-rise architecture.[^cite_note-obit-41]

Skyscraper construction surged throughout the 1960s. The impetus behind the upswing was a series of transformative innovations[^cite_note-books.google.com-42] which made it possible for people to live and work in "cities in the sky".[^cite_note-lehigh-43]

[^cite_note-44]

In the early 1960s [Bangladeshi-American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladeshi-American) structural engineer [Fazlur Rahman Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fazlur_Rahman_Khan), considered the "father of [tubular designs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tube_\(structure\))" for high-rises,[^cite_note-45] discovered that the dominating rigid [steel frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel_frame) structure was not the only system apt for tall buildings, marking a new era of skyscraper construction in terms of multiple [structural systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_system).[^cite_note-46] His central innovation in [skyscraper design and construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skyscraper_design_and_construction) was the concept of the ["tube" structural system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tube_\(structure\)), including the "framed tube", "trussed tube", and "bundled tube".[^cite_note-Ali-47] His "tube concept", using all the exterior wall perimeter structure of a building to simulate a thin-walled tube, revolutionized tall building design.[^cite_note-48] These systems allow greater economic efficiency,[^cite_note-Britannica-49] and also allow skyscrapers to take on various shapes, no longer needing to be rectangular and box-shaped.[^cite_note-Bayley-50] The first building to employ the tube structure was the [Chestnut De-Witt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plaza_on_DeWitt) apartment building,[^cite_note-books.google.com-42] considered to be a major development in modern architecture.[^cite_note-books.google.com-42] These new designs opened an economic door for contractors, engineers, architects, and investors, providing vast amounts of real estate space on minimal plots of land.[^cite_note-lehigh-43] Over the next fifteen years, many towers were built by Fazlur Rahman Khan and the "[Second Chicago School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_School_\(architecture\))",[^cite_note-Billington-51] including the hundred-story [John Hancock Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hancock_Center) and the massive 442 m (1,450 ft) [Willis Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willis_Tower).[^cite_note-52] Other pioneers of this field include [Hal Iyengar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Srinivasa_'Hal'_Iyengar), [William LeMessurier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_LeMessurier), and [Minoru Yamasaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minoru_Yamasaki), the architect of the [World Trade Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Trade_Center_\(1973-2001\)).

Many buildings designed in the 1970s lacked a particular style and recalled ornamentation from earlier buildings designed before the 1950s. These design plans ignored the environment and loaded structures with decorative elements and extravagant finishes.[^cite_note-Sarkisian_Fazlur_Khan's_legacy-53] This approach to design was opposed by Fazlur Khan and he considered the designs to be whimsical rather than rational. Moreover, he considered the work to be a waste of precious natural resources.[^cite_note-Sarkisian_Fazlur_Khan's_legacy-53] Khan's work promoted [structures integrated with architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_Art) and the least use of material resulting in the smallest impact on the environment.[^cite_note-54] The next era of skyscrapers will focus on the environment including performance of structures, types of material, construction practices, absolute minimal use of materials/natural resources, embodied energy within the structures, and more importantly, a holistically integrated building systems approach.[^cite_note-Sarkisian_Fazlur_Khan's_legacy-53]

## Design and construction

Main article: [Skyscraper design and construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skyscraper_design_and_construction)



The Sky Garden in London's [20 Fenchurch Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/20_Fenchurch_Street) is an example of where top floors are used as restaurants or viewing areas, and in this case, both.



Contemporary skyscrapers in [Shanghai](27643.md)

The design and construction of skyscrapers involves creating safe, habitable spaces in very tall buildings. The buildings must support their weight, resist wind and earthquakes, and protect occupants from fire. Yet they must also be conveniently accessible, even on the upper floors, and provide utilities and a comfortable climate for the occupants. The problems posed in skyscraper design are considered among the most complex encountered given the balances required between [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics), [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Engineering), and [construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Construction) management.

One common feature of skyscrapers is a steel framework from which curtain walls are suspended, rather than load-bearing walls of conventional construction. Most skyscrapers have a steel frame that enables them to be built taller than typical load-bearing walls of reinforced concrete. Skyscrapers usually have a particularly small surface area of what are conventionally thought of as walls. Because the walls are not load-bearing most skyscrapers are characterized by surface areas of windows made possible by the concept of steel frame and curtain wall. However, skyscrapers can also have curtain walls that mimic conventional walls and have a small surface area of windows.

The concept of a skyscraper is a product of the [industrialized age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_society), made possible by cheap [fossil fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel) derived energy and industrially refined raw materials such as [steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel) and [concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concrete). The construction of skyscrapers was enabled by [steel frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel_frame) construction that surpassed [brick and mortar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brick_and_mortar) construction starting at the end of the 19th century and finally surpassing it in the 20th century together with [reinforced concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforced_concrete) construction as the price of steel decreased and labor costs increased.

The steel frames become inefficient and uneconomic for supertall buildings as usable floor space is reduced for progressively larger supporting columns.[^cite_note-lehigh.edu-63] Since about 1960, tubular designs have been used for high rises. This reduces the usage of material (more efficient in economic terms – [Willis Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willis_Tower) uses a third less steel than the Empire State Building) yet allows greater height. It allows fewer interior columns, and so creates more usable floor space. It further enables buildings to take on various shapes.

[Elevators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevator) are characteristic to skyscrapers. In 1852 Elisha Otis introduced the safety elevator, allowing convenient and safe passenger movement to upper floors. Today major manufacturers of elevators include [Otis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otis_Elevator_Company), [ThyssenKrupp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ThyssenKrupp_AG), [Schindler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schindler_Group), and [KONE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KONE).

Advances in construction techniques have allowed skyscrapers to narrow in width, while increasing in height. Some of these new techniques include [mass dampers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuned_mass_damper) to reduce vibrations and swaying, and gaps to allow air to pass through, reducing wind shear.[^cite_note-64]

### Basic design considerations



A view of the interior structural design can be seen in this residential skyscraper that was constructed in [Florida](18933066.md), the [Jade Signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings_in_Sunny_Isles_Beach).

Good structural design is important in most building design, but particularly for skyscrapers since even a small chance of catastrophic failure is unacceptable given the tremendous damage such failure would cause. This presents a paradox to [civil engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_engineering): the only way to assure a lack of failure is to test for all modes of failure, in both the laboratory and the real world. But the only way to know of all modes of failure is to learn from previous failures. Thus, no engineer can be absolutely sure that a given structure will resist all loadings that could cause failure; instead, one can only have large enough margins of safety such that a failure is acceptably unlikely. When buildings do fail, engineers question whether the failure was due to some lack of foresight or due to some unknown factor.

### Loading and vibration

The load a skyscraper experiences is largely from the force of the building material itself. In most building designs, the weight of the structure is much larger than the weight of the material that it will support beyond its own weight. In technical terms, the [dead load](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_load), the load of the structure, is larger than the [live load](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_load), the weight of things in the structure (people, furniture, vehicles, etc.). As such, the amount of structural material required within the lower levels of a skyscraper will be much larger than the material required within higher levels. This is not always visually apparent. The [Empire State Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_State_Building)'s [setbacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Setback_\(architecture\)) are actually a result of the building code at the time ([1916 Zoning Resolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1916_Zoning_Resolution)), and were not structurally required. On the other hand, [John Hancock Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hancock_Center)'s shape is uniquely the result of how it supports loads. Vertical supports can come in several types, among which the most common for skyscrapers can be categorized as steel frames, concrete cores, tube within tube design, and shear walls.

The wind loading on a skyscraper is also considerable. In fact, the lateral wind load imposed on supertall structures is generally the governing factor in the structural design. Wind pressure increases with height, so for very tall buildings, the loads associated with wind are larger than dead or live loads.

Other vertical and horizontal loading factors come from varied, unpredictable sources, such as earthquakes.

### Steel frame

By 1895, [steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel) had replaced [cast iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cast_iron) as skyscrapers' structural material. Its malleability allowed it to be formed into a variety of shapes, and it could be riveted, ensuring strong connections.[^cite_note-65] The simplicity of a steel frame eliminated the inefficient part of a shear wall, the central portion, and consolidated support members in a much stronger fashion by allowing both horizontal and vertical supports throughout. Among steel's drawbacks is that as more material must be supported as height increases, the distance between supporting members must decrease, which in turn increases the amount of material that must be supported. This becomes inefficient and uneconomic for buildings above 40 stories tall as usable floor spaces are reduced for supporting column and due to more usage of steel.[^cite_note-lehigh.edu-63]

### Tube structural systems

See also: [Tube (structure)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tube_\(structure\))



The [Willis Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willis_Tower) in Chicago visibly expresses the bundled tube frame. Tube frame variations are commonly used in tall modern skyscapers.

A new structural system of framed tubes was developed by [Fazlur Rahman Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fazlur_Rahman_Khan) in 1963. The framed tube structure is defined as "a three dimensional space structure composed of three, four, or possibly more frames, braced frames, or shear walls, joined at or near their edges to form a vertical tube-like structural system capable of resisting lateral forces in any direction by cantilevering from the foundation".[^cite_note-66][^cite_note-67] Closely spaced interconnected exterior columns form the tube. Horizontal loads (primarily wind) are supported by the structure as a whole. Framed tubes allow fewer interior columns, and so create more usable floor space, and about half the exterior surface is available for windows. Where larger openings like garage doors are required, the tube frame must be interrupted, with transfer girders used to maintain structural integrity. Tube structures cut down costs, at the same time allowing buildings to reach greater heights. Concrete tube-frame construction[^cite_note-Ali-47] was first used in the [DeWitt-Chestnut Apartment Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DeWitt-Chestnut_Apartment_Building), completed in [Chicago](6886.md) in 1963,[^cite_note-68] and soon after in the [John Hancock Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hancock_Center) and [World Trade Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Construction_of_the_World_Trade_Center).

The [tubular systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tube_\(structure\)) are fundamental to tall building design. Most buildings over 40 stories constructed since the 1960s now use a tube design derived from Khan's structural engineering principles,[^cite_note-lehigh.edu-63][^cite_note-constructionweekonline.com-69] examples including the [construction of the World Trade Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Construction_of_the_World_Trade_Center), [Aon Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aon_Center_\(Chicago\)), [Petronas Towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petronas_Towers), [Jin Mao Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jin_Mao_Building), and most other supertall skyscrapers since the 1960s.[^cite_note-Ali-47] The strong influence of tube structure design is also evident in the construction of the current tallest skyscraper, the [Burj Khalifa](794957.md),[^cite_note-Bayley-50] which uses a [Buttressed core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buttressed_core).[^cite_note-Baker_Pawlikowski_Higher_and_Higher-70]

**Trussed tube and X-bracing:**



Changes of structure with height; the [tubular systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tube_\(structure\)) are fundamental for supertall buildings.

Khan pioneered several other variations of the tube structure design. One of these was the concept of [X-bracing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-bracing), or the [trussed tube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tube_\(structure\)#Trussed_tube), first employed for the [John Hancock Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hancock_Center). This concept reduced the lateral load on the building by transferring the load into the exterior columns. This allows for a reduced need for interior columns thus creating more floor space. This concept can be seen in the John Hancock Center, designed in 1965 and completed in 1969. One of the most famous buildings of the [structural expressionist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_Expressionism) style, the skyscraper's distinctive X-bracing exterior is actually a hint that the structure's skin is indeed part of its 'tubular system'. This idea is one of the architectural techniques the building used to climb to record heights (the tubular system is essentially the spine that helps the building stand upright during wind and [earthquake loads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seismic_loading)). This X-bracing allows for both higher performance from tall structures and the ability to open up the inside floorplan (and usable floor space) if the architect desires.

The [John Hancock Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hancock_Center) was far more efficient than earlier [steel-frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel_frame) structures. Where the [Empire State Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_State_Building) (1931), required about 206 kilograms of steel per square metre and [28 Liberty Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/28_Liberty_Street) (1961) required 275, the John Hancock Center required only 145.[^cite_note-Britannica-49] The trussed tube concept was applied to many later skyscrapers, including the [Onterie Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onterie_Center), [Citigroup Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citigroup_Center) and [Bank of China Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_China_Tower,_Hong_Kong).[^cite_note-Introduction_to_Tall_Building_architectures-71]



The [Bank of China Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_China_Tower_\(Hong_Kong\)) in Hong Kong uses a trussed tube design

**Bundled tube:** An important variation on the tube frame is the [bundled tube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tube_\(structure\)#Bundled_tube), which uses several interconnected tube frames. The [Willis Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willis_Tower) in Chicago used this design, employing nine tubes of varying height to achieve its distinct appearance. The bundled tube structure meant that "buildings no longer need be boxlike in appearance: they could become sculpture."[^cite_note-Bayley-50]

**Tube in tube:** Tube-in-tube system takes advantage of core shear wall tubes in addition to exterior tubes. The inner tube and outer tube work together to resist gravity loads and lateral loads and to provide additional rigidity to the structure to prevent significant deflections at the top. This design was first used in [One Shell Plaza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Shell_Plaza).[^cite_note-72] Later buildings to use this structural system include the [Petronas Towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petronas_Towers).[^cite_note-73]

**Outrigger and belt truss:** The outrigger and belt truss system is a lateral load resisting system in which the tube structure is connected to the central core wall with very stiff outriggers and belt trusses at one or more levels.[^cite_note-support1-74] [BHP House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/140_William_Street,_Melbourne) was the first building to use this structural system followed by the First Wisconsin Center, since renamed [U.S. Bank Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Bank_Center_\(Milwaukee\)), in Milwaukee. The center rises 601 feet, with three belt trusses at the bottom, middle and top of the building. The exposed belt trusses serve aesthetic and structural purposes.[^cite_note-princeton1-75] Later buildings to use this include [Shanghai World Financial Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanghai_World_Financial_Center).[^cite_note-support1-74]

**Concrete tube structures:** The last major buildings engineered by Khan were the [One Magnificent Mile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Magnificent_Mile) and [Onterie Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onterie_Center) in Chicago, which employed his bundled tube and trussed tube system designs respectively. In contrast to his earlier buildings, which were mainly steel, his last two buildings were concrete. His earlier [DeWitt-Chestnut Apartments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DeWitt-Chestnut_Apartments) building, built in 1963 in Chicago, was also a concrete building with a tube structure.[^cite_note-Ali-47] [Trump Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trump_Tower) in New York City is also another example that adapted this system.[^cite_note-76]

**Shear wall frame interaction system:**



The [Cook County Administration Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cook_County_Administration_Building) in Chicago was the first to utilize a shear wall frame interaction system

Khan developed the shear wall frame interaction system for mid high-rise buildings. This structural system uses combinations of shear walls and frames designed to resist lateral forces.[^cite_note-77] The first building to use this structural system was the 35-stories Brunswick Building.[^cite_note-princeton1-75] The Brunswick building (today known as the "[Cook County Administration Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cook_County_Administration_Building)") was completed in 1965 and became the tallest reinforced concrete structure of its time. The structural system of Brunswick Building consists of a concrete shear wall core surrounded by an outer concrete frame of columns and spandrels.[^cite_note-78] Apartment buildings up to 70 stories high have successfully used this concept.[^cite_note-79]

### The elevator conundrum

The invention of the [elevator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevator) was a precondition for the invention of skyscrapers, given that most people would not (or could not) climb more than a few flights of stairs at a time. The elevators in a skyscraper are not simply a necessary utility, like running water and electricity, but are in fact closely related to the design of the whole structure: a taller building requires more elevators to service the additional floors, but the elevator shafts consume valuable floor space. If the service core, which contains the elevator shafts, becomes too big, it can reduce the profitability of the building. Architects must therefore balance the value gained by adding height against the value lost to the expanding service core.[^cite_note-HSW3-80]



Sky lobby at [Central Plaza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Plaza_\(Hong_Kong\)) in [Hong Kong](13404.md) which has clear signage of the floors served by the different elevators.

Many tall buildings use elevators in a non-standard configuration to reduce their footprint. Buildings such as the former [World Trade Center Towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Trade_Center_\(1973%E2%80%932001\)) and Chicago's [John Hancock Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hancock_Center) use [sky lobbies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky_lobby), where express elevators take passengers to upper floors which serve as the base for local elevators. This allows architects and engineers to place elevator shafts on top of each other, saving space. Sky lobbies and express elevators take up a significant amount of space, however, and add to the amount of time spent commuting between floors.

Other buildings, such as the [Petronas Towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petronas_Towers), use [double-deck elevators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double-deck_elevator), allowing more people to fit in a single elevator, and reaching two floors at every stop. It is possible to use even more than two levels on an elevator, although this has never been done. The main problem with double-deck elevators is that they cause everyone in the elevator to stop when only person on one level needs to get off at a given floor.

Buildings with sky lobbies include the [World Trade Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Trade_Center_\(2001%E2%80%93present\)), [Petronas Twin Towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petronas_Twin_Towers), [Willis Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willis_Tower) and [Taipei 101](203801.md). The 44th-floor sky lobby of the John Hancock Center also featured the first [high-rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-rise) indoor [swimming pool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swimming_pool), which remains the highest in the United States.[^cite_note-Emporis-81]

## Economic rationale



Hong Kong's high land prices and geographic limitations justify the construction of skyscrapers

[^cite_note-82]

Skyscrapers are usually situated in [city centres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_centre) where the price of land is high. Constructing a skyscraper becomes justified if the price of land is so high that it makes [economic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics) sense to build upward as to minimize the cost of the land per the total floor area of a building. Thus the construction of skyscrapers is dictated by economics and results in skyscrapers in a certain part of a large city unless a [building code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Building_code) restricts the height of buildings.

Skyscrapers are rarely seen in small cities and they are characteristic of large cities, because of the critical importance of high land prices for the construction of skyscrapers. Usually only office, commercial and hotel users can afford the rents in the city center and thus most tenants of skyscrapers are of these classes.

Today, skyscrapers are an increasingly common sight where land is expensive, as in the centres of big cities, because they provide such a high ratio of rentable floor space per unit area of land.

Another disadvantage of very high skyscrapers is the loss of usable floorspace, as many elevator shafts are needed to enable performant vertical travelling. This led to the introduction of express lifts and [sky lobbies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky_lobby) where transfer to slower distribution lifts can be done.

## Environmental impact

Further information: [Bird-skyscraper collisions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird-skyscraper_collisions)



[The Gherkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gherkin) in London is an example of a modern environmentally friendly skyscraper.

Constructing a single skyscraper requires large quantities of materials like steel, concrete, and glass, and these materials represent significant [embodied energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embodied_energy). Skyscrapers are thus material and energy intensive buildings.

Skyscrapers have considerable mass, requiring a stronger foundation than a shorter, lighter building. In construction, building materials must be lifted to the top of a skyscraper during construction, requiring more energy than would be necessary at lower heights. Furthermore, a skyscraper consumes much electricity because [potable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potable) and non-potable water have to be pumped to the highest occupied floors, skyscrapers are usually designed to be [mechanically ventilated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HVAC), elevators are generally used instead of stairs, and electric lights are needed in rooms far from the windows and windowless spaces such as elevators, bathrooms and stairwells.

Skyscrapers can be artificially lit and the energy requirements can be covered by [renewable energy](25784.md) or other electricity generation with low [greenhouse gas emissions](9528025.md). Heating and cooling of skyscrapers can be efficient, because of centralized [HVAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HVAC) systems, heat radiation blocking [windows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Window) and small surface area of the building. There is [Leadership in Energy and Environmental Design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leadership_in_Energy_and_Environmental_Design) (LEED) certification for skyscrapers. For example, the Empire State Building received a gold Leadership in Energy and Environmental Design rating in September 2011 and the Empire State Building is the tallest LEED certified building in the United States,[^cite_note-83] proving that skyscrapers can be environmentally friendly. [The Gherkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gherkin) in [London](17867.md), the United Kingdom is another example of an environmentally friendly skyscraper.[^cite_note-84]

In the lower levels of a skyscraper a larger percentage of the building floor area must be devoted to the building structure and services than is required for lower buildings:

- More structure – because it must be stronger to support more floors above

- [The elevator conundrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skyscraper_design_and_construction#Elevators) creates the need for more lift shafts—everyone comes in at the bottom and they all have to pass through the lower part of the building to get to the upper levels.

- [Building services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Building_services) – power and water enter the building from below and have to pass through the lower levels to get to the upper levels.

In low-rise structures, the support rooms ([chillers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiller), [transformers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformer), [boilers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boiler), [pumps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pump) and [air handling units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_handling_unit)) can be put in basements or roof space—areas which have low rental value. There is, however, a limit to how far this plant can be located from the area it serves. The farther away it is the larger the risers for ducts and pipes from this plant to the floors they serve and the more floor area these risers take. In practice this means that in highrise buildings this plant is located on 'plant levels' at intervals up the building.

### Operational energy

The building sector accounts for approximately 50% of greenhouse gas emissions, with operational energy accounting for 80-90% of building related energy use.[^cite_note-\:2-85] Operational energy use is affected by the magnitude of conduction between the interior and exterior, convection from infiltrating air, and radiation through [glazing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glazing_\(window\)). The extent to which these factors affect the operational energy vary depending on the [microclimate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microclimate) of the skyscraper, with increased wind speeds as the height of the skyscraper increases, and a decrease in the [dry bulb temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dry-bulb_temperature) as the altitude increases.[^cite_note-\:2-85] For example, when moving from 1.5 meters to 284 meters, the dry bulb temperature decreased by 1.85 °C while the wind speeds increased from 2.46 meters per seconds to 7.75 meters per second, which led to a 2.4% decrease in summer cooling in reference to the [Freedom Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_Tower) in New York City. However, for the same building it was found that the annual energy use intensity was 9.26% higher because of the lack of shading at high altitudes which increased the cooling loads for the remainder of the year while a combination of temperature, wind, shading, and the effects of reflections led to a combined 13.13% increase in annual energy use intensity.[^cite_note-86]

In a study performed by Leung and Ray in 2013, it was found that the average [energy use intensity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_use_intensity) of a structure with between 0 and 9 floors was approximately 80 kBtu/ft/yr, while the energy use intensity of a structure with more than 50 floors was about 117 kBtu/ft/yr. The slight decrease in energy use intensity over 30-39 floors can be attributed to the fact that the increase in pressure within the heating, cooling, and water distribution systems levels out at a point between 40 and 49 floors and the energy savings due to the microclimate of higher floors are able to be seen.[^cite_note-\:3-87] There is a gap in data in which another study looking at the same information but for taller buildings is needed.

#### Elevators

A portion of the operational energy increase in tall buildings is related to the usage of elevators because the distance traveled and the speed at which they travel increases as the height of the building increases. Between 5 and 25% of the total energy consumed in a tall building is from the use of [elevators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevator). As the height of the building increases it is also more inefficient because of the presence of higher drag and friction losses.[^cite_note-88]

### Embodied energy

The [embodied energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embodied_energy) associated with the construction of skyscrapers varies based on the materials used. Embodied energy is quantified per unit of material. Skyscrapers inherently have higher embodied energy than low-rise buildings due to the increase in material used as more floors are built. For all floor types except for steel-concrete floors, it was found that after 60 stories, there was a decrease in unit embodied energy but when considering all floors, there was exponential growth due to a double dependence on height. The first of which is the relationship between an increase in height leading to an increase in the quantity of materials used, and the second being the increase in height leading to an increase in size of elements to increase the structural capacity of the building. A careful choice in building materials can likely reduce the embodied energy without reducing the number of floors constructed within the bounds presented.[^cite_note-89]

### Embodied carbon

Similar to embodied energy, the [embodied carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embodied_carbon_emissions) of a building is dependent on the materials chosen for its construction. Both methods of measuring the embodied carbon show that there is a point where the embodied carbon is lowest before increasing again as the height increases. For the total embodied carbon it is dependent on the structure type, but is either around 40 stories, or approximately 60 stories. For the square meter of gross floor area, the lowest embodied carbon was found at either 40 stories, or approximately 70 stories.[^cite_note-90]

### Air pollution

In urban areas, the configuration of buildings can lead to exacerbated wind patterns and an uneven dispersion of [pollutants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollutant). When the height of buildings surrounding a source of [air pollution](10934212.md) is increased, the size and occurrence of both "dead-zones" and "hotspots" were increased in areas where there were almost no pollutants and high concentrations of pollutants, respectively. This progression shows how as the height of Building F increases, the dispersion of pollutants decreases, but the concentration within the building cluster increases. The variation of [velocity fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velocity_field) can be affected by the construction of new buildings as well, rather than solely the increase in height.[^cite_note-91]

As urban centers continue to expand upward and outward, the present velocity fields will continue to trap polluted air close to the tall buildings within the city. Specifically within major cities, a majority of air pollution is derived from transportation, whether it be cars, trains, planes, or boats. As [urban sprawl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_sprawl) continues and pollutants continue to be emitted, the air pollutants will continue to be trapped within these urban centers.[^cite_note-92] Different pollutants can be detrimental to human health in different ways. For example, [particulate matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particulates) from vehicular exhaust and power generation can cause asthma, bronchitis, and cancer, while [nitrogen dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_dioxide) from motor engine combustion processes can cause neurological disfunction and asphyxiation.[^cite_note-93]

### LEED/green building rating



[Shanghai Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanghai_Tower), the tallest and largest LEED Platinum certified building in the world since 2015.

Like with all other buildings, if special measures are taken to incorporate [sustainable design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_design) methods early on in the design process, it is possible to obtain a green building rating, such as a [Leadership in Energy and Environmental Design (LEED)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leadership_in_Energy_and_Environmental_Design) certification. An [integrated design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_design) approach is crucial in making sure that design decisions that positively impact the whole building are made at the beginning of the process. Because of the massive scale of skyscrapers, the decisions made by the design team must take all factors into account, including the buildings impact on the surrounding community, the effect of the building on the direction in which air and water move, and the impact of the construction process, must be taken into account. There are several design methods that could be employed in the construction of a skyscraper that would take advantage of the height of the building.[^cite_note-94]

The microclimates that exist as the height of the building increases can be taken advantage of to increase the [natural ventilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passive_ventilation), decrease the cooling load, and increase daylighting. Natural ventilation can be increased by utilizing the [stack effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stack_effect), in which warm air moves upward and increases the movement of the air within the building. If utilizing the stack effect, buildings must take extra care to design for fire separation techniques, as the stack effect can also exacerbate the severity of a fire.[^cite_note-95] Skyscrapers are considered to be internally dominated buildings because of their size as well as the fact that a majority are used as some sort of office building with high cooling loads. Due to the microclimate created at the upper floors with the increased wind speed and the decreased dry bulb temperatures, the cooling load will naturally be reduced because of infiltration through the thermal envelope. By taking advantage of the naturally cooler temperatures at higher altitudes, skyscrapers can reduce their cooling loads passively. On the other side of this argument, is the lack of shading at higher altitudes by other buildings, so the [solar heat gain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_heat_gain) will be larger for higher floors than for floors at the lower end of the building. Special measures should be taken to shade upper floors from sunlight during the overheated period to ensure thermal comfort without increasing the cooling load.[^cite_note-\:3-87]

## History of the tallest skyscrapers

Main articles: [History of the tallest buildings in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_tallest_buildings_in_the_world) and [List of tallest buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_buildings)

At the beginning of the [20th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/20th_century), [New York City](645042.md) was a center for the [Beaux-Arts architectural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaux-Arts_architecture) movement, attracting the talents of such great architects as [Stanford White](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_White) and [Carrere and Hastings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrere_and_Hastings). As better construction and engineering technology became available as the century progressed, [New York City](645042.md) and [Chicago](6886.md) became the focal point of the competition for the tallest building in the world. Momentum in setting records passed from the United States to other nations with the opening of the [Petronas Twin Towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petronas_Twin_Towers) in Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia, in 1998. The record for the world's tallest building has remained in Asia since the opening of [Taipei 101](203801.md) in Taipei, Taiwan, in 2004. A number of architectural records, including those of the world's tallest building and tallest free-standing structure, moved to the Middle East with the opening of the [Burj Khalifa](794957.md) in Dubai, United Arab Emirates.

This geographical transition is accompanied by a change in approach to skyscraper design. For much of the 20th century large buildings took the form of simple geometrical shapes. This reflected the "international style" or [modernist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_architecture) philosophy shaped by [Bauhaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bauhaus) architects early in the century. The last of these, the Willis Tower and World Trade Center towers in New York, erected in the 1970s, reflect the philosophy. Tastes shifted in the decade which followed, and new skyscrapers began to exhibit [postmodernist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postmodernism) influences. This approach to design avails itself of historical elements, often adapted and re-interpreted, in creating technologically modern structures. The Petronas Twin Towers recall Asian [pagoda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pagoda) architecture and Islamic geometric principles. Taipei 101 likewise reflects the [pagoda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pagoda) tradition as it incorporates ancient [motifs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motif_\(visual_arts\)) such as the [ruyi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruyi_\(scepter\)) symbol. The Burj Khalifa draws inspiration from traditional [Islamic art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_art). Architects in recent years have sought to create structures that would not appear equally at home if set in any part of the world, but that reflect the culture thriving in the spot where they stand.

### Wooden skyscrapers

Main article: [List of tallest wooden buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tallest_wooden_buildings)

Several wooden skyscraper designs have been designed and built. A 14-story housing project in [Bergen, Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bergen) known as 'Treet' or 'The Tree' became the world's tallest wooden apartment block when it was completed in late 2015.[^cite_note-113] The Tree's record was eclipsed by [Brock Commons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brock_Commons_Tallwood_House), an 18-story, 53 m wooden [dormitory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dormitory) at the [University of British Columbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_British_Columbia) in Canada, when it was completed in September 2016.[^cite_note-114] Mjostirnet in [Brumunddal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brumunddal), Norway,[^cite_note-\:4-115] finished in 2019, rises 85.4 m, and the 2022 construction of the 25 storey Ascent MKE Building in [Milwaukee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milwaukee), made it (in 2026) the tallest habitable structural timber building (or 'plyscraper') at 86.56 m.[^cite_note-116]

A 40-story residential building 'Trätoppen' has been proposed by architect Anders Berensson to be built in [Stockholm, Sweden](26741.md).[^cite_note-117] Trätoppen would be the tallest building in Stockholm, though there are no immediate plans to begin construction.[^cite_note-118] The tallest currently-planned wooden skyscraper is the 70-story [W350 Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W350_Project) in Tokyo, to be built by the Japanese wood products company Sumitomo Forestry Co. to celebrate its 350th anniversary in 2041.[^cite_note-\:1-119] An 80-story wooden skyscraper, the River Beech Tower, has been proposed by a team including architects [Perkins + Will](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perkins_+_Will) and the [University of Cambridge](25978572.md). The River Beech Tower, on the banks of the [Chicago River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_River) in [Chicago, Illinois](6886.md), would be 348 feet shorter than the W350 Project despite having 10 more stories.[^cite_note-120][^cite_note-\:1-119]

Wooden skyscrapers are estimated to be around a quarter of the weight of an equivalent [reinforced-concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforced_concrete) structure as well as reducing the building carbon footprint by 60–75%.[^cite_note-\:4-115] Buildings have been designed using [cross-laminated timber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_laminated_timber) (CLT) which gives a higher rigidity and strength to wooden structures.[^cite_note-\:0-121] CLT panels are prefabricated and can therefore save on building time.[^cite_note-122]
