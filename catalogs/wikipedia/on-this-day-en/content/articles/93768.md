# Government bond

"Government investment" redirects here. For investment by governments, see [Government spending](1203070.md).



U.S. government bond: 1976 8% Treasury Note

A **government bond** or **sovereign bond** is a form of [bond](60737.md) issued by a [government](12229.md) to support [public spending](1203070.md).[^cite_note-1] It generally includes a commitment to pay periodic [interest](146738.md), called [coupon payments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coupon_\(finance\))*,* and to repay the face value on the [maturity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maturity_\(finance\)) date.[^cite_note-2] The ratio of the annual interest payment to the current market price of the bond is called the [current yield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_yield). The yield tends to decrease when the [government budget balance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_budget_balance) improves.[^cite_note-l377-3]

For example, a bondholder invests \$20,000, called face value or principal, into a ten-year government bond with a 10% annual coupon; the government would pay the bondholder 10% interest (\$2000 in this case) each year and repay the \$20,000 original face value at the date of maturity (i.e. after ten years).

Government bonds can be denominated in a foreign [currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency) or the government's domestic currency.[^cite_note-ECB_foreign_currency_bonds_overview-4] Countries with less stable economies tend to denominate their bonds in the currency of a country with a more stable economy (i.e. a [hard currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_currency)).[^cite_note-ECB_foreign_currency_bonds_overview-4] International [credit rating agencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credit_rating_agency) provide ratings for each country's bonds.[^cite_note-5] Bondholders generally demand higher yields from riskier bonds; for example, during the [Greek government-debt crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_government-debt_crisis), the [spread](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yield_spread) (difference) in yields between two and ten-year Greek and German government bonds peaked at 260% and 40%, respectively.[^cite_note-6] Governments close to a [default](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Default_\(finance\)) are sometimes referred to as being in a [sovereign debt crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovereign_debt_crisis).[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8]

One of the first assets resembling government bonds were the forced loans, or *prestiti*, that the [Republic of Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Venice) first issued in 1172 to fund wars and defence spending.[^cite_note-9] These paid a nominal interest rate of 5% per year on the face value, in two half-yearly instalments, and could be sold in the open market for a lump sum.[^cite_note-10]

In 1694, [William III of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_III_of_England) used a syndicate of 1268 investors to purchase debt to fund the [Nine Years' War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nine_Years'_War).[^cite_note-BoE_History-11][^cite_note-12] This syndicate was granted a [Royal charter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_charter), becoming the [Bank of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_England).[^cite_note-BoE_History-11] Much of the initial debt issuance by the English government took an unconventional form by current standards, including [annuities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annuity) and lotteries as parts of their design, but alongside these were a number of [perpetual bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpetual_bonds) offering different coupon rates, and by 1752 these perpetual bonds were consolidated ([consols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consol_\(bond\))) in a smaller number of distinct stocks offering fixed coupon payments, and the bond market took a more recognisably modern form.[^cite_note-13]

In the United States of America, bonds date back to the [American Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolution), where private citizens purchased US\$27 million of government bonds to help finance the war.[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15] Today, the market for US government bonds (known as [US Treasury securities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Treasury_security)) is the largest and most liquid market for government securities in the world,[^cite_note-16] averaging \$900bn in transactions per day.[^cite_note-17]

## Risks

### Credit risk

A government bond in a country's own currency is strictly speaking a [risk-free bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk-free_bond), because the government can if necessary [create additional currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Money_creation) in order to redeem the bond at [maturity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maturity_\(finance\)).[^cite_note-18] There have been instances where a government has chosen to default on its domestic currency debt rather than create additional currency, such as [Russia](25391.md) in 1998 (the ["ruble crisis"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1998_Russian_financial_crisis)).[^cite_note-19] Furthermore, if a government bond is issued in a foreign currency then the government cannot simply create additional currency to redeem the bond, but must instead use its foreign currency reserves.[^cite_note-ECB_foreign_currency_bonds_overview-4]

Investors may use rating agencies to assess credit risk. In the United States, the [Securities and Exchange Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Securities_and_Exchange_Commission) (SEC) has designated ten rating agencies as [nationally recognized statistical rating organizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationally_recognized_statistical_rating_organization).[^cite_note-20]

### Currency risk

In general, currency risk (or [foreign exchange risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_exchange_risk)) refers to the exposure to exchange rate fluctuations faced by investors when purchasing assets priced in a different currency.[^cite_note-21] For example, a German investor would consider United States bonds to have more currency risk than German bonds (since the dollar may go down relative to the euro); similarly, a United States investor would consider German bonds to have more currency risk than United States bonds (since the euro may go down relative to the dollar). A bond paying in a currency that does not have a history of keeping its value may not be a good deal even if a high interest rate is offered.[^cite_note-22]

### Inflation risk

Inflation risk is the risk that changes in the *real* [rate of return](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rate_of_return) (i.e. after adjusting for [inflation](38286.md)) realized by an investor will be negative.[^cite_note-23] Inflation is defined as an increase in average price levels, and thus causes a reduction in the [purchasing power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purchasing_power) of money.[^cite_note-24] A bond issued at a fixed interest rate is therefore susceptible to inflation risk (for example, if a bond is purchased at an interest rate of 5%, but the rate of inflation is 4.5%, then the real rate of return is only 0.5%).[^cite_note-25] Many governments issue [inflation-indexed bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation-indexed_bond), which protect investors against inflation risk by linking both interest payments and maturity payments to a consumer price index. See, for example, [US Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Treasury_security#TIPS).[^cite_note-TIPS-26]

### Interest rate risk

[Interest rate risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interest_rate_risk) is defined as the risk that a bond or other fixed-income asset to declines due to fluctuations in interest rates.[^cite_note-27] [Interest rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interest_rates) and bond prices have an inverse relationship, so bond prices fall when interest rates rise.[^cite_note-28] For example, suppose an investor purchases a ten-year \$1000 bond paying a 3% [coupon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coupon_\(finance\)). If a year later interest rates rise to 4%, then although the bond purchased by the investor still pays a 3% coupon, a \$1000 bond issued after the interest rate rise will pay out a 4% coupon, making the original bond less attractive to other investors, unless sold at a discount.[^cite_note-29]

## United Kingdom

In the UK, government bonds are called [gilts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilt-edged_securities). Older issues have names such as "Treasury Stock" and newer issues are called "Treasury Gilt".[^cite_note-30][^cite_note-31] There are two main types of gilt: *conventional*, which have a fixed interest rate and length (maturity) and *index-linked*, whose interest rate and overall loan amount (principal) are automatically adjusted for inflation.[^cite_note-32] The issuance of gilts is managed by the [UK Debt Management Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debt_Management_Office_\(United_Kingdom\)), an executive agency of [HM Treasury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HM_Treasury). Prior to April 1998, gilts were issued by the [Bank of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_England).[^cite_note-33] Purchase and sales services are managed by [Computershare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computershare).[^cite_note-34]

UK gilts have maturities stretching much further into the future than other European government bonds, which has influenced the development of pension and life insurance markets in the respective countries.

A conventional UK gilt might look like this – "Treasury stock 3% 2020".[^cite_note-35] On 3 July 2025 the yield on UK ten-year government bonds was 4.45%[^cite_note-36] and the official Bank of England Bank Rate was 4.25%.[^cite_note-37] As of January 2025, the [Standard and Poors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_and_Poors) credit rating for the UK was AA, with a 'stable' outlook.[^cite_note-38]

## United States

US government bonds are known as [United States Treasury securities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Treasury_security). They are issued by the [United States Department of the Treasury](53667.md), and the US public debt is managed by the [Bureau of the Fiscal Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_of_the_Fiscal_Service).[^cite_note-39]

The US Treasury offers both marketable and non-marketable bonds; the former can be sold in [secondary markets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_market) before the bond reaches maturity, while the latter is registered to the buyers' social security numbers and cannot be transferred.[^cite_note-40] The US Treasury offers five kinds of marketable securities:[^cite_note-TreasuryDirect_history_of_marketable_securities-41]

1. [Treasury bills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasury_bills): [zero-coupon bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-coupon_bond) that mature in one year or less. They are bought at a discount of the par value and, instead of paying a coupon interest, are eventually redeemed at that par value to create a positive yield to maturity.

   [^cite_note-42]

2. [Treasury notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasury_notes): maturity of these bonds is two, three, five or ten years, they provided fixed coupon payments every six months and are sold in increments of \$100.

   [^cite_note-43]

3. [Treasury bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasury_bonds) (T-bonds or long bonds): treasury bonds with the longest maturity, from twenty years to thirty years. They also have a [coupon payment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coupon_\(bond\)) every six months.

   [^cite_note-44]

4. [Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treasury_Inflation-Protected_Securities) (TIPS): an [inflation-indexed bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation-indexed_bond). The principal of these bonds is adjusted to the [Consumer Price Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Consumer_Price_Index). In other words, the principal increases with inflation and decreases with deflation.

   [^cite_note-TIPS-26]

5. [Floating rate notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating_rate_note): two-year bonds with an interest rate that can change (float) over time, and pay interest four times per year.

   [^cite_note-45]

Interest income from Treasury bills, notes and bonds is subject to federal income tax, but exempt from state and local taxes.[^cite_note-46]

US Treasury Securities are initially sold by the government through an [auction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auction) process.[^cite_note-47][^cite_note-48] Once issued, marketable securities can then be bought and sold on secondary markets.[^cite_note-TreasuryDirect_history_of_marketable_securities-41] [TreasuryDirect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TreasuryDirect) is the official website where investors can purchase treasury securities directly from the US Treasury.[^cite_note-49]
