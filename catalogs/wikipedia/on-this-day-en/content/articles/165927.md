**Flamingos** or **flamingoes**[^cite_note-2] ([/fləˈmɪŋɡoʊz/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help\:IPA/English)) are a type of [wading bird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wading_bird) in the [family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_\(taxonomy\)) **Phoenicopteridae**, which is the only extant family in the order [Phoenicopteriformes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenicopteriformes). There are four flamingo species distributed throughout the Americas (including the Caribbean), and two species native to [Afro-Eurasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afro-Eurasia).

## Etymology

The name *flamingo* comes from [Portuguese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portuguese_language) or [Spanish](26825.md) *flamengo* 'flame-coloured'; in turn, the word comes from [Provençal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Occitan) *flamenc* – a combination of *flama* 'flame' and a Germanic-like suffix *[-ing](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/-ing#Etymology_3)*. The word may also have been influenced by the Spanish ethnonym *flamenco* 'Fleming' or 'Flemish'. The name of the genus, *Phoenicopterus*, is from [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language)&#x20;*&#x20;*&#x3C6;οινικόπτερο&#x3C2;*&#x20;(phoinikopteros)* '[crimson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimson)/red-feathered';[^cite_note-3] other genera names include *[Phoeniconaias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesser_flamingo),* which means 'crimson/red [water nymph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naiad) (or naiad)', and *[Phoenicoparrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenicoparrus),* which means 'crimson/red bird (though, an unknown bird of [omen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omen))'.

A group of flamingos is called a "flamboyance",[^cite_note-4] or a "stand".[^cite_note-5]

## Taxonomy and systematics

The family Phoenicopteridae was introduced by the French zoologist [Charles Lucien Bonaparte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Lucien_Bonaparte) in 1831, with *[Phoenicopterus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenicopterus)* as the [type genus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_genus).[^cite_note-6][^cite_note-7]

Traditionally, the long-legged [Ciconiiformes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ciconiiformes), probably a [paraphyletic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraphyletic) assemblage, have been considered the flamingos' closest relatives and the family was included in the order. Usually, the [ibises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibis) and [spoonbills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoonbill) of the [Threskiornithidae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threskiornithidae) were considered their closest relatives within this order. Earlier genetic studies, such as those of [Charles Sibley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Sibley) and colleagues, also supported this relationship.[^cite_note-8] Relationships to the [waterfowl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterfowl) were considered as well,[^cite_note-Sibley69-9] especially as flamingos are [parasitized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasite) by [feather lice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feather_louse) of the genus *[Anaticola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaticola)*, which are otherwise exclusively found on ducks and geese.[^cite_note-10] The peculiar [presbyornithids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyornithids) were used to argue for a close relationship between flamingos, waterfowl, and waders.[^cite_note-11] A 2002 paper concluded they are waterfowl,[^cite_note-12] but a 2014 comprehensive study of bird orders found that flamingos and grebes are not waterfowl, but rather are part of [Columbea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbea), along with [doves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doves), [sandgrouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandgrouse), and [mesites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesite).[^cite_note-Jarvis2014-13]

### Relationship with grebes



Many molecular and morphological studies support a relationship between [grebes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grebe) and flamingos.

Recent molecular studies have suggested a relation with [grebes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grebe),[^cite_note-14][^cite_note-15][^cite_note-16] while morphological evidence also strongly supports a relationship between flamingos and grebes. They hold at least 11 morphological traits in common, which are not found in other birds. Many of these characteristics have been previously identified on flamingos, but not on grebes.[^cite_note-17] The fossil [palaelodids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palaelodus) can be considered evolutionarily, and ecologically, intermediate between flamingos and grebes.[^cite_note-Gottingen-18]

For the grebe-flamingo [clade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clade), the [taxon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxon) [Mirandornithes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirandornithes) ("miraculous birds" due to their extreme divergence and apomorphies) has been proposed. Alternatively, they could be placed in one order, with Phoenocopteriformes taking priority.[^cite_note-Gottingen-18]

### Species

Six extant flamingo species are accepted by all recent sources.[^cite_note-avilist-21] They were formerly placed in one genus (have common characteristics) *Phoenicopterus*. As a result of a 2014 publication,[^cite_note-Torres2014-1] the family was reclassified into two genera.[^cite_note-22] In 2020, the family had three recognised genera, according to [HBW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handbook_of_the_Birds_of_the_World).[^cite_note-23] A 2026 study suggested a return to two genera, with *Phoeniconaias* and *Phoenicoparrus* sufficiently closely related that merging them into one genus made better sense; when merged, the genus *Phoenicoparrus* (published in 1856) takes priority over *Phoeniconaias* (1869).[^cite_note-Díaz-20] The two genera resulting can then be easily defined, with *Phoenicopterus* having a deep [keel bone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keel_\(bird_anatomy\)), and *Phoenicoparrus* a shallow keel bone.[^cite_note-Díaz-20]

## Description



Two flamingos with their ankles circled in red



[Andean flamingo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andean_flamingo) amidst a flock of [James's flamingos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James's_flamingo), in [Bolivia](3462.md)

The [greater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_flamingo) and [American flamingos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_flamingo) are the largest of the six different species of flamingos, both standing at 120–145 centimetres (47–57 in) tall, and weighing 2.1–4.1 kilograms (4.6–9.0 lb), while [lesser flamingo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesser_flamingo) is the smallest species, with a height of 80–90 centimetres (31–35 in) and weighing 1.5–2 kilograms (3.3–4.4 lb); [James's flamingo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James's_flamingo) is only slightly larger at 90–92 centimetres (35–36 in) tall and 2 kilograms (4.4 lb). Flamingos can have a wingspan as small as 95–100 centimetres (37–39 in) in lesser flamingo, to as large as 140–165 centimetres (55–65 in) in greater and American flamingos.[^cite_note-Hoyo-34][^cite_note-HBW-35]

Flamingos usually stand on one leg with the other tucked beneath the body. The reason for this behaviour is not fully understood. One theory is that standing on one leg allows the birds to conserve more body heat, given that they spend a significant amount of time wading in cold water.[^cite_note-36] However, the behaviour also takes place in warm water and is also observed in birds that do not typically stand in water. An alternative theory is that standing on one leg reduces the energy expenditure for producing muscular effort to stand and balance on one leg. A study on cadavers showed that the one-legged pose could be held without any muscle activity, while living flamingos demonstrate substantially less body sway in a one-legged posture.[^cite_note-37]

While walking, a flamingo's legs may appear to bend backwards. This appearance is due to the middle joint on their legs being their ankle, not their knee.[^cite_note-\:03-38] Flamingos also have webbed feet that aid with swimming and they may stamp their feet in the mud to stir up food from the bottom.[^cite_note-\:03-38][^cite_note-39]

Flamingos are capable flyers, and flamingos in captivity often require [wing clipping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wing_clipping) to prevent escape. A pair of African flamingos which had not yet had their wings clipped escaped from the [Wichita, Kansas](114932.md), zoo in 2005. One was spotted in Texas 14 years later. It had been seen previously by birders in Texas, Wisconsin and Louisiana.[^cite_note-Florida-24]



Mixed flock of [greater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_flamingo) and [lesser flamingos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesser_flamingo), in [Tanzania](30118.md)

Young flamingos hatch with greyish-red plumage, but adults range from light pink to bright red due to aqueous bacteria and [beta-carotene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta-carotene) obtained from their food supply. A well-fed, healthy flamingo is more vibrantly coloured, thus a more desirable mate; a white or pale flamingo is usually unhealthy or malnourished. [Captive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Captivity_\(animal\)) flamingos are a notable exception; even if adequately nourished, they may turn a pale pink if they are not fed carotene at levels comparable to the wild.[^cite_note-40]

Flamingos can open their bills by raising the upper jaw as well as by dropping the lower.[^cite_note-41]

## Behaviour and ecology

### Feeding



Closeup of a [greater flamingo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_flamingo) showing the beak's [lamellae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamella_\(surface_anatomy\)), in France&#x20;

Captive [American flamingos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_flamingo) feeding

Flamingos are omnivores who [filter-feed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filter_feeder) on [brine shrimp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brine_shrimp), [cyanobacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanobacteria), [larvae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larva), [insects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insect), [mollusks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mollusca) and [crustaceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crustacean). Their bills are adapted to separate mud and silt from the food they eat and are uniquely used upside-down. The filtering of food is assisted by hairy structures called [lamellae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamella_\(surface_anatomy\)), which line the [mandible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandible), and their large, rough tongue. By rapidly retracting their head, flamingos generate vortexes that stir up sediment and shrimp. Flamingos further induce directional flows by chattering their beaks, while their stomping creates [eddies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eddy_\(fluid_dynamics\)) to trap invertebrates.[^cite_note-42][^cite_note-43]

The pink or reddish colour of flamingos comes from [carotenoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carotenoids) in their diet of animal and plant [plankton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plankton). American flamingos are a brighter red colour because of the beta carotene availability in their food while the lesser flamingos are a paler pink due to ingesting a smaller amount of this pigment. These carotenoids are broken down into pigments by liver enzymes.[^cite_note-44] The source of this varies by species, and affects the colour saturation. Flamingos whose sole diet is blue-green algae are darker than those that get it second-hand by eating animals that have digested blue-green algae.[^cite_note-45]



Feeding traces from a Chilean flamingo

Though flamingos prefer to drink freshwater, they are equipped with glands under their eyes that remove extra salt from their bodies. This organ allows them to drink saltwater as well.[^cite_note-\:02-46]

### Vocal behaviour

American flamingos calling at the [Stone Zoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stone_Zoo) in [Stoneham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoneham,_Massachusetts), [Massachusetts](1645518.md), US

Flamingos are considered very noisy birds with their [calls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird_vocalization) ranging from grunting or growling to nasal honking. Vocalizations play an important role in parent-chick recognition, ritualized displays, and keeping large flocks together. Variations in vocalizations exist in the voices of different species of flamingos.[^cite_note-47][^cite_note-48]

### Life cycle

Flamingos are very social birds; they live in colonies whose population can number in the thousands. These large colonies are believed to serve three purposes for the flamingos: avoiding predators, maximizing food intake, and using scarce suitable nesting sites more efficiently.[^cite_note-Picket1994-49] Before breeding, flamingo colonies split into breeding groups of about 15 to 50 birds. Both males and females in these groups perform synchronized ritual displays.[^cite_note-Ogilvie1986-50] The members of a group stand together and display to each other by stretching their necks upwards, then uttering calls while head-flagging, and then flapping their wings.[^cite_note-Studer-Thiersch_1975-51] The displays do not seem directed towards an individual, but occur randomly.[^cite_note-Studer-Thiersch_1975-51] These displays stimulate "synchronous nesting" (see below) and help pair up those birds that do not already have mates.[^cite_note-Ogilvie1986-50]

Flamingos form strong [pair bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pair_bond), although in larger colonies, flamingos sometimes change mates, presumably because more mates are available to choose.[^cite_note-Studer-Thiersch_2000-52] Flamingo pairs establish and defend nesting territories. They locate a suitable spot on the mudflat to build a nest (the female usually selects the place).[^cite_note-Studer-Thiersch_1975-51] Copulation usually occurs during nest building, which is sometimes interrupted by another flamingo pair trying to commandeer the nesting site for their use. Flamingos aggressively defend their nesting sites. Both the male and the female contribute to building the nest, and to protecting the nest and egg.[^cite_note-53] Same-sex pairs have been reported.[^cite_note-Bag-Flamingo-54]

After the chicks hatch, the only parental expense is feeding.[^cite_note-55] Both the male and the female feed their chicks with a kind of [crop milk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop_milk), produced in glands lining the whole of the upper digestive tract (not just the crop). The hormone [prolactin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prolactin) stimulates production. Crop milk contains both fat and protein, as with mammalian milk, but unlike mammalian milk, it contains no carbohydrates.[^cite_note-56] ([Pigeons and doves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbidae) also produce crop milk, though just in the glands lining the crop, which contains less fat and more protein than flamingo crop milk.)[^cite_note-57]

For the first six days after the chicks hatch, the adults and chicks stay in the nesting sites. At around 7–12 days old, the chicks begin to move out of their nests and explore their surroundings. When they are two weeks old, the chicks congregate in groups, called "microcrèches", and their parents leave them alone. After a while, the microcrèches merge into "crèches" containing thousands of chicks. Chicks that do not stay in their crèches are vulnerable to predators.[^cite_note-58] When young flamingos are around three to three and a half months old, their flight feathers will finish growing in, allowing them to fly.[^cite_note-\:04-59]

### In captivity



Flamingos at Paradise Park, a zoo in Cornwall

The first flamingo hatched in a European zoo was a [Chilean flamingo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chilean_flamingo) at [Zoo Basel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoo_Basel) in Switzerland in 1958. Since then, over 389 flamingos have grown up in Basel and been distributed to other zoos around the globe.[^cite_note-60]

[Greater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_\(flamingo\)), a [greater flamingo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_flamingo) who was at least 83 years old and believed to be the oldest in the world, died at the [Adelaide Zoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adelaide_Zoo) in [Australia](4689264.md) in January 2014.[^cite_note-61]

Zoos have used mirrors to encourage flamingos to breed. The mirrors are thought to give the flamingos the impression that they are in a larger flock than they actually are.[^cite_note-62]

## Relationship with humans

### Ancient Roman cuisine



Pink flamingo prepared for cooking ([Bardo Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bardo_Museum))

While many different kinds of birds were valued items in Roman food, flamingos were among the most prized in Ancient Roman cuisine. An early reference to their consumption, and especially of their tongues, is found in [Pliny the Elder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliny_the_Elder), who states in the *[Natural History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_History_\(Pliny\))*:

> [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language): *phoenicopteri linguam praecipui saporis esse apicius docuit, nepotum omnium altissimus gurges* \[Translated:] [Apicius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apicius), that very deepest whirlpool of all our [epicures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicure_\(gourmet\)), has informed us that the tongue of the phœnicopterus is of the most exquisite flavour.

— *Natural History*, liber X, chapter 67[^cite_note-63][^cite_note-64]

Although a few recipes for flamingos are found in Apicius' extant works, none refer specifically to flamingo tongues. The three flamingo recipes in the *De re coquinaria* (*On the Subject of Cooking*) involve the whole creature:

- 220: roasted with an egg sauce, a recipe for [wood pigeons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_wood_pigeon), squabs, fattened fowl; flamingo is an afterthought.

- 230: boiled; [parrot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parrot) may be substituted.

- 231: roasted with a must sauce.

  [^cite_note-65]

[Suetonius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suetonius) mentions flamingo tongues in his *Life of [Vitellius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitellius)*:[^cite_note-66]

> Most notorious of all was the dinner given by his brother to celebrate the emperor's arrival in [Rome](25458.md), at which two thousand of the choicest fishes and seven thousand birds are said to have been served. He himself eclipsed even this at the dedication of a platter, which on account of its enormous size he called the "Shield of [Minerva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minerva), Defender of the City." In this he mingled the livers of pike, the brains of [pheasants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pheasants) and [peacocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peacocks), the tongues of flamingos and the [milt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milt) of [lampreys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lampreys), brought by his captains and [triremes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triremes) from the whole empire, from [Parthia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthia) to the [Spanish](26667.md) [strait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strait_of_Gibraltar).

— Suetonius, *Life of Vitellius*[^cite_note-67]

[Martial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martial), the poet, devoted an ironic [epigram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epigram), alluding to flamingo tongues:

> [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language):&#x20;
>
> *Dat mihi penna rubens nomen; sed lingua gulosis*
>
> *Nostra sapit: quid, si garrula lingua foret?*
>
> &#x20;\[Translated:] My red wing gives me my name; but it is my tongue that is considered savoury by epicures. What, if my tongue had been able to sing?

— *Epigrammata* 71, Book 13[^cite_note-68][^cite_note-69]

There is also a mention of flamingo brains in a later, highly contentious source, detailing, in the life of [Elagabalus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elagabalus), a food item not apparently to his liking as much as [camels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camels)' heels and parrot tongues, in the belief that the latter was a [prophylactic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophylactic):

> In imitation of Apicius he frequently ate camels-heels and also cocks-combs taken from the living birds, and the tongues of peacocks and [nightingales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_nightingale), because he was told that one who ate them was immune from the plague. He served to the palace-attendants, moreover, huge platters heaped up with the [viscera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscera) of [mullets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mullet_\(fish\)), and flamingo-brains, [partridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partridge)-eggs, [thrush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrush_\(bird\))-brains, and the heads of parrots, pheasants, and peacocks.[^cite_note-70]

  &#x20;They were first designed by [Don Featherstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Don_Featherstone_\(artist\)) in 1957.
