***Death of a Salesman*** is a 1949 [stage play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stage_play) written by the American [playwright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playwright) [Arthur Miller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Miller). The play premiered on [Broadway](725252.md) on February 10, 1949, running for 742 performances. It is a two-act [tragedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragedy)—the subtitle of the play is "Certain Private Conversations in Two Acts and a Requiem"—set in late 1940s Brooklyn and told through a montage of memories, dreams, and arguments of the protagonist [Willy Loman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willy_Loman), a [traveling salesman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peddler) who is despondent with his life and appears to be slipping into [senility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dementia). The play addresses a variety of themes, such as the [American Dream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Dream), the anatomy of truth, and infidelity. It won the 1949 [Pulitzer Prize for Drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize_for_Drama) and [Tony Award for Best Play](250941.md). Since its premiere, the play has been revived on Broadway five times, winning a to-this-day unprecedented four [Tony Awards for Best Revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Award_for_Best_Revival). It has been adapted for the cinema ten times. In 1999, *[New Yorker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Yorker)* drama critic [John Lahr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lahr) said that with 11 million copies sold, it was "probably the most successful modern play ever published."[^cite_note-\:2-1]

## Background

The genesis of the play was a chance encounter between Miller and his uncle Manny Newman, a salesman, whom he met in 1947 in the lobby of a Boston theater that was playing *[All My Sons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_My_Sons)*.[^cite_note-\:2-1] Miller later recounted that when he saw Manny at the theater, "I could see the grim hotel room behind him, the long trip up from New York in his little car, the hopeless hope of the day's business." Without acknowledging Miller's greeting or congratulating him on the play, Manny said "Buddy is doing very well."[^cite_note-\:2-1] Buddy was Manny's son, and Manny saw Miller and his older brother as "running neck and neck" with his two sons "in some race that never stopped in his mind." When visiting Manny as a youth, Miller felt "gangling and unhandsome" and usually heard "some kind of insinuation of my entire life's probable failure."[^cite_note-\:1-2] Seeing him again in Boston, Manny seemed to the playwright to be "absurd" and "so elaborate in his fantastic inventions," that "he possessed my imagination."[^cite_note-\:0-3] Manny died by suicide soon after.[^cite_note-\:1-2]

Miller had been thinking about a play about a salesman for years. He also had new interest in the simultaneity of the past and present. Miller sought to "do a play without any transitions at all, dialogue that would simply leap from bone to bone of a skeleton that would not for an instant cease being added to, an organism as strictly economic as a leaf, as trim as an ant."[^cite_note-\:0-3] Miller was himself the model of the young Bernard.[^cite_note-\:0-3]

## Plot

The play takes place in 1949. The setting is the Loman home in [Brooklyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooklyn),[^cite_note-4] located amidst a typical row of urban apartment buildings.

**Act I**



Willy Loman returns home (John Gobau in Toneelgroep Comedia's 1950 production of *Death of a Salesman)*

[Willy Loman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willy_Loman) suddenly returns home in the middle of the night, exhausted after a failed business trip to Boston. Worried over Willy's rapidly declining state of mind and a recent near-fatal car accident, his wife Linda suggests that he ask his boss, Howard, to allow him to work in his home city so he will no longer have to travel. Willy complains to Linda about their son, Biff, who is 34 years old and has yet to do something meaningful with his life. Despite Biff having a promising high school football career with many scholarship offers to prestigious universities, he failed in mathematics and was therefore unable to enter a university, and spent years drifting around the Western United States working many odd jobs with no clear goal.

Biff and his younger brother, Happy, who is temporarily staying with Willy and Linda after Biff's unexpected return from the West, reminisce about their childhood together. They discuss their father's mental degeneration, which they have witnessed in the form of his constant indecisiveness and daydreaming about the boys' high school years. Eventually, Willy walks in, angry that the two boys have never amounted to anything. In an effort to pacify their father, Biff and Happy tell him that Biff plans to make an ambitious business proposition the next day.

**Act II**



Willy and his children having dinner

The next day, Willy goes to Howard's office for a non-traveling job in town while Biff goes to make a business proposition, but they both fail. Howard staunchly refuses to give Willy a New York job, despite his desperate pleas and ignoring Willy's 34 years of devotion to the company. Willy then loses his temper and ends up getting fired when Howard tells him that he needs a long rest and is no longer allowed to represent the Wagner Company. On the other hand, Biff waits hours to see a former employer named Bill Oliver, who does not remember him and turns him down. In response, the devastated Biff impulsively steals a fountain pen. Willy then goes to the business office of his neighbor Charley, where he runs into Charley's son Bernard, whom Willy used to arrogantly dismiss as an "anemic" loser back when Willy's sons were in high school. Bernard is now a wealthy and successful lawyer about to argue a case in front of the [Supreme Court](31737.md), and is happily married with two children of his own. Bernard tells him that Biff originally intended to go to summer school to make up for failing math, but something happened in Boston when Biff went to visit his father that changed his mind. Charley then offers Willy a stable do-nothing job, but Willy vehemently refuses despite having just lost his job. Charley, who feels insulted, reluctantly gives the now-unemployed Willy money to pay off his [life-insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life-insurance) premium, and Willy shocks him by remarking that, ultimately, a man is "worth more dead than alive."



The family in a 1950 premiere of the play in Amsterdam

Happy, Biff, and Willy meet for dinner at a restaurant called Frank's Chop House, but Willy refuses to hear the bad news from Biff and constantly interrupts. Happy tries to coax Biff to lie to their father. Biff angrily tries to tell him what actually happened as Willy gets frustrated, withdraws to the restaurant's bathroom, and slips into a [flashback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flashback_\(narrative\)) of what happened in Boston the day Biff came to see him: Biff had come to Boston to ask Willy to convince his teacher to curve his failing math grade so he could graduate. However, Willy was in the middle of an extramarital affair with a receptionist named Miss Francis when Biff arrived unexpectedly, and saw the half-dressed woman with him. Biff did not accept his father's cover-up story for her presence, and angrily dismissed him as a liar and a fake before storming out. From that moment, Biff's views of his father changed and set him adrift.



The funeral scene

Biff leaves the restaurant in frustration, followed by Happy, flanked by a pair of attractive women named Miss Forsythe and Letta, leaving a confused and devastated Willy behind. When they later return home, Linda scolds them for abandoning their father, while Willy remains outside talking to himself. Biff tries to reconcile with Willy, but the discussion quickly escalates into a heated argument. Biff conveys plainly to his father that he is not meant for anything great, insisting that both of them are simply ordinary, mediocre men meant to lead ordinary lives. The argument reaches an apparent climax as Biff hugs Willy and begins to cry as he pleads for Willy to let go of his unrealistic expectations. Rather than listen to what Biff actually says, Willy appears to believe his son has forgiven him and will follow in his footsteps, and after Linda goes upstairs to bed, lapses one final time into a [hallucination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallucination), thinking he is talking to his long-dead estranged wealthy brother Ben. In Willy's mind, Ben "approves" of the scheme Willy has dreamed up to take his own life in order to give Biff his life insurance money to help him start a business, that his funeral will be well attended with all his admirers, and that this will leave Biff feeling "thunderstruck". Willy then promptly exits the house, and Biff and Linda cry out in despair as the sound of Willy's car blares up and fades out. The car crashes, and Willy is killed instantly.

The final scene takes place at Willy's funeral. Linda and Happy stand in surprise after Willy's funeral is sparsely attended only by his family, Charley, and Bernard, who does not speak during the scene. Biff upholds his belief that he is no longer interested in becoming a businessman like his father and decides to go back West. Happy, on the other hand, chooses to follow in his father's footsteps. Linda, who has finally paid off the family’s last debts with the life insurance payout, begins to sob, repeating "We're free..." All exit, and the curtain falls.

## Themes

### Reality and illusion

Reality and illusion are prominent themes in *Death of a Salesman*. The play uses flashbacks to present Willy's memories, but it is unclear whether they are accurate. He makes up lies about his and Biff's success. The more he indulges in the illusion, the harder it is for him to face reality. Biff realizes the problem and wants to face the truth. In this conflict, the play shows how the American Dream could be a lie.[^cite_note-6]

### Tragedy

In several statements, Arthur Miller compared the play and its characters to [Greek tragedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_tragedy). Miller wanted to show that the common man and those with status had much in common.[^cite_note-7][^cite_note-8]

Writing in *The New York Times* in 1999, journalist [John Tierney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Tierney_\(journalist\)) argued that the play was not constructed like a classical tragedy. He observed that the mental illness suffered by Loman was a "biochemical abnormality" that was "not the sort of tragic flaw that makes a classic play." But he noted that "Willy's fate is supposed to be partly a result of his own moral failings, in particular the adulterous affair \[...], he is haunted by the memory of his infidelity and by the fear that it ruined his son's life."[^cite_note-\:1-2]

## Reception

### United States

*Death of a Salesman* first opened on February 10, 1949, to great success. Drama critic [John Gassner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Gassner) wrote that "the ecstatic reception accorded *Death of Salesman* has been reverberating for some time wherever there is an ear for theatre, and it is undoubtedly the best American play since *[A Streetcar Named Desire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Streetcar_Named_Desire)*."[^cite_note-\:4-9] [Eric Bentley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Bentley) saw the play as "a potential tragedy deflected from its true course by Marxist sympathies."[^cite_note-\:4-9]

### United Kingdom

The play opened in London on July 28, 1949. British responses were mixed, but mostly favorable. *[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times)* criticized it, saying that "the strongest play of New York theatrical season should be transferred to London in the deadest week of the year." Eric Keown, theatre critic of *[Punch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punch_\(magazine\))*, praised the production for its "imagination and good theatre-sense", noting that "Mr. Elia Kazan makes a complicated production seem extraordinarily natural."[^cite_note-\:4-9]

### Germany

*Death of a Salesman* opened at the [Hebbel Theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebbel_Theater) in Berlin in the week of June 21, 1950.[^cite_note-10] [Friedrich Luft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Luft) said the play was hailed as "amongst the most important and successful nights" at the Hebbel in that season and that "it was impossible to get the audience to leave the theatre" afterwards.[^cite_note-Studies_in_Death_of_a_Salesman-11]

### India

Compared to Tennessee Williams and Samuel Beckett, Arthur Miller and his *Death of a Salesman* were less influential. Rajinder Paul said that "*Death of a Salesman* has only an indirect influence on Indian theatre."[^cite_note-\:4-9] However, it was translated and produced in Bengali as *'Pheriwalar Mrityu'* by the theater group Nandikar. Director Feroz Khan adapted the play in Hindi and English by the name "Salesman Ramlal" played by [Satish Kaushik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satish_Kaushik), the son was portrayed by Kishore Kadam.[^cite_note-12][^cite_note-13]

### China

Arthur Miller directed the play himself in China, stating that it was easier for the Chinese public to understand the relationship between father and son because "One thing about the play that is very Chinese is the way Willy tries to make his sons successful." Many traditional Chinese fathers want their sons to be 'dragons.'[^cite_note-14]

### Poland

The play's Polish premiere took place on January 23, 1960, at the Nowy Theatre in Łódź. That same year, it was performed by six different theatres across the country. Since then, Miller's play has been regularly performed in Poland, most recently in 2025.[^cite_note-15]

## Productions

### United States

The original Broadway production was produced by [Kermit Bloomgarden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kermit_Bloomgarden) and Walter Fried. The play opened at the [Morosco Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morosco_Theatre) on February 10, 1949, closing on November 18, 1950, after 742 performances. The play starred [Lee J. Cobb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_J._Cobb) as Willy Loman, [Mildred Dunnock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mildred_Dunnock) as Linda, [Arthur Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Kennedy) as Biff, [Howard Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Smith_\(actor\)) as Charley and [Cameron Mitchell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cameron_Mitchell_\(actor\)) as Happy. [Albert Dekker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Dekker) and [Gene Lockhart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_Lockhart) later played Willy Loman during the original Broadway run. It won the [Tony Award](54741.md) for Best Play, Best Supporting or Featured Actor (Arthur Kennedy), Best Scenic Design ([Jo Mielziner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jo_Mielziner)), Producer (Dramatic), Author ([Arthur Miller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Miller)), and Director ([Elia Kazan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elia_Kazan)), as well as the 1949 [Pulitzer Prize](24230.md) for Drama and the [New York Drama Critics' Circle Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Drama_Critics'_Circle_Award) for Best Play. [Jayne Mansfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jayne_Mansfield) performed in a production of the play in [Dallas](53838.md), [Texas](29810.md), in October 1953. Her performance in the play attracted [Paramount Pictures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paramount_Pictures) to hire her for the studio's film productions.[^cite_note-16]

### United Kingdom

In 1966, [Shay Gorman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shay_Gorman) played Willy Loman in a production by London's [Unity Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unity_Theatre,_London) directed by [Harry Landis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Landis).[^cite_note-Chambers-1989-24]

After [Warren Mitchell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warren_Mitchell) had played the title role in [Perth](24355.md), [Western Australia](33613.md), under the direction of [Stephen Barry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Barry) in March 1979,[^cite_note-whatson-25] he reprised the role for the [Royal National Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_National_Theatre) in London in September 1979. He won both an [Evening Standard Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evening_Standard_Award) and an [Olivier Award](16432264.md) for this performance, and was highly praised by [Peter Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Hall_\(director\)). Arthur Miller described it as "one of the best interpretations of the part he had ever seen".[^cite_note-whatson-25][^cite_note-26]

[Antony Sher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antony_Sher) played Willy Loman in the first [Royal Shakespeare Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Shakespeare_Company) production of the play directed by [Gregory Doran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregory_Doran) in [Stratford-upon-Avon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratford-upon-Avon) in the spring of 2015, with [Harriet Walter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harriet_Walter) as Linda Loman. This production transferred to [London](17867.md)'s [West End](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_End_of_London), at the [Noël Coward Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No%C3%ABl_Coward_Theatre) for ten weeks in the summer of 2015. This production was part of the centenary celebrations for playwright Arthur Miller.[^cite_note-27]

The play ran from 24 October 2019 until 4 January 2020 at the [Piccadilly Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piccadilly_Theatre) in London, starring [Sharon D. Clarke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharon_D._Clarke) and [Wendell Pierce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wendell_Pierce).[^cite_note-28]

[David Hayman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hayman) played Willy Loman in a [Trafalgar Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trafalgar_Theatre) production in London's West End, directed by [Andy Arnold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andy_Arnold) which toured the United Kingdom and Ireland in the spring of 2025.[^cite_note-29]

[Paul Mescal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Mescal) will play Biff Loman in a [Lyttelton Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyttelton_Theatre) production in 2027.[^cite_note-30]

### Australia

In March 1979, [Warren Mitchell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warren_Mitchell) played Loman in a production at the [Playhouse Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playhouse_Theatre_\(Perth\)) in [Perth](24355.md), under the direction of [Stephen Barry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Barry). It was later staged in London.[^cite_note-whatson-25]

[Anthony LaPaglia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_LaPaglia) starred as Loman in the Australian production at [Her Majesty's Theatre, Melbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Her_Majesty's_Theatre,_Melbourne), in 2023 and the [Theatre Royal Sydney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_Royal_Sydney) in 2024. The play was directed by [Neil Armfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neil_Armfield). The cast included [Alison Whyte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alison_Whyte) as Linda Loman, [Josh Helman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josh_Helman) as Biff Loman, and [Ben O'Toole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_O'Toole) as Happy. The production opened to positive reviews in both cities.[^cite_note-31][^cite_note-32] Reviewer Joyce Morgan of *[The Sydney Morning Herald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sydney_Morning_Herald)* gave the show 4.5 stars out of 5.[^cite_note-33] This production was staged again in 2026 in [Brisbane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brisbane) and [Adelaide](1148.md)[^cite_note-marsh2026-34] (the cast including [Mark Saturno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Saturno?action=edit\&redlink=1)[^cite_note-saturno2026-35]) co-produced by Torben and Richelle Brookman. By the end of the run it will have completed over 130 performances, in front of more than 125,000 people.[^cite_note-marsh2026-34]
